This study analyzes Egypt’s exceptional reading achievement gains during the COVID-19 school closures, a
period when most countries experienced declines. Drawing on data from the 2016 and 2021 Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS), we find a 48-point improvement—equivalent to two years of learning—with notable gains among boys. Using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) and projected trend models, we
confirm the significance of this rise. Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition attributes only one-third of the improvement
to observed factors. The study finds that Egypt’s progress likely reflects systemic reforms, but more research is
needed to confirm that.

1. Introduction
Egypt has historically struggled with low reading achievement. In
the 2016 Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS),
Egypt ranked near the bottom globally, outperforming only South Africa
(PIRLS, 2016; World Bank, 2018). Data from the Early Grade Reading
Assessment (EGRA) further revealed that just 11 percent of Egyptian
third-grade students met the minimum benchmark for reading fluency
(RTI, 2014). These persistent deficiencies prompted the Ministry of
Education (MOE) to initiate foundational literacy reforms, culminating
in a major competency-based education reform launched in 2018, with a
stated goal of ensuring that all students could read proficiently by the
end of third grade (MOE Ministry of Education, 1999; Langsten et al.,
2022). This reform sought to replace rote memorization with skill
development and critical thinking, supported by digital infrastructure
and revised curricula (Boyle and Salah, 2017; Moustafa et al., 2022).
Early reviews suggest positive changes in classroom practice, though
challenges such as infrastructure limitations and resistance to change
continue to hinder full implementation (Elbadawy, 2020).
Despite these policy efforts, few would have predicted that Egypt
would experience measurable learning gains during the COVID-19
pandemic—a period during which students in most countries experienced significant setbacks. Several studies have found that school closures and disruptions during the pandemic led to substantial learning
losses worldwide, primarily due to reduced instructional time, unequal
access to remote learning, and declines in student engagement

(Betthäuser et al., 2023; Dela Cruz et al., 2025; Jakubowski et al., 2023).
On average, student achievement fell by the equivalent of seven months
of schooling in countries with moderate-length closures, with even
greater losses in countries with extended shutdowns. These trends were
especially pronounced among disadvantaged populations lacking access
to digital learning tools (UNICEF, 2022; UNESCO, 2023).
Yet, Egypt presents an unexpected case. Between 2016 and 2021, the
country’s reading scores improved significantly—even as schools closed
during the pandemic. This paper investigates this surprising outcome.
Specifically, we ask: to what extent are the improvements in reading
achievement associated with the 2018 education reform, and how might
other contextual factors, including responses to COVID-19, have
contributed?
A growing body of research identifies key determinants of student
cognitive outcomes and the role of education policies in shaping
learning trajectories. Hanushek and Woessmann (2011) highlight the
importance of student background, school quality, and systemic governance as primary drivers of learning, emphasizing that institutional
factors can play as large a role as individual characteristics. Evans and
Popova (2016) synthesize evidence from multiple reviews and find that
interventions such as improved pedagogy, teacher training, and
accountability reforms are most effective in improving student outcomes, particularly in low- and middle-income contexts. Banerjee et al.
(2007) show that well-designed remedial education and
technology-supported instruction can significantly boost learning
among the lowest-performing students. Pradhan et al. (2014) provide

While other countries also implemented education reforms during
this period, few achieved measurable progress amid the global learning
crisis. While most countries experienced learning losses during the
COVID-19 pandemic, largely due to prolonged school closures, the
magnitude and direction of these effects varied. A review of learning
outcomes across 41 countries found that nearly all experienced some
degree of learning loss, with the sole exception of Sweden, which kept
schools open during the pandemic (Patrinos, 2023). In most cases, instruction shifted to distance learning—more accurately described as
emergency remote teaching—and the duration of school closures was
positively correlated with the extent of learning loss (Goldhaber et al.,
2022). Just a few countries defied this global trend. For example,
Denmark showed minimal or no learning losses in the early grades.
Birkelund and Karlson (2023) found that students in grades 2 and 4
improved by about five percentile points, while grade 8 students experienced a modest three-point decline. These outcomes may be partly
explained by shorter school closures for younger students compared to
older students, along with effective mitigation policies. Similarly, Iqbal
and Patrinos (2025) show that in Uzbekistan, despite school closures,
learning in mathematics among grade 5 students improved significantly—by 0.29 standard deviations (SD) across cohorts, and by 0.72
SDs in a panel of students tracked from 2019 to 2021. Their study attributes this resilience to targeted interventions and systemic efforts to
ensure continuity of learning. These exceptions illustrate that learning
loss was not universal and that policy responses played a critical role in
shaping outcomes. Learning continuity through digital infrastructure,
one of the components of the Egyptian reform, can promote test score
gains, especially for academically weaker students (Muralidharan et al.,
2019).
Understanding Egypt’s experience may thus offer insights into how
systems can become more resilient and effective—even under adverse
conditions. We begin by comparing Egypt’s performance to global
trends in PIRLS, then apply a decomposition framework to assess the
potential role of observed characteristics and systemic changes.
This paper focuses on changes in student achievement in Egypt between 2016 and 2021. The reform launched in 2018 was one of the
factors that could affect achievement change. The 2016 data would in
this case reflect achievement before the implementation of the reform,
while 2021 data could reflect, at least partly, the expected positive effects of the reform. However, the change between 2016 and 2021 was
also affected by the COVID-19 pandemic and related school closures.
This paper tries to decompose these two changes by first estimating the
change in student achievement in Egypt comparing to global achievement trends and, second, by decomposing the achievement change to
see to what extent the factors related to reading instruction, school
environment and digital infrastructure that were targeted by the reform
could be also related to achievement change.
This paper does not aim to establish definitive causal claims about
the reform’s effectiveness. Rather, it documents an unusual and policyrelevant pattern in student learning during a period of global crisis,
offering a foundation for future causal and comparative research.

PIRLS is a cross-sectional survey that in each round collects a sample
of students representative to the population of all students in the 4th
grade or in the grade where most 10-year-olds are taught in each
country. In our approach we estimate time trends using cross-sectional
samples and adjusting for changes in student socio-demographic characteristics or the mode of the assessment. Thus, effectively the results are
estimated using longitudinal panel data with the average country-level
results but adjusted for within-year differences using individual student characteristics.
PIRLS data include the plausible values measuring reading achievement and information on student socio-demographic characteristics (e.
g., age, grade, and gender). In addition, microdata from PIRLS allow to
estimate effects for students at different achievement levels, testing
whether the achievement changes across time are similar for low- and
high-achieving students.

(Jakubowski et al., 2023). We estimate a linear regression model on
pooled data from all assessments and all countries, assuming a common

change in student achievement over time, but with a possibility to estimate country-specific changes in achievement in the last round.
In general, PIRLS surveys 4th-grade students who are around ten
years old at the assessment time. However, students might take the test
earlier or later. Some are repeating, and some are advancing a grade.
Also, some countries decided to test students in 3rd, 5th, or even 6th
grade. Moreover, while most countries sampled the same population
over the years, some changed the target population. Finally, due to the
pandemic, some countries postponed the assessment to the beginning of
the fifth grade. To consider these differences, we additionally control for
student age (10 dummies, one for each age decile, to allow for a nonlinear age effect), student grade, and for the average age effect calculated as a mean age for each round and country.
PIRLS data are collected through a complex stratified survey with
schools sampled as primary sampling units and classrooms with students
sampled at the second stage. We used the jackknife replicate weights
provided by the PIRLS database to estimate the sampling error. We use
plausible values for reading achievement to adjust for the measurement
error.

Second, to further understand the factors behind the achievement
change in Egypt, we apply decomposition methods to understand what
variables are associated with the change between 2016 and 2021. The
Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition technique was originally used in labor
economics to decompose earnings gaps and to estimate the level of
discrimination. The decomposition method was popularized in the
economics literature by Oaxaca (1973) and Blinder (1973), although it
was used earlier in demography (Kitagawa, 1955). Although in the
economics literature it was first used to analyze the determinants of
male/female earnings differentials, the decomposition technique has
been used since to analyze other social issues, including education,
where it can be used to assess how much of a gap is due to differences in
observed characteristics (explained variation) – such as test scores or
learning levels—that can be attributed to changes in observable characteristics over time or between groups, such as student demographics,
school inputs, instructional practices, while the unexplained portion
(sometimes called the coefficient effect) captures the part of the
outcome change not attributable to changes in observable characteristics, reflecting changes in returns to characteristics, unobserved factors,
measurement error or structural change (systemic shifts, reforms), and
policy shifts (Ammermueller, 2007; Barrera-Osorio et al., 2011; Fortin
et al., 2011; Jann, 2008; Rodríguez-Planas, 2012). It is in this sense that
we use it here.
We apply the decomposition technique to analyze the increase in
Egypt’s reading score. The gap over time is decomposed into its constituent components based on the estimation of cognitive achievement
production functions. To understand decomposition results we also
compare the average values for each explanatory variable between 2016
and 2021, and we also compare regression coefficients between these
two editions of PIRLS in Egypt.
A set of explanatory variables was constructed from teacher and
principal responses related to reading instruction and school environment. In addition, individual student characteristics like gender, socioeconomic background, and preschool attendance were used to increase
explanatory power of the model but also to control for any changes in
these aspects between 2016 and 2021. The variables were chosen to
explain how potential changes in reading instruction and school environment could be related to achievement changes. The construction of
questions and the selection of indices was also driven by the PIRLS
theoretical framework (see Mullis and Martin, 2019). Finally, the choice
of explanatory variables was limited to those with available data in both
2016 and 2021, thus, questions asked in one of the PIRLS rounds were
naturally excluded from the analyses.
To facilitate regression and decomposition analysis, individual items
(teacher responses) measuring reading activities, instruction, and postreading questioning were used to construct three indices. These
indices were estimated using IRT 2PL (Item Response Theory Two
Parameter Model) and IRT GPRM (Item Response Theory Graded Partial
Response Model) models on the 2016 and 2021 data pooled. Similarly,
the socioeconomic status (SES) index was constructed using information
on the number of books, parents’ education, and home possessions

While most countries suffered significant learning losses during
COVID-19 as a result of the school closures (Jakubowski et al., 2023), a
few improved their scores. The biggest improvements are in Malta and
several Middle East and North African countries. Fig. 1 presents the

The model-based estimates are obtained by assuming a common time

The first bar shows the raw unadjusted change in PIRLS results for Egypt,

model-based estimates for Egypt are larger than raw estimates as other

3.1. Differences between student groups
The differences in reading achievement within Egypt are larger than
on average in PIRLS. Table 2 compares SDs of student achievement in
2016 and 2021. As the SD on the PIRLS scale is equal to 100, these data
show variation in student achievement is larger in Egypt. Table 2 also
provides results by gender, showing a larger variation among boys.
Student reading achievement variation decreased from 2016 to
2021. Moreover, the decrease in reading achievement variation between
2016 and 2021 is larger among boys. On the other hand, achievement
still varies more among boys than among girls.
In Egypt, as in most countries, girls perform better in reading. In
2016, the gender gap was around 37 points. However, between 2016
and 2021, the gender gap diminished to 16 points. Fig. 3 shows that the
overall improvement of reading achievement in Egypt between 2016
and 2021 was driven by large improvements among the lowestachieving students, especially boys. Fig. 3 also compares reading
achievement at quantiles, from the 1st decile (the lowest-achieving
students) to the 9th decile (the highest-achieving students). Performance of the lowest-achieving boys (1st and 2nd deciles) improved by
around 80 points, which is around 2/3 of a SD. Performance of the
lowest-achieving girls improved by around 40–50 points, which is less
than ½ of a SD. At the top of the reading achievement distribution,
performance of girls improved by around 30 points and of boys around
40 points.

The means of the variables used in regression and decomposition for
2016 and 2021 are presented in Table 3. Comparing the values of the
variables across 2016 and 2021, one can note a considerable improvement in reading achievement. The values of the three indices capturing
characteristics of instruction in the national language decreased between 2016 and 2021. However, only the index of reading activities and
frequency of homework showed statistically significantly lower values
in 2021. This can indicate a more balanced approach to reading instruction, with less focus on single activities. Principal opinions about
the school’s characteristics are more positive in 2021, with the difference larger and statistically significant in the imputed sample. Teacher
and principal experience increased, as did their formal education levels,
which might be related to teacher age. Finally, access to digital books
and the number of computers available for the 4th graders’ instruction
increased between 2016 and 2021.
The regression results explaining student reading achievement using
the same variables separately for 2016 and 2021 data are presented in
Table 4. Note that the original estimation sample is much smaller than
the available sample due to missing values (mainly information provided by teachers and principals). As reported in Annex Table 3, the full
sample for Egypt in 2016 is 6957 and in 2021 it is 7979. Thus, the
sample size used for the regression and decomposition analyses without
imputation uses only 67 percent for 2016 data and 72 percent of the
original data. To check if the results are not biased for this reason, we
also present regression and decomposition results based on the fully
imputed sample.
The SES index and gender are strongly associated with reading
achievement. Also, the index of principal opinions and participation in
preschool education (only in imputed sample) have moderately positive
relation with achievement. The final column of the table provides results
of the statistical test for the change in the coefficients between years.
Only the decline in the coefficients for SES is statistically significant at
the 0.01 level. Change in the coefficient for the principal formal education level is statistically significant at the 0.05 level, even though the
coefficients themselves are not significant, and on the imputed sample
also changes in the gender difference and reading homework, even
though the coefficients themselves are not significant, are significant at
this level. The decline in the coefficient for SES could be interpreted as
showing the narrowing disparities between students with different socioeconomic background.
For most variables, individual coefficients are insignificant showing
lack of or relatively weak relationship with achievement, and also that
this relationship did not change between 2016 and 2021. However,
insignificant results might be caused by collinearity of variables that
focus on the same aspects, for example, reading instruction. For these
reasons, in the decomposition analysis we focus on comparisons for
groups of variables, for example, all variables related to reading instruction, rather than individual indices.

4.1. Oaxaca decomposition
The Oaxaca decomposition results are presented in Tables 5 and 6,
assuming the same regression coefficients for the pooled sample from
2016 and 2021. Table 5 is based on a model with all explanatory variables. Table 6 reports a more parsimonious model, only with variables
significantly associated with changes in reading achievement over time.
In Table 5, the first column shows effects for all variables, while the
second column reports combined effects for groups of variables.
One-third of the improvement in reading achievement (15.2 points
out of 46.2 points) is associated with changes in the explanatory variables. Thus, the decomposition model associates the increase in
6

H.A. Patrinos et al.

International Journal of Educational Development 117 (2025) 103371

presented in Table 5.
This means that most of the test score increase was unexplained, or,
in other words, was due to changes in the returns to the characteristics
rather than due to changes in the characteristics themselves. The unexplained portion (sometimes called the coefficient effect) captures the
part of the outcome change not attributable to changes in observable
characteristics (student background, school inputs, teaching practices).
Instead, it reflects changes in the returns to these characteristics – that is,
how effective a given input is in improving outcomes across time periods
(Fortin et al., 2011). It also absorbs the influence of unobserved or
omitted factors – such as motivation or fidelity of reform implementation (Rodríguez-Planas, 2012). Additionally, the unexplained portion
may capture measurement error or structural changes in the education
system (Jann, 2008). Thus, when analyzing the change in Egypt’s
reading achievement from 2016 to 2021, the unexplained portion can be
interpreted as reflecting both latent effects of the 2018 education reform
not fully captured in observed variables and pandemic-related disruptions that changed the effectiveness of school inputs. Nevertheless,
Egypt was able to better educate its students in 2021 – in terms of being
able to transform given inputs into higher student performance over
time more efficiently – regardless of the characteristics of those students.
The effects estimated in the decomposition analysis on the original
sample are summarized in Fig. 4. Note that on the imputed sample only
the effect of SES and the principal’s opinion about school characteristics
remain significantly related to the change in achievement. In what follows, we focus on the principal-provided characteristics of schools and
how they are associated with reading improvements.
Regarding school characteristics as evaluated by school principals,
only two significantly improved between 2016 and 2021. Principals
claim that (1) collaboration to plan instruction and (2) parental
involvement increased. Fig. 5 compares percentages of students in
schools with different responses regarding collaboration to plan instruction in 2016 and 2021. While in 2016 more than 30 percent of
students were in schools with medium or low collaboration, in 2021 less
than 10 percent were in such schools. Despite the increase in the share of
schools claiming high teacher collaboration, and despite that this group
of schools scored higher already in 2016, the reading performance in
schools with high collaboration increased further by around 40 points
between 2016 and 2021 (about 37 percent of a SD). The reading
achievement also improved among students in schools reporting medium or low collaboration, but the increase was lower (by 16 points or
15 percent of a SD).
A similar analysis of the principal’s opinion about parental
involvement is shown in Fig. 6, which is less favorable than the opinion
about teacher collaboration. Regardless, in 2021, around 27 percent of
students in schools where principals evaluated parental involvement
positively, compared to only 14 percent in 2016. The reading achievement improved in this group despite that it doubled in size, but the
improvement was relatively small, by around 14 points (13 percent of a
SD). On the other hand, the reading achievement improved significantly
in the group with medium or low involvement, from 326 to 374 points.
Consequently, in 2021, the difference between schools with low, medium, or high parental involvement was much smaller than in 2016.

5. Conclusion
Students in most countries were adversely affected by the COVID-19
pandemic-induced school closures. However, not all countries experienced losses. In fact, some improved outcomes. Egypt is one such
country. We use evidence from the Progress in International Reading
Literacy Study (PIRLS) to estimate achievement change controlling for
the overall time trend in participating countries and changes in student
characteristics. Reading scores in Egypt between 2016 and 2021
increased by 48 points, almost two years’ worth of learning. We model
the change in student achievement in 2021 by predicting the deviation
of the most recent 2021 results from an estimated linear trend in reading

Only one-third of the improvement in reading achievement is associated with changes in the explanatory variables. Of these, the improved
results are mainly explained by increased socioeconomic status.
Improved school characteristics and improved access to digital textbooks are associated with small positive changes. This means that most
of the test score increase was unexplained, or, in other words, was due to
changes in the returns to the characteristics rather than due to changes
in the characteristics themselves. Egypt was able to better educate its
students in 2021 – in terms of being able to transform given inputs into
higher student performance over time more efficiently – regardless of
the characteristics of those students. How much of the positive change is
due to the reform is a subject for future research.
Information provided by school principals helps identify potential
drivers of the observed learning gains and offers avenues for further
research. Principals reported increases in instructional collaboration
and parental engagement during the reform period. Whereas in 2016
over 30 percent of students were enrolled in schools with medium or low
teacher collaboration, by 2021 this share had fallen below 10 percent.
Notably, reading performance in highly collaborative schools improved
by 40 points between 2016 and 2021. These trends align with the
explained portion of the Oaxaca decomposition, which attributes gains

to observable factors such as reading instruction, school environment,
and digital infrastructure—consistent with the goals of Egypt’s 2018
education reform (Fortin et al., 2011). Still, a substantial share of the
overall improvement remains unexplained, indicating that unmeasured
elements—such as broader system resilience or behavioral adaptations—may have contributed, particularly for boys and
lower-performing students. For example, targeted catch-up programs,
increased parental involvement, or an intensified focus on foundational
skills may have mitigated pandemic-related setbacks. These findings
underscore the importance of equity-sensitive policies in times of crisis
and the need for further research to understand and institutionalize
effective responses.
