This study investigates the utilization of digital resources among students across three economic categories:
lower-middle-income countries, upper-middle-income countries, and high-income countries. This study focuses
on several dimensions, including digital resource usage, student engagement, self-efficacy, subject learning, and
feedback mechanisms. Leveraging data from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022,
we analyzed responses from 237,844 students. Statistical techniques included analysis of variance and structural
equation modeling. The findings reveal that students from high-income countries demonstrate higher digital
resource utilization and self-efficacy compared to their counterparts in upper-middle-income and lower-middleincome countries. However, educational software and digital feedback tools remain underutilized across all
economic contexts. Digital resources for language, mathematics, and science learning exhibit poor usage patterns. A significant proportion of students rarely utilize these resources for academic purposes, raising concerns
about digital distractions. Upper-middle-income countries’ students engage more actively in digital learning
activities outside the classroom compared to their peers in lower-middle-income and high-income countries.
Policymakers and educators should address disparities in digital resource utilization to enhance educational
equity and bridge gaps, particularly in resource-limited settings.

1. Introduction
Despite the global emphasis on digital education, the digital competencies of school students, as well as the use and adoption of digital
resources, differ greatly among countries with varying economic statuses (Timotheou et al., 2023). High-income countries, upper
middle-income countries, and lower middle-income countries display
unique patterns of digital resource usage due to differences in infrastructure and technology access. For example, high-income countries
typically have better infrastructure and detailed backup plans (Derder
et al., 2023). These differences raise important questions about the
effective use of digital resources in diverse economic contexts and their
influence on educational outcomes (United Nations Children’s Fund,
2021). In the digital age, the integration of technology into education

systems has become a pivotal factor in shaping learning experiences
both inside and outside school environments (Haleem et al., 2022),
thereby influencing student outcomes worldwide (Timotheou et al.,
2023). Gallup (2019) reports that fewer students (38 %) use digital
learning tools outside of school every day compared to those who use
them inside school often (57 %). Equally important, this technological
infusion is revolutionizing conventional teaching approaches and offering new opportunities for learning and the development of skills
(Alenezi et al., 2023). Complementing this transformation, digital
learning tools and devices such as computers, laptops, tablets, and digital resources like educational software are being increasingly utilized
both within and outside of school environments (Haleem et al., 2022),
enhancing learning by providing interactive and personalized educational experiences.

Digital learning tools also include websites, applications, online tutorials, online games, and videos or programs used to teach and support
student learning and schoolwork (Gallup, 2019). Digital resources
encompass a wide array of tools and platforms, including educational
software, online databases, interactive simulations, and multimedia resources. These resources offer students opportunities for personalized
learning experiences (Engelbrecht et al., 2020), motivate and strengthen
learning (Røsvik & Haukedal, 2017), provide immediate feedback and
foster collaborative engagement (Barana et al., 2021; Pinto & Leite,
2020; Sadaf & Gezer, 2020), thereby augmenting traditional instructional methods. These resources not only foster student engagement but
also support the development of digital competencies critical for the
21st century (Li et al., 2024; Alenezi, 2023). The utilization of digital
technologies by students has transformed traditional educational practices, offering new avenues for accessing information, engaging with
course materials, and interacting with peers and instructors (Srivastava
& Srivastava, 2024).
Furthermore, the economic status of a nation profoundly impacts its
educational infrastructure and policies, which in turn shapes the
accessibility and quality of digital resources available to students
(Simamora, 2020). As technology becomes a cornerstone in the evolution of educational practices, the current era emerges as a pivotal period
for the adoption of innovative technologies within the education system
(Mhlongo et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). It is imperative to understand
the patterns and extent of digital resource utilization among students
from diverse economic backgrounds. Such insights are crucial for
developing effective educational strategies that harness the power of
technology to amplify learning outcomes and bridge the digital divide,
ensuring equitable access to educational opportunities for all students
(Eden et al., 2024). This understanding is essential to uncover disparities
and identify areas where technology can be leveraged to improve
educational outcomes.
Proficiency in digital skills and tools can enhance fundamental motor
abilities (Bedford et al., 2016), socio-emotional competencies (Berson
et al., 2019; Luo et al., 2021), and cognitive capacities (Janisse et al.,
2018). Teachers can boost student engagement and participation by
integrating interactive digital technologies in the classroom (Pinto &
Leite, 2020; Wekerle et al., 2022), while also fostering critical thinking
skills through digital resources (Svendsen & Svendsen, 2021). Digital
tools are valuable not only for in-class activities but also for pursuits like
physical education and health-oriented teaching methods (Goodyear
et al., 2019). Self-efficacy in digital skills, rooted in Albert Bandura’s
social cognitive theory, is the belief in one’s ability to effectively use
digital tools to achieve specific goals (Barnett, 2023; Matthew et al.,
2000; Galindo-Domínguez, 2021). It significantly influences students’
acceptance, use, and literacy of digital resources (Rohatgi et al., 2016;
Jimenez et al., 2021), as highlighted in mobile learning (Qashou, 2021)
and learning management systems (Rivers, 2021). Students’ self-efficacy
in digital resources affects how they utilize these tools for educational
purposes (Joo et al., 2013; Prifti, 2022) and is strengthened through
regular participation in digital activities, which enhances their practical
experience and problem-solving abilities (Cassidy & Eachus, 2002;
Usher & Pajares, 2008). Understanding the role of self-efficacy in
technology-enhanced learning is crucial for improving student motivation, effort, persistence, and academic achievement (Matovu, 2020;
Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2021).
Digital competence involves mastering digital tools for tasks like
information retrieval and setting alerts, closely aligning with digital
skills. Information literacy builds on this by requiring effective information search, evaluation, and communication, encompassing abilities
such as digital communication, data management, and critical thinking
about digital content (Mbandje et al., 2023; Bryn Mawr College, 2016).
Most digital applications are used to provide verification feedback, with
fewer offering explanatory feedback or encouragement. Digital platforms often use feedback mechanisms to guide user behavior, offering
personalized responses on assignments, collaboration, and

supplementary materials (Maier & Klotz, 2022).
While existing research has largely explored the impact and general
benefits of digital resources in education (Timotheou et al., 2023;
Abdulrahaman et al., 2020; Adeshina, 2024; Wang et al., 2024), a critical gap remains in understanding the specific patterns of digital
resource usage across different economic contexts. Prior studies have
highlighted the digital divide between students from high-income
countries and those from upper middle-income countries and lower
middle-income countries, with students in high-income countries
generally having better access to and utilization of digital resources
(United Nations Educational, Scientific & Cultural Organization, 2023).
However, beyond this established divide, limited research has systematically compared how specific usage patterns, rather than access alone,
influence students’ digital competencies and learning outcomes across
these different economic settings. For example, few studies address the
fact that about 25 % of teens from lower-income families do not have
access to home computers or reliable internet connections (Auxier &
Anderson, 2020), which contributes to disparities in learning outcomes.
The World Development Report (2017) also highlights that many children and young people in lower-middle-income countries and
upper-middle-income countries perform below expected levels. However, little attention has been paid to how the specific uses of digital
resources, whether for leisure, academic work, or hybrid purposes,
might bridge or widen this gap in educational outcomes.
This study seeks to fill this gap by exploring how digital resources are
utilized differently in high-income countries, upper middle-income
countries, and lower middle-income countries and how these usage
patterns impact students’ self-efficacy, digital competencies, and performance in core subjects like language, mathematics, and science. By
focusing on digital resource utilization in different contexts (home,
school, academic, and non-academic) and their effects on self-efficacy,
this research aims to uncover nuanced insights into how mastery experiences, facilitated through digital tools, indirectly shape students’ academic success (Prifti, 2022; Rivers, 2021; Usher & Pajares, 2008). This
more specific perspective on digital resource usage moves beyond the
general narrative of the digital divide and offers a deeper understanding
of how students’ experiences with digital tools in various economic
settings influence their educational outcomes. Thus, the primary goal of
this study is to investigate the relationship between students’ use of
digital resources, their self-efficacy in digital competencies, and their
performance in standardized tests, particularly in the context of
different economic environments. The study aims to answer the
following research questions:
1. How do patterns of digital resource usage for school-related activities
differ among students in high-income countries, upper middleincome countries, and lower middle-income countries, and what
impact do these patterns have on student self-efficacy and engagement in feedback mechanisms?
2. How do students in high-income countries, upper middle-income
countries, and lower middle-income countries differ in their use of
digital resources for school activities outside the classroom, and what
factors contribute to these differences?
3. How does students’ self-efficacy in digital competencies vary across
different economic contexts, and what factors contribute to these
variations in basic and advanced digital skills among students in
high-income countries, upper middle-income countries, and lower
middle-income countries?
4. What factors contribute to the variations in students’ self-efficacy in
basic and advanced digital competencies across different economic
contexts, and how do these variations manifest among students in
high-income countries, upper middle-income countries, and lower
middle-income countries?
5. How do digital resources, such as those for academic support, feedback, school activities outside the classroom, and self-efficacy in

Self-efficacy in digital competencies, the construct used in this study,
offers a more precise and contemporary framework. It includes the
ability to perform tasks related to digital literacy, such as information
searching, digital communication, problem-solving, and critical
thinking (Hatlevik & Hatlevik, 2018). This concept goes beyond mere
confidence in using digital tools—it reflects a broader competency in
managing digital tasks effectively, aligning more closely with the digital
demands of modern educational settings. Unlike digital self-efficacy,
which may focus primarily on confidence in basic usage, self-efficacy
in digital competencies encompasses a range of higher-order skills
essential for academic success and adaptability to technological changes
(Getenet et al., 2024).
By focusing on self-efficacy in digital competencies, this study aims
to assess students’ ability to engage with digital resources in ways that
reflect the comprehensive nature of digital literacy required in today’s
learning environments. This construct addresses the limitations of older
measures of digital and technological self-efficacy by incorporating a
broader set of competencies relevant to modern education. Furthermore,
research demonstrates that students with high self-efficacy in digital
competencies are better equipped to navigate technological advancements, engage in collaborative learning, and achieve better academic
outcomes (Tannert & Gröschner, 2021). Given these distinctions, this
study employs self-efficacy in digital competencies for its analyses, as it
provides a more robust and relevant framework for understanding students’ engagement with digital learning tools.

2. Literature review and concept
2.1. Patterns of digital resource usage at school across economic levels
(SES)
Students at schools in high-income countries are more likely to use
computers for both in-class and out-of-class studies and possess superior
digital skills compared to their peers in upper middle-income and lower
middle-income countries. These disparities persist even when considering school-level resources. Notably, the digital competence gap between schools correlates inversely with economic development and
education spending, while directly relating to income inequality (Ma,
2021). Research on disparities in digital resource access across economic
contexts highlights how exposure to digital tools impacts the use of these
resources. For instance, Joshi et al. (2024) found significant effects of
socioeconomic factors on technology access, engagement, and academic
performance. The recurring theme of the digital divide underscores
disparities in device ownership, internet connectivity, and effective
technology use. Studies by van de Werfhorst et al. (2022) and Hamidi
et al. (2011) further contribute to our understanding of these patterns
within different socioeconomic contexts. In schools, digital resource
usage significantly varies based on the economic context (van de
Werfhorst et al., 2022). Students from socially advantaged families are
more likely to use digital skills at home for educational and productive
purposes, feel confident applying these skills in school, receive recognition from teachers, and ultimately benefit more from digital resources
compared to students from disadvantaged families (Ma & Cheng, 2022).
A study conducted by van de Werfhorst (2022) across 45 countries
found little evidence for the hypothesized ’multi-level’ digital divide
resulting from systematic gradients in school environments’ readiness
for digital education based on student composition. Instead, the key
drivers of the digital divide during the COVID-19 pandemic are students’
digital skills, which strongly correlate with their socioeconomic background. High-income countries benefit from superior infrastructure,
more devices, and integrated digital curricula. In contrast, lower
middle-income countries encounter challenges related to access, connectivity, and teacher training (Aung & Khaing, 2016). These disparities
significantly impact students’ exposure to digital resources. For
example, students in high-income countries often have regular access to
online textbooks, interactive simulations, and educational apps, while
their counterparts in lower middle-income and upper middle-income
countries rely on limited resources (Hamidi et al., 2011). Understanding these patterns is crucial because exposure to digital tools can
enhance students’ confidence, problem-solving abilities, and adaptability. However, disparities in access may exacerbate existing inequalities (Getenet et al., 2024). The disparities in digital resource usage
and competencies among students in high-income countries, upper
middle-income countries, and lower middle-income countries are
influenced by socioeconomic factors, infrastructure, and educational
spending, directly impacting students’ self-efficacy, engagement, and
academic outcomes. Understanding these patterns is crucial for
addressing the digital divide and its implications on student learning and
feedback mechanisms across different economic contexts.

2.3. Use of digital resources for subjective learning activities
Digital resources for learning subjective activities have emerged as
powerful tools in education, overcoming the limitations of traditional
teaching methods. They offer personalized, interactive experiences that
foster critical thinking and curiosity. Researchers have explored their
impact on student learning outcomes (Tibaná-Herrera et al., 2018),
teacher-student interactions (Chen et al., 2022), and effective instructional design (Behl et al., 2022) in subjects like science, mathematics,
and language learning. Additionally, digital literacy and technical
competencies are crucial for effective use (Behl et al., 2022; Chen et al.,
2022; Tibaná-Herrera et al., 2018). Recent studies by Basilotta-Gómez-Pablos et al. (2022) emphasize the role of technology use
and the development of digital skills in scientific education, providing
insights into successful strategies for integrating digital resources in
various subjects. However, excessive use of digital resources can lead to
significant distractions and attention problems in learning (Foroughi
et al., 2022; Hawi & Samaha, 2016; Khaola et al., 2022; Tülübaş et al.,
2023), as students may use them for purposes unrelated to learning. In
some cases, this can result in negative emotions (Ghimire & Khanal,
2021) and frustrations with e-texts (Novak et al., 2023).

students utilize digital tools for self-directed activities such as exploration, practice, and reflection (Maziane et al., 2023). In a digital,

2.5. Use of digital resources for support of feedback (USF)
Research has extensively explored personalized feedback within
digital learning environments, examining it at micro, meso, and macro
levels (Maier & Klotz, 2022). Tärning (2018) evaluated digital applications used in Swedish schools and found that 77 % of apps offered
verification feedback, while only 12 % provided explanatory feedback,
and 55 % included encouragement. Digital platforms often implement
feedback mechanisms to influence user behavior. These mechanisms
enable personalized feedback on assignments, online collaboration, and
exploration of supplementary materials (Maier & Klotz, 2022). Steur
and Seiter (2021) identified prevalent design characteristics, including
query methods and submission categories. Although adherence to
feedback mechanisms varies, they exhibit low precision but high
sensitivity and verifiability. Despite progress, a gap remains in the
clarity of how digital tools should be used for feedback and how it
should be interpreted (Selvaraj & Azman, 2020). Across different economic levels, digital resources play a crucial role in supporting
personalized learning through feedback. Major and Tsapali (2021)
conducted a meta-analysis indicating that personalized learning technology positively influences learning outcomes in low- and
middle-income countries. They found that approaches specifically
tailored to individual learners’ levels had a significantly greater impact
than methods focused solely on learners’ interests or offering general
feedback. Effective feedback helps students identify gaps in their
learning and provides suggestions for improvement. It fosters responsive
learners, sharpens teaching strategies, and delivers constructive insights
for both students and teachers (Isaac, 2019). Students are more motivated to engage in both giving and receiving feedback, clarifying misunderstandings, and demonstrating improved learning. Additionally,
participating in guided peer feedback environments enhances
self-regulation, critical thinking skills, and communication (Goold,
2016). However, it’s important to note that simply providing feedback
does not necessarily lead to improved learning outcomes. The literature
emphasizes the significance of personalized feedback in digital learning
environments, highlighting its impact on student engagement and
learning outcomes across various levels and economic contexts. It also
underscores the need for clear and adaptive feedback mechanisms to
effectively support personalized learning and enhance academic
performance.

2.4. Factors influencing use of digital resources inside and outside of
school on academic performance
Using digital resources within schools involves integrating technology into educational settings. Many high schools now implement a oneto-one device model, providing each student with a laptop or tablet upon
enrollment (Dillon, 2023). Factors influencing this use include device
availability, curriculum integration, and internet connectivity quality.
Outside of school hours, students’ engagement with digital tools extends
to the use of digital resources for out-of-class activities. Factors that
affect this usage include the types of resources (apps, tutorials, e-books)
and the socioeconomic context, which encompasses access to technology and time allocation. We argue that students’ use of digital resources
beyond school hours has a significant impact on their academic performance (Ogunbodede et al., 2022).
The use of digital resources for didactic activities outside the classroom focuses on instructional activities conducted beyond traditional
classroom settings. Examples include video lectures, online quizzes, and
collaborative projects (Haleem, 2022). Key factors such as the type of
resources (educational apps, online tutorials), time spent, and socioeconomic context play a vital role (Limniou, 2021). Oyetade et al.
(2023) suggest that e-learning technologies consistently enhance student engagement, regardless of whether activities occur inside or
outside the classroom. In high-income countries, personalized learning
through adaptive platforms is common, while in lower middle-income
countries, students may rely on shared devices or limited internet access (Major & Tsapali, 2021). Researchers should explore whether
increased digital resource usage correlates with improved outcomes or
potential risks, such as distraction (Jiang et al., 2024). Academic performance is measured through standardized tests and classroom grades
(Lai, 2023) in both upper middle-income and lower middle-income
countries. Additionally, understanding the impact of digital learning
on subjects like language, mathematics, and science across different
economic levels informs policy decisions. Participants in studies showed
exceptionally high engagement in activities such as searching for information, communicating with classmates, friends, and family, and
interacting on social networks like Moments and QQ Zone (Jiang et al.,
2024). However, many students also used the internet for socializing,
watching movies, and listening to music, which contributed to
time-wasting, delays in schoolwork submission, poor academic results,
and school avoidance (Almasi et al., 2017). This literature review
highlights how digital resource usage, both in and out of school, affects
student engagement and academic performance. It underscores the need
to explore how these patterns vary by economic context and influence
learning outcomes, which is essential for understanding the effectiveness and challenges of digital integration in education.

2.6. Conceptual framework
The conceptual framework of this study is based on the premise that
digital resources play a pivotal role in shaping students’ learning experiences and outcomes. When investigating the use of digital resources
across high-income countries, upper-middle-income countries, and
lower-middle-income countries, it is crucial to differentiate between
teachers’ use of these resources during instruction and students’ actual
learning experiences. The variable selected from PISA data, ’Use of
Digital Resources at School,’ specifically pertains to teachers’ incorporation of digital tools in classroom settings. While this influences the
learning environment, it does not directly measure students’ utilization
of these resources or their learning outcomes. Therefore, we recognize
that students’ learning is mediated by other factors, such as their
engagement with digital resources both in and out of school and how
these resources are used for subject-specific learning. By making this
distinction, we avoid conflating teachers’ instructional practices with
students’ independent use of digital resources, which is essential for
understanding the true impact on learning.
To further justify the model, we focus on the role of self-efficacy in
digital competencies as both an outcome and a potential predictor of
digital resource use. We propose that increased access to and usage of
digital tools can enhance students’ digital self-efficacy. However, we
also acknowledge the possibility of reverse causality—students with
higher self-efficacy in using digital technologies may be more inclined to

The findings align with Compeau and Higgins’ (1995) conceptualization of self-efficacy, highlighting the importance of digital literacy in
fostering student engagement and confidence. However, the results
challenge Aung and Khaing’s (2016) assertion that superior infrastructure guarantees better digital resource utilization. Instead, they
emphasize the role of socio-economic factors in shaping access and efficacy. For example, Reisdorf and Fernandez (2021) found that reliance
on mobile devices limits task complexity and reduces learning outcomes,
which aligns with the observed disparities in digital feedback usage.
Similarly, Ma and Cheng (2022) noted that socially advantaged students
are better able to leverage digital skills, reflecting the intra-country inequalities observed in this study.
Despite these consistencies, the study diverges from Hamidi et al.
(2011), who suggested that students in high-income countries benefit
significantly from interactive digital tools. In contrast, this study found
that students in lower-middle-income countries displayed higher
engagement with educational software and feedback tools, potentially
compensating for infrastructural limitations through resourcefulness
and adaptability. Supporters of digital integration argue that increasing
technology use in education fosters engagement and self-efficacy, as
demonstrated by Timotheou et al. (2023). Digital tools are viewed as
transformative, bridging gaps in access and improving learning outcomes. For instance, Getenet et al. (2024) emphasized the positive
impact of digital literacy on student engagement and confidence. In
contrast, critics argue that digital resources can exacerbate inequalities,
particularly when access is uneven. Khanal (2024) highlighted the
addictive nature of online games, which can divert attention from academic focus. Similarly, Foroughi et al. (2022) and Tülübas et al. (2023)
cautioned about the negative consequences of over-reliance on digital
devices, including attention deficits and reduced learning outcomes.
Remarkably, the study reveals significant disparities in the use of
digital feedback tools based on the economic context of the countries
involved. Mean scores for feedback support items were higher in lowermiddle-income countries compared to upper-middle-income and higherincome countries. This trend is particularly noteworthy, given that
lower-middle-income countries often face challenges related to resource
constraints and educational technology infrastructure. The higher mean
score in these countries suggests a stronger emphasis on utilizing
available digital tools due to limited resources. In contexts where
traditional feedback mechanisms are less feasible, digital solutions
become more attractive alternatives. This disparity highlights the potential of digital tools to bridge educational gaps in resource-limited
settings, provided adequate support and training are available. Results
of this study confirm substantial differences in feedback support across
country categories, reinforcing the influence of economic context on the
adoption and effectiveness of digital feedback tools. Additionally, disparities in household internet access exist between higher-income and
lower-middle-income countries, as well as between rural and urban
areas, with rural or low-income regions experiencing lower connectivity. Students from low-income households also face limited internet
access, further contributing to a socioeconomic technology divide (Afzal
et al., 2023). To address low engagement levels, targeted interventions
and support are crucial for integrating digital feedback mechanisms into
educational systems in lower-middle-income countries. Enhancing
accessibility, usability, and perceived value requires comprehensive
training, seamless curriculum integration, and user-friendly interfaces.

6. Discussion
6.1. Digital resource usage, out of school, student engagement, and selfefficacy (SE), subject learning, and feedback mechanisms across income
contexts
Although digital resources in education have been widely studied,
gaps remain in understanding their usage across different income contexts. Research has shown that high-income countries benefit from
strong digital infrastructure, but challenges in lower and upper middleincome countries, such as limited resources and socio-economic disparities, are often overlooked. Access to mobile devices alone does not
guarantee effective learning, especially when usage is restricted by
shared devices or lack of educational software (Reisdorf & Fernandez,
2021). This study examines digital resource usage, self-efficacy, subject
learning, and feedback mechanisms across income levels, addressing
these gaps.
This study revealed diverse patterns of digital resource utilization
among students. Approximately one-third of students in lower-middleincome countries, upper-middle-income countries, and high-income
countries reported having access to computers or laptops with internet
connectivity. Notably, tablet usage was higher in lower-middle-income
countries compared to upper-middle-income and high-income countries, challenging assumptions about digital dominance in wealthier
nations. Out-of-school access to digital devices was widespread, with
students in high-income countries exhibiting the highest levels of
engagement and self-efficacy (Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Marakas et al.,
2007). While desktops, laptops, and smartphones were regularly used,
the adoption of educational software, games, and apps remained low,
with lower-middle-income countries showing greater adoption of these
tools relative to upper-middle-income and high-income countries.
Digital resource usage for academic subjects such as mathematics,
science, and language were limited, with only 15.3–16.7 % of students
reporting consistent use during lessons. Science learning demonstrated

This discrepancy may arise from differences in educational structures,

6.3. Exploring students’ digital competence across economic contexts:
factors influencing self-efficacy in basic and advanced digital skills
The findings from this study offer valuable insights into students’
self-efficacy in digital competencies across diverse economic contexts.
Overall, the results indicate that students generally feel confident in
their digital skills, but there are notable variations based on specific
competencies and the economic background of their countries. A substantial proportion of students reported high self-efficacy in basic digital
tasks, with 85.5 % indicating their ability to search for and find relevant
information online. This suggests that students across these 51 countries
are comfortable with fundamental digital literacy skills, which are
essential for academic success in an increasingly digital world. The
confidence in these basic skills likely stems from the widespread integration of the internet into daily life, making such tasks familiar and
routine for most students. However, self-efficacy in more advanced
digital skills—such as creating, updating, and maintaining a webpage or
blog—was significantly lower at 66 %. This disparity highlights an area
for improvement in digital education. Digital literacy encompasses not
only technical abilities but also critical thinking and discernment
(Audrin & Audrin, 2022). Advanced digital skills demand not only access to technology but also specific training and opportunities for
practice. Unfortunately, these resources may not be equally accessible
across varying economic contexts. Recognizing the importance of
advanced digital skills in today’s labor market (Stofkova, 2022), policymakers and educators must address these disparities. Bridging the gap
requires targeted interventions that provide equitable access to training
and foster self-efficacy in complex digital competencies.
As the internet continues to evolve, developing digital skills becomes
essential for informed decision-making, responsible digital citizenship,
and lifelong learning. Advanced digital competencies are crucial for
students’ future career prospects and their ability to engage in complex
digital tasks. The lower self-efficacy in these areas may result from
limited exposure or fewer opportunities to practice these skills within
the educational curriculum (Hinduja et al., 2024). However, a
comparative analysis across different economic contexts reveals significant disparities in digital self-efficacy. Students in higher-income
countries reported the highest self-efficacy scores, followed by those
in upper-middle-income countries and lower-middle-income countries.
These differences, confirmed by ANOVA results, underscore the impact
of economic resources on students’ confidence in their digital abilities.
Higher-income countries’ students’ higher self-efficacy can be attributed
to better access to digital resources, more comprehensive digital education programs, and greater overall exposure to technology. In
contrast, students in lower-middle-income countries face challenges
such as limited access to technology, insufficient digital infrastructure,
and less emphasis on digital skills in the curriculum.

6.2. Digital learning resource use across income categories: a comparative
analysis of students’ out-of-classroom activities (OCA)
The study’s findings on the usage of digital resources for school activities outside the classroom reveal notable trends across 51 countries.
Students engage with digital tools at varying levels for specific tasks. For
example, they frequently use resources to check grades (52.2 %), browse
the internet for schoolwork (34.7 %), communicate with peers about
assignments (56.5 %), and search for school-related information (50.5
%), often on a weekly or even daily basis. These findings align with Jiang
et al.’s (2024) research in China, where students show high engagement
in tasks such as information searching, communication, and social
networking, indicating that they recognize the value of digital tools for
academic performance and convenience. However, engagement in digital communication with teachers (37.4 %) is lower, likely due to barriers like inadequate communication platforms, limited teacher
availability, or students’ preference for face-to-face interactions. This
lower engagement hinders timely feedback and support, which are
crucial for effective learning. As noted by Falloon (2020) and Romrell
et al. (2014), the use of technology for modification and redefinition is
not at the desired level. Another possibility is that teachers may rely on
students’ progress through platforms and email, rather than using digital
tools to initiate communication.
Analysis of engagement levels across different economic contexts
reveals additional trends. In high-income countries, activities such as
receiving or downloading assignments and uploading work to school
portals are more prevalent, likely due to better infrastructure and access
to advanced educational technologies. Upper-middle-income countries
show higher engagement in activities like browsing the internet for
lesson follow-up, peer communication, and searching for school-related
information. This proactive use of digital tools enhances learning and
collaboration. Meanwhile, lower-middle-income countries exhibit
greater engagement in communicating with teachers. However, uppermiddle-income countries surpass lower-middle-income countries in
leveraging digital resources for school activities, suggesting more
progress in utilizing technology for education. Students in lower-middleincome countries may rely on digital communication due to distance,
resource limitations, or a greater reliance on teacher-provided content.

7. Implication of the study
1. The study highlights significant disparities in digital competency
across economic contexts, with high-income countries demonstrating higher self-efficacy scores compared to upper-middle income
countries and lower-middle income countries. This underscores the
impact of economic resources on students’ confidence in their digital
abilities. Policymakers and educators must consider these disparities
when developing digital education policies and interventions to
ensure all students can develop strong digital competencies.
2. Although students in high-income countries exhibit higher digital
resource utilization and self-efficacy scores, the study reveals a
paradox where better infrastructure does not always translate to
increased usage. The results show that the status of using digital
resources in school and for subject learning in digital competencies

D.R. Joshi et al.

International Journal of Educational Research Open 8 (2025) 100443

was better in lower-income countries, whereas the use of digital resources was more prevalent in high-income countries. This suggests
that policymakers and educators need to not only improve infrastructure but also ensure equitable access and encourage the effective use of digital tools, particularly in low- and middle-income
countries.
3. The disparity in self-efficacy for basic versus advanced digital skills
highlights an area for improvement in digital education. While students generally feel confident in basic digital tasks, a notable gap
exists in advanced digital skills across all 51 countries. Educational
strategies should prioritize providing opportunities for students to
practice and develop these advanced skills, which are essential for
their future career prospects and their ability to engage in complex
digital tasks.
4. Despite the availability of digital devices, the usage of educational
software and digital feedback tools remains limited. This is concerning, given the potential of these tools to enhance learning and
engagement. The study highlights the need for greater integration of
educational software into daily learning activities, as well as a
stronger emphasis on digital feedback mechanisms to improve student engagement and overall learning outcomes.
5. The study reveals varying levels of engagement in digital collaboration across different income categories. Promoting platforms that
facilitate student collaboration and information sharing is particularly crucial in upper-middle-income countries, where students
demonstrate higher engagement with digital tools for learning and
collaboration. Encouraging such collaboration can help maximize
the benefits seen in these contexts, enhancing the overall learning
experience and fostering more effective educational outcomes.
6. The study suggests that lower-middle-income countries could benefit
more from digital feedback tools compared to upper-middle-income
and high-income countries, likely due to resource constraints. This
finding highlights the potential of digital tools to bridge educational
gaps in resource-limited settings. By providing adequate support,
training, and seamless integration of digital feedback mechanisms,
these tools can significantly enhance learning experiences in such
contexts.
7. The underutilization of digital resources for educational activities,
especially in lower-income contexts, underscores the need to integrate digital tools into the curriculum. Doing so will ensure consistent and effective use of technology across all activities, thereby
enhancing students’ engagement and learning outcomes. Schools
should implement digital literacy programs that offer systematic
training in both basic and advanced digital skills.
8. The findings emphasize the necessity for targeted interventions to
optimize the use of digital resources. While students often use digital
tools for tasks like checking grades and browsing the internet for
schoolwork, there is a notable gap in their use for communication
with teachers. Overcoming these barriers through appropriate
communication platforms and increasing teacher availability can
improve the learning process by enabling timely feedback and support. The lower engagement in teacher-student digital communication highlights the need for comprehensive training and support for
educators. Professional development programs should focus on
equipping teachers with the skills to incorporate digital communication tools into their teaching practices, improving the frequency
and quality of student interactions. This will foster better engagement and enhanced learning outcomes.
9. Given the dynamic evolution of the internet and digital technologies,
it is crucial to foster digital literacy that encompasses not only
technical abilities but also critical thinking and discernment. Schools
should prioritize the development of these skills to prepare students
for informed decision-making, responsible digital citizenship, and
lifelong learning in an increasingly digital world.
