A B S T R A C T
While the need for mentoring, coaching, and induction for Early Career Teachers (ECTs) is well-established, the 
evidence is limited by inconsistent terminology and practices implied by each term, underscored by competing 
purposes. This registered review examined empirical literature from Global North contexts from 2008 relating to 
formal mentoring, coaching, and induction of ECTs to scope the evidence base and understand how each form of 
support is defined and described. The review confirmed the lack of coherence in terminology and practices and 
identified four themes describing the different purposes underlying measures of effectiveness, with implications 
for further systematic review.
1. Introduction
Induction is well-established as a mechanism for supporting Early 
Career Teachers (ECTs) as they transition into the profession (Ingersoll 
& Strong, 2011). Induction programmes frequently include an element 
of mentoring and, increasingly, coaching in addition to other forms of 
professional learning (Long et al., 2012). In recent years, global teaching 
workforce shortages – real and perceived – have increased political and 
public attention on the need to reduce ECT attrition, partially through 
effective support programmes. In the United States, this is reflected in 
Federal and State policy mandating induction programmes (Glazerman 
et al., 2010). In England, this is reflected in the Early Career Framework 
with its focus on mentoring (Daly et al., 2023; Department for Educa­
tion, 2019). While the Australian ECT Induction framework remains 
under review (Australian Institute for Teaching and School Leadership, 
2023), increasing attention on initial teacher education and workforce 
measures signals a future convergence on ECT support as an area for 
policy intervention (Australian Government, 2024).
However, despite acceptance at the policy level of the role of support 
for ECTs, the research does not provide conclusive findings about the 
nature and effectiveness of induction, coaching, and mentoring. This 
scoping review substantiated these inconsistencies in terminology and 
competing purposes, with a key finding that definitions and included 
practices cannot be applied or compared across studies. The outcomes 
that studies sought to measure were grouped into four themes of pur­
poses for providing support to ECTs: teacher development of self, 
retention, teaching practice, and student achievement. This scoping 
review contributes a rigorous overview of the literature and direction for 
further study using systematic reviews of identified themes to generate 
robust policy recommendations aligned to clear purposes.
2. Background
In this section, a brief review of the literature will demonstrate the 
uncertainty regarding effectiveness of mentoring, induction, and 
coaching for ECT, which stems from shifting terminology and competing 
purposes for providing support.
2.1. Shifting terminology
Across the global literature, there is inconsistent use of ‘induction’, 
‘mentoring’ and ‘coaching’ as they relate to the support of ECTs 
(Kutsyuruba, 2019b; Moss, 2010). Clarity in the meaning of these terms 
and the practices they include is essential for ensuring a shared under­
standing in policy design and implementation of the support structures 
and their intended outcomes.
In addition to the complexity within each term’s use and practices, 
there is confusion regarding how these terms relate to each other. As 
noted by Ingersoll and Smith (2004), ‘mentoring’ and ‘induction’ have 
been used interchangeably. However, several recent studies examining 
the effectiveness of policy initiatives conceptualise mentoring as an 
element of induction (Barnard et al., 2022; Burke et al., 2015; Kut­
syuruba et al., 2014; Larsen et al., 2023; McCormack et al., 2006; 
Nadeau, 2019). This appears to be the majority view, although in Topliss

While coaching and mentoring have also been used interchangeably 
(Kwok et al., 2021), differing uses often reflect contextual nuances. 
‘Coaching’ in the United States overlaps with ‘mentoring’ in Australia, 
including ‘job-embedded, sustained, classroom-based’ support (Shernoff 
et al., 2015). As shown by Daly et al. (2023), the increased focus in 
England’s Early Career Framework on accountability for the develop­
ment of specific and targeted teaching practices among ECTs has 
resulted in a shift in mentors’ work towards coaching approaches as 
conceptualised in that context. It is critical to identify and resolve these 
differences and changes in the use of terms as, with the urgency created 
by workforce shortages, policy makers are more likely to borrow from 
other jurisdictions, increasing the potential for confusion and conflation 
of practices and expectations.
2.2. Competing purposes
There are varying perspectives about the intended outcomes, or 
purposes, of supporting ECTs. While the most pragmatic purpose is to 
increase retention, given workforce pressures, a narrowed focus on 
retention may overshadow other benefits, including harnessing the 
value that ECTs bring to the profession (Surrette, 2020). Other purposes 
for supporting ECTs have been proposed, each as an end in themselves 
whilst also indirectly increasing retention.
One approach focuses on teaching practice, the improvement of 
which prevents attrition (Ingersoll & Strong, 2011; Larsen et al., 2023; 
Patrick, 2018). The logic is clear: ECTs who experience success in the 
classroom are more likely to remain than those who have negative ex­
periences (Gordon, 2020; Kelly et al., 2018). This is especially important 
for ECTs whose first teaching experience is in a challenging context 
(Naidoo & Wagner, 2020). However, this perspective implies that ECTs 
are unprepared for the classroom upon entry into the profession despite 
having completed their formal training (Akin-Otiko, 2019; Barnard 
et al., 2022). Although this view has implications for the role of initial 
teacher education, it aligns with the framing of the ECT phase as an early 
stage of professional development (Lofthouse, 2018; Surrette, 2020) and 
mentoring as a way to co-develop knowledge and skills (Pennanen et al., 
2016; Tynj¨al¨a et al., 2021).
Another approach is the development of teacher identity. This is 
prevalent in the literature about mentors and mentoring, in which 
experienced teachers guide novices in their development of the less 
tangible aspects of being a teacher (Andreasen et al., 2019; Long et al., 
2012; Topliss, 2020). This includes the need to become socialised to the 
context of their school, as teachers are more likely to remain if they are 
enculturated to the environment (Andreasen et al., 2019; Naidoo & 
Wagner, 2020). Taking this approach may assume that ECTs who are 
suited to the profession or their school are more likely to remain, 
regardless of their teaching practice (Palmer, 1997). More likely, 
though, there is a mediating effect of suitability and identity on practice 
which reduces attrition (Burke et al., 2015; Ingersoll & Strong, 2011).
In addition to these examples of different purposes, other lines of 
argument are evident in the literature, each of which have corre­
sponding measures of effectiveness. Critically, without clarity about the 
purpose of supporting ECTs, there is no framework for measuring 
effectiveness beyond retention rates which can be unclear and incon­
clusive (Borman & Dowling, 2008). For example, there is variation in 
how ‘retention’ and ‘attrition’ are defined in teacher workforce data, 
and whether the data differentiates between teachers who move schools 
but remain in teaching and those who leave the profession altogether 
(Kang, 2010). Given the uncertainties, it is cause for concern that pol­
icies which mandate forms of support continue to proliferate without a 
clear mechanism for measuring effectiveness. Without clarity from the 
research, policy makers are at risk of borrowing policy to little effect (Gu 
et al., 2023).
3. Methodology
This scoping review was undertaken to provide an overview of the 
literature to highlight the conflicts in terminology and purposes for 
supporting ECTs and make recommendations for a subsequent system­
atic review (Arksey & O’Malley, 2005; Cacchione, 2016). Despite their 
relative newness as a methodological approach, scoping reviews follow 
replicable procedures to describe and identify what is known, the cur­
rent gaps in knowledge, and whether a systematic review is warranted 
(Arksey & O’Malley, 2005; Aromataris & Munn, 2020). The protocol for 
this scoping review was registered with the International Database of 
Education Systematic Reviews (IDESR) to ensure accountability and 
rigour (Cacchione, 2016; IDESR, n.d.). The IDESR ID is IDESR000154.
3.1. Review questions
The scoping review was guided by two questions. 
1. What is the nature of the body of literature about mentoring, in­
duction, and coaching for ECTs? 
a. What methodological approaches have been undertaken to 
describe or measure the phenomena?
b. In which contexts have published research studies taken place?
c. What kind of conclusions can be drawn from the literature?
2. What is the nature and purpose of mentoring, induction, and 
coaching for ECTs? 
a. In what ways have ‘mentoring’, ‘induction’, and ‘coaching’ been 
described or defined as they relate to ECTs?
b. What are the stated or implied purposes of mentoring, induction, 
and coaching for ECTs described in the literature?
3.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria
The inclusion criteria were defined to strike a balance between 
capturing the breadth of literature while maintaining a feasible volume 
for analysis (see Table 1).
The date range was studies published since 2008 (inclusive) to 
demarcate an inflection point in international policy regarding the 
training of teachers with the first Teaching and Learning International 
Survey (TALIS) conducted in that year (OECD, 2009). While there were 
certainly shifts before the survey was conducted, and various points 
since which could be used as cut-off points, the time period since 2008 
also aligns with critical national policy shifts in the jurisdictions of in­
terest for the doctoral project to which this scoping study belongs.
The inclusion of literature from Global North settings narrowed the 
review to education systems whose characteristics are comparable 
across a range of parameters, and minimised the confounding influence 
of international organisations such as the World Bank and UNESCO.
The definition of ECTs as having a formal qualification acknowledges 
that teachers training while teaching through an employment-based 
pathway or who are authorised to teach without a qualification face 
unique challenges relating to their induction. Similarly, special educa­
tion teachers operate in unique settings and experience different chal­
lenges and so these cases were excluded. This scoping review is the first 
stage in a doctoral project relating to a jurisdiction in which the target 
population is formally qualified mainstream (that is, not special edu­
cation) ECTs. The narrowing of the review ensures the relevance of 
findings to this target population.

3.4. Charting the results
The database searches yielded 10 649 results. Rayyan detected 4445 
duplicates, most of which (2690) were deleted, leaving 7959 references 
for title and abstract screening. The title and abstract screening excluded 
7483 references. A peer reviewer screened a 5 % sample of the abstracts. 
The inter-rater agreement rate was 97.5 %, and the ten conflicts were 
resolved by discussion. The remaining 476 references were reviewed as 
full texts, through which 400 were excluded, leaving 76 papers. Through 
citation chaining, two papers were added, resulting in 78 studies. Fig. 1
shows this process.
3.5. Data analysis
Twenty-three items of data from each study were extracted into an 
Excel spreadsheet (see Table 2). The data extraction informed the 
organisation of studies by common characteristics and themes using 
descriptive codes, which were grouped into broader themes.
3.6. Limitations
While the researcher sought the advice of supervisors and a specialist 
research librarian, the final phrasing of keywords could have excluded 
relevant studies. The necessity of defining a date range will have 
excluded otherwise relevant contributions. Similarly, studies about 
teachers without a formal qualification and those teaching special ed­
ucation were excluded.
Although there was no explicit exclusion of studies in languages 
other than English, the publication bias of databases reduces the likeli­
hood that relevant studies in other languages were captured. There was 
also publication bias among the dissertations captured by the search. 
Although there was a large group of dissertations, all were from a few 
universities in the United States. Given that it is unlikely that this is 
representative of all dissertations relating to the support of ECTs across 
jurisdictions which have produced a high volume of research in this 
topic, this is likely a product of publication bias. Further, the focus of the 
review on effectiveness necessarily excluded studies that were purely 
descriptive.
Finally, as this is a scoping review, the studies were not subjected to 
critical appraisal (also referred to as quality assessment) through a risk 
of bias assessment, nor were the findings evaluated using a confidence in 
cumulative conclusions framework, both of which are applied to sys­
tematic reviews (Cacchione, 2016). The choice of a sequential design 
means that this first stage will be comparatively limited in the rigour of 
its conclusions.
4. Results
The findings of this review were organised to distinguish between 
descriptive findings and substantive findings. The descriptive findings 
relate to the nature and composition of the included studies, whereas the 
substantive findings relate to the emerging definitions, practices, and 
purposes which affect measures of effectiveness.
4.1. Descriptive overview
• Publication type. There were 30 Doctor of Education (EdD) and 9 
Doctor of Philosophy (PhD) dissertations from the United States. The 
next largest category was peer-reviewed journal articles (35). Two 
chapters, one conference paper, and one research report were 
included.
• Context. The studies originated from a variety of contexts, with the 
most from the United States (59). Four studies were from The 
Netherlands, three from Australia, three from New Zealand, two 
from the United Kingdom, two from Canada, one from Singapore, 
and one from Norway. There were three studies that took a 
comparative approach across at least two contexts.
• Study design. Most studies were observational, those which sought to 
quantify a relationship between variables (Ranganathan & Aggar­
wal, 2019), with five experiments, two quasi-experiments, 14 sec­
ondary data analyses, and 39 programme evaluations. The minority 
of studies were descriptive, which sought to describe existing char­
acteristics (Aggarwal & Ranganathan, 2019), with 12 case studies

4.2. Substantive overview
This scoping review confirmed the lack of clarity in the use of ter­
minology evident in the wide variation of definitions and included 
practices across studies. Analysis of the outcomes measured in each 
study provided clarity regarding the purposes of providing support.
The studies were grouped by their main outcome, determined by 
how the authors framed the purpose of providing support and how 
success according to that purpose was measured. The main outcomes 
identified were: teacher development of self, retention, teaching prac­
tice, and student achievement. Each study was counted once across 
themes, determined by the primary outcome. Within themes, studies 
which relied on multiple outcome measures were counted multiple 
times.

outside of teaching practice, with 31 studies. There are two possible 
reasons for the high frequency of this theme. First, these non-practice 
characteristics are critical for ECT development, as they capture the 
motivation and connection of teachers to their work. Second, in cases 
where it is difficult to measure the effect of support on teaching prac­
tices, self-efficacy or self-reported growth can be a useful proxy while

4.2.1.1. Self-efficacy. The most frequently occurring sub-theme was 
teacher self-efficacy. As mentioned above, this is likely due to the 
challenge of measuring actual efficacy in addition to the existence of a 
robust framework and validated measures: Bandura’s theory of self- 
efficacy and the Teacher Self-Efficacy Scales (Tschannen-Moran et al., 
1998).
In some cases, the scales were used as the main data source 
(Ackermann, 2012). In Lowrey (2012), the scales were included in a 
larger suite of surveys. In other cases, the scales were complemented by 
focus groups, interviews, and observations (George, 2016; Haigh & 
Anthony, 2012; Stanley, 2017; Woods, 2018). There was variation in the 
study design, which affected the measurement of self-efficacy and the 
conclusions that could be drawn. In Stanley (2017) and Ackermann 
(2012), self-efficacy was only measured after the intervention, and both 
researchers applied a correlational analysis between the intervention 
and self-efficacy. This design limited the strength of the conclusions that 
could be drawn, because it could not account for the effects of other 
factors on self-efficacy. As discussed by Stanley (2017), although the 
participants had high self-efficacy scores, they reported negative expe­
riences of induction, indicating that the programme was not the reason 
for their high self-efficacy scores. On the other hand, Ackermann (2012)
identified a significant difference between the self-efficacy scores of 
those who reported a positive experience of induction compared to those 
who reported a negative experience. In contrast, studies which used 
repeated measures of self-efficacy were able to capture an increase 
across all scales, although the lack of comparison groups limited the 
extent to which the change could be attributed to the intervention 
(George, 2016; Haigh & Anthony, 2012; Woods, 2018).
Three studies incorporated Bandura’s theory and Teacher Self- 
Efficacy Scales into a broader framework that theorised a link between 
teacher stress demands and stress resources (Blase, 1982). Within this 
conceptual framework, self-efficacy is a stress resource adding to the 
ECT’s capacity to meet the stress demands of their role, and participa­
tion in induction programmes is a mechanism of increasing self-efficacy 
(Desmeules et al., 2017; Helms-Lorenz et al., 2012, 2013). These studies 
found an increase in participants’ self-efficacy after they experienced the 
induction arrangements (Desmeules et al., 2017; Helms-Lorenz et al., 
2012). One study showed a significant difference for the experimental 
group when compared with a control group, strengthening the conclu­
sions regarding the effectiveness of induction in increasing self-efficacy 
(Helms-Lorenz et al., 2013). Notably, these studies which used Bandu­
ra’s framework were overwhelmingly observational in design, with only 
one descriptive study, likely due to the availability of validated mea­
surement tools.
In contrast, four studies measured the effect of induction, mentoring, 
and coaching on self-efficacy without using Bandura’s framework. These 
studies were descriptive, and used case study and phenomenology 
methodologies to capture participants’ experiences (Cornejo-Sanchez, 
2021; Munshi, 2018; Myers, 2013; Wechsler et al., 2012). Cornejo-­
Sanchez (2021) measured the impact of instructional coaches’ practices 
on participants’ self-efficacy, and relied on a conceptual framework 
from an existing coaching programme. This was a unique approach to 
the design, and the use of a critical incident reporting technique pro­
vided for a novel approach to understanding how participants experi­
enced the support.
Five studies did not directly measure self-efficacy, but the outcomes 
and design resulted in effectively doing so. These studies measured 
changes in participants’ perception of their practice (Alexander-Rami, 
2011; Davenport, 2019; Kenney, 2015) and their beliefs regarding their 
teaching efficacy (Kwok et al., 2021; Luft, 2009).
4.2.1.2. Stress and resilience. Seven studies measured the effect of in­
duction, mentoring, and coaching on stress outcomes for ECTs. These 
include the occupational stress risk that first year teachers face, relying 
on the transactional model of stress and coping (Boyle et al., 2023), and 
the perceived stress caused by aspects of the work (Harmsen et al., 2019; 
Helms-Lorenz et al., 2012, 2013; Mosley & McCarthy, 2023). The studies 
led by Helms-Lorenz and others originate from The Netherlands, and 
indicate a conceptual framework developed and tested over time. As 
discussed above, these studies rely on the theory that the extent to which 
first year teachers (and indeed all ECTs) experience a ‘stressful’ transi­
tion into the profession is a function of their stress resources relative to 
the stress demands of the role (Helms-Lorenz et al., 2013, p. 1269). 
Therefore, support bolsters stress resources, intrinsically by increasing 
self-efficacy and extrinsically by providing a coach or mentor, or access 
to professional learning (Helms-Lorenz et al., 2013). These studies found 
that support significantly increased the stress resources of ECTs, 
contributing to improved teaching practice and retention (Boyle et al., 
2023; Harmsen et al., 2019; Helms-Lorenz et al., 2012, 2013; Mosley & 
McCarthy, 2023).
Similarly, Sossick et al. (2019) found that a coach was a protective 
factor for newly qualified teachers in England, increasing their resil­
ience. This study was conducted before the implementation of England’s 
Early Career Framework, and the researchers noted the need to ‘ring-­
fence’ the role of the coach in the broader suite of support to maintain its 
function as a third space not focussed on accountability (McIntyre & 
Hobson, 2016). Desmeules et al. (2017) similarly relied on self-reported 
changes in resilience to measure the effectiveness of induction, but that 
study did not find a statistically significant correlation between pro­
gramme completion and resilience levels.
4.2.1.3. Development and growth. Four studies measured the influence 
of induction, mentoring, and coaching on ECTs’ self-reported growth 
and development. Two were secondary data analyses, conducting 
correlational analyses between self-reported teaching practice and 
mentoring experiences (Olivadoti, 2012; Pickering, 2013). However, the 
use of existing data limited researchers’ ability to measure the variables 
directly, and so satisfaction with mentoring experience was used as a 
proxy. Two studies used descriptive designs to capture rich representa­
tions of ECTs’ experience of growth – as participants chose to define it – 
in their teaching practice (Wechsler et al., 2012) and how their 
perception shifted over time (Langdon, 2011). Langdon (2011) captured 
how ECTs’ mindset shifted from being focussed on survival towards how 
their teaching practice related to student learning in the New Zealand 
context.
4.2.1.4. Identity and professional construct, socialisation, motivation, and 
wellbeing. Three studies measured the effect of mentoring on ECTs’ 
identity and professional construct (Cameron & Grant, 2017; Mac­
Gregor, 2012; Zinni, 2011). Two related to the development of identity 
as teachers of specialised subjects: MacGregor (2012) described the ef­
fect of mentoring on the professional knowledge and identity formation 
of Design and Technology teachers in Australia, while Cameron and 
Grant (2017) measured similar developments in Physics teachers in 
England. Zinni (2011) measured professional self-concept generally and 
the effect of different mentoring models. In that study, ECTs who were 
assigned a one-to-one mentor demonstrated a more significant change in 
professional self-concept than those who were assigned cohort mentors 
(Zinni, 2011).
Two studies explored the ways induction practices affected social­
isation, although each framed socialisation differently. Piggot-Irvine 
et al. (2009) described how successful sites approached socialisation and 
made connections between support and Maori value systems, indicating 
the role of culturally relevant socialisation in New Zealand. Antonsen 
et al. (2023) used data from a programme evaluation to describe the 
ways in which newly qualified teachers in Norway experienced

6 

socialisation based on the architectures of induction that they received. 
This was an extension of practice architecture theories to describe the 
experiences of ‘becoming’ teachers (Antonsen et al., 2023; Kemmis & 
Grootenboer, 2008).
Desmeules et al. (2017) sought to capture the effect of an induction 
programme on motivation of ECTs in Canada, as framed by motivation 
theory. However, the effect identified was small, which may in part be 
due to issues with the scale used for Work Motivation, which had weak 
internal consistency (Desmeules et al., 2017). In contrast, Kutsyuruba 
et al. (2019a) found a strong correlation between mentoring and well­
being of ECTs in Canada using the PERMA framework for wellbeing 
(Seligman, 2018).
These studies point to the challenges of capturing constructs relating 
to the development of ECTs outside of their teaching practice. The 
contrast in strength of findings with studies measuring self-efficacy 
could highlight the role of strong conceptual frameworks and tools 
such as the Teacher Self-Efficacy Scales and PERMA.
4.2.2. Retention
Despite the prevalence of induction policies intended to increase 
retention of ECTs, there is no certainty among the studies about the 
extent to which induction or other forms of support increases retention. 
This may be due to the many mediating factors affecting an ECT’s de­
cision to remain in the classroom, and so studies which with a small or 
biased sample are unable to capture enough data to see beyond those 
mediating effects. Furthermore, there are differences in the measure­
ment of retention across studies, including the extent to which teachers 
who leave the school but continue teaching elsewhere are counted as 
attrition. This is especially problematic in studies which are localised to 
a school district or a particular programme, and whose data cannot 
capture ‘movers’ away from the district as distinct from ‘leavers’ (Kang, 
2010).
These complexities are further compounded by studies which used 
intention to remain and job satisfaction as proxies for real retention.
4.2.2.1. Real retention. Of the 25 studies in which retention was the 
primary outcome, 12 used real retention data, and all were observa­
tional designs. Five were secondary data analyses in the United States 
and sought to establish trends using large datasets, namely the Schools 
and Staffing Surveys, although over different time periods. While they 
each found a positive correlation between support and the likelihood of 
retention, the studies approached their research in different ways. 
Critically, two studies did not differentiate between types of attrition, 
grouping voluntary and involuntary departures together (Maready et al., 
2021; Williams, 2012). While Ronfeldt and McQueen (2017) made a 
basic distinction between teachers who migrated (moved to a different 
school) and those who left the profession, Kang (2010) and Kang and 
Berliner (2012) went further and distinguished between those who left 
for unavoidable reasons and those who left voluntarily.
The remaining seven studies which used real retention data took a 
programme evaluation approach and focussed on the effectiveness of 
specific programmes in a localised context. As a result, these studies had 
much smaller samples, and their data had a stronger focus on partici­
pants’ experience of the programme. Four studies found no statistically 
significant relationship with retention (Akin-Otiko, 2019; Hendrick­
s-Harris, 2012; Kneer et al., 2009; Kuhaneck, 2015). The three studies 
which found a significant effect had more specific independent vari­
ables, such as the amount of time that a mentor spent with their mentee, 
rather than whether the mentee was assigned a mentor (Crown, 2008). 
Similarly, when comparing district-level coaching and in-school men­
toring models, Hallam et al. (2012) found a stronger relationship be­
tween in-school mentors and retention. Wiens et al. (2019) was able to 
control for school-level contextual differences to identify a strong rela­
tionship between a specific mentoring programme and retention. It may 
be that by approaching the study with more precise independent 
variables, their effect is more statistically evident with a small sample 
size, whereas the effect of more general variables such as the assignment 
of a mentor and participation in induction may only be evident in larger 
samples.
4.2.2.2. Intention to remain. Nine studies measured participants’ 
intention to remain in the profession. The approaches and findings 
varied somewhat. While Antoine (2011) used the Schools and Staffing 
Survey data, the respondents’ intention to remain was used as the 
outcome variable, rather than actual retention through follow-up data. 
This is likely because the intention to remain aligned with the study’s 
secondary outcome, job satisfaction, and the broader conceptual 
framework of that study (Antoine, 2011). However, that study found no 
statistically significant relationship between participation in induction 
and intention to remain (Antoine, 2011). Parker et al. (2009) used a 
large data set that is representative of teachers in North Carolina and 
identified that varying aspects of mentoring, including the mentor 
match, proximity, and frequency of interactions were all associated with 
an increased likelihood of an intention to remain. Matsko (2010) also 
distinguished between mentoring practices, finding that some activities 
predicted a higher likelihood of intention to remain, based on the degree 
to which the participants perceived them to be ‘helpful’.
When measuring the effect of components of induction and men­
toring programmes, Reeder (2013), Larabee (2009) and Morina (2010)
each found a correlation between some elements and the intention to 
remain. Conversely, McCamley (2014), Portis-Woodson (2014), and 
McMichael (2009) found that while participants reported a high inten­
tion to remain in the classroom, they did not attribute that intention to 
the mentoring and induction programmes. It could be that the former 
group of studies did not ask the degree to which participants attributed 
influence to support, or that another mediating factor affected the 
intention to remain.
4.2.2.3. Job satisfaction. Eight studies used job satisfaction as a sup­
plementary outcome or proxy for retention, relying on the theory that 
low job satisfaction is a key reason for attrition. However, only one study 
provided an explicit theoretical framing, utilising the Systems Theory 
Framework of Career Development (Auletto, 2021). That study 
measured satisfaction and commitment as separate mechanisms for 
improving teaching quality, and found a strong positive correlation 
between mentoring and job satisfaction (Auletto, 2021).
The remaining seven studies framed job satisfaction as directly 
influencing retention. These studies were all dissertations, and while 
some found a correlation between support and job satisfaction (Larabee, 
2009; Norris, 2016; Reeder, 2013; Williams, 2012), others found no 
statistically significant relationship (Block, 2017; Hendricks-Harris, 
2012; Williams, 2018). Those which found an effect had larger samples 
and framed the independent variable as participants’ satisfaction with 
programme elements rather than participation in or completion of a 
programme (see, for example, Reeder, 2013).
4.2.3. Teaching practice
Despite the prevalent narrative that support improves teaching 
practice to result in greater retention proposed by Smith and Ingersoll 
(2004), a small number of studies measured teaching practice as an 
outcome. This may be due to the relative ease of measuring other out­
comes, especially where there is readily available data, compared with 
the complexity of measuring teaching practice.
Five studies framed teaching practice as a mechanism for improving 
retention. Two studies found an improvement in teaching practice as 
measured by district- and school-level evaluation tools as a result of 
mentoring, without measuring subsequent retention (Stanulis & Floden, 
2009; Yuen-Apulu, 2017). When they measured retention in addition to 
teaching performance, Bastian and Marks (2017) found an improvement 
in both teaching evaluation results and retention for participants of the

subject-specific induction programme. Importantly, those researchers 
also found that both teacher performance and retention increased in 
correlation with the intensity of induction activities, indicating a dosage 
effect (Bastian & Marks, 2017). This contrasted with the findings of 
Wainwright (2018) and Glazerman et al. (2010), which found a positive 
effect on teaching practice, but no effect on retention. The Glazerman 
et al. study was notable as the only randomised controlled trial in the 
scoping review, and the design allowed for a delayed dosage effect to be 
captured, in which participants of a two year induction programme 
showed improved practice in their third year of teaching, but not before 
(Glazerman et al., 2010). This may point to a non-linear trajectory of 
practice development.
Nine studies framed the purpose of supporting ECTs as improved 
teaching quality. In some studies, this was connected to student out­
comes (Reeves et al., 2022; Scott-Coleman, 2017) and, in others, to 
improving educational equity, arguing that teachers of students in low 
socioeconomic schools need to be more effective to make up for stu­
dents’ lost ground (Bianchini & Brenner, 2010). Some results showed no 
relationship between support and teaching practice, or that induction 
did not contribute to teachers’ improvement (Bianchini & Brenner, 
2010; Reynolds, 2014). In other cases, the findings were unclear, with 
some elements of teaching practice correlated with some elements of 
support, as reported by teachers or measured by evaluations (Hairon 
et al., 2020; Mavroulis, 2012; Schwartz & Ticknor, 2018; Scott-Coleman, 
2017). In studies which used student evaluations, researchers found a 
clearer influence of coaching and mentoring on teaching practice 
(Harmsen et al., 2019; Pickett & Fraser, 2009). This was particularly 
evident in Maulana et al.’s (2015) study, where there was a significant 
difference for the experimental group who received induction support, 
compared with the control group, based on students’ evaluation of their 
teachers’ practice. This could point to the inherent challenges of 
measuring teaching practice, and the methodological advantages of 
some study designs over others. Harmsen et al.’s (2019) and Pickett and 
Fraser’s (2009) studies were also distinct as they measured the effect of 
programmes over two years, rather than one year or at a point in time, 
potentially contributing to their identification of a stronger effect. 
Reeves et al. (2022) analysed data from the Teaching and Learning In­
ternational Survey (TALIS). As this study used a large sample and 
included various response items for both the independent and depen­
dent variable, the researchers identified some correlations, although the 
degree to which a point in time self-report of teaching practice 
improvement can be extrapolated or attributed to induction pro­
grammes is contestable (Reeves et al., 2022).
4.2.4. Student achievement
Two studies measured a change in student achievement as a result of 
induction and mentoring for the ECT. Findings were mixed, with weak 
correlations identified in the observational study (Shadwell, 2012). In 
that study, student learning outcomes were measured according to the 
state’s assessment framework, with a focus on the outcomes of ethni­
cally diverse students relative to their White peers (Shadwell, 2012). In 
the descriptive study, the case study approach allowed for some con­
clusions to be drawn about the role of school culture, from which a 
model for ECT development was proposed (Sadler, 2016). The 
researcher chose the schools based on the achievement scores of stu­
dents with special needs in mainstream classrooms, which were notably 
higher than that of comparable districts (Sadler, 2016). The fact that 
both studies framed student achievement as relative measures – relative 
to peers, and to their scores at the beginning of the year – may indicate 
the difficulty of measuring the flow-on effects of intervention at the 
teacher level to outcomes at the student level.
4.2.5. Other outcomes
There were six studies whose outcomes did not fit within the previ­
ously discussed themes. Two of these were reviews, one systematic and 
one meta-analysis. Keese et al.’s (2023) meta-analysis drew some 
conclusions about the literature, specifically about the difference in 
findings between journal articles and dissertations, and the role of 
publication bias. Kutsyuruba et al.’s (2019a) systematic review focussed 
on identifying the contextual factors that mediate the effect of induction 
programmes for ECTs in the international literature.
There were three studies which, using a case study design, captured 
the nature of practices where induction programmes were deemed to be 
effective, although the criteria for effectiveness varied between studies 
(Floyd, 2018; Kearney, 2017; Pennanen et al., 2016). These studies and 
their findings touched on elements of each theme mentioned above, but 
their orientation as exploratory case studies aimed at furthering the 
theoretical frameworks for mentoring separated them from the above 
studies. Finally, Burke et al. (2015) sought to measure the relative value 
that ECTs place on different forms of mentoring, a novel mechanism for 
understanding the value of mentoring practices and their perceived 
effectiveness.

5. Discussion
5.1. Answering review questions
Review question 1: What is the nature of the body of literature about 
mentoring, induction, and coaching for ECTs? 
• Methodological approaches: Observational studies were most 
frequently used, especially case control studies, for the evaluation of 
existing programmes. Quantitative methods were more frequently 
used than qualitative methods.
• Contexts: The vast majority of studies originated from the United 
States.
• Types of conclusions that can be drawn: Correlational conclusions 
are the strongest form of conclusions that can be drawn from this 
body of literature.
Review question 2: What is the nature and purpose of mentoring, 
induction, and coaching for ECTs? 
• Definitions of support: The definitions for induction are relatively 
consistent, referring to a programme of support at a specific career 
stage. The definitions of mentoring and coaching are less consistent, 
shown in Table 3.
• Stated or implied purposes of support: The studies were grouped 
based on their outcome measures, which provide the stated purposes 
of support, including retention, teaching practice, teacher develop­
ment, and student achievement. There was largely consistency be­
tween the outcome measure, and therefore the stated purpose, and 
the narrative framing of each study.
While there are many aspects from which the findings of this review 
can be discussed, this paper will focus on the tension between the type of 
support as declared by authors and the extent to which underlying 
practices can be captured.
5.2. Type of support: tensions between author-reported support and the 
practices
This scoping review sought to compile the different understandings 
of induction, coaching, and mentoring, and the implied practices. As 
discussed above, the lack of consistency regarding the practices included 
in each form of support has implications for policy.
As shown in Table 3, there is a variation in how each study defined 
the support investigated. While there are four themes which define in­
duction, the defining features of mentoring and coaching are less clear. 
Across these two support types, there were definitions offered, based on 
G.-Y. Luong                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      
Teaching and Teacher Education 163 (2025) 105065 
8 

the experience of the parties involved, the nature of their relationship, 
and how this is reflected in employment arrangements, but there was a 
significant proportion of studies which offered no definition. The simi­
larity between the features of mentoring and coaching highlighted the 
need for closer examination of the practices involved to understand the 
activities that took place within each relationship type and therefore 
how they are distinct. However, upon examination of the practices 
described by studies, overlap between the activities reported to have 
taken place across mentoring and coaching became clear. There were 
also studies which did not provide any descriptions of the practices. The 
opacity as to the practices limited the ability of this scoping review to 
unpack the effectiveness of practices which make up each form of sup­
port, and therefore to describe the support structures at a more granular 
level. As a result, the analysis of this scoping review was limited to 
grouping the studies by the interventions as the authors described them, 
rather than interrogating the practices which took place and providing 
insight into the effect of those practices, as was intended.
One reason for this opacity could be that the parameters of this re­
view limited the detailed understanding of the constructs measured. The 
constructs that need to be described and measured in detail for a deeper 
understanding of the comprising practices are complex and deeply 
qualitative. However, by limiting the review questions to the effective­
ness of support, this scoping review excluded those studies which are 
more likely to provide the detailed understanding of the practices pro­
vided to and experienced by ECTs. The included studies, which met the 
inclusion criteria of having outcome measures, may have compromised 
on their ability to describe the support in detail in favour of evaluating 
effectiveness. Thus, being at the intersection of complex constructs and 
the need to understand effectiveness at scale may be the main limitation 
in this scoping review. An alternative would have been to conduct a 
scoping review of descriptive studies about induction, coaching, and 
mentoring to capture the practices that ECTs experience. From there, a 
grounded approach to analysis could have provided the starting point 
for developing a taxonomical understanding of the terms.
The overlapping use and conflation of practices is significant as 
policy makers, practitioners and researchers each have their own un­
derstanding and expectations of the practices involved, and the lack of 
common language leaves space for misinterpretation of requirements 
and recommendations. As a result, it is unclear what form of support 
ECTs are receiving, and which practices are effective under which cir­
cumstances. There is also a need for clarity from the perspective of the 
ECTs as recipients of support, especially as misalignment between ex­
pectations and reality can contribute to a negative experience of support 
(as in Matsko, 2010).
5.3. Further study
There is scope for a systematic review to conduct a quality assess­
ment and more thorough synthesis of findings. Within the themes of 
teacher development of self and retention, there are sufficient studies for 
a systematic review that narrows in on either one of those outcome 
measures. There may also be a large enough group of studies measuring 
teaching practice for more detailed and systematic review. There is also 
scope for asking different review questions altogether to capture the 
nuances regarding the terms which could not be achieved in this scoping 
review.
6. Conclusions
In this paper, the findings of a scoping review about the role of 
mentoring, induction, and coaching for ECTs were reported, including a 
descriptive and substantive exploration of the findings and a discussion 
of the extent to which the review questions were answered. Based on the 
findings of this study, the IDESR protocol (ID: IDESR000154) has been 
updated to include a systematic review of the effects of mentoring, 
coaching, and induction on ECT self-efficacy, stress resources, and 
resilience, with a view to further synthesising the literature to make 
recommendations for policy makers and practitioners.
