Editor: Craig A. Albers

Decades of research have indicated that reading self-concept is an important predictor of reading
achievement. During this period, the population of emergent bilinguals has continued to increase
within United States’ schools. However, the existing literature has tended to examine native
English speakers’ and emergent bilinguals’ reading self-concept in the aggregate, thereby
potentially obfuscating the unique pathways through which reading self-concept predicts reading
achievement. Furthermore, due to the overreliance of native English speakers in samples relating
to theory development, researchers attempting to examine predictors of reading achievement
may a priori select variables that are more aligned with native English speakers’ experiences. To
address this issue, we adopted Elastic Net, which is a theoretically agnostic methodology and
machine learning approach to variable selection to identify the proximal and distal predictors of
reading self-concept for the entire population; in our study, participants from the United States
who participated in PISA 2018 served as the baseline group to determine significant predictors of
reading self-concept with the intent of identifying potential new directions for future researchers.
Based on Elastic Net analysis, 20 variables at the student level, three variables at the teacher level,
and 12 variables at the school level were identified as the most salient predictors of reading selfconcept. We then utilized a multilevel modeling approach to test model generalizability of the
identified predictors of reading self-concept for emergent bilinguals and native English speakers.
We disaggregated and compared findings for both emergent bilinguals and native English
speakers. Our results indicate that although some predictors were important for both groups (e.g.,
perceived information and communications technologies competence), other predictors were not
(e.g., competitiveness). Suggestions for future directions and implications of the present study are
examined.

1. Introduction
1.1. Reading achievement and emergent bilinguals
Reading ability is considered an essential skill for successfully accomplishing both academic and non-academic life tasks (Clemens
et al., 2021; Retelsdorf et al., 2014). According to the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA; Organization for
Economic Cooperation and Development [OECD], 2019a), reading performance of students in the United States was significantly
lower than that of their counterparts in other developed countries. For example, only 14% of United States’ students reached Level 5 or
Level 6, which correspond to levels at which students can understand long texts, abstract concepts, and distinguish the differences
between facts and opinions (OECD, 2019b). In short, recent PISA assessments suggest that many United States’ high school students are
not strong readers. This finding also has been reflected with younger students as the 2022 National Assessment of Educational Progress
(National Assessment of Educational Progress [NAEP], 2022) indicated that within the United States, over 67% of fourth grade and
69% of eighth grade students were not proficient in reading; these numbers were similar for the 2019 NAEP, which indicated that 65%
of fourth and 66% of eighth grade students were not proficient in reading. Thus, most students did not demonstrate reading competency in challenging subjects (NAEP, 2019, 2022. However, the situation is even more dire for emergent bilinguals in the United
States as the 2019 and 2022 NAEP indicated that for fourth grade emergent bilingual students, only 10% (for both the 2019 and the
2022 NAEP) performed at the proficient; for eighth grade emergent bilingual students, 4% (on the 2019 NAEP) and 5% (on the 2022
NAEP) of students performed at the proficient level in NAEP reading.
1.2. Reading self-concept
Research on emergent bilinguals’ reading achievement continues to show that these students often encounter significant challenges
in obtaining reading proficiency (e.g., Batalova et al., 2007; Chu & Flores, 2011; Dai et al., 2023; Klingner et al., 2012; Valencia et al.,
2001). Furthermore, NAEP results consistently indicate an achievement advantage for native English speakers as compared to their
emergent bilingual counterparts, with this achievement gap being unchanged between 2000 and 2022 (Murphey, 2014; NAEP, 2019,
2022). A potential reason for these difficulties may be low reading self-concept as self-concept is seen as a prerequisite for achievement
according to Calsyn and Kenny’s (1977) self-enhancement hypothesis. Given that there are only a few isolated studies on emergent
bilinguals’ reading self-concept (e.g., Mercer, 2011a, 2011b), large-scale assessments (e.g., PISA) can serve as a source to study this
issue because these assessments gather extensive information about emergent bilinguals’ unique experiences (OECD, 2019c, 2019d).
We operationally defined reading self-concept as a person’s perceptions of their competence in reading (Conradi et al., 2014;
Retelsdorf et al., 2011) or their competency beliefs relating to how good an individual is at a given activity (Morgan & Fuchs, 2007).
Consequently, reading self-concept is a desirable outcome of education (Lafontaine et al., 2019) as it plays a key role in understanding
and improving student reading achievement (Ma et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018). Reading self-concept often is found to be a central
predictor of reading achievement, with greater reading self-concept predicting higher reading achievement and lower levels of reading
self-concept predicting lower reading achievement (e.g., Arens et al., 2013; Karimova & Csapó, 2021; Katzir et al., 2009; Locher et al.,
2021; Marsh & O’Mara, 2008; Morgan & Fuchs, 2007). Given the importance of self-concept in reading, the challenge is how to foster
students’ thinking of themselves as proficient readers.
Understanding reading self-concept can provide insights to parents, teachers, and school administrators in developing strategies
that facilitate students’ positive perceptions of their competence and subsequently better academic performance (Nobre & Valentini,
2019), which is critical given that the United States’ public education system has become increasingly diverse (Pilger Suhr et al., 2021).
National statistics show that approximately 10% of the K–12 students in United States’ public schools were emergent bilinguals (Office
of English Language Acquisition, 2018) with this number expected to continue to increase in the coming years (National Center for
Education Statistics, 2018). Although reforms continue to be implemented in the United States’ educational system to address the
issues that students from minoritized backgrounds (including emergent bilinguals) encounter, many still struggle with reading
achievement (Rivera, 2021). For instance, Rodriguez et al. (2022) indicated that based on teacher report, many emergent bilingual
students have difficulties in understanding reading passages and that the students often had to invest a significant amount of time in
understanding these passages that then resulted in lack of the time for the students to participate in other academic tasks, thereby
potentially undermining their access to practical academic experiences. These issues have raised concerns among policymakers,
legislators, and governors (Allington & McGill-Franzen, 2021) that emergent bilinguals may fall further behind in school. Additionally,
Orth et al. (2021) indicated that within their meta-analysis examining self-evaluations, the included studies primarily were focused on
White/European samples’ self-perceptions (i.e., self-concept). Given Orth et al.’s meta-analytic study, we suggest a significant need for
research that that includes and examines diverse samples (e.g., emergent bilinguals), which is particularly important given that nonEnglish languages have become more common in the United States (Rumbaut & Massey, 2013). Similarly, it has been well-documented
that educators operating from a set of frames of reference, experiences, and knowledge that differ from those of their students often
perpetuate inequities in the educational experiences, trajectories, and outcomes of those same students (Banwo et al., 2022; McGee,
2021; Ray, 2022; Schwarzenthal et al., 2023). The “whitestreaming” (Urrieta, 2010) of schooling devalues the linguistic, literate, and
cultural practices of youth of color whose ways of knowing and being are filtered through “amused contempt and pity” (Du Bois, 1965,
p. 45; Oloniyo et al., in preparation). In other words, racism and other biases that diverse students face can impact their feelings and
beliefs, as well as the feelings and beliefs of teachers, administrators, and staff within the school setting, undermining students’ academic success (Paris, 2012). Fortunately, due to large-scale assessments like PISA, large amounts of data exist from these populations.

1.3. Grounded theory
Given the abundance of data provided by PISA and some of the methodological concerns of previous approaches (e.g., limited
participant samples), in the present study we adopted a grounded theory approach to better understand potential differences as
experienced by learners from minoritized backgrounds. Glaser and Strauss (1967) introduced grounded theory, which provides a
procedure to derive theory from data (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). At its core, grounded theory is a bottom-up approach that builds theory
from observations (i.e., data) rather than more traditional approaches wherein researchers choose what variables to measure, when to
measure, and how often to measure based on existing theory as the guide. The advantages of grounded theory are many; for example,
in the present study, we did not limit our search only to those variables previously identified as salient predictors because such an
approach would serve to reinforce existing theories. As indicated by Doyle and Paget (1891), “It is a capital mistake to theorize before
one has data. Insensibly one begins to twist facts to suit theories, instead of theories to suit facts” (p. 7). Given the large scope of PISA
datasets, others have made similar arguments relating to the need to let data be our guide (e.g., Nelson, 2020).
1.4. Machine learning approach
The variable selection machine learning feature used in our study is particularly well-suited to handle the nature of large-scale data
sets as it allows researchers to explore robust data patterns, potentially leading to (dis)confirming or inductively building theory
(Choudhury et al., 2018; Nelson, 2020). That is, a variable selection machine learning approach makes fewer a priori assumptions
about the underlying model that best represents the data and allows for replicable pattern identification (Choudhury et al., 2018). For
example, a three-step computational grounded theory framework (Nelson, 2020) comes from the field of content analysis. In this
framework, machine learning (a) serves as a tool for pattern detection in Step 1, (b) allows for pattern refinement through researchers’
“interpretive engagement with the data” (p. 3) in Step 2, and (c) allows for pattern confirmation through additional computational or
statistical techniques in Step 3. Thus, machine learning gives the researchers the responsibility to make use of data through different
methodological approaches because grounded theory is seen as both the process and the methodology (Pulla, 2016). By using machine
learning approaches, data can be used to produce insights that may lead to inductive theory building (Choudhury et al., 2018). For
example, to obtain the best predictive model of a construct, machine learning methods (e.g., Elastic Net) employ algorithmic induction
only from the data (Zou & Hastie, 2005). We suggest that PISA large-scale assessment data be used to either refine existing theories or
to generate new theories by employing a grounded theory approach that utilizes machine learning algorithms (e.g., Elastic Net).
Below, we review the relevant research on predictors of reading self-concept. Similar to work focused on reading performance, the
literature review that follows does not aim to comprehensively summarize all relevant predictors of reading self-concept. Rather, this
review seeks to highlight “a sense of the range of factors within the student and arising from students’ interactions in relevant social
environments” (Bernardo et al., 2021, p. 3) consistent with socioecological systems theory of human development and learning
(Bronfenbrenner, 1977).
1.5. Literature review
1.5.1. An overview of self-concept
In general, self-concept can be defined as the cognitive and evaluative perceptions of self (Shavelson et al., 1976; Wylie, 1979) that
involve self-perceptions of competency, influence, and positive self-worth (Beck et al., 2005; e.g., “I am a worthy and adequate
person”; Landsman, 1962, p. 289). These perceptions are actively constructed over time and organized in a hierarchical fashion to form
an individual’s self-concept (Shavelson et al., 1976; Vignoles et al., 2006). According to the Shavelson hierarchical model, general selfconcept is considered as having two main facets, consisting of (a) non-academic self-concept that can be divided into social and
emotional self-concepts, and (b) academic self-concept. The non-academic self-concept is typically an aspect of an individual’s general
self-concept and it can be defined as a person’s self-perception of themselves in everyday life environment beyond educational environments and academic achievements. Non-academic self-concept comprises emotional, social, and physical components and has
been associated with healthy social relationships, mental health, and satisfaction with life (e.g., Geary & Xu, 2022; Ogle et al., 2016;
Shavelson et al., 1976). Academic self-concept comprises self-concept associated with academic practices (Marsh, 2014; Marsh &
Martin, 2011) and it can be further specified in subject-specific domains (e.g., mathematics self-concept, reading self-concept; Gogol
et al., 2016) that can influence recent and future subject-specific achievement (Susperreguy et al., 2018). Academic self-concept is
present not just in school, but rather, it refers to academic skills like reading and mathematics. As these are academic skills that are
used throughout one’s lifetime, having a positive self-concept as a reader should predict future reading later in life; thus, even though
15-year-olds are nearing the end of compulsory schooling in most countries, reading self-concept still is important (Hornery et al.,
2014).
According to Walker (2015), emergent bilinguals’ reading self-concept is complex and may have a significant influence on the
learning processes of language learners, but not on the broader student population. This often happens because individuals interact
with learning environments by considering their own view of themselves potentially through the lens of their cultures (Vo & French,
2021). Given the unique challenges faced by emergent bilinguals, researchers should study these students’ self-concepts to explore
such challenges. However, only a few studies have specifically examined emergent bilinguals’ reading self-concept (e.g., Mercer,
2011a, 2011b).

1.5.2. Demographics-related factors
Various studies have examined the relationships between reading self-concept and individuals’ demographic background characteristics. Hall (2014) found that there were no significant differences between socioeconomic groups on reading self-concept ratings
(F (1, 66) = 0.00, p = .99, η2 = 0.00), whereas reading achievement was reported as the strongest predictor of reading self-concept.
Gender was also found to be an important predictor of reading self-concept. For instance, Katzir et al. (2018) reported that girls
demonstrated lower reading self-concept than boys (β = 0.19, p = .02), although girls performed better than boys in reading accuracy
(β = − 0.18, p = .03). Sedgwick (2014) indicated that age was associated with reading self-concept in that when the participating
students became older, they tended to have more negative reading self-concept (r = − 0.161, p < .05).
1.5.3. Reading-related factors
Reading-related factors have revealed mixed associations with reading self-concept over time. For instance, Retelsdorf et al. (2014)
found strong support for reading achievement with the important role of social comparison as a predictor of reading self-concept where
β ranged from 0.09 to 0.82 (p < .05), suggesting that students who are aware that they have better reading skills than their classmates
may use these beliefs to develop positive self-evaluations of their own reading skills. According to Kasperski et al. (2016), reading
abilities (i.e., reading fast) can positively contribute to reading self-concept because individuals who can read fast may perceive
themselves as more capable readers (r = 0.39, p < .01); subsequently, they may find the reading tasks less difficult to accomplish.
Sewasew and Koester (2019) studied reading competence and reading self-concept separately by utilizing longitudinal large-scale
assessment data. However, they found that reading self-concept and reading competence were interlocked in a developmental
perspective as reading self-concept and reading competence were interrelated and cooperatively reinforcing over time (e.g., β =. 42, p
< .05). Locher et al. (2021) asked students about their reading habits in and out of school settings to examine the mediation role of
reading habits on the relationship between reading achievement and reading self-concept, with results indicating that reading habits
were neither a significant predictor of reading achievement nor a mediator. Chapman and Tunmer (1997) conducted a longitudinal
study to examine the interplay between reading self-concept and reading performance and concluded that there was not a significant
relationship between pre-reading skills and initial reading self-concept in either the first or second year of schooling (B = 0.04, p =
.33). However, Griffin et al. (2020) examined bi/multilingual Latinx adolescents’ reading self-concept regarding its relationship with
reading motivation, finding that reading self-concept and reading attitude were significantly interrelated. Thus, reading self-concept
has been reported to have positive relationships with reading attitudes, suggesting that better reading attitude could be associated with
better reading self-concept.
1.5.4. Motivation-related factors
In terms of motivational aspects, Van Kraayenoord and Schneider (1999) found that students with more metacognitive knowledge
about reading strategies were expected to have a more positive interest in reading (β = 0.84, p < .05) and higher levels of reading selfconcept (β = 0.34, p < .0001). For reading-specific motivations, Schiefele et al. (2012) reviewed the literature related to the constructs
of reading motivation and reported evidence from several qualitative and quantitative studies that intrinsic reading motivation
positively contributed to reading self-concept and that extrinsic reading motivation negatively contributed to reading self-concept. In
Clem et al. (2021), literacy self-concept was positively linked to enjoyment in literacy (r = 0.44, p < .01), whereas it was negatively
associated with anxiety in literacy (r = − 0.40, p < .01).
1.5.5. Teacher-related factors
There are only a few studies that have investigated the influence of teacher-related factors on students’ reading self-concept. Jensen
et al. (2019) indicated that teachers’ emotional support provided to students can contribute to both higher levels of reading-related
self-concept (r = 0.21, p < .01) and subsequently higher reading achievement (r = 0.10, p < .01). Clem et al. (2021) also found
that students’ reading self-concept was positively related to closeness with teachers (r = 0.15, p < .01) and negatively associated with
conflict with teachers (r = − 0.15, p < .01).
1.5.6. Learning environment-related factors
Learning environments (e.g., schools, classrooms) have been considered as influential factors on reading self-concept. For instance,
Yaratan and Yucesoylu (2010) investigated the effects of school type (i.e., private and state elementary schools) on student selfconcepts, including reading self-concept. They reported that there was no significant difference between students’ self-concept by
school type, t (105) = 0.869, p = .387. School bullying, which is related to victimization, was found to be a significant predictor of
reduced reading self-concept (β = − 0.07, p < .001; Caputo, 2014). By considering peers’ effects, Flores and Duran (2013) showed that
a positive working climate, the positive assessment of the efforts, reading-aloud and active listening to a peer, and inter-peer metacognitive reflections on the progress contributed to positive reading self-concepts.
1.6. The present study
Considering the multidimensional structure of self-concept (Arens & Jansen, 2016), the PISA is a beneficial source to study
emergent bilinguals’ reading self-concept as PISA collects information regarding hundreds of contextual variables at multiple levels,
both for native English speakers and for emergent bilinguals (Kastberg et al., 2021; OECD, 2019c, 2019d). Thus, the main goals of this
study were to (a) use a machine learning approach to identify the most salient predictors of reading self-concept using the most recent
2018 PISA reading-focused assessment data in the United States, and (b) examine the relative effects of the identified variables on

The following research questions guided our research in the present study:
1. What predictors of students’ reading self-concept are most salient at the student, teacher, and school level?
2. What are the relative contributions of the most salient student-, teacher-, and school-level predictors to students’ reading selfconcept?
3. What are the relative contributions of the most salient student-, teacher-, and school-level predictors to students’ reading selfconcept when disaggregated by home language?
2. Method
2.1. Data and participants
We used the 2018 United States’ PISA reading assessment data for our study. PISA is one of the largest international large-scale
survey and assessment programs. In addition to providing reading performance outcomes, PISA gathers background information at
the student, teacher, and school levels, such as family, economic, social, and cultural background of participating 15-year-old students.
Additionally, PISA also collects information regarding (a) students’ motivational, cognitive, and metacognitive aspects (e.g., perceptions, attitudes, well-being); (b) teacher-related instructional components (e.g., feedback, in-class activities); and (c) school
characteristics (i.e., school type, class size, or school size; Kastberg et al., 2021; OECD, 2019c, 2019d). In the 2018 assessment cycle,
reading was a major domain that allowed for a more in-depth assessment and understanding of students’ reading performances. PISA’s
comprehensive list of variables that are collected allowed us to explore potential factors associated with students’ reading self-concept
(Kastberg et al., 2021; OECD, 2019c, 2019d).
A total of 4838 adolescents in the United States participated in the PISA 2018 reading assessment. The average age of the participants was 15.84 (SD = 0.29) years. Approximately 49% (n = 2376) of participants self-reported their gender as female and 50.9%
(n = 2462) as male. For immigration status, 76.4% (n = 3696) of participants indicated their immigration status as native, 15.4% (n =
746) as second-generation, and 5% (n = 244) as first-generation. For language status, 83.8% (n = 4054) of participants reported
English and 15.2% (n = 736) reported another language as the most spoken language at home. In addition to the students, 3526
teachers also participated in the PISA. Their average age was 42.87 (SD = 11.13) years; 50.6% (n = 1784) self-reported their gender as
female and 28.6% (n = 1019) as male. Additionally, 51.4% (n = 1812) were classified as general education teachers and 29.8% (n =
1049) were classified as reading/language-specific teachers. In total, 164 schools across the U.S. were selected to participate in PISA
2018, of which 93.3% (n = 153) were public schools and 6.1% (n = 10) were private schools; one school did not indicate whether they
were public or private.
2.2. Variables
Students’ reading self-concept scores were the outcome of interest in the present study. In PISA 2018, students’ reading self-concept
was measured by a scale of three questions: (a) “I am a good reader”, (b) “I am able to understand difficult texts”, and (c) “I read
fluently” (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.84; McDonald’s Omega = 0.84). Principal component analysis for reading self-concept resulted in an
eigenvalue of 2.282 and was the only component that yielded an eigenvalue larger than 1; 76.059% of the variance was explained by
this component. It appears that PISA adapted these items to measure reading self-concept from Chapman and Tunmer (1995) and
Chapman and Tunmer (1999) Reading Self-Concept Scale, which was also adapted for the Progress in International Reading Literacy
Study (PIRLS) that is a reading-focused international large-scale assessment in all cycles (i.e., PIRLS 2001, PIRLS 2006, PIRLS 2011,
and PIRLS 2016; Mullis et al., 2003, 2007, 2012; Mullis & Martin, 2015). In addition to large-scale educational assessments, empirical
literature has also employed Chapman and Tunmer’s Reading Self-Concept Scale (e.g., Katzir et al., 2009; Thijssen et al., 2022). The
Reading Self-Concept Scale originally comprised 30 items that were categorized within the following three subdimensions: (a) Perceptions of competence in reading, (b) Perceptions of difficulty in reading, and (c) Attitudes towards reading. The original scale
showed longitudinally good reliability which ranged from Cronbach’s alpha = 0.84 to Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85. The scale was also
strongly correlated with reading comprehension which again ranged from r = 0.47 to r = 0.60 (p < .01) over time. Additionally,
correlations between the subdimensions of the reading self-concept scale ranged from 0.17 to 0.28 (p < .05; see Chapman & Tunmer,
1995).
To address the research questions and identify the most salient predictors of student reading self-concept, we applied a multi-step
strategy and started the analysis with all variables collected by PISA. Specifically, we used all variables (N = 1482) at the student,
teacher, and school levels; these variables are briefly described below based on Kastberg et al. (2021) and the OECD (2019c, 2019d).
2.2.1. Student-level variables
In PISA 2018, student participants responded to a questionnaire that included questions about their demographic backgrounds,
school and learning experiences, familiarity with and use of information and communications technologies, well-being and life
satisfaction, and preparations for future career. In total, data were available for 943 variables.

2.2.2. Teacher-level variables

2.3. Data analysis
To address our research questions, we firstly applied the Elastic Net (ENET) approach (Zou & Hastie, 2005), which is a theoretically
agnostic variable selection method in machine learning designed to select the most statistically salient predictors of students’ reading
self-concept at the student, teacher, and school levels. After screening and reviewing the ENET identified variables based on theory and
relevant literature, we examined the variables that were identified via ENET analysis by conducting a two-level multilevel modeling
analysis with the entire United States’ student sample as the reference model and then investigated the reference model’s generalizability for emergent bilinguals.
2.4. Elastic net analysis
ENET is a commonly used feature/variable selection approach within machine learning for datasets in which the number of
predictors is significantly large. Developed by Zou and Hastie (2005), this approach combines two other penalized or regularized
regression approaches consisting of L1-norm penalty (lasso) and L2-norm penalty (ridge) to select the best performing predictive model.
Implementation of ENET removes the limitations when lasso and ridge are separately used by (a) performing continuous shrinkage and
automatic variable selection (Zou & Hastie, 2005), (b) encouraging the grouping effect (Zhou, 2013) by allowing the models to select a
group of highly correlated variables, and (c) removing the limitation regarding the number of selected variables (Chen et al., 2019; Wei
et al., 2019). ENET is different from and has advantages over traditional regression analyses; for instance, ENET can be employed as an
appropriate alternative to stepwise regression or other model selection and dimensionality reduction analysis because it uses a lasso
regression’s function, which means that when the penalty term is set to be increased, lasso regression shrinks more coefficients to zero,
subsequently resulting in a smaller model with a smaller number of predictors. This also pertains to variable selection among
redundant variables because ENET shrinks the redundant variables’ coefficients to zero, which helps with the improvement of the
model prediction accuracy as ENET stabilizes the L1-norm penalty regularization. Furthermore, as stated above, ENET can perform
variable selection with highly correlated variables, which provides an advantage over conventional regression analyses (i.e., stepwise
regression) as stepwise regression simply assumes that the variables that are included in the model are not highly correlated (Hastie
et al., 2009; Liu & Li, 2017; Tibshirani, 1996; Zou & Hastie, 2005). Thus, ENET can deal with the issue of “large p, small n” (West et al.,
2001; Zou & Hastie, 2005), whereas backward elimination encounters difficulties when it is set to do so. Similarly, backward elimination and forward selection include or remove variables one by one. Therefore, these methods cause stability issues when they are
used to construct a model (Yoo, 2018). Conversely, ENET performs continuous shrinkage and automatic variable selection that stabilize the model construction with regularizations and increased prediction accuracy (Price & Sherwood, 2018; Zou & Hastie, 2005).
However, one limitation of the ENET (and other penalized regressions) is the lack of reliable significance tests for the coefficients due
to penalization. A solution to this issue in practice is to evaluate the relative statistical importance of the variables in the model using
standardized coefficients.
Researchers have used ENET in recent educational and psychological studies to identify the most salient factors for student learning
outcomes within big data (e.g., Dai et al., 2023; Goretzko & Bühner, 2020; Ho et al., 2021; Mei et al., 2021; Watts et al., 2021; Yoo,
2018). In the present study, two packages in R (R Core Team, 2020), including caret (Kuhn, 2008, 2021) and glmnet (Friedman et al.,
2010, 2021), were used to accomplish the ENET analysis. Considering that the ENET method focuses on best-performing model
prediction rather than statistical significance (i.e., p-values are usually not available in ENET), we selected the variables based on their
relative statistical contribution using standardized coefficients (i.e., reading self-concept). Given the complex and hierarchical sampling design of PISA, the hierarchical nested structure of the data should be considered (OECD, 2019d). To deal with the hierarchical
nested data structure in the present study, the ENET analysis was conducted separately for each level. Specifically, we conducted ENET
analysis at the student level, teacher level, and school level independently before we aggregated and merged the three datasets.
2.4.1. Multilevel modeling
Multilevel modeling (MLM) was used to explore the relationship between reading self-concept and the identified variables from the
first two ENET steps (Bryk & Raudenbush, 1992; Goldstein, 1995; OECD, 2009). In the present study, a series of two-level MLM
analyses were conducted for three separate samples (i.e., entire student sample, emergent bilinguals, and native English speakers). We
initially planned to utilize a three-level MLM approach (i.e., student level, teacher level, and school level); however, PISA has not
identified the linkage between students and teachers, although there have been linkages between students and schools and between as
teachers and school. Thus, we aggregated the teacher-level variables at the school level and instead conducted a two-level MLM
analyses. To ensure statistical accuracy in the present study, replication weights were included in the model to account for betweencluster variance (e.g., schools) as recommended by the PISA manual (OECD, 2009) and the PISA analytical framework (Organization
for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2019c). These analyses were conducted using lme4 (Bates et al., 2021) and mitml (Grund

et al., 2021) packages in R (R Core Team, 2020). The multilevel modeling specifications are included in Appendix A.
2.5. Data operations
2.5.1. Missing data
Missing data are a common concern in psychological and educational research as it sometimes leads to a drop in precision and bias
in parameter estimates; however, the effect of missing data on obtained results differ depending on how missing data are treated (Cox
et al., 2014). Importantly, missing data issues are often encountered when large-scale assessment data is used (e.g., Weirich et al.,
2014). In the present study, a 20% cutoff point was first applied to exclude the variables that had a missing data rate beyond 20%
(Enders, 2003). Second, for the ENET analysis, the predictive mean matching (PMM) method was employed to handle the missing
proportions of the remaining variables after applying the 20% cutoff point by using R studio mice package (R Core Team, 2020; Van
Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2021). Third, the two-level fully conditional specification imputation method was applied to handle
the missing data for the multilevel analyses that were conducted (Enders et al., 2018; Van Buuren, 2007). For the analysis, the R
packages mice (Van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2021) and miceadds (Robitzsch & Grund, 2021) were used.
2.5.2. Variable screening and reviewing
Based on prior studies, we retained the top 20 predictors with the highest coefficients (Bernardo et al., 2021; Dai et al., 2023). After
the list of ENET-suggested variables was obtained, a panel of four experts in reading literacy and self-concept reviewed and screened
the variables using theory and prior research as a guide. This panel was comprised of two professors and two doctoral candidates whose
research was centered in either reading literacy or self-concept. The panel worked independently to determine if the variables selected
by the algorithm would make coherent sense if included in the model. For example, if the variable “The school’s use of assessments of
students to make decisions about students’ retention or promotion” was selected, the panel might conclude that this relationship is
spurious or otherwise outside the bounds of the present study. In this fashion, the panel served not to select variables as a priori, but
rather to act as human oversight to ensure the algorithm was not selecting inappropriate variables. The panel members each independently examined the variables selected and unanimously agreed on the included variables. This step resulted in a refined list of the
variables that were included for further multilevel modeling analysis.
The variable selection procedure was guided by several theoretical frameworks utilizing both a “bottom-up” (see Glaser & Strauss,
1967, and Nelson, 2020, for computational grounded theory) and “top-down” (see Bronfenbrenner, 1977, for the ecological model
framework; see OECD, 2019c, for the PISA 2018 reading literacy framework) approaches. Using the bottom-up approach, computational grounded theory underpinned the use of the ENET analysis to identify the most salient variables related to student PISA 2018
reading self-concept. Utilizing this approach allowed for the improvement of the accuracy, stability, and predictive power of the model
by reducing the number of irrelevant or redundant variables and focusing on the most salient predictors for understanding students’
reading self-concept in the context of PISA 2018. As noted by others (e.g., Dai et al., 2023; Kubsch et al., 2023), computational
grounded theory provides a rigorous and evidence-based support to the process of selecting variables for analysis in complex datasets
allowing researchers to identify patterns or relationships in the data that might not be immediately apparent otherwise. This bottomup approach to variable selection was conducted in concert with a more top-down hierarchical approach that sought to engage variable
selection across various systemic levels.
Bronfenbrenner’s (1977) ecological model emphasizes the significance of examining and understanding the complex and dynamic
relationships among various systems and levels of influence in one’s environment from the microsystems of individual characteristics
to the macrosystems of social and cultural experiences (i.e., classrooms and schools). Aligned with the Bronfenbrenner’s ecological
model, the use of ENET led us to examine the most salient predictors of student reading self-concept in the context of PISA 2018 across
student, teacher, and school levels. By leveraging this theoretical approach to variable selection, we were able to gain a more
comprehensive understanding of the complex mechanisms and interactions among student-related outcomes across a wide range of
cognitive and non-cognitive domains, such as contextual factors (e.g., student well-being), learning environments (e.g., activities
offered by school), and socio-economic factors (e.g., percentage of students from socioeconomically disadvantaged homes). Finally,
the identified predictors of student reading self-concept provided insight into the underlying factors that influence educational outcomes in accordance with the PISA 2018 framework, which emphasizes influences across levels, including student background
characteristics (e.g., attitudes, out of school reading), teaching and learning environments (e.g., teacher preparation, curriculum), and
school governance and policies (e.g., climate, routines). In summary, computational grounded theory, the ecological model framework, and the PISA 2018 framework provided justification for the variables that were identified by the ENET analysis and included in
the present study. Integrating Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model, the ENET analysis, and the PISA 2018 reading literacy framework
(OECD, 2019c) also has been applied to study students’ reading achievement in PISA 2018 (Dai et al., 2023).
2.5.3. Variable preparation
The composite variables created by PISA, when available, were used. However, in addition to the original PISA composite variables, there were still many individual items that were identified in previous steps, thereby raising concerns of over-parameterization,
model nonconvergence, and assumption violations (e.g., multicollinearity) for MLM. To address this issue, a grouping procedure was
applied to construct composite variables out of selected individual items. Explicitly, the item groups or composite variables were first
achieved by the same panel of content experts based on their content specification. Reliability and validity evidence supporting the use
of these variables were then obtained using McDonald’s ω (McDonald, 1999) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), respectively. If
model fit indices yielded a satisfactory model fit (e.g., a non-significant Chi-square test, CFI and TLI > 0.90, SRMR and RMSEA <0.08),
7

Journal of School Psychology 101 (2023) 101254

O. Ramazan et al.

the factor scores are saved as the composite variable and were included in the analysis. When the CFA analyses yielded poor model fit,
principal component analysis (PCA) was used and the scores of the first component were obtained as the composite variable (OECD,
2019c). The purpose of PCA is to retain the maximum amount of variance (i.e., the first component) accounted for by the items in a
group.
2.5.4. Sampling weights and centering
Student sampling weights, which were scaled, were included in each analysis (see Rabe-Hesketh & Skrondal, 2006). For the MLM
analysis, data were centered by using the cluster mean at Level 1 and the grand mean at Level 2 to obtain an interpretable and
meaningful zero point (Enders & Tofighi, 2007).

3.1. ENET analysis
In what follows, we report and discuss those predictors of reading self-concept that were most salient at the student, teacher, and

are reported in Table 1 for the 20 variables at the student level, Table 2 for the three variables at the teacher level, and Table 3 for the

At the student level, 20 variables were selected as the proximal and distal predictors of reading self-concept. We noticed that
although some of the variables showed positive associations, other variables revealed a negative relationship with reading selfconcept. Specifically, positive variables that were identified included the (a) three variables of students’ reading habits (i.e., “How
often do students read the newspapers?”, “How often do students read fiction books?”, and “How often do students read non-fiction
books?”), (b) two home resources variables consisting of having a digital device or televisions at home, (c) work mastery, (d) perceived
information and communications technologies competence, (e) general fear of failure, (f) competitiveness at school, (g) father’s education level, (h) having difficulties with reading, and (i) the concentration on the parts of the text that are easy to understand. The
variables with negative associations included information and communications technologies related variables (e.g., use of digital

school” was negatively associated with reading self-concept, whereas students’ thoughts on “Trying hard at school would help them
get a good job” was positively related to reading self-concept. Students’ beliefs about whether their teachers in the English-related
lessons understood them were negatively associated with reading self-concept.

variables were found to be negatively associated with reading self-concept.

First, a CFA was used to create the composite variables as factor scores given that CFA can be employed as an approach for validity in social science studies (Hernandez, 2010).

4. Discussion
There is a significant need to study the influential factors of reading self-concept, which is a vital component of reading motivation
and reading performance (Griffin et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018), especially for adolescent emergent bilinguals, who
are a a largely understudied student population in the United States (Griffin et al., 2020). This is particularly important as the enhanced
self-concept as a reader (operationalized as reading self-concept) may lead to increased reading performance (Arens et al., 2013; Katzir
et al., 2009; Locher et al., 2021; Marsh & O’Mara, 2008; Morgan & Fuchs, 2007). However, research findings in this area have raised
concerns among policymakers, legislators, and governors (Allington & McGill-Franzen, 2021) that emergent bilinguals may fall further
behind in reading performance. Following calls from policymakers and scholars, we sought to examine the unique set of challenges
that may be faced by adolescent emergent bilinguals, who are students approaching the end of compulsory education but who may still
experience difficulties with reading in the language of the schooling and testing.
Guided by computational grounded theory underpinning the use of Elastic Net analysis as the variable selection machine learning
approach, Bronfenbrenner’s (1977) ecological model framework to examine the multi-layered systems, and the PISA 2018 reading
literacy framework, we first applied Elastic Net, which is a variable selection machine learning method, to identify the most salient
predictors of reading self-concept for the entire population using PISA 2018. To further examine the relative and statistically significant contributions of the identified predictors, we used multilevel modeling on the entire population first and then separately to
emergent bilingual and native English-speaking subpopulations.
4.1. Summary of the findings
Based on the highest coefficients in the entire population, the Elastic Net results indicated that students’ reading habits and the
number of televisions at home (which may be interpreted as a proxy for SES; e.g., OECD, 2019d) had the strongest positive associations
with reading self-concept at the student level. In turn, students’ feelings of being miserable, the amount of internet use outside of the
school, and source credibility when reading emails (all plausible distractors from reading per se or contributors to negative reading
attitudes) had the strongest negative associations with reading self-concept. All variables selected at the teacher level (i.e., physical
education in teacher training, teachers’ description of reading books, and overlap between teacher education and teaching in modern
foreign languages) showed negative associations with reading self-concept. Finally, at the school level, three selected variables (i.e.,
preparatory program for English language skills before transferring to regular classes, the percentage of total funding from the government, and school’s use of assessments of students to make decisions about students’ retention or promotion) revealed positive
relationships with reading self-concept. The strongest negative associations at the school level were students not being attentive,
percentage of students with special needs, and whether the schools hosted visiting teachers from other countries.
The overall results indicated that 20 variables at the student level, three variables at the teacher level, and 12 variables at the school
level were proximal and distal correlates of reading self-concept. Prior to the MLM analysis, various conceptually related variables
were combined, yielding 11, three, and nine variables at the student, teacher, and school levels, respectively.
To answer the second (What are the relative contributions of the most salient student-, teacher-, and school-level predictors to
students’ reading self-concept when disaggregated by home language?) and third (What are the relative contributions of the most
salient student-, teacher-, and school-level predictors to students’ reading self-concept?) research questions, we used a two-level MLM
analysis on the entire population to test for statistical significance and relevant contributions across student, teacher, and school levels
of reading self-concept’s predictors identified as most salient by the Elastic Net analysis. Considering impacts across all three levels,
nine of the 11 predictors examined emerged as statistically significant at the student level. Specifically, students’ reading habits (i.e.,

“How often do students read the newspapers?”, “How often do students read fiction books?”, and “How often do students read nonfiction books?”), perceived information and communications technologies competence, work mastery (e.g., persisting until the task is
finished), and teacher understanding (e.g., “Thinking of past two reading lessons: I felt my teacher understood me”) emerged as the
strongest positive predictors of reading self-concept at the student level. The remaining statistically positive predictors included
competitiveness, attitude towards school, and father’s education level. Availability and usage of ICT and subjective well-being
emerged as statistically significant negative contributors to reading self-concept. At the teacher level, MLM analysis identified no
statistically significant predictors of reading self-concept. At the school level, four statistically significant predictors emerged. The
school’s equity-oriented policies and percentage of total funding from the government were positive predictors of reading self-concept.
Schools hosting visiting teachers from other countries and percentage of students from socioeconomically disadvantaged homes were
negative predictors of reading self-concept.
In sum, when simultaneously considering impacts across all levels, the MLM analyses on the full entire sample identified nine
student-level and four school-level statistically significant predictors. Importantly, all but one student-level (i.e., father’s education
level) predictors are instructionally or institutionally manipulable.
4.2. Findings and their relevance to previous literature
Similar to earlier findings across other learning domains (Ramazan et al., 2023), the overall results suggest that the final model for
native English speaker students more closely mirrors that for the combined student sample. In particular, eight out of nine student-level
and three out of four school-level variables were statistically significant predictors of native English speakers’ reading self-concept. In
contrast, only four of the student-level predictors, none of the school-level predictors, and only one teacher-level predictor was not
significant for the combined sample but were statistically significant predictors for emergent bilinguals’ reading self-concept.
At the student level, the four positive, statistically significant predictors for both student populations (i.e., reading habits, teacher
understanding, work mastery, and perceived information and communications technologies competence) revealed somewhat different
saliences for emergent bilinguals’ and native English speakers’ reading self-concept. In particular, reading habits was a much stronger
predictor for native English speakers as compared to emergent bilinguals, which may be attributed to differences in reading habits (e.
g., reading in English only versus reading in both English and the native language) for native English speakers vs. emergent bilinguals.
Although there is strong evidence that reading and metacognitive skills transfer across languages (Genesee et al., 2006), cross linguistic
transfer of reading habits may impact self-concept as a reader in English to a lesser degree for emergent bilinguals, which suggests that
emergent bilinguals may need to be more explicitly introduced to the benefits of bilingualism and biliteracy (Adesope et al., 2010;
Genesee et al., 2006) to more strongly impact their self-concepts as both multilingual and English readers. Furthermore, students with
proficient reading levels are typically aware of their reading skills as an indicator of their reading self-concept and they demonstrate a
tendency to participate in reading-related activities more often. This also can be seen as a strong relationship between reading selfconcept and reading habits (Harackiewicz et al., 2008; Marsh et al., 2005). By contrast, the impacts of the three remaining shared
statistically significant predictors (i.e., perceived information and communications technologies competence, work mastery, and
teacher understanding) were somewhat larger for emergent bilinguals than for native English speakers, suggesting that emergent
bilinguals may rely more on developing computer-related competences, mastery goal orientations, and understanding teachers, which
are all factors that teachers and schools can instructionally support or foster through professional development to develop their reading
self-concept. For instance, Özcan and Bulus (2022) used PISA 2018 data and found that across multiple countries, students with higher
levels of perceived information and communications technologies competence and mastery goal orientation tended to be academically
more resilient than the students with lower levels of perceived information and communications technologies competence and mastery
goal orientation. More specifically, individuals with higher levels of perceived information and communications technologies
competence may be able to adapt the contemporary digital environments and navigate and integrate the online resources to their
learning settings (Haleem et al., 2022). This process could potentially lead students to use the online resources for better academic
achievement. As mastery goal-oriented students appear to be more resilient and they tend to see the academic challenges as an opportunity for learning (e.g., Splan et al., 2011), the challenges they encounter may help students have a positive mindset regarding
themselves (e.g., reading self-concept). These factors can subsequently be seen as indicators of a better perception of self when performing an academic task, which is associated with better performance (i.e., Ma et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018).
The remaining four factors that were statistically significant for native English speakers but not for emergent bilinguals included
competitiveness, attitude towards school, availability and usage of information and communications technologies, and father’s education level. For example, father’s education level was associated with higher levels of native English speakers’ reading self-concept.
Although we know from previous research (e.g., Hall, 2014) that father’s education level is associated with reading self-concept across
multiple student populations and educational settings, our findings indicated that this factor might be predictive for all individuals as it
did not yield salience for emergent bilinguals’ reading self-concept. This may have been because emergent bilinguals’ reading selfconcept could be impacted differentially or by other components of economic, social, and cultural status. In particular, the discrepancies in emergent bilinguals and native English speakers’ findings may be due to the influences of mainstream (i.e., middle class,
white) American values, reflecting the accumulated forms of cultural and social capital (Bourdieu, 1986) associated with higher levels
of education and emphasizing self-reliance and individualism, benefiting some student populations over others. For emergent bilinguals, the forms of accumulated capital and expectations may differ from those valued by the school (Egbert & Ernst-Slavit, 2018),
potentially leading forms of school-valued economic (e.g., information and communications technologies availability, father’s education level), cultural (e.g., competitiveness), and social (e.g., school attitudes) capital failing to contribute to emergent bilinguals’
reading self-concept.

At the teacher level, only physical education in teacher training, perhaps plausibly associated with valuing a particular body image
or lifestyle, was a statistically significant negative predictor of reading self-concept for emergent bilinguals only. This finding may need
a closer examination through qualitative means, such as classroom observations and interviews with teachers and students. One
possible explanation may be that emergent bilingual populations, especially adolescent emergent bilinguals, may be more vulnerable
to language barriers due to limited language support from their teachers (e.g., insufficient instructions), stereotyping and bias (e.g.,
differential treatment), low expectations, or lack of inclusive practices (e.g., Cichy et al., 2022; Sato et al., 2017). The ways in which
teachers interact with emergent bilinguals may expectedly negatively impact students’ learning (Appel et al., 2015) and self-image,
which is often a product of repeated experiences (Guthrie et al., 2013) potentially across both social and academic domains. The
idea of self-image is aligned with James’ (1890) material self, impacting one’s physical self-concept (e.g., Goño & Goñi, 2008). This can
be interpreted as one’s perceptions of their physical selves can influence their perceptions of their academic selves when they perform a
given academic task, such as reading.
Findings at the school level indicated no statistically significant effects for emergent bilinguals, suggesting that simply having
certain formal policies may not be sufficient for supporting all students. It is surprising that factors such as school equity-oriented
policies (a non-significant predictor for both groups), activities offered by the school, and hosting visiting teachers from other
countries (both negative significant predictors for native English speaker students) had no or negative impacts on reading self-concept.
Similarly surprising is the positive statistical impact for native English speakers—or rather a contrasting lack of thereof for emergent
bilinguals—relating to the percentage of funding from the government, which can be interpreted as a component of the school’s
socioeconomic status (see Chingos & Blagg, 2017). A plausible explanation for this is the failure of these programs and policies to be
explicitly tailored to student needs or teacher, administrator, and staff attitudes that can undermine the effects of such programs and
policies. Indeed, research suggests that it is often not the programs or reforms per se, but rather their quality or the degree of
implementation, often associated with teacher beliefs, including beliefs about students’ abilities, that matter (Cramer et al., 2021;
Datnow et al., 2005; Merle et al., 2022). Further exploring this finding is particularly important as our results suggest that funding
allocations in schools advantage native English speakers, who appear to benefit from richer resources at school in terms of their
perceptions about themselves as readers, but this was not the case for emergent bilinguals.
4.3. Implications
4.3.1. Implications for theory
Our study study’s findings have various implications for advancing theory, research, and practice. In particular, grounded theory
has the potential to refine existing theories or to generate new theories from the data using bottom-up approaches included in Elastic
Net. Given that existing theories aiming to explain real-life phenomena often fail to differentiate the unique experiences of students—such as the unique experiences of adolescent emergent bilinguals as compared to those of adolescent native English speakers
investigated in this study—more studies are needed to address this issue. The use of PISA data provides some unique advantages with
this regard by including, and giving scholars access to, more representative samples reflecting the current diversity of the population
and by including a large range of variables capturing various social-cultural factors that could impact students (OECD, 2019c, 2019d).
As illustrated in the present study, the use of grounded theory approaches to large-scale data analysis may provide unique insights
and theoretical justifications for combining these approaches. At their core, both grounded theory and machine learning are theoretically agnostic in that they are aware that theories around their area of study may exist, but both approach their domains without
the theoretical assumptions to guide their process. They both are fundamentally about finding and making sense of patterns in the data,
and we suggest that there is no “larger” data set than the human experience (where grounded theory itself took root). We suggest that
machine learning, which is a relatively new approach to large scale data, might benefit from engaging with the literature around
grounded theory. Likewise, the grounded theory community, which largely engages with qualitative approaches to understanding
phenomena, may benefit by turning their expertise towards interpreting patterns produced by machine learning algorithms.
4.3.2. Implications for research
This study’s results include implications for future research. First, the use of Elastic Net analysis in large-scale assessment data is
just beginning. Researchers can leverage the use of Elastic Net as a machine learning variable selection approach that can benefit
research by introducing “big data” (e.g., large-scale educational assessments) to mainstream research. As an advanced statistical tool,
Elastic Net becomes particularly useful when the number of variables is substantial (Zou & Hastie, 2005); this approach helps researchers identify new variables to examine and experimentally manipulate in their empirical studies. Using Elastic Net and leveraging
large-scale data sets like PISA allow researchers to access and utilize these publicly available datasets. This approach also avoids the
limitations of traditional regression analyses and instead allows for the use of other statistical approaches to examine more complex
relationships between variables. By doing so, Elastic Net also allows for exploring relationships among variables previously not
explored in the literature, including by integrating multiple-layer variables from student, teacher, and school levels. For example,
future research could use Elastic Net to examine various national and international large-scale assessments in education (e.g., Trends in
International Mathematics and Science Study, Progress in International Reading Literacy Study, NAEP) to identify most salient predictors of different educational domains (e.g., science, mathematics) and their relative contributions across various subgroups (e.g.,
race, gender, socioeconomic status). This examination can also help researchers provide insights to inform decisionmakers at local,
state, and national levels. Subsequently, these findings can be used to identify at-risk students early and to address disparities such as
narrowing achievement gaps.
Additionally, researchers can further study the variables that have been identified by these large-scale analyses with experimental

intervention approaches. For example, leveraging the variables identified in the present investigation (e.g., work mastery, perceived
information and communications technologies competence), researchers could design specialized interventions and empirically
determine if these interventions impact these variables and whether this impact is different across groups. Similarly, other researchers
might take a more qualitative approach and seek to explore how learners experience work mastery and how specific classroom experiences impact their perceived information and communications technologies competence.
4.3.3. Implications for practice and emergent bilinguals
From a practical perspective, this study highlights what policymakers and practitioners have been saying for decades—individual
differences and social-cultural factors matter across students and students’ experiences are not easily understood with one-size-fits-all
models. We highlight that models developed based on the broader population may simply not hold for all students. These results
highlight the fact that we need to make sure that the classrooms and schools provide unique environments for emergent bilinguals. It
also is necessary to create learning environments with more opportunities for emergent bilinguals because factors that apply to the
broader population may not apply to this unique student population. As noted earlier, the four positive, statistically significant predictors (i.e., work mastery, reading habits, teacher understanding, and perceived information and communications technologies
competence) of emergent bilinguals’ reading self-concept identified in the present study are instructionally manipulable and have
somewhat different saliences for native English speakers.
PISA gauges work mastery with such items as “Part of the enjoyment I get from doing things is when I improve on my past performance” and “I find satisfaction in working as hard as I can”. In the literature, work mastery is defined as the typical behavioral
patterns associated with students who “(a) engage in challenging tasks, (b) are persistent and remain focused even when they face
failure, and (c) are intrinsically motivated and retain future expectations of success” (Filippello et al., 2020, p. 7). Research has linked
work mastery with higher academic achievement across student populations (e.g., Filippello et al., 2020; Zubković & Kolić-Vehovec,
2014). For example, Torppa et al. (2018) found that among the variables examined, mastery orientations and reading habits (see
McGeown et al., 2015) were the strongest predictors of reading achievement, including positive associations across reading tasks (i.e.,
retrieve, interpret, and evaluate) and response formats (e.g., multiple choice, written response).
In turn, research suggests that work mastery orientations and intrinsic motivation among students may be fostered by autonomysupportive teaching styles and practices (e.g., Filippello et al., 2020; Reeve & Jang, 2006), potentially leading to improved reading
habits and reading achievement among students. In general, autonomy-supportive practices are likely to enhance student engagement
through fostering “internally-focused, volitional intentions to act” (Reeve & Jang, 2006, p. 210) when students’ interests, preferences,
values, and psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are valued and actualized. This is particularly important
for emergent bilinguals whose values and needs may differ from others within the school, requiring teachers, who are often white and
middle class, to understand emergent bilinguals “first as persons and then as students” (Griffin et al., 2022, p. 171).
To help emergent bilinguals maintain positive reading attitudes, increase reading motivation, and persist in tasks, Griffin et al.
(2020, 2022) recommended providing choices for reading materials that are culturally appropriate, design reading tasks in which
students are likely to succeed, gradually building in complexity over time to support consistent growth, and adjust reading tasks in
response to fluctuations in students’ reading attitudes over time. To design tasks that potentially satisfy emergent bilinguals’ personal
needs, social needs, and wants, it is recommended that teachers make such tasks culturally appropriate and sustaining (Paris, 2012)
and that teachers convey the task’s relevance, personal significance, and social significance in ways such that high-task-performance
will help satisfy needs (Eisenberger et al., 1999; see Ardasheva et al., 2012, for discussion of this). Preparing teachers to understanding
emergent bilinguals “first as persons and then as students” is particularly important as teacher understanding was among the main
predictors of emergent bilinguals’ reading self-concept and perception of competence in our study, yet many teachers still feel (under)
prepared to meet the needs of emergent bilinguals (Butcher, 2020; Okhremtchouk & Sellu, 2019; Samson & Collins, 2012), thus
decreasing the likelihood of being able to provide instructional supports that are most effective for enhancing emergent bilinguals’
outcomes in reading (Dai et al., 2023).
To further enhance reading motivation—and by extension reading habits and achievement (Griffin et al., 2020)—educators should
be able to capitalize on technology tools that are increasingly available for enhancing teaching and learning. Within the present study,
students’ perceived information and communications technologies competence, and not the use of the technology tools per se, predicted reading self-concept among emergent bilinguals, further highlighting the need for teachers to adopt autonomy-supportive
teaching styles in all aspects of teaching. This is particularly important as perceived information and communications technologies
competence is associated with higher achievement across student populations (Hu et al., 2018) and technology-supported activities
may help to engage students in the process of learning an additional language and act as a motivational factor (Kopinska, 2020;
Shadiev & Huang, 2022).
Overall, there is a need to prepare teachers with autonomy-supportive teaching styles so that teachers are capable of (a) listening,
attending to, and valuing students’ needs, voices, and points of view; (b) providing for student choices and encouraging self-initiation
and self-management; and (c) encouraging conversations and using praise as informational feedback on ways to improve, all of which
are methods likely to foster an internal locus of motivation among students (Filippello et al., 2020; Griffin et al., 2022; Reeve & Jang,
2006). Students’ self-concept in reading may be related to their self-beliefs in the physical domain; for example, students who perceive
themselves as active and sporty may prefer to merely use language as opposed to sitting down and studying or reading in the language
(Mercer, 2011b). However, students’ self-concept can be dynamic (Mercer, 2011b). To increase reading motivation and help emergent
bilinguals progressively modify their reading self-concept, teachers should provide access to engaging, culturally appropriate, and
sustaining reading materials, including with the use of technology.

4.3.4. Limitations and future directions
This study’s results are drawn from self-report data and are mainly correlational, not causal, in nature. Therefore, it is recommended to consider using experimental designs and/or multi-wave approaches to be able to draw causative conclusions. In addition,
one of the aims of this study was to explore variables identified by the Elastic Net to highlight the benefits of this approach for
educational research. It was beyond the scope of this study to also examine variables not identified by the Elastic Net, but previously
examined in the literature. Future comparative studies should consider examining the relative contributions of theoretically vs.
empirically identified predictors across research contexts and student populations.
Another limitation of the study lies in the construction of the composite variables to reduce the number of predictors in multilevel
modeling. Although this procedure prevents potential issues such as multicollinearity and over-parameterization of the regression
models, it raises concerns in the validity support of these variables when the CFA models do not yield satisfactory model fit and the PCA
are applied. That is, principal components in PCA are extracted from the total variance, which is different from factors in CFA that are
computed from the common variance across the individual items. Therefore, some of the components may lack a substantive meaning,
especially when they only account for a limited proportion of the total variance. In our study, all obtained components accounted for a
majority of the variance, ranging from 51% to 67%. In practice, it is always recommended to evaluate the eigenvalues and proportions
of the variance when principal components are used.
5. Conclusions
This study enriches the literature of reading self-concept with the particular focus on emergent bilinguals. As this research used
large-scale assessment data (i.e., PISA 2018) as the main data source, it offered a more comprehensive understanding of reading selfconcept predictors in a more representative sample. The use of the Elastic Net variable selection method helped us identify those
factors that lead to the best performing model of reading self-concept for all students, including emergent bilingual groups and native
English speaker populations with a theoretically agnostic approach. This enabled us to identify relevant factors that may not have
previously been identified in the literature. Considering the hierarchical nature of the data and of the reading self-concept, the
application of the multilevel approach was helpful to examine the relative contributions of the identified variables.
