Promotion of creative thinking and students’ well-being are now seen as important indicators of human capital;
however, role of creativity skills and self-beliefs in academic success remains unclear. This study analyzes the
relationship between creativity-related factors and student achievement in Spain using PISA 2022 data from
30,800 fifteen-year-old students across 966 schools. Two-level cross-sectional multilevel modeling provided
manner to examine both student and school levels of predictors of outcomes — mathematics achievement, life
satisfaction, and stress resistance. Analysis indicated that creative thinking had positive relationship (p < .001)
with mathematics achievement at student level (β = 3.95) and school level (β = 2.55). On the other hand,
different factors explained well-being outcomes; all life satisfaction and stress resistance were positively predicted by creative self-efficacy (β = 0.17 and β = 0.10, p <.001). This study highlights unique roles of creative
performance (related to academics) and creative confidence (related to well-being).

1. Introduction
As a steadily evolving global economy progresses, educational systems are increasingly pressured to develop not only solid academic
knowledge but also the agile competencies needed in the workforce of
the 21st century. Best called creative thinking, it is becoming increasingly recognized as an important segment of human capital to ignite
innovation, complex issue solving, and building new economic growth
(OECD, 2018). By shifting to an approach that goes beyond traditional
educational success criteria (e.g., GPA, tests, and academics) to include a
consideration of student well-being, all facets of development can be
acknowledged. Scholars have argued, on the other side of the student
well-being inclusion point, that high academic achievement should not
come at the expense of the students’ mental and emotional well-being.
Indices such as life satisfaction and stress are important outcomes for
long-term, sustained learning and success in the labor market (Govorova
et al., 2020).
However, the relationships between cognitive skills, such as creative
thinking; core subject areas, such as mathematics; and non-cognitive
outcomes, such as well-being, are indeterminate and unexplored.
Considering the relationship between creativity and academic

achievement has been an area of concentrated research for decades, and
whilst numerous studies provide implications for a positive relationship
between the two, there is less consideration in terms of reasoning. For
example, creative thinking and mathematical thinking are not separate
skills but overlap in core cognitive processes such as flexible problemsolving, reasoning, and pattern recognition. Recent work by Schoevers
et al. (2021) found more recent evidence here; within their data-driven
study of school students, they provided evidence for a relationship between creativity and performance in mathematics examinations. This
finding resonates with the earlier research done by Pitta-Pantazi et al.
(2013), who found that students scoring higher in creativity tests were
better performing in mathematics classes. Regardless, creativity is not a
uniform concept. Importantly, to foster an in-depth understanding of the
relationship between thinking and creativity is to identify creative
performance vs. creative self-efficacy. Creative self- efficacy, highlighted by Bandura (1993), is one’s beliefs about their own creative
capabilities. Creative self-efficacy not only facilitates imaginative ways
of thinking but is strongly correlated with indicators of positive psychological outcomes, such as higher persistence and greater well-being
(Céspedes et al., 2021).

1.3. Creative self-efficacy, creativity, and well-being
It is important to differentiate the cognitive skill of creative thinking
from the psychosocial belief of creative self-efficacy (Bandura, 1977,
1993) or confidence in your own creative ability. Concerning creative
thinking, self-efficacy is a strong predictor of behavior. Self-efficacy has
three key components, including the level of confidence individuals
have, the effort they allocate towards the goal, and the perseverance that
they are willing to exert when faced with challenges. So, students with a
higher creative self-efficacy are likely to pursue creative thinking pieces,
are more likely to be willing to engage in intellectual risk-taking and are
more likely to view their challenges as opportunities for growth (Richter
et al., 2012).
This belief system has significant consequences for the well-being of
students. There is a large body of evidence asserting significant positive
links between general self-efficacy and measures of subjective wellbeing (Céspedes et al., 2021; Lera et al., 2023). A greater sense of confidence in overcoming obstacles and in the pursuit of creative expression
can serve as a significant bulwark against academic and social pressure.
In addition, there are studies that have documented a "trade-off" where
high academic achievement is sometimes linked with lower subjective
well-being (Meng & Liu, 2022). Exploring the different dimensions of
creative performance versus creative self-efficacy may help us understand this notion better. It seems likely that while a focus on high creative performance may be associated with pressure and stress, it might
be our belief in our creative identity that contributes most directly to
positive well-being.

1.1. Theoretical framework—human capital
The development of human capital directly relates to the economic
prosperity of nations and labor market outcomes for individuals. In the
study (Becker, 1964), human capital theory suggests it is an investment
in education, skills, and health that provides an economic return in
productivity. An increasingly globalized and technology-based economy
demanded significantly more than basic literacy and numeracy skills.
For many years, international organizations have promoted a wider set
of literacies and competencies that are important to navigate a future of
uncertainty and rapid change and that include critical thinking,
collaboration, and creativity (OECD, 2018).
As part of a modern human capital understanding, creative thinking
is no longer a skill that is limited to the arts and is now viewed as a core
competency required to innovate and adapt in all sectors. The skills of
idea generation, evaluation, and improvement are instrumental to economic development and productivity gains from the technological advances of a knowledge society Araya (2023). At the same time, there is
increased recognition that student well-being is an integral, and often
overlooked, component of human capital. Students who report higher
life satisfaction and lower stress are likely to be more engaged, effective
learners, and have better long-term health and productivity (Govorova
et al., 2020). It is widely recognized that education systems have an
increasing responsibility to develop young people whose whole self is
considered and the cognitive capabilities and psychological attributes to
succeed. This study takes a human capital perspective that argues creative skills and student well- being are unique dimensions of a student’s
human capital and a critical asset to move forward with.

1.4. The role of the school environment
Individual student characteristics—whether cognitive or psychosocial—are developed and expressed within the nested ecological system
of the school (Gelmez Burakgazi & Reiss, 2025). The learning environment of a school—its pedagogy, its relationships between staff and
students, and the culture among and between peers—serves its own
purpose in developing student outcomes. A school environment that is
perceived to be supportive of creativity, where students’ ideas are
valued and experimentation and failure are encouraged, is possibly
essential to project a student’s creative potential into academic success
and personal well-being.
Research that involves a multilevel modeling design and the use of
large-scale datasets to investigate the impact of contextual factors in
terms of school effects on students has shown that there is an effect—most commonly small—of the school on student well-being (Govorova
et al., 2020).
Moreover, the school context could also function as a moderator,
whereby it could change or moderate the strength or direction of the
relationships on a student level. For example, let’s consider the positive

1.5. Summary and gaps in the literature

2. Methods

The literature indicates that creativity is positively associated with
academic achievement, that self-efficacy is positively associated with
well- being, and that the school context represents an important
contextual factor. Nevertheless, there are gaps in the literature that the
present study sets out to address. First, the 2022 PISA cycle is the first to
include a large-scale, direct assessment of creative thinking, and so it
offers a unique opportunity to examine these relationships with largescale, representative, cross-sectional data. Second, no study to date
has examined the predictive roles of creative performance and creative
self-efficacy on separate outcomes of academic achievement and wellbeing within the same model simultaneously. Third, many studies
either do not pay attention to the nested nature of the educational data
(Yang et al., 2024) or do not empirically explore whether or how
school-level influences are implemented within their findings. Finally,
while cross-national comparisons of PISA data are common and valuable, an in-depth, multilevel analysis of data collected within a single
country, in this case, Spain, offers the potential to provide even richer,
policy-relevant evidence while holding the national education system
constant. The present study was designed to address these existing gaps.
The present study considers the complexities of these relationships
within the specific educational context of Spain. While many of the
studies of this type are cross-national in nature, conducting a singlecountry analysis allows for deeper investigation of mechanisms operating in particular policy and cultural contexts—without the confounding effects of overwhelming systemic differences between nations.
Prior research on Spanish students has uncovered the importance of
psycho-emotional factors in students’ academic success, but the influence of creativity-related factors has been underexplored
(Molina-Muñoz et al., 2023). The recently released 2022 Programme for
International Student Assessment (PISA) is an exciting and timely opportunity for this investigation because it is the first cycle to directly
assess creative thinking in addition to traditional measures of mathematics and student well-being at a large scale.
The research investigates the relationship between student and
school-level creativity factors and their impact on Spanish 15-year-old
students’ mathematics performance and life satisfaction. The study
uses PISA 2022 data to analyze these relationships through multilevel
modeling (MLM). The research follows this main question and these
particular hypotheses:
What role do creativity-related factors at the individual student and
broader school levels play in shaping mathematics achievement and
overall student well-being, encompassing both life satisfaction and stress
levels?

This section describes the process followed to investigate the
research questions. It provides a description of the data source and the
sample of participants who were characterized with the assessment of
PISA 2022 in Spain. The study presents a detailed definition of all
dependent, independent, and control variables considered in the analysis. Finally, the study presents the data analysis procedures, including
how to address missing data, along with the description of the two-level
hierarchical linear models (HLM) that were fitted to the data to test the
hypotheses of the study.
2.1. Data source and participants
he study uses data from the 2022 cycle of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), a large-scale, cross-sectional
assessment of 15-year-old students nearing the end of compulsory education that is coordinated by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD).1 PISA aims to measure the
knowledge, skills, and general competencies of students between the
ages of 15 and 16 across industrialized countries. This was a crosssectional assessment, which means the students in the sample are only
assessed once, and participants include a nationally representative
sample of students in the 2022 PISA cycle. This analysis uses the nationally representative sample from Spain, selected as the study population due to recent changes to its education system context. The new
curriculum standards have placed a renewed focus on transversal competencies, including creativity. Therefore, the relationship between
creative skills and academic outcomes can be studied in Spain to
determine if there is a link.2 Once cases with missing data on any core
demographic variables were deleted (listwise), the final analytical
sample consisted of 30,800 students nested in 966 schools within Spain.
The sample of students had a relatively balanced gender distribution
(50.5 % female), and on average, students were aged 15.8 years (SD =
0.29).

2.3.3. Hypothesis testing
The stated hypotheses were tested using the fixed effects estimates
from the multilevel models estimated by the lme4 package in R.

2.3. Data analysis strategy
The analysis was conducted in stages, including data preparation and
the fitting of the multilevel models to test the study’s hypotheses. All the
variables in this study were analyzed in their original PISA metrics or as
standardized WLE scores to facilitate more meaningful interpretations
for educational policy purposes, rather than standardizing all of the
variables using Z-score transformations.

• The significance and direction of the student-level predictors (β coefficients) were examined to test H1, H3, and the student-level
components of H4.
• The significance and direction of additional school-level predictors (γ
coefficients) were examined to test H2 and the school-level components of H4.
• Interaction hypotheses (H4 and H5) were tested by including crosslevel and within- level product terms (e.g., CreativeThinking x
SchoolCreativeEnv) in the final model and evaluating the significance of the coefficient of the interaction term.

3.2. Multilevel model results
To account for the nested structure of the data (students within
schools), a series of two-level multilevel models were estimated. The
first analysis was an unconditional null model (a model with no predictors) for the mathematics achievement outcome. This yielded an
intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.16, meaning that 16 % of the
total variance in mathematics scores can be attributed to differences
between schools. This considerable between-school variance suggests
that a multilevel modelling approach is appropriate and required for this
analysis. The results of the multilevel models testing the study’s hypotheses are presented in Tables 3, 4, and 5 in the appendix.

4. Discussion
This study explored the complex relationships of creativity-related
factors, math achievement, and well-being of 15-year-old students in
Spain. The multilevel analysis created a different aspect with an
important split; while performances of cognitive creative thinking were
positively and strongly related to both students’ and schools’ math
achievement, it was students’ psychological belief in their creative
ability, their creative self-efficacy, that was the strong predictor of better
well-being. This study adds to the literature because it empirically distinguishes the psychologically distinct pathways of how creative selfefficacy and creative performance relate to different student outcomes.
The strong positive association found between creative thinking and
mathematics achievement is consistent with earlier research and supports the notion of creativity as central to the notion of human capital,
similar to foundational STEM skills (Schoevers et al., 2021). Perhaps
more compellingly, our results cast doubt on perhaps an oversimplified
notion of creativity’s relationship to well-being. Our finding that creative self-efficacy was a more important predictor of higher life satisfaction and greater stress resistance than performance is consistent with

The small negative association between creative performance and life

There are important practical implications of these findings relating
to the Spanish educational context. First, the results provide strong
justification for integrating direct creative thinking pedagogy into core
academic programmes with content areas like mathematics. Second, and
perhaps more importantly, schools could develop practices that target
and support students’ creative self-efficacy to promote students’ wellbeing. Sharing positive and supportive experiences where the process
(rather than the product) and appropriate feedback can help build students’ confidence is essential when developing students’ creative selfefficacy.
Of course, there are limitations of this study. Given that the design
for PISA data is cross- sectional, it means we could only discuss associations without establishing causal connections, and we cannot rule out
reverse causality. Moreover, our findings are contextual to Spain, and it
is unclear if they would be generalizable to other educational systems. A
methodological limitation is that using only the first plausible value in
the main analyses did not assess all available plausible values that had
differing conclusions and findings. Future research can consider using
longitudinal study designs to explore the developmental pathways between these constructs and utilize more specified measures to compare
the types of school practices that promote student well-being and
creativity.

6. Educational relevance statement
The present research demonstrates that creative thinking (skill) and
creative self-efficacy are differentially important among students—stronger creative thinking is associated with higher math achievement,
while higher creative self-efficacy is associated with higher life satisfaction and greater stress resistance. By means of a national representative sample of 15-year-old Spaniards (OECD, 2023) and multilevel
models, this study shows that while cognitive creative competences
predict academic performance, students’ self-belief in creative abilities
predicts well-being. On a practical level, educational systems should not
only provide training in creative problem-solving to achieve academic
accomplishment but also intentionally develop students’ creative confidence to enhance their mental well-being and resilience.
CRediT authorship contribution statement

This research adds to the literature on human capital by looking
empirically at the ways in which creativity relates to student outcomes.
Through multilevel modeling based on PISA 2022 data from Spain, we
find strong support for the cognitive skill of creative thinking being
associated with academic outcomes, and we find strong evidence that
the psychological belief of creative self-efficacy is a predictor of student
well-being.
The main contribution of this work in the field of human capital is
the differentiation between creative thinking, as a cognitive skill, and
creative self-efficacy, as a psychological belief. Also, finding has
