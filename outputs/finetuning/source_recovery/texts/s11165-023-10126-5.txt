The Relationship Between Students’ Awareness
of Environmental Issues and Attitudes Toward Science
and Epistemological Beliefs—Moderating Effect of Informal
Science Activities
Yong Xie1 · Yonghe Zheng · Yang Yang2
Accepted: 3 August 2023 / Published online: 4 September 2023
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature B.V. 2023, corrected publication 2023

Abstract
This study aims to examine the moderating effect of participation in informal science
activities on the associations among students’ awareness of environmental issues and
their attitudes toward science and epistemological beliefs. The data set included 9841
15-year-old students from four Chinese provinces (Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu, and
Guangdong) who took part in the Programme for International Student Assessment
(PISA) in 2015. Correlation analysis showed that there was a significant positive correlation between awareness of environmental issues and students’ science self-efficacy,
interest in science, enjoyment of science, and science epistemological beliefs. Multiple regression analysis further revealed that informal science activities had no moderating effect on the relationship between awareness of environmental issues and interest
in science and science epistemological beliefs, but played a moderating effect on the
relationship between awareness of environmental issues and enjoyment of science and
science self-efficacy. This study revealed the benefits of awareness of environmental
issues, informal science activities, and their interaction in science learning and provided
insights in implementation of the results in science teaching.
Keywords Socio-scientific issue · Awareness of environmental issues · Informal science
learning · Attitude toward science · Epistemological belief

Introduction
The goals of science education include not only helping students achieve good science
performance, but also developing positive attitudes toward science and complex epistemological beliefs (OECD, 2016). The European Union’s report, “Increasing achievement
and motivation in mathematics and science learning in schools,” placed the improvement
of positive attitudes toward science on science achievement (Horváth et al., 2022). The
“Science Curriculum Standards for Compulsory Education” in China also emphasized
the cultivation of students’ attitudes toward science and science epistemological beliefs
through science curriculum. Attitudes toward science encompass science interest, enjoyment of science, and science self-efficacy (Osborne et al., 2003). Science epistemological
beliefs refer to beliefs about the nature of scientific knowledge and science as a way to
develop scientific cognition(Conley et al., 2004). Previous research showed that students’
attitudes toward science and epistemological beliefs were closely related to their science
achievements (Jansen et al., 2016; Kizilgunes et al., 2009), and affected their major as
well as future career choices (Bøe, 2012; Renninger et al., 2015).
However, many students still hold naive epistemological beliefs (Sandoval &
Morrison, 2003) and their attitudes toward science seem to decline as they grow up
(Braund & Driver, 2005; Tröbst et al., 2016). Some students even develop an antiscience stance, avoiding higher level science learning (Littledyke, 2008). Therefore,
many countries such as China, the USA, and Canada have begun to integrate science,
technology, society, and environment in science education (Next Generation Science
Standards, 2013) and teach in the context of socio-science issues (SSI) to help students
understand the value of science and technology in real social contexts and environmental issues.
With the integration of SSI in science curriculum, students’ awareness of environmental issues has been enhanced. Awareness of environmental issues refers to students’ knowledge of SSI related to environment. China has included the development
students’ awareness of environmental issues in “Science Curriculum Standards for
Compulsory Education.” Students with higher awareness of environmental issues are
more able to rationally view the value of science and technology in social development
and environmental issues, which is the basis for developing positive attitudes toward
science and epistemological beliefs (Deng et al., 2011; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). But
students have very limited opportunities to participate in SSI discussions at school in
China because of high exam-oriented science learning (Tan, 2020). Thus, students are
more encouraged to gain more experience on the environment issues by joining informal science activities. Informal science activities, including browsing science webs
and attending science clubs, are regarded as a key approach to science learning and
play a positive role in promoting the development of students’ attitudes toward science
and epistemological beliefs. Although both SSI-based teaching and informal science
activities are believed to promote the development of students’ attitudes toward science and epistemological beliefs (Lin & Schunn, 2016; Sadler & Dawson, 2012), little is known about the role of informal science activities in the relationship between
environment-related SSI learning activities and attitudes toward science and epistemological beliefs. Therefore, this study focuses on the moderating effect of students’
participation in informal science activities on the relationship between awareness of
environmental issues and attitudes toward science and epistemological beliefs.

Conceptual Framework
Attitudes Toward Science and Epistemological Beliefs
Attitudes toward science are “the feelings, beliefs and values students held about an
object that may be the enterprise of science, school science, the impact of science on
society, or scientists themselves” (Osborne et al., 2003). Positive attitudes toward science can motivate students to participate in science practice and deepen their understanding of science (National Research Council, 2012). Although no consensus has been
reached on the dimensions of attitudes toward science (Osborne et al., 2003), science
interest, enjoyment of science, and science self-efficacy are commonly considered as
three sub-dimensions of attitudes toward science in previous research (Chi et al., 2021).
Enjoyment of science refers to the pleasure and satisfaction people experience when
participating in science activities (Liou, 2021), which is a temporary psychological state
under specific circumstances. Enjoyment of science plays an important role in carrying out in-depth science learning and achieving better science achievements for students (Liou, 2021). Areepattamannil et al. (2011) surveyed 15-year-old students in 431
schools in Canada and found that students’ enjoyment of science had a significantly
positive effect on their science learning outcomes. Long and colleagues (2022) showed
that students’ enjoyment of science had a positive effect on their science achievement by
using data from American students in PISA 2015.
Interest in science is a steady tendency, which refers to the state of a student’s concentration on a particular scientific topic (Tröbst et al., 2016). Science interest closely
relates to science achievement and career choice. Shah et al. (2013) found that students
performed better in science when they were interested in science. Studies also showed
that science interest affected students’ science career expectations (Lv et al., 2022).
Self-efficacy is the self-referent belief of capability (Bandura, 1986), which is based
on individual’s perception of their ability to be successful or perform well in the given
task and activity. Students’ science self-efficacy played an important role in their choice
of science-related majors and future careers (Liou, 2021). Potvin and Hasni (2014)
reviewed 228 research articles from 2000 to 2012 and found that students with higher
science self-efficacy rather than better academic performance tended to choose sciencerelated majors and careers. This study also used science interest, enjoyment of science,
and science self-efficacy as three sub-dimensions of attitudes toward science.
Science epistemological beliefs have received widespread attention from researchers
in science education recently. Epistemological beliefs refer to individual beliefs about
the nature of knowledge and cognitive process, while science epistemological beliefs
refer to beliefs about the nature of scientific knowledge and science as a way to develop
scientific cognition (Conley et al., 2004). Science epistemological beliefs can guide students to acquire science knowledge (Songer & Linn, 1991), and shape their higher-order
thinking ability (Fulmer, 2014; C. Lin & Tsai, 2008). Yang and Tsai surveyed 62 sixthgrade students on their epistemological beliefs and science reasoning abilities, and the
results showed that students with more complex epistemological beliefs performed better in science reasoning (Yang & Tsai, 2010). Furthermore, a strong link had been identified between attitudes toward science and science epistemological beliefs. Fulmer’s
research found that students’ epistemological beliefs influenced their attitudes toward
science (Chi et al., 2021; Fulmer, 2014).

Environmental Issue Awareness and Attitudes Toward Science and Epistemological
Beliefs
Science teaching based on constructivism attached importance to the close connection
between scientific ideas and real-life phenomena (Littledyke, 2008). Therefore, scholars
and practitioners of science education called for the integration of SSI into science classroom and the teaching science in the context of such issues. Sadler and Dawson (2012)
reviewed classroom implementations of socio-scientific issues and found that the teaching
based on socio-scientific issues can effectively develop students’ attitudes toward science
and complex epistemological beliefs.
Environmental issues were commonly used in SSI-based teaching, such as global warming (Matkins & Bell, 2007), the use of biotechnology (Dori et al., 2003), and water pollution (Bulte et al., 2006). Environmental issues drew on scientific principles from different
fields of science (Coertjens et al., 2010), and were closely related to everyday life (Scerri,
2009); thus, they were often used as the context of science teaching. Dori et al. (2003)
taught biotechnology based on environmental issue cases, and students reported enjoying
learning in this way. Matkins and Bell (2007) taught science epistemological beliefs in the
context of environmental issues, and found that students’ science epistemological beliefs
significantly improved.
Teaching based on environmental issues can directly improve students’ awareness of
environmental issues, because the issues can serve as a powerful framework within which
students can engage in meaningful discussions and negotiations related to science, society,
and environment (Macalalag et al., 2020), a process that allows them to gain a deeper and
more critical understanding of environmental issues. Higher awareness of environmental
issues can promote learners’ attitudes toward science and epistemological beliefs. If one
has a higher awareness of environmental issues, a more comprehensive understanding of
the role of science and technology in such issues is likely to form, thereby stimulating positive attitudes toward science (Goldberg et al., 2020; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). Constructivists also believe that a deeper understanding of environmental issues plays a positive role in
learners’ construction of complex epistemological beliefs (Deng et al., 2011).

Informal Science Activities and Attitudes Toward Science and Epistemological
Beliefs
Informal science activities take place outside of formal education settings such as
classrooms. According to Dierking et al. (2003), science learning is a process of accumulating various everyday experiences. In addition to formal science activities, many
daily activities affect individual’s science learning, such as reading, watching television, and browsing science websites. Informal science activities played a positive role
in students’ attitudes toward science and beliefs. Brody (2005) confirmed that informal
science activities can stimulate learners’ curiosity, improve motivation and attitudes,
and promote participation and social interaction. Lin and Schunn (2016) also found
that informal science activities play an important role in cultivating students’ attitudes
toward science and epistemological beliefs.
Although both formal science teaching strategies and informal science activities can
influence students’ attitudes toward science and epistemological beliefs, informal science
activities have more advantages than formal science teaching strategies and can be used

as a supplement to formal science learning. Firstly, integrating SSI into traditional science
classrooms can promote the development of students’ attitudes toward science and epistemological beliefs (Sadler & Dawson, 2012), but such teaching strategies are limitedly
applied in China due to high exam-oriented science teaching (Tan, 2020). While informal
science activities are not limited by curriculum requirements, students have more opportunities to learn about topics that interest them. In addition, many formal science activities require students to follow the teacher’s or textbook’s procedure, while informal science
activities can be chosen and participated in by students themselves (Lin & Schunn, 2016).
Students not only have more enthusiasm to participate in, but also are more willing to construct scientific notion actively. Besides, students have limited time for science learning in
school, and informal science activities provide more opportunities for students to immerse
themselves in a scientific learning environment (Bell et al., 2009).
To sum up, informal science activities can fill the gap in formal science teaching and science learning and provide students more opportunities to experience science. Science learning does not stop at discussing SSI with classmates in school and gaining a higher awareness
of environmental issues. Students are also encouraged to obtain information related to socioscientific issues from more informal activities, such as browsing relevant websites, joining
science clubs, reading books and newspapers, and using computer simulations (Littledyke,
2008). Constructivists believe that students can build on the awareness of environmental
issues gained through SSI discussions and further integrate evidence and reorganize their
ideas through various informal science learning activities, thus forming a more dialectical
and profound understanding of controversial environmental issues (Sandoval, 2005).

The Influence of Economic, Social, and Cultural Status on Attitudes Toward Science
and Epistemological Beliefs
Students’ economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS) is closely related to their attitudes
toward science and scientific epistemological beliefs (Conley et al., 2004; Hu et al., 2018).
Therefore, it was considered when scholars focused on the moderating effect. ESCS usually includes family education resources and parental education and occupation. Regarding
family education resources, Chen et al. (2012) used general learning resources and scientific learning resources in families as indicators of family education resources in TIMSS
2003 data. They found that family education resources had a significant positive impact
on the attitudes toward science of 4th and 8th graders in Taiwan. Parental education could
indirectly affect students’ attitudes toward science, and parents’ understanding of science
would affect their attitudes toward pursuing science (Simpkins et al., 2006). In terms of
parental occupation, father’s occupation was not related to students’ attitudes toward science, while mother’s occupation affected students’ attitudes toward science (Shah et al.,
2013). Mothers who work in business or government were positively related to their children’s attitudes toward science. Ozkal et al. (2010) also found that parents’ educational
level and current occupational situation influenced students’ scientific epistemological
beliefs. Chi et al. (2021) also used PISA 2015 data to explore students’ attitudes toward
science and science epistemological beliefs, and found that students’ ESCS was a positive
predictor of scientific epistemology beliefs, science enjoyment, and science self-efficacy,
while negatively predicting students’ interest in science (Chi et al., 2021). Considering the
significant relationships between ESCS and other focused variables in this study, the ESCS
variable was set as a control variable in the analysis.

Research Questions
Students’ declining attitudes toward science and poor epistemological beliefs are problems
that need to be addressed in the field of science education. Improving students’ awareness
of environmental issues through science teaching based on environmental issues can contribute to developing positive attitudes toward science and complex epistemological beliefs
(Lin & Schunn, 2016; Sadler & Dawson, 2012). Furthermore, students need to participate
in more scientific practices to experience science and accumulate experience about science
(Dierking et al., 2003), to interact with their enhanced awareness of environmental issues
and further promote their attitude toward science and epistemological beliefs. However,
due to the high exam-oriented science teaching and learning in China (Tan, 2020), time
and opportunities for in-school science activities were limited, so students were encouraged to engage more in informal science activities. Previous studies mainly focused on the
impact of teaching based on environmental issues and informal science activities on attitudes toward science and epistemological beliefs (Dori et al., 2003; Matkins & Bell, 2007).
Few empirical studies focused on the relationship between awareness of environmental
issues and attitude toward science and epistemological beliefs, as well as the moderating
effect of informal science activities on the above relationships.
Therefore, this study explored the moderating effect of informal science activities on
the relationship between awareness of environmental issues and students’ attitudes toward
science and epistemological beliefs by using PISA 2015 BSJG student data. This study
revealed the benefits of awareness of environmental issues and informal science activities
on the development of attitudes toward science and epistemological beliefs, and provided
implications for science learning and teaching. The specific research questions guiding this
study are as follows:
1. What is the relationship, if any, between students’ awareness of environmental issues and
their enjoyment of science, interest in science, science self-efficacy, and epistemological
beliefs?
2. Can participation in informal scientific activities moderate the relationship between
awareness of environmental issues and enjoyment of science, interest in science, science
self-efficacy, and epistemological beliefs, with students’ ESCS as a control variable?

Research Method
Data Set and Participants
The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) is a global assessment by the
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) that measures scientific, reading, and mathematical literacy of 15-year-old students every 3 years. The assessment in 2015 focused on students’ scientific literacy. This study used 2015 PISA dataset
and selected the data of BSJG (China) students. BSJG refers to Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu,
and Guangdong in China, and the data of 9841 students were obtained by two-stage stratified sampling. The overall missing rate for this dataset ranges from 0.4 to 7.1%, which is
less than 10%. To solve the problem of missing values, this study used multiple imputation
method, which has been widely used to deal with missing data in large-scale assessments

(Cordero et al., 2022; Khine et al., 2020). Multiple imputation methods replace missing
values by considering all variables in the model according to the specific characteristics
of each variable (Royston, 2004). After 5 times of interpolation by SPSS, a total of 9841
cases were included (4682 female students, and 5159 male students), representing a population of 1,331,794 students.

Measurement and Variables
The PISA 2015 assessment used the situational questionnaire to evaluate students’ economic, social, and cultural status; interest in science; enjoyment of science; science selfefficacy; epistemological beliefs; awareness of environmental issues; and participation
in informal science activities. Each variable was synthesized by multiple items, and the
responds were on a 4-point Likert or 5-point Likert scale. To enhance the comparability of
the data, weighted likelihood estimation (WLE) was performed on students’ answers based
on item response theory (IRT), and then, the values were standardized based on the mean
and standard deviation of OECD countries. Therefore, on each specific variable, the higher
the estimated value, the better the student’s performance.

Dependent Variables
The variable of interest in science referred to the extend to which students were interested
in scientific topics such as the biosphere, motion and forces, energy and its transformation, the universe and its history, and how science can help us prevent disease. This variable was measured by using a 5-point Likert scale: “Not interested,” “Hardly interested,”
“Interested,” “Very interested,” and “I don’t know what this is.” The higher the standardized scores, the higher the students’ interest in science.
Enjoyment of science measured students’ affinity for science activities on a 4-point Likert scale that included “Strongly agree,” “Agree,” “Disagree,” and “Strongly disagree.” A
typical question was “I generally have fun when I am learning broad science topics.” The
responses were reverse-coded and then normalized, and the higher the score, the greater
the student’s enjoyment of science.
Science self-efficacy was measured by asking students to rate their performance on various science tasks on a 4-point Likert scale, including “I can do this easily,” “I can do this
with a bit of effort,” “I would struggle to do this on my own,” and “I couldn’t do this.”
The tasks included recognizing the science question that underlies a newspaper report on
a health issue. The responses were reverse-coded and then standardized. The higher the
index, the greater the students’ self-efficacy.
Science epistemological beliefs focused on student perceptions of the scientific method
and were assessed on a 4-point Likert scale consisting of “Strongly disagree,” “Disagree,”
“Agree,” and “Strongly agree.” Questions included that “A good way to know if something
is true is to do an experiment.” The responses were reverse-coded and then normalized, so
the higher the index, the more sophisticated the students’ science epistemological beliefs.

Independent Variables
Awareness of environmental issues measured students’ knowledge of environmental issues
using a 4-point Likert scale, including “I have never heard of this,” “I have heard about this
but I would not be able to explain what it is really about,” “I know somethings about this

and could explain the general issues,” and “I am familiar with this and I would be able to
explain this well.” Environmental issues included the increase in greenhouse gases in the
atmosphere, the use of genetically modified organisms, nuclear waste, air pollution, and
water shortages. The higher the index, the stronger the students’ awareness of environmental issues.
The variable of informal science activity measured student involvement in science activities outside the school environment using a 4-point Likert scale of “Very often,” “Regularly,” “Sometimes,” and “Never or hardly ever.” Activities included watching science TV
programs, reading science articles in science magazines or newspapers, browsing sciencerelated websites, attending science clubs, and using virtual computer simulation of natural
phenomena and technical processes. The responses were reverse-coded and normalized, so
that the higher the index, the more frequently students engaged in these activities.

Control Variables
The ESCS index was a measure of the economic, social, and cultural status of a student’s
family, which includes three aspects: the highest educational level of the parents, the highest occupational status of the parents, and the family possessions that represent their economic status. This variable was also standardized based on the mean and standard deviation
of the OECD countries, with the higher index indicating a better-off family for students.

Analysis of Data
To answer question 1, correlation analysis was used to explore the relationship between
students’ awareness of environmental issues and their enjoyment of science, interest in science, science self-efficacy, and science epistemological beliefs. For research question 2,
multiple regressions were applied by using the interaction between awareness of environmental issues and informal science activities participation, enjoyment of science, interest
in science, science self-efficacy, and epistemological beliefs as dependent variables. The
Bonferroni correction for multiple correlations was carried out to avoid type 1 errors. The
data were analyzed using SPSS 25.

Moderating Effect of Informal Science Activities on the Relationship Between
Awareness of Environmental Issues and Science Attitude
To visualize the interaction effects, both awareness of environmental issues and participation in
informal scientific activities were then converted into categorical variables (one standard deviation
below the mean as low level and one standard deviation above the mean as high level). Figures 1
and 2 show the moderating effects of informal participation in science activities on the relationship
between awareness of environmental issues and enjoyment of science and science self-efficacy,
respectively. Students’ enjoyment of science and self-efficacy in science increase with increasing
awareness of environmental issues or participation in more informal scientific activities.

Discussion
The Relationship Between Students’ Awareness of Environmental Issues and Their
Enjoyment of Science, Interest in Science, Science Self‑efficacy, and Epistemological
Beliefs
The results showed that students’ awareness of environmental issues was significantly and
positively correlated with their enjoyment of science, interest in science, science self-efficacy, and science epistemological beliefs, which was consistent with previous research that
teaching based on SSI promoted students’ attitudes toward science and science epistemological beliefs (Zeidler, 2014). Among them, students’ awareness of environmental issues had
the strongest relationship with science self-efficacy. The main factor in the formation of selfefficacy is students’ mastery experience (Bandura, 1986), and students’ awareness of environmental issues improves when they actively participate in environmental issues discussion in science lessons and build knowledge about environmental issues successfully. This
also leads to a deeper understanding of the value of science and technology, which greatly
benefits the strengthening of their science self-efficacy. Moreover, students’ awareness of
environmental issues and science self-efficacy are obtained through student self-assessment
(OECD, 2016). Students who believe that they have a good understanding of environmental
issues related to science and technology tend to believe that they are capable of completing
various scientific tasks, so the correlation between the two tends to be higher.
Furthermore, a significant and positive correlation between students’ awareness of environmental issues and their interest and enjoyment of science was also found. Interest in science emphasizes students’ attention to the main scientific topics (Tröbst et al., 2016), while
enjoyment of science focuses on students’ pleasure and satisfaction in science activities
(Liou, 2021), and both are influenced by students’ cognitive experience (Petty & Cacioppo,
1986). The higher students’ awareness of environmental issues means that they have constructed a framework of understanding of environment-related SSI, and that different scientific topics are part of this framework. When students with awareness of environmental
issues participate in science activities or learn science content, they will take the initiative
to pay attention to and explain the content with cognitive framework, and they are more
likely to find the fun in science, which explains the significant correlation between awareness of environmental issues and interest in science and enjoyment of science.
In addition, there was a positive correlation between awareness of environmental issues
and science epistemological beliefs. Environmental issues related to science and technology help students develop their epistemological beliefs (Zeidler, 2014). In the framework
of environmental issues, students will learn different views on science, society, and environment, and there will be conflicts among these views, and there will also be conflicts
among the beliefs originally held by individuals and the views learned. In the process of
thinking about these conflicts, students will not only have a deeper understanding of environmental issues, but also gradually develop more sophisticated epistemological beliefs.
Therefore, there is a significant positive correlation between students’ awareness of environmental issues and scientific epistemological beliefs.

The Moderating Effect of Participation in Informal Science Activities
The results showed that participation in informal science activities had a significant moderating effect on the relationship between awareness of environmental issues and science

13

1197

self-efficacy and enjoyment of science, but had no moderating effect on the relationship
between awareness of environmental issues and interest in science and science epistemological beliefs. The formation of complex science epistemological beliefs and keen interest in science require learners to experience viewpoint conflicts and think systematically
(Deng et al., 2011). Informal science activities are usually aimed at popularizing science
knowledge and providing students with more science experience, so their content is relatively scattered and superficial. Compared with students’ thinking on environmental issues,
informal science activities play a weaker role in regulating the relationship between environmental issues awareness and science epistemological beliefs and interest in science.
Analysis of the moderating effects of enjoyment of science showed that higher participation in informal science activities can enhance the correlation between awareness of
environmental issues and enjoyment of science. If students were involved in more informal
science activities, their enjoyment of science would increase as they became more aware of
environmental issues. Enjoyment of science is exactly the pleasure and satisfaction of students participating in scientific activities (Liou, 2021), which is a state of emotional experience. Students who actively participate in informal science activities experience greater
happiness as their cognitive framework allows them to think more about technology and
the environment.
For science self-efficacy, although the data revealed the existence of the moderating
effect, the two lines in the moderating effect chart had little difference and seemed to be
parallel. As can be seen from the result of multiple regression analysis, the interaction
coefficient is much smaller than the environmental issue awareness coefficient, so the moderating effect is small. Its significant moderating effect may be caused by a large sample
size. The weighted sample size of this study is 1,331,794, more than one million, which is
easier to meet the requirements of significance level test (Lantz, 2013).

Conclusion
This study not only found a significant positive correlation between students’ awareness
of environmental issues and science self-efficacy, science interest, science enjoyment, and
epistemological belief, but also found that informal science activities have additional benefits for SSI-based teaching. More participation in informal science activities can strengthen
the positive relationship between students’ SSI learning and enjoyment of science. Therefore, both environment issues and informal science activities should be more considered in
the implementation of science teaching.
Firstly, more environment issues content can be incorporated into traditional science teaching to cultivate students’ awareness of environmental issues. In the teaching
introduction process, the original teaching theme can be introduced through environment issues, and related environment issues activities can be added to teaching cases,
such as watching controversial environment issues videos and discussing environment
issues. Secondly, students should be encouraged to participate in more informal science
activities after class. Teachers can encourage students to engage in extended learning through informal science activities, whether it is a project curriculum based on
environment issues or an integrated science teaching in an environment issues context.
Besides, environment issues-based argument curriculum can be implemented. Teachers can help students form basic awareness of environmental issues and form their own
claims on environmental issues through courses or videos. Since class time is limited,

students are encouraged to collect evidence to support their claim through informal
learning activities, and then organize students to carry out a classroom environment
issues argumentation activity. This approach provides students with the opportunity to
think about the conflict between development of science and technology and environment issues in a real context. They can understand scientific concepts through collecting data and arguing issues. It also promotes the development of students’ attitudes
toward science and epistemological beliefs.

Direction for Future Study
This study provided ideas and direction for future research. First of all, researchers
should focus more on the factors affecting students’ attitudes toward science and epistemological beliefs. The goals of science education include not only improving academic performance in science, but also forming positive science attitudes and complex
epistemological beliefs. Researchers generally pay attention to students’ academic performance in science and explore the factors that affect their science performance. More
studies need to pay attention to the factors that influence students’ attitudes toward science and epistemological beliefs.
Secondly, how science learning occurs under teaching activities required more
exploration. Many forms of teaching activities in science education have attracted the
attention of researchers and teachers, such as teaching based on socio-scientific issues,
project-based teaching, and inquire-based teaching. Researchers generally pay attention to the effects of these teaching activities on students’ science results and how to
apply these teaching activities, but little is known about how these teaching activities affect students’ awareness and science learning. Students’ recognition affected
by teaching activities is not only crucial for teachers and researchers to understand
how learning occurs, but also plays an important role in assessing students’ science
learning. Therefore, future research also needs to pay more attention to how students’
awareness influences science learning under different teaching activities.
Finally, we should focus on the interaction of science education activities outside
and inside school. The construction of students’ scientific knowledge and the formation of positive scientific attitudes are not only influenced by the science education in
school, but also by the daily life experience and popularization of science and technology. To explain students’ science learning, it is necessary to consider not only the
role of internal factors, but also the possible interaction of internal and external factors to have a more comprehensive understanding of the occurrence of science learning. Therefore, future research should also fully consider the interaction effects of both
internal and external factors on science learning.

Limitation
There are some limitations in the study. First, this study used cross-sectional data; longitudinal studies could be carried out to provide more convincing evidence. Second, the
contents of environmental issue awareness and informal science activities discussed in
this study only involve the environmental issues and science activities concerned by PISA
