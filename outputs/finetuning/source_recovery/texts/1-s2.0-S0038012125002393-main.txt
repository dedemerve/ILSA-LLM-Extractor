A B S T R A C T
This paper focuses on the comprehensive assessment of regional performance in attaining the 2030 Strategic 
Framework for Education and Training (ET2030) established by the European Union. To this end, we propose 
a composite indicator framework based on robust Benefit-of-the-doubt models empirically validated through 
an extensive analysis of data spanning 32 countries and 101 NUTS-I level regions for 2019. We integrate 
contextual variables into a robust conditional model to ensure an equitable evaluation among regions grappling 
with distinct circumstances. Specifically, the unemployment rate and the percentage of the population holding 
national citizenship are considered. Moreover, the research identifies best practices from high-performing 
regions that can serve as benchmarks for underperforming areas. Analyzing regional-level data is crucial for 
understanding disparities between European regions and within countries.
1. Introduction
Education stands as one of the fundamental pillars of a more eq-
uitable, sustainable, and resilient society, recognized as a fundamental 
human right by the United Nations [1] and a child’s right by United 
Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund [2]. The profound 
impact of quality education on multiple aspects of society cannot be 
overstated. It fosters peace by promoting tolerance and mutual respect, 
thereby reducing society’s vulnerability to violent conflict [3]. In the 
field of health, education is associated with improved well-being and 
longer life expectancy compared to those with lower levels of edu-
cational attainment [4]. Furthermore, there is strong evidence of the 
positive effect of education on economic growth [5], as it empowers 
marginalized individuals to engage in civic life and overcome cycles of 
poverty.
Recognizing the pivotal role of education in society development, 
the European Council [6] established the goal of becoming the most 
competitive and dynamic knowledge-based economy in the world. To 
translate this vision into action, the Strategic Framework for Education 
and Training 2010 (ET2010) was launched in 2001, establishing strate-
gic objectives, benchmarks, and indicators to be achieved by 2010. The 
progress achieved under this framework was duly acknowledged and 
served as the foundation for subsequent iterations, notably the Strategic

As a result, education remains firmly anchored in the European

be monitored, described as ‘‘a series of reference levels of European

year, referred to as ‘‘EU-level targets’’ [8, Annex II]. Condensing the 
multidimensional scope of ET2030 into a single, easily interpretable 
metric provides valuable input for policymaking.

socio-economic disparities are even more pronounced. Regions dif-
fer significantly in terms of factors such as unemployment rates and 
migrant composition, and standardized performance comparisons risk 
misrepresenting both the challenges and capacities of these regions. For 
instance, in Spain in 2019, the Basque Country recorded one of the 
lowest rates of early leavers from education and training (6.7%), while 
Andalusia registered one of the highest (21.6%) [source: Eurostat]. 
Similarly, the Basque Country had one of the lowest unemployment 
rates (9.2%), whereas Andalusia reported one of the highest (21.2%) 
[source: Eurostat]. Assessing Spain as a whole would obscure these sub-
stantial regional disparities and context-specific challenges that current 
synthetic performance measures often fail to capture.
In this paper, we propose a comprehensive metric that accommo-
dates the heterogeneity across regions while remaining aligned with 
the common targets set by ET2030. Our approach combines a new 
sequence of models with granular-level data to assess performance at 
the regional level, thereby offering a more nuanced view of educational 
outcomes in Europe beyond the national perspective. This enables 
the identification of intra-country disparities and the detection of un-
derlying structural patterns, ultimately supporting more targeted and 
effective policy interventions. Given Europe’s commitment to deliver-
ing quality education for all, disaggregating performance measurement 
by region enhances the visibility of underperforming areas and enables 
the design of tailored improvement strategies. It also facilitates the 
recognition of high-performing regions that may serve as models of 
best practice. Crucially, ensuring fairness and equity in the evaluation 
of geographic areas requires that regional performance be assessed 
within its specific socio-economic context, thereby allowing for a more 
accurate definition of benchmarks and targets for improvement.
To address the challenge of assessing the relative performance of 
European regions with respect to the ET2030 indicators, a Benefit-of-
the-Doubt composite indicator will be constructed in a robust approach. 
This approach is designed to overcome the deterministic limitations 
of the conventional Benefit-of-the-Doubt technique while accounting 
for potential outliers in the data. In a subsequent phase, a robust 
conditional model will be applied, incorporating relevant contextual 
variables to ensure that comparisons are fair and equitable. By con-
ditioning performance assessment on the socio-economic environment, 
this method acknowledges and highlights regions that attain relatively 
strong outcomes despite operating under challenging circumstances. 
As such, it is particularly well suited for defining realistic targets 
and supporting the design of strategies for continuous improvement. 
Furthermore, the study analyses regional disparities within countries. 
The BoD analysis is replicated by jointly considering both regional and 
national levels, allowing for an understanding of baseline performance 
at the country level and the positioning of individual regions relative 
to that baseline.
The findings provide a strong analytical foundation to support 
evidence-based policy decisions and to guide the development of strate-
gies that foster equitable and sustainable progress in education across 
Europe.

of the literature on the assessment of performance in the educational

study is presented in Section 3. Section 4 describes the process of 
gathering and processing the data and explains the model specifica-
tions used for the empirical application of the methodology to this 
study. In Section 5, the attention turns towards presenting the results 
and discussing their implications. Finally, Section 6 presents the main 
conclusions, highlighting the key findings and their significance. Ad-
ditionally, the limitations of the present study and future work that 
should follow is outlined.
2. Literature review
The evaluation of educational performance across different levels 
is pivotal for informing effective policymaking and fostering equitable 
outcomes. Frameworks like the International Standard Classification 
of Education (ISCED) and international assessments such as PISA and 
TIMSS provide foundational tools for comparative analyses [9–11]. 
However, these tools often fail to capture regional disparities, partic-
ularly in the context of European Union (EU) strategic frameworks like 
ET2020 and ET2030 [8]. This review synthesizes existing literature, 
organizing it thematically to identify trends, unresolved issues, and 
future research opportunities. Table 1 overviews the literature on cross-
country and regional performance evaluation. Studies are classified by 
stage, data source, and method. We signal whether sensitivity analysis 
has been performed to validate the results, whether contextual vari-
ables have been included in the study, and whether the country or 
regional level has been considered.
The ISCED framework provides a standardized categorization of ed-
ucation levels, enabling consistent comparisons globally [9]. It outlines 
primary (Level 1), lower secondary (Level 2), and upper secondary 
(Level 3) education stages, accommodating variations in duration and 
entry ages across countries. This classification underpins much of the 
comparative research in education.
PISA and TIMSS are the most utilized assessments for evaluating ed-
ucational outcomes. PISA assesses students aged 15 years in secondary 
education [10], while TIMSS focuses on grades 4 and 8, capturing both 
primary and secondary stages [11]. These assessments provide critical 
data, yet the reliance on such standardized tests often limits the scope 
of analysis to cognitive outcomes.
The EU’s strategic frameworks, ET2020 and ET2030, emphasize 
equity and quality in education, with specific targets for member 
states [8]. While these frameworks have inspired national-level re-
forms, their regional application remains underexplored [12,13].
2.1. Trends in educational performance research
Secondary education dominates the literature, with 21 of 44 studies 
focusing exclusively on this level [18,44]. This trend reflects the avail-
ability of data from PISA and TIMSS, although studies encompassing 
primary or tertiary education remain limited [45,55]. Giménez et al. 
[41] evaluated the performance of preschool education organizations, 
which has not previously been considered from an activity analysis 
methods’ perspective. Segovia-González and Contreras [49] proposed 
a composite indicator to strive whether the performance in academic 
results and in the social-transforming role of the systems differs for 
boys and girls. There are only four studies covering exclusively tertiary 
education, namely Agasisti [16] that use data from OECD, Kocher 
et al. [45] with data from other data sources, such as journals and 
published rankings, Marto et al. [47] using data from Eurostat and Wu 
et al. [55] that use data at the national level. Ahec Šonje et al. [20] 
investigates the efficiency in both secondary and tertiary educational 
levels, using PISA, World Bank, and UNESCO (United Nations Educa-

primary and secondary education, with Hanushek and Luque [44] 
using only TIMSS results, Clements [28] using TIMSS and also OCDE 
data, and, finally, Miningou [48] using OECD, World Bank, and also 
World Development Indicators databases. Agasisti et al. [19] covers not

Eurostat. Aristovnik [23] and Bogetoft et al. [25] study the three levels 
of education, with the first using PISA, OECD, and World Bank data and 
the second mostly Eurostat and OECD data, also using PISA results as 
indicators of student input quality in upper secondary education. Stum-
briene et al. [12] and Camanho et al. [13] both analyze the educational

Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) is the most frequently employed 
technique, utilized in 22 studies, often with bootstrapping to address 
sensitivity issues [56,57]. This procedure helps overcome the outlier 
sensitivity of DEA and addresses the deterministic problem by con-
sidering the possibility of random noise. This method, which involves 
comparing the unit under analysis with subsamples of units and calcu-
lating the final efficiency based on the average efficiency obtained from 
each subsampling calculation, was utilized in the studies conducted 
by Agasisti [18] and Giambona et al. [37]. Three studies conducted a 
sensitivity analysis by comparing the results of their main model with 
alternative methodologies. Agasisti et al. [19] employed the NAIADE 
method (Novel Approach to Imprecise Assessment and Decision Envi-
ronments), a specific multicriteria evaluation approach that integrates 
all input and output items together and defines efficiency as a com-
promise solution between inputs and outputs. In Afonso and Aubyn 
[14] and Agasisti [16], the DEA model results were compared with 
the FDH model results. Another approach commonly used to guarantee 
the model’s reliability is introducing variations in the model’s variables 
or assumptions, which has been employed in 7 studies. Agasisti [16] 
varied the sample of countries and Ahec Šonje et al. [20] replaced the 
variable ‘unemployed with tertiary education in total unemployment’ 
by ‘best-ranked universities from observed countries in the world uni-
versity ranking list’. Bogetoft et al. [25] applied the same model to data 
from a different year, while Giménez et al. [39] compared results under 
different returns to scale assumptions. Miningou [48] tested different 
distributions for a specific parameter used in its model, and Stumbriene 
et al. [12] examined different weight restrictions.
Innovations such as bi-objective DEA and composite indicators ex-
tend its applicability [34,50]. Yet, inconsistencies in model assump-
tions remain a key challenge. Stumbrieṅe et al. [50] used DEA to set 
country-specific targets and identify different strategies for improving 
national performance towards strategic objectives. Stumbrieṅe et al. 
[51] proposed a benchmarking approach through a bi-objective DEA 
with performance indicators disaggregated by gender to close the gap 
between actual performances. Dominguez-Gil et al. [34] constructed 
a multidimensional composite indicator for the evaluation of educa-
tional systems in OECD countries, including a group of indicators that 
measures the social dimension of the educational system.
Many papers included other techniques such as Free Disposal Hull 
(FDH) [e.g., 14,16,39], Regression [e.g., 15,18,25], Directional Dis-
tance Function (DDF) [e.g., 35,40], SFA [e.g., 55]. The studies that did 
not include DEA have opted for different methodologies: Miningou [48] 
and Sutherland et al. [52] employed SFA, Thieme et al. [53] utilized 
DDF, Hanushek and Luque [44] employed linear regression, Clements 
[28] used FDH, Crespo-Cebada et al. [33] and Ferrera et al. [36] 
used Parametric Distance Function (PDF), and Agasisti and Cordero-
Ferrera [17] employed Hierarchical Linear Modeling, a specific regres-
sion method.
Incorporating contextual variables, such as socioeconomic and de-
mographic factors, enhances analysis but is inconsistently applied 
across studies. Methodological variations, including one-stage and two-
stage approaches, reflect this gap. The non-discretionary variables 
can be categorized into three key dimensions to facilitate analysis: 
student characteristics, demographic and national economic factors, 
and school characteristics. Student characteristics include parental edu-
cation attainment, parent occupational level, attitude towards studying,

family income level, the PISA index of economic, social and cultural 
status (ESCS), retention rate, immigrant status, gender and availability 
of resources at home. Demographic and national economic factors 
constitute the most prominent dimension and include variables such as 
GDP per capita, government spending on different educational stages, 
inflation rate, countries’ income group, public subsidies, population and

the school dimension include the percentage of public and government-

The methods to incorporate such variables ranged from one-stage, 
two-stage, and three-stage approaches to simpler descriptive statistical 
analyses. Giménez et al. [38] and Thieme et al. [53] employed a 
one-stage model similar to Lozano-Vivas et al. [58,59] to incorpo-
rate contextual variables from the category of student characteristics. 
This approach evaluated technical efficiency by considering only the 
inputs inherent in the productive process and its outputs. The envi-
ronmental variables were included in a second linear program, which 
decomposed the efficiency scores to obtain the maximum potential 
output and incorporate the role of managerial efficiency. Afonso and 
St. Aubyn [15] incorporated student characteristics, as well as de-
mographic and national economic factors, and Agasisti [16] included 
demographic and national economic factors, along with school char-
acteristics, both through the employment of a two-stage method. This 
approach involved utilizing a Tobit analysis in the second stage, where 
the estimated efficiency scores from the first stage were corrected 
with the non-discretionary variables. The correction was based on 
regressions of the first-stage efficiency score estimates on these vari-
ables. Agasisti [18] also utilized a regression model, specifically an 
Ordinal Least Squares (OLS) approach, to incorporate demographic 
and national economic factors and school characteristics. In another 
approach, Marto et al. [47] employed a spatial two-stage least squares 
model. They also utilized Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) 
clusters to create more comparable groups, considering the values of 
non-discretionary variables included in the demographic and national 
economic factors. Wu et al. [55] utilized a three-stage model proposed 
by Fried et al. [60] to include the influence of school characteristics and 
demographical and national economic factors. This involved including 
the environmental variables in the DEA model in the first stage. Then, 
the second stage employed SFA to regress the performance measures 
obtained from the first stage against a set of environmental variables. 
Finally, a traditional DEA model was used in the third step to re-
evaluate the units’ performance under analysis after removing the 
influence of environmental effects and statistical noise.
Other methodologies used to control the effect of non-discretionary 
variables included Propensity Score Matching (PSM), as employed 
by Crespo-Cebada et al. [33] and Camanho et al. [26] to consider school 
and student characteristics. Simpler approaches were also employed, 
such as descriptively analyzing the correlation of environmental vari-
ables from the three categories with efficiency scores, as done by Aga-
sisti et al. [19]. Ferrera et al. [36] utilized variance decomposition to 
understand the attribution of efficiency to different factors from the 
student and school characteristics categories.
Studies addressing EU strategic frameworks rarely incorporate re-
gional heterogeneity, a critical oversight given the disparities within 
member states. Stumbriene et al. [12] assessed the performance of Eu-
ropean countries concerning the ET2020. This evaluation summarized 
the framework’s indicators into a single performance measure using 
the BoD method. A sensitivity analysis was performed by varying the 
weight restrictions of the model from fully flexible to fixed weight-
ing schemes. This analysis utilized data from 2014 and 2015. One 
limitation of the study was not considering multiple time periods in 
the evaluation. Addressing this limitation, Camanho et al. [13] con-
ducted a study encompassing a broader time span by creating a Global 
Malmquist Index. This index evaluated trends from 2009 to 2018 at 
three-year intervals. The study also examined 𝜎 and 𝛽-convergence 
over time, where 𝜎-convergence have showed positive results only from 
2015 to 2018, and 𝛽-convergence, showed negative results, implying 
a widening gap between countries’ performance levels. Similar to the 
study by Stumbriene et al. [12], Camanho et al. [13] also highlighted 
that achieving certain ET2020 target values at the country level may 
be impossible for some countries, suggesting a need to re-evaluate the 
values. Future research should leverage regional data to refine national 
targets and inform tailored interventions.
Only eight studies focus on regional disparities, underscoring a 
significant gap in the literature [17,47]. Regional analysis is crucial 
for addressing intra-national inequities, particularly in decentralized 
education systems like Spain and Canada [35].
To date, only a limited number of studies have addressed edu-
cational performance at the regional level. Yet, analyzing education 
systems at this level is essential for policymakers and stakeholders 
seeking to address intra-national disparities and to develop targeted 
interventions that foster more equitable educational outcomes.
A regional-level analysis allows for the identification of specific 
underperforming areas and supports the development of tailored policy 
responses, ultimately contributing to more effective and inclusive edu-
cation systems. Despite the importance of this approach, the literature 
remains scarce, particularly in the context of European Union policy 
targets. In fact, only two studies have so far evaluated the performance 
of EU Member States in achieving the goals established under the 
ET2020 framework.
In this context, our study introduces an innovative empirical ap-
proach by employing regional-level data within the ET2030 framework, 
thereby extending the existing literature. This approach not only fa-
cilitates the monitoring of progress towards EU-level targets, but also 
sheds light on within-country heterogeneity, ultimately allowing for the 
proposal of adjusted national targets. Such adjustments are in line with 
the recommendations of the EU legislator stated in the Council Reso-
lution [8, Annex II], which encourages the consideration of regional 
disparities when defining national goals.
This paper builds on these opportunities and advances current re-
search by implementing a robust conditional analysis that allows us not 
only to monitor the progress within the framework outlined by the EU-
level targets but also to shed light on the within-country heterogeneity. 
The results provide policymakers with enhanced analytical tools and 
evidence to better understand the inequalities faced by different regions 
in achieving strong educational performance. These insights are cru-
cial for the formulation of targeted policies and interventions aimed 
at reducing regional disparities and fostering improved educational 
outcomes across Europe.
Expanding methodological diversity by integrating spatial analy-
ses and multi-method approaches can enhance the reliability of find-
ings [19,47]. Sensitivity analysis and benchmarking techniques, though 
underutilized, offer opportunities for refining models [14,25].
3. Methodology
This section presents the composite indicator framework used for 
the assessment of regional performance in attaining the 2030 Strategic 
Framework for Education and Training targets. The description of the 
original Benefit-of-the-Doubt framework is followed by the presentation 
of the robust formulation. The conditional approach is also described, 
which allows an equitable evaluation among regions grappling distinct 
environments.
3.1. Benefit-of-the-doubt
A composite indicator is a metric that combines the values of multi-
ple individual indicators into a single measure. It is intended to provide 
a thorough evaluation of a complex reality, facilitating interpreta-
tion and effective comparison across entities or over time. Composite

4 

M.J. Durães et al.
indicators are becoming increasingly used for benchmarking, policy 
analysis, and public communication, with applications ranging from the 
economy to environmental studies and education.
There are several steps when constructing a Composite Indicator, as 
mentioned in Nardo et al. [61]. The first steps include the establishment 
of the theoretical framework to be used and the data selection. The 
importance of having complete databases and reliable data makes the 
treatment of outliers and missing data a critical stage of this process. 
Another crucial step is the weight selection before performing a final 
aggregation of the chosen indicators. If using a standard fixed weight-
ing scheme, they must be determined prior to running the model, 
typically through expert opinion. This is frequently criticized due to the 
inherent subjectivity involved in this process. There is also frequently 
a lack of consensus among experts regarding the appropriate weights 
to be used in the aggregation functions. As a result, the importance 
level assigned to each indicator by each unit is ignored, complicating 
the investigation of root causes of poor performance. Comparing the 
multidimensional performance of units and subjecting them to a fixed 
set of weights may prevent stakeholders from accepting the results. 
Each unit has its own characteristics, and inevitably different policy 
priorities will coexist, which should be considered.
To address this issue and allow for greater flexibility in reflecting 
diverse priorities, a more adaptive approach is needed. The Benefit 
of the Doubt approach is able to tackle this challenge. It is a data-
driven method popularized by Cherchye et al. [62] and based on a 
linear programming model, the Data Envelopment Analysis [63]. As 
in DEA, the BoD technique identifies a subset of best practice units (or 
benchmarks) and constructs the best-practice frontier using them. The 
weights are endogenous [64], allowing for flexibility and adaptation to 
the units of measurement, and chosen so to maximize the composite 
indicator score of each region under analysis. Since weights are not 
predetermined, subjective selection is eliminated, and decision making 
units (DMUs) cannot blame poor performance on it. This property gives 
rise to the term Benefit-of-the-Doubt, as each DMU under assessment 
is portrayed in the most favorable light. The underlying assumption is 
that a unit’s superior performance in a particular indicator reflects a 
higher valuation or prioritization of policies related to that indicator. 
A unit’s poor performance in a given indicator, on the other hand, 
indicates that it is given less importance. BoD allows different DMUs to 
assign different weights to the indicators. If, even when viewed in its 
best light, the DMU does not have the best performance, this indicates 
that there is room for improvement evidenced by the comparison with 
peers, as any other weighting scheme would place it in an even worse 
relative position, by direct comparisons among observed units and 
based on observed evidence.
While the flexible weighting of BoD offers various advantages, it 
also gives the model excessive freedom, allowing it to ignore indi-
cators with poor performance. To address this issue, it is possible 
to set weight restrictions to ensure that all indicators are taken into 
account in the performance evaluation [62]. In this context, the weight 
restriction proposed by Zanella et al. [65] will be further explored. 
This formulation enables the incorporation of information about the 
relative importance of indicators by imposing bounds on the weight 
ratios, using restrictions in the form of Assurance Regions Type I (ARI) 
first proposed by Thompson et al. [66]. The specific type of restriction 
presented by Zanella et al. [65] allows for the specification of bounds in 
percentage terms, thereby reflecting the relative importance of the out-
puts considered in the assessment. Typically, the formulation of a BoD 
problem is analogous to an input-oriented DEA problem with constant 
returns to scale (CRS), where the outputs represent the indicators to be 
aggregated, while a single dummy variable equal to one serves as the 
input [62]. This choice is more intuitive as it focuses on the aggregation 
of the outputs. However, when weight restrictions are introduced to 
reflect the relative importance of outputs, the model should adopt an 
output-oriented formulation, as described in Van Puyenbroeck [67]. 
This study adopted the output-oriented formulation with CRS presented

3.2. Robust and robust conditional approaches
The deterministic nature of DEA, and consequently of BoD, implies 
that all deviations of observed DMUs from the frontier are attributed 
solely to poor performance. This makes the model susceptible to atypi-
cal observations, such as outliers or measurement errors, which signif-
icantly impact the evaluation and distort the results. To address this 
limitation, a Monte Carlo simulation procedure is used to obtain a 
robust version of the BoD technique based on the works of Cazals et al. 
[68] and Daraio and Simar [69].
First, a subsample 𝑏 of 𝑚 observations (where 𝑚≤𝑛) is randomly 
drawn with replacement from the observations. The performance of the 
DMU 𝑘 under analysis, [𝐶𝐼𝑘(1, 𝑌𝑘)]𝑏,𝑚, is then assessed based on the 
subsample. This process is repeated 𝐵 times (𝑏= 1, … , 𝐵), and the final 
composite indicator is obtained by calculating the arithmetic average of 
the composite indicator values obtained for each subsample, as shown 
in (3).

It is important to note that this robust approach may result in 
the appearance of super-performing units. This occurs when the DMU 
under analysis is not included in the subsample used to construct the 
frontier. Consequently, if the DMU is a best performer, it will be located 
beyond the frontier, leading to a performance measure greater than 
one. These indicate that the unit under assessment is performing better 
than the average of the 𝑚 units in the reference sample and it is referred 
to as super-performing unit [70].
The conditional version of the robust BoD estimator addresses the 
heterogeneity among Decision Making Units (DMUs) by considering 
the context in which they operate. The Monte Carlo procedure is 
adapted to incorporate contextual variables directly into the estimation 
process, without assuming that these variables have no effect on the 
attainable set or the estimated frontier, that is, without imposing the 
separability condition [71]. It is worth noting that conditional effi-
ciency estimators are consistent regardless of whether the separability 
condition holds, while unconditional estimators produce reliable results 
only when separability is met [71]. Although unconditional estimators 
typically converge faster, it is not of practical concern in our case. In 
the context of education, the conditional approach is increasingly used 
and we observe that economic and socio-demographic factors influence 
the attainable set [see for example 31,35,72]. This approach helps dis-
tinguish whether performance differences stem from actual educational 
underperformance or from challenging institutional conditions.
In this case, the units are drawn not with a uniform probabil-
ity as described above, but with a probability based on an kernel 
density function estimated for the contextual variables. The DMU 𝑘
under analysis is compared with 𝑚 units operating in more similar 
contexts [for a more extensive discussion, see for example 70,73]. 
This procedure captures the role of the context and enables a fairer 
assessment by comparing each unit with other ones sharing a similar 
environment. As a by-product, we can identify peers with similar 
contexts, allowing for a fairer assessment of improvement potential 
despite unfavorable conditions [35]. This information would not be 
accessible without the conditional analysis: it sheds light on the sources 
of potential underperformance and accordingly on the most appropriate 
forms of policy intervention. Policy-makers should foresee investments 
at a more systemic level if the underperformance is mainly driven by 
the operating conditions. Otherwise, they should promote more specific 
policies and look at the identified best practices. The remaining steps 
of the composite indicator calculation follow a similar process to the 
one described for the robust analysis. Naturally, the conditional case 
can also result in scores greater than one.
4. Data and model development
As elucidated in Section 1, the primary objective of this research is 
to benchmark European regions’ performance regarding the attainment 
of the ET2030 indicators. It is also proposed to incorporate the con-
textual factors in a robust conditional approach for a more equitable 
comparison leading to the estimation of fair targets. An additional 
analysis is proposed, encompassing both the regional and country levels 
simultaneously, in order to gain insight into the disparities between 
regions within their respective countries.
Consequently, to implement the methodology outlined in Section 3, 
it is essential to gather data encompassing the ET2030 indicators and 
the relevant contextual variables across the various European regions 
and countries. The ET2030 sets common targets, but each country has 
different characteristics and operates in a different context. The refined 
level of collected data together with the implementation of state-of-the-
art modeling strategy defines a new framework that allows us to explore 
in an unique way within-country heterogeneity, to identify the sources 
of regional underperformance and to devise the most suitable forms of 
policy interventions.
Concerning the geographical areas, the Nomenclature of Territorial 
Units for Statistics (NUTS) classification [74] was adopted to collect the 
data. This classification is an agreed-upon manner for the EU and the 
UK. It intends to segment the economic territory to allow the standard-
ization of European statistical data collection, conduct socioeconomic 
analysis at the regional level, and define EU regional policies. The data 
have been collected for all the geographical areas in NUTS classification 
at country and NUTS-I levels. The year 2019 was chosen as it was the 
most recent with enough available data.

4.2. Contextual variables specification
De Witte and López-Torres [78] present a systematic review of the 
contextual variables used in the literature, dividing them into four 
categories: student, family, educational institution and community. 
Indeed, the choice of contextual variables reflects the level at which 
the analysis is conducted and what factors may potentially influence 
the performance being measured. Cordero et al. [30,31,32] have used 
a conditional approach to measure educational performance across 
countries at school level. What affects the school performance might 
be related to school characteristics as well as country characteristics, 
due to the international nature of the benchmarking exercise. Country 
characteristics were found to be more relevant in explaining the dif-
ferences in observed efficiency levels, and this applies in particular to 
economic indicators like gross domestic product (GDP) per capita and 
public expenditure per student on secondary education as a percentage 
of GDP per capita [see for example 31]. Pereira et al. [72] monitor 
country educational performance with respect to the learning mobil-
ity in European higher education and use the Human Development 
Index (HDI) as an overall socioeconomic indicator combining health, 
education, and standard of living. In D’Inverno et al. [35], educational 
systems are assessed and three contextual variables have been chosen 
to explain educational equity. The Gini index has been considered as

The empirical framework we present in this paper is slightly dif-
ferent from the previous papers dealing with international assessment 
and using a conditional approach (and hence choosing suitable envi-
ronmental variables). In this case, we monitor all aspects of education, 
from childhood early education to tertiary education until adult par-
ticipation. Accordingly, we looked for variables that could capture in 
the most comprehensive way factors that in the European Commission 
reports as well OECD reports are listed as the main critical factors 
explaining differences in educational performance at any level [79,80]. 
Since the public expenditure per student might vary substantially across 
different educational stages [79], we cannot include the total expendi-
ture, as it may mask meaningful differences, nor the expenditures for 
each educational stage, as doing so would significantly increase the di-
mensionality of the analysis. Due to the strict relationship as suggested 
by the economic Okun’s Law [81] between GDP and unemployment 
and a greater data availability for the latter, we have opted for the un-
employment rate. From the perspective of human capital theory [82], 
education is pursued when the expected future benefits of continuing 
education outweigh its costs, with labour market conditions influencing 
both. In line with this, previous research supports the inclusion of 
labour market indicators. For instance, Charles et al. [83] investigated 
how the housing boom and bust affected labour market opportunities 
and, in turn, college enrolment during the 2000s. The results identified 
the causal effect of housing demand shocks on labour market outcomes, 
college attendance, and educational attainment. De Witte et al. [84] 
investigated how the institutional context, education, and labour mar-
ket policies correlate to the level of early school leaving by exploiting 
variation over time and across countries. Their studies show that youth 
unemployment has a positive influence on early leavers from formal 
education, and a negative effect on the proportion of early school 
leavers in training. Petrongolo and San Segundo [85] investigated the 
impact of family characteristics and local labour market conditions on 
the demand for post-compulsory education. They found evidence of a 
positive effect of youth unemployment and a negative effect of adult 
unemployment.
As concerns the socio-demographic dimension affecting the edu-
cational performance across countries at any educational stage, the 
immigrant status is recurrent and one of the most crucial factors 
explaining performance differences due to challenges linked to cultural 
adaptation, language acquisition and career development [80,86,87]. 
Through the literature, the integration of immigrant students is a 
central concern in many countries. Research on student achievement 
and school participation reveals a significant performance gap between 
immigrant and native students. Giannelli and Rapallini [88] showed 
that immigrant students’ performance in math is less unequal than in 
reading and writing, and, therefore, education programs for integration 
should mainly concentrate on improving the learning of language 
skills. The recent rapid increase in immigration into Europe also in-
cluded refugees. Green and Iversen [89] demonstrate negative effects 
of refugees on the math scores of native primary school children, with 
an important magnitude. Moreover, the existence of stereotypes may 
influence the native-immigrant grade gap. Alesina et al. [90] states that 
immigrant students in Italian schools receive lower grades compared 
to native students with the same performance in standardized tests, 
and they relate this gap in grades to teachers’ implicit stereotypes. 
From a different perspective to analyze the effect of immigration in the 
educational sector, Jensen and Rasmussen [91] showed that children in 
schools with a high immigrant concentration have lower reading and 
math test scores. Following the same perspective, Brunello and Rocco 
[92] showed that doubling the share of immigrant pupils in secondary 
schools would reduce the test score of natives and that reducing the 
dispersion of this share between schools has small positive effects on 
the test scores of natives.
Data regarding the population’s citizenship composition was avail-
able, specifically the rate of the population with citizenship from 
the reported country, foreign country, stateless and no response. The 
variable with fewer missing values was the citizenship rate from the 
reported country. Therefore, it was chosen since it corresponds to the 
complement of the immigrant rate and exerts the same effect on the 
model.
Thus, in order to ensure a more equitable comparison between 
geographical areas, this study takes into consideration the two con-
textual variables as detailed in Table 4, both of which are part of the 
community category and extracted also from the Eurostat database.

4.4. Model specifications
To employ the robust and the robust conditional approaches, the 
parameters 𝐵 (number of sampling iterations) and 𝑚 (number of ob-
servations in each subsample) had to be determined first. To obtain 
reliable results and minimize the effect of outliers in calculating aver-
ages, 𝐵 should be a very high number. Thus, 𝐵= 2000 was selected 
for this research [see for example 70,93]. As for the choice of 𝑚, 
any finite value of 𝑚 would mitigate the impact of potential outlying 
observations, because only a partial number of observations would 
be drawn at each iteration for the construction of the best practice 
frontier [68]. However, the value of 𝑚 should be set neither too low nor 
too high, such that the percentage of super-performing units decreases 
only marginally and gets rather stable [see for example 68,70,93]. A 
small value can result in a too large number of super-performing units, 
while a very large value (𝑚→∞) can quickly converge to the full-
frontier results. To guide the selection of 𝑚, we follow a graphical 
approach commonly adopted in the literature [see for example 94–96]. 
Specifically, we plot the percentage of super-performing units across a 
range of 𝑚 values and look for an ‘‘elbow’’ in the curve, that is typically 
a sharp drop followed by a flatter slope. This elbow point serves as an 
empirical threshold beyond which further increases in 𝑚 lead to only 
marginal changes in the results. We focus on values of 𝑚 to the left of 
this point, as they reflect a more stable and robust reference set while 
still capturing the essential contextual comparability of the evaluated 
units [94].
Therefore, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine the 
proper value of 𝑚 to use for the regional analysis (whose results are 
discussed in Sections 5.1 and 5.2) and the pooled regional and country 
analysis (discussed in Section 5.3).
The range 𝑚NUTS-I = {10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90} was tested for 
the regional level analysis. After evaluating the proportion of super-
performing units through Fig. 1, the value of 𝑚NUTS-I = 40 was chosen 
as it reduces the presence of super-performing units while remaining 
relatively stable for higher values.
The definition of the parameters enabled the application of the 
robust and the robust conditional approaches, which in turn allowed 
for obtaining the performance metric 𝐶𝐼𝑘(1, 𝑌𝑘) for each region, as well 
as other BoD-related metrics, which are the weights 𝑢𝑟(𝑟= 1, … , 𝑠), and 
intensity variables 𝜆𝑗(𝑗= 1, … , 𝑛). Only the peers with 𝜆𝑗 greater than 
10% were considered relevant.
To employ the BoD method considering not only the regional-
level data but also the country-level data simultaneously, a similar 
sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine the appropriate value 
of parameter 𝑚 in the range 𝑚All data = {15, 30, 45, 60, 75, 90, 105, 120}. 
After evaluating the proportion of super-performing units through Fig. 
2, the value of 𝑚All data = 60 was chosen as it reduces the presence 
of super-performing units while remaining relatively stable for higher 
values.
By employing the approach with the pooled sample of regions and 
countries, it was possible to estimate a robust performance metric 
𝐶𝐼𝑘(1, 𝑌𝑘) for each region and country 𝑘 (𝑘= 1, … , 𝑁). These results 
enable a subsequent analysis of the disparities between regions within 
the same country and recognize patterns in those disparities. As a final 
note, the results presented and discussed in the next section are robust 
to different lower bound specifications for the weight restrictions. 
The values 𝜙𝑟= 0.01, 0.1 have been alternatively tested. Results are 
available upon request.

5.2. Robust conditional BoD results at regional level
The conditional model considers contextual variables to address 
the environmental heterogeneity among DMUs by enabling compar-
isons between units that share similar environments. This approach 
is required to estimate fair and realistic targets. In this study, unem-
ployment and citizenship from the reported country (complementing 
the immigrant rate) were chosen as contextual variables since the 
literature has demonstrated their influence on educational performance 
outcomes.
Fig. 4 displays scatter plots depicting the robust and conditional BoD 
scores and ranks for all NUTS-I level regions. The plots are accompanied 
by a 45-degree line, which allows for an evaluation of the impact of 
using a conditional model compared to a robust model. The spread of 
data points indicates significant variations in results when comparing 
the robust and conditional models. While some data points maintain 
similar BoD scores, the scatter plot reveals that many regions exhibited 
improved performance in the conditional model, with a notable number 
of regions joining the best practice frontier and becoming relatively 
best performers once their context is taken into account. The scatter 
plot of ranks reveals a considerable dispersion of points, indicating 
substantial changes in the regional rankings. Therefore, it is evident 
that the conditional assessment has a notable impact on performance 
results.
Fig. 5 provides a visual representation of the distribution of the 
conditional BoD scores across European regions in 2019. Overall, there 
is a noticeable positive difference in results across all of Europe when 
compared to the robust results. Sur (ES6) and Canarias (ES7) in Spain 
and Attiki (EL3) and Voreia Ellada (EL5) in Greece had performance 
scores near 0.9 in the robust analysis. However, in the conditional 
model, these regions were identified as best-performers. Nisia Aigaiou 
Kriti (EL4) from Greece experienced a substantial average absolute 
difference, transitioning from a score of 0.849 in the robust model to 
0.971 in the conditional model. In addition, all the Greek regions, half 
of the Spanish regions (ES4, ES6, ES7), Srbija - jug in Serbia (RS2), 
Régions ultrapériphériques françaises in France (FRY) and Isole and 
Sur in Italy (ITG and ITF) exhibited the highest differences in the BoD 
scores from the robust to the robust conditional assessment. Further 
investigation reveals that these 11 regions were among the top 12 
regions with the highest unemployment rates in 2019. As explained 
earlier, the unemployment rate is closely correlated with GDP per 
capita and serves as an indicator of overall economic well-being. High 
unemployment rates often signify a struggling economy, which can 
have seriously detrimental implications for educational performance. 
By setting realistic expectations based on a region’s economic capacity, 
regions demonstrate that they are performing relatively well within 
their respective contexts, but not in absolute terms. In light of this, 
it becomes crucial to address environmental inequalities through poli-
cies that aim to enhance educational opportunities, promote economic 
growth, and reduce unemployment rates. These measures will help 
create a more equitable educational landscape and pave the way for 
sustainable development [35].

average is determined by dividing the sum of the intensity variable 
values of the peer 𝑗, 𝜆𝑗, by the frequency of its appearance as a peer. 
This metric provides insight into the significance of the peer within 
the specific regions it reaches. Finally, the absolute intensity average 
is obtained by dividing the sum of the intensity variable values of the 
peer 𝑗, 𝜆𝑗, by the total number of regions analyzed in the model (101 
regions). This metric reflects the overall importance of the peer as a 
source of learning best practices for Europe as a whole, considering 
both its reach and significance in influencing other regions. Therefore, 
a peer that appears frequently but with low-intensity values, or a peer 
that appears infrequently but with high-intensity values, will have a 
similar level of importance as a benchmark for Europe reflected by 
the absolute intensity average. Further examination can be conducted 
to understand whether this level of importance is attributed to a high 
frequency of appearance or high-intensity values.
The conditional analysis identified 35 peers, with 29 being peers of 
other regions besides themselves. Not surprisingly, the peers are the 
regions found top performers. The under-performing regions should 
look at the successful strategies adopted by these top performers and 
the way they have reached high teacher qualification, funded effective 
investments and promoted inclusive practices, among others. South 
East (UK) has the maximum frequency, appearing as a peer 33 times. 
The maximum absolute intensity value is 16.57%, and the average 
value among all peers is 2.48%. It is noteworthy that the partial 
intensity average is considerably high, with an average value among 
all peers of 45.44%. This implies that when a peer is associated with 
a particular region, it exhibits significant similarity to that region. 
Therefore, it is advisable to prioritize learning and adoption of the best 
practices based on the analysis of peers that were assigned high values 
of the intensity variable 𝜆𝑗.
Note that comparing a robust assessment with a conditional assess-
ment, the number of peers is likely to increase in the latter. Further-
more, a higher partial intensity average will reflect a more tailored 
guidance on best practices by considering peers with higher similarity 
with the environments of each region under assessment. This is crucial 
to ensure that policies are sought from other regions with similar 
contextual variables, rather than relying on regions with significantly 
more favorable conditions, where the effectiveness of their policies 
may not be applicable or realistic for implementation in less favorable 
conditions.

environment.

of the region encompassing the capital may contribute to the lower 
performance observed. Nevertheless, conducting further analysis at a 
more granular level, such as the NUTS-II level, would be necessary to 
validate this observation.
Analyzing data at the regional level is crucial for understanding 
disparities within countries. Capitals generally outperform other re-
gions, potentially due to better environments or higher achievement 
in indicators. However, this highlights the need for policies fostering 
greater homogeneity and addressing environmental gaps in underper-
forming regions, particularly islands, as for example the ‘Agenda Sud’ 
in Italy and the ‘+ Youth’ Package in Portugal. Prioritizing investment 
in strategically less important regions is essential to prevent widening 
gaps and ensure inclusive development.
The European Union monitoring reports on the strategic frame-
work for education and training have been instrumental in providing 
valuable insights through country-level analyses of indicators over the 
years. However, as demonstrated by this analysis, behind the seemingly 
good performance at the country level, significant disparities exist 
between regions. Merely analyzing performance at the country level 
is insufficient to ensure fairness and equity in education. It is essential 
to conduct an in-depth analysis at the regional level, comprehend the

6. Conclusion
The existing literature on educational performance measurement 
has seen significant development. However, research in this area is still 
limited when considering regions across multiple countries. Within the 
European framework of education and training, two studies have been 
conducted, namely Stumbriene et al. [12] and Camanho et al. [13], 
both focusing on country-level analysis. This work bridges part of the 
knowledge gap by explicitly focusing on the scope and methodology 
required to evaluate the strategic framework.
First, this study incorporates indicators for ET2030, enabling more 
updated and forward-thinking results for the future. The approach 
allows us to monitor the progress within the framework outlined by the 
EU-level targets, shed light on the within-country heterogeneity, and 
eventually suggest adjusted national targets as recommended by the EU 
legislator and tailored to the disparities observed at the regional level. 
Subsequently, it proposes collecting and analyzing data at the regional 
level. It is crucial for policymakers and stakeholders to address regional 
disparities within a country and implement targeted interventions to 
promote equitable educational outcomes. The regional performance 
allows to pinpoint specific under-performers and tailor policy interven-
tions accordingly, leading to more effective and equitable educational 
systems. By doing so, the study identifies and addresses heterogeneity 
within countries and across Europe.
From a methodological perspective, this paper addressed the deter-
ministic limitations in the DEA and BoD approaches, which were not 
previously explored in the studies conducted by Stumbriene et al. [12] 
and Camanho et al. [13]. It also employed a robust conditional analysis 
to consider contextual variables that may influence the performance 
of different geographies. This method of incorporating environmental 
factors differs from the approaches followed by the existing studies 
outlined in the literature review section.
The analysis of the BoD composite indicator at the regional level re-
veals significant variations in educational performance across European 
regions. Eastern areas exhibit lower performance scores, while North-
ern areas tend to show better results. Poor performing countries should 
look at the actions put forth by the best practices as concerns effective 
investments, new arrival programs, strategies to improve teacher qual-
ity and minimum teacher qualifications and inclusive practices [87]. 
The conditional model, which considers contextual variables, has a 
notable impact on performance results and leads to more tailored 
guidance on best practices by considering the unique environments 
experienced by each region. Analyzing regional and country-level re-
sults reveals disparities in educational performance within countries, 
with notable examples for example among Spanish and Greek regions. 
The results provide additional tools to policymakers, helping to identify 
inequalities different regions face in achieving good performance. The 
findings are valuable for designing targeted policies and interventions 
to promote better performance in education systems. Capitals tend to 
outperform other regions in the robust approach, but islands face signif-
icant challenges influencing their overall performance, as for example 
in the Italian and Portuguese case. In line with the programs adopted by 
several countries and the European Commission, addressing disparities 
within countries and investing in underperforming regions are essential 
for achieving fairness and equity in education across Europe (see for 
example the initiatives promoted by Greece and Romania, [97,102]).
The findings of this analysis hold substantial importance and clearly 
identify critical areas and regions where governments should give 
priority attention. However, lack of timely data availability and full 
indicators’ coverage at granular level prevents the proposed framework 
from exploring its full potential as an early warning assessment tool. 
Moreover, these constraints prevented a comprehensive investigation of 
other contextual variables that could potentially influence educational 
outcomes, as we did not have access to the necessary data to study 
them. Another limitation is the exclusion of the target goals established 
by Europe for each indicator when evaluating regional performance. 
Taking into account these European target goals in constructing the 
frontier could offer additional insights into how regions position them-
selves in relation to these established targets. Future research should 
foresee the possibility of including more contextual variables and go 
beyond the use of cross-sectional data to assess the evolution over time 
of the educational performance with respect to the targets. In this vein, 
alternative model specifications should account for the possibility of 
non-proportional adjustments to better reflect certain policy scenarios, 
while reflecting each country educational priorities.
In conclusion, future work must be done to achieve greater ho-
mogeneity and equal opportunities for all European citizens. Regional 
interventions will play a crucial role in providing targeted support. 
This research can contribute to the design of data-driven policies that 
improve educational outcomes across Europe.
