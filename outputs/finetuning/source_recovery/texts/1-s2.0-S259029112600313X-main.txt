The impact of financial education in schools on the financial literacy of
Brazilian students
Natali Morgana Cassola , Kalinca Léia Becker , Kelmara Mendes Vieira *
Federal University of Santa Maria, Santa Maria, Brazil

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Financial education
Financial literacy
PISA 2022
Propensity score matching
Brazilian schools

This study investigates the impact of school-based financial education on the financial literacy levels of Brazilian
students, using data from PISA 2022. An empirical approach combining different methodological strategies was
adopted: (i) estimation via Propensity Score Matching (PSM) to compare students with and without access to
financial education at school; (ii) the use of quantile regression to capture heterogeneous effects across the
proficiency distribution; and (iii) disaggregation of school-based financial education effects into four specific
topics: credit and debt, investments, financial planning, and economic concepts. The results indicate that
financial education has a positive and statistically significant impact, increasing students’ Plausible Value of
Financial Literacy (PVFL) by an average of 45.57 points, as estimated through PSM. Disaggregated analysis
shows that topics related to financial planning and credit and debt generate the largest gains in financial literacy.
Finally, quantile regression revealed that the effects of financial education are positive across the entire proficiency distribution, with greater intensity among higher-performing students, although they are also relevant for
lower-performing students.

1. Introduction
A lack of financial literacy within a population constitutes a socially
significant problem, as it affects the well-being of individuals and families and undermines socioeconomic development. Insufficient financial
knowledge hinders effective household budget management, increases
the likelihood of poor financial decisions, and consequently raises the
risk of indebtedness (Disney & Gathergood, 2011; Lusardi & Tufano,
2015; Noviarini et al., 2021). These factors exacerbate social inequalities, heighten family vulnerability, and reinforce the intergenerational cycle of poverty (Kaiser et al., 2022; Lusardi & Mitchell, 2023;
Mariatti et al., 2024).
Among OECD countries, about 18% of students did not reach the
minimum level of proficiency in financial literacy in the 2022 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA). In developing
countries, the problem is even more prevalent (Mendez Prado et al.,
2022). In the case of Brazil, around 45% of students are below the basic
level of financial literacy in PISA 2022. At the same time, according to
the Consumer Debt and Default Survey, 77.2% of Brazilian households
were in debt (CNC, 2024).

In this context, the implementation of financial education in schools
constitutes a strategy through which governments can foster the
development of essential financial skills from the early years of
schooling (Gerardi et al., 2010; Hasan et al., 2021; Lührmann et al.,
2018; OECD, 2023a). With this purpose, the Brazilian government
included financial education in the National Common Curricular Base
(Base Nacional Comum Curricular – BNCC), the normative document
that defines the essential learning outcomes to be developed throughout
basic education, guiding the curricula of public and private education
systems nationwide (Brasil. Ministério da Educação, 2018).
Although many studies indicate positive effects of financial education on students’ knowledge, these effects tend to be modest and shortlived when teachers are not adequately trained, when programs are not
continuously integrated into the school curriculum, or when they are
limited to isolated theoretical lessons (Bruhn et al., 2016; Cordero et al.,
2019; Ferreira & de Lima, 2024; Kaiser et al., 2022). Regarding the latter
point, topics such as credit and debt, which are often addressed in a
practical manner, tend to generate faster and more noticeable improvements in knowledge, as students can more easily relate them to
everyday situations. Other topics, such as abstract economic concepts,

Moreover, the difficulty of financial education in generating
knowledge and changing behavior tends to be more pronounced in
contexts of high economic vulnerability, where external factors, such as
insufficient income, limited access to financial products, and a lower
stock of prior knowledge, may hinder the practical application of what is
learned (Bruhn et al., 2016; Lusardi & Messy, 2023; OECD, 2023b).
In this regard, students with lower levels of proficiency may face
cognitive, institutional, or family-related constraints, which limit the
assimilation and application of the presented content. In contrast, individuals with a greater prior knowledge base tend to absorb formal
instruction more readily, further developing already established skills
(Carneiro & Heckman, 2003; Glewwe & Kremer, 2006; Heckman et al.,
2006; Kaiser et al., 2022; Lusardi & Mitchell, 2023; Mariatti et al.,
2024). On the other hand, the relative impact of financial education may
be more pronounced among those starting with very low levels of
knowledge, as even modest information gains can lead to significant
improvements in understanding and financial practices (Bruhn et al.,
2016). For individuals with a higher knowledge base, new content tends
to play a complementary or marginal role, resulting in more limited
proportional gains.
Thus, despite the relevance of policies and initiatives aimed at promoting financial education in schools, the literature does not present a
consensus regarding its effectiveness, as outcomes vary depending on
the context and strategy adopted. Consequently, further evaluations are
still necessary, especially in developing countries such as Brazil, where
high levels of socioeconomic vulnerability and educational inequality
can both increase the importance of financial education and limit its
effects, making it essential to carefully analyze how these policies are
implemented and under what conditions they produce results.
In this context, the main objective of this study is to estimate the
effect of financial education in Brazilian schools on students' Plausible
Value of Financial Literacy (PVFL) as assessed in PISA 2022. As secondary objectives, the study seeks to: (i) examine the heterogeneity of
financial education effects across the PVFL distribution; and (ii) identify
which groups of financial education topics − credit and debt, investments, financial planning, and general economic concepts − are
most strongly associated with students’ PVFL.
For this purpose, the Propensity Score Matching (PSM) methodology
is employed to estimate the impact of financial education on PVFL by
comparing students who have had access to financial education with
those who have not, based on their observable characteristics. This
procedure is important because exposure to financial education does not
occur randomly, being associated with characteristics of the students,
their families, and their schools. Thus, this method helps reduce selection bias, strengthening causal inference. The results of these estimates
can inform the implementation of financial education within the scope
of the BNCC, providing evidence that may support improvements in
curriculum design, teacher training, and teaching strategies, thereby
enhancing effectiveness.
Quantile regressions are also estimated to assess whether the effects
of financial education vary across the PVFL distribution, in line with
secondary objective (i). This approach allows identification of whether
impacts are more pronounced among individuals with initially low
levels of financial knowledge or whether they are concentrated at higher
levels. It thus contributes to understanding whether initial knowledge
stocks are essential for generating financial literacy gains or whether
marginal gains are greater among those lacking such prior knowledge,
helping to design more targeted strategies to achieve results according
to the characteristics of the target population.

2. Literature review
2.1. Financial education as a component of human capital
According to the Life-Cycle Hypothesis, when making consumption
and saving decisions, individuals seek to maintain a consistent standard
of living while considering the financial fluctuations that occur during
different stages of life, such as professional career and retirement
(Modigliani & Parkin, 1975). These financial decisions are closely
related to the concept of human capital, as defined by Theodore Schultz
(1961). Human capital refers to an individual's ability to apply their
knowledge, skills, and personal attributes in the workplace, resulting in
the creation of economic value. The author also highlights the importance of educational capital, which represents specific investments in
education as an essential component of human capital development.
In this context, Heckman (2006) emphasizes the importance of
investing in skills and competencies from the earliest years of life. He
argues that early interventions, such as high-quality preschool education, can have a significant impact on the development of children's
abilities, leading to improved outcomes throughout life. In Fig. 1, the
different life stages are represented on the horizontal axis and the rate of

Financial literacy can be understood as a specific form of human
capital, insofar as it encompasses knowledge and skills that influence
individuals' economic behavior and their ability to make efficient decisions over the life cycle. In line with Schultz (1961), investments in
education and knowledge enhance individuals’ capacity to use resources
productively, providing a theoretical basis for treating financial competencies as an investment in human capital. Moreover, as argued by
Heckman (2006), skills acquired early in life tend to generate higher and
more persistent returns, suggesting that financial competencies developed during schooling may produce lasting effects. This perspective
justifies the empirical evaluation of financial education in the school
environment, understood as a central channel for the initial formation of
these skills.

Bottazzi and Lusardi (2021) investigated gender disparities in
financial literacy among Italian adolescents using data from the 2012
PISA, revealing that historical and cultural factors significantly influence financial knowledge, especially among girls. An important finding
is that the mother's paid employment is positively associated with the
daughters' financial literacy. Similarly, the study by Preston and Wright
(2022), which examines data from the HILDA survey in Australia, indicates that gender disparities in financial literacy emerge early and are
influenced by gender stereotypes and self-confidence, which disadvantage girls. Complementarily, Iwatsubo et al. (2025), drawing on data
from a national survey conducted in Japan, found that although women
demonstrate greater knowledge of household management and adopt
more prudent financial attitudes, they exhibit less mastery of financial
concepts and numerical skills—elements directly linked to retirement
planning and more complex financial decision-making. Taken together,
these findings underscore the importance of incorporating family and
social influences into models analyzing financial education, as well as
accounting for gender differences.
Another study using PISA (2012) data, conducted by Cordero et al.
(2019), showed that financial education courses have a significant and
positive impact on students' financial literacy scores. In addition, factors
such as gender, socioeconomic status, and the number of books available
at home also contribute positively to students' financial performance. In
contrast, parents' educational level did not show a significant influence
on students’ financial literacy scores.
Salas-Velasco et al. (2021), when measuring the impact of financial
education using PISA 2012 data, highlighted that the way financial
education is incorporated into the school curriculum is fundamental to
its results. The study revealed that strategies such as including financial
education in business or economics courses and as an interdisciplinary
subject generated the greatest gains in students’ financial performance.
Approaches such as integrating financial education into mathematics or
offering it as an extracurricular activity also had positive effects,
although of smaller magnitude compared to other forms of
incorporation.
Despite the growing international literature based on PISA data,
evidence from the Brazilian context remains limited, particularly studies
that rely on PISA microdata or apply causal inference techniques such as
Propensity Score Matching. To the best of our knowledge, only one study
using Brazilian data from PISA 2015 has been identified. da Silva Carlo
and Leonel de Carvalho (2021) describe the results of selected PISA
financial literacy items and provide a comparative assessment of Brazil's
performance; however, their analysis is essentially descriptive and does
not employ statistical testing or econometric modeling.
Bruhn et al. (2016) provide causal evidence on the effects of financial
education in Brazilian high schools using a large-scale randomized
controlled trial. The authors find that exposure to a structured financial
education program led to improvements in students' financial knowledge and selected financial behaviors, with some effects persisting over
time. More recently, Reisdorfer-da-Silva et al. (2025) examined the effects of using board games in financial education classes in Brazil.
Employing Propensity Score Matching to estimate treatment effects, the
authors found positive gains across all three dimensions of financial
literacy. The largest effect was observed in students’ financial knowledge, followed by improvements in financial behavior and attitudes
toward personal finances.
In contrast, much of the Brazilian literature on financial literacy has
focused on adult populations and relies predominantly on survey-based
approaches. For example, Potrich et al. (2024), using a sample of 2888
Brazilian adults, found persistent gender differences in financial literacy
that have not diminished across generations.

2.2. Financial education: concepts, impacts and challenges
The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development
(2013) defines financial education as the process by which individuals
develop their ability to understand financial products and concepts,
seeking to become more aware of financial risks and opportunities,
thereby making decisions that contribute to their financial well-being.
Financial knowledge can be defined as a form of human capital,
considering that dedicating oneself to acquiring such knowledge is a
form of investment, which later provides individuals with a greater capacity to manage income, expenses, and savings (Delavande et al.,
2008).
Financial education is essential to ensure consumer protection and
financial inclusion in society, becoming indispensable for managing and
planning resources in a sustainable way over the long term (Messy &
Monticone, 2016). However, the lack of access to knowledge on this
subject distances the population from habits that contribute to financial
stability. According to the Central Bank of Brazil – BCB (2013), when an
individual decides to save, they accumulate resources with the intention
of using them in the future, which is essential for making investments,
since investment is the application of saved resources with the expectation of obtaining returns. Saving and investing consciously can lead to
improvements in quality of life, greater financial stability, and security,
especially if this practice begins soon after entering the labor market.
The impacts of financial education on the macroeconomy have been
widely discussed. First, it is worth noting that financial literacy affects
all countries, regardless of their stage of economic development
(Frisancho, 2023; Gerrans et al., 2023; Ansar et al., 2023). The second
principle highlights that it is essential to have financial skills, both to
deal with basic financial instruments, such as bank accounts, and with
more complex ones, such as stocks and investments, since these skills
make it possible to minimize risks and maximize benefits. Finally,
improving the effectiveness of financial education requires effort,
creativity, and fosters enhancements in economic policies and programs
(Lusardi & Messy, 2023).
In this sense, teaching initiatives on financial education in schools
become even more relevant. A mandatory course for students can have a
more significant impact on financial knowledge and behavior when
compared to voluntary programs. This strategy becomes even more
interesting since its impacts tend to be relatively uniform across individual characteristics (Frisancho, 2020).
The study by Mariatti et al. (2024), based on PISA data from 2012,
2015, and 2018, analyzed the influence of socioeconomic and macroeconomic factors on student performance in financial education. The
results show that socioeconomic inequalities, such as income distribution, negatively affect students’ financial literacy. Similarly, the study by
Khusaini et al. (2022) with university students found that those from
families with higher socioeconomic status tend to perform better in
financial knowledge. This highlights the importance of considering

3. Methodology

• Financial education course outside of school: Dummy variable
indicating whether the student participated in a financial education
course outside the school environment. This variable takes the value
1 if the student attended a course and 0 otherwise.
• Gender: Dummy variable taking the value 1 for male students and
0 for female students.
• ESCS (Economic, Social, and Cultural Status Index): Variable
representing the socioeconomic status of students based on four main
components. The first is parents' educational level, which indicates
their academic background (e.g., primary, secondary, or higher education). The second component is parents' occupation, reflecting
the family's social and economic status. The third aspect is the
number of books at home, an indicator of the household environment
that supports learning and cultural access. Finally, the fourth
component refers to material possessions available at home,
including technologies such as computers and durable goods such as
cars, indicating the family's economic resources. The index is standardized, with a global mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1,
which facilitates comparisons. Positive ESCS values indicate a socioeconomic status above the global average, while negative values
suggest a status below average (OECD, 2024).
• Grade repetition: Dummy variable that takes the value 1 if the
student repeated at least one grade level and 0 otherwise.
• School quality: Index measuring the perceived quality of schools by
parents, based on their responses to seven items from the PISA 2022
questionnaire. This variable evaluates dimensions such as teacher
competence, academic standards, and overall satisfaction with the
school environment. The statements assessed were: “Most teachers at
my child's school seem competent and dedicated,” “Performance
standards are high at my child's school,” “I am satisfied with the
content taught and the teaching methods used at my child's school,”
“I am satisfied with the disciplinary climate at my child's school,”
“My child's progress is carefully monitored by the school,” “My
child's school provides regular and useful information about his/her
progress,” and “My child's school does a good job of educating students.” Parents answered these statements on a 4-point scale, ranging
from “Strongly agree” to “Strongly disagree.” The index was constructed from these responses, following the scaling methodology
used in PISA 2018. This methodology transforms individual responses into a synthetic value that captures the multiple dimensions
evaluated (OECD, 2024).
• Brazilian regions: To classify students according to Brazil's five
regions, four covariates were generated: (1) Northeast, (2) South, (3)
Southeast, and (4) Center-West. These categories are compared to
the reference category (North). In the matching process, they help
balance regional differences between groups, allowing adjusted estimates of treatment effects.

3.1. Database, variables, and treatment definition
To analyze the impact of teaching financial education in schools on
students’ financial literacy scores, data from PISA 2022,1 an international student assessment program conducted by the OECD, will be used.
The “Financial Literacy” section of PISA will be employed, which
gathers general information about students, such as gender and parental
education, as well as specific data on financial education. These data
include, for instance, whether the student had financial education
classes or access to resources on the subject, such as books and classroom
content related to financial concepts like budgeting and loans, thus
contributing to the assessment of the financial competence of 15-yearold students (OECD, 2024).
Prior to the empirical analysis, the PISA 2022 microdata were preprocessed following a standardized procedure. First, all non-responses
and invalid response codes defined by the OECD were recoded as
missing values. Second, observations with missing information in the
outcome variable (Plausible Values of Financial Literacy) and in the key
covariates used for the matching procedure were excluded. Third, the
variables were inspected for outliers. As the financial literacy scores are
standardized and scaled by the OECD, no additional trimming or winsorization was applied. Finally, the analytical sample was restricted to
students with complete information required for the Propensity Score
Matching and quantile regression analyses.
From the initial PISA 2022 sample of 5491 students, observations
with missing values in at least one covariate were excluded. This
resulted in the exclusion of 2033 students (37.02% of the initial sample),
yielding a final analytical sample of 3458 students. Although the
exclusion of incomplete observations may introduce selection bias if
missingness is not random, this procedure follows standard practice in
propensity score matching to ensure comparability between treated and
control groups, and all subsequent analyses were conducted on this
restricted and balanced sample (see Appendix B).
The dependent variable will be the weighted average of the students'
Plausible Value of Financial Literacy (PVFL), a free translation of “plausible value financial literacy.” This is an estimate that takes into account
the student's performance on the test as well as the uncertainty associated with the assessment of skills that were not directly measured.
Plausible values are generated through multiple estimates using
response modeling and structural equation techniques, combining students' test performance with other variables, such as individual and
socioeconomic characteristics (OECD, 2024).
The treatment variable will be “Financial education at school,” and
the covariates used in the model are described below.
• Financial education at school: The original variable ranges from
0 to 16 and reflects the number of specific financial topics with which
the student had contact in school. These topics include: “interest
payment,” “compound interest,” “exchange rate,” “depreciation,”
“stocks,” “return on investment,” “dividend,” “diversification,”
“debit card,” “bank loan,” “pension plan,” “budget,” “salary,”
“entrepreneur,” “central bank,” and “income tax.” For analysis, the
variable was transformed into a binary dummy, where: 1 indicates
that the student had contact with at least one of these financial
topics, regardless of the number; and 0 indicates that the student had
no contact with any of the topics (OECD, 2024).

To refine the analysis of the effects of teaching financial education in
schools, the contents addressed were disaggregated into four main
thematic groups, according to the topics included in the “Financial Literacy” section of PISA 2022. Each group was transformed into a binary
treatment variable, taking the value 1 if the student had school exposure
to at least one of the topics in that category, and 0 otherwise. The four
groups defined were.
• Credit and indebtedness: includes the topics interest payment,
compound interest, debit card, and bank loan;
• Investments: includes the topics stocks, return on investments,
dividends, and diversification;
• Financial planning: includes budget, salary, and pension plan;
• General economic concepts: includes entrepreneurship, central
bank, and income tax.

With the estimated propensity scores, the next step involves checking
the common support, which compares the distribution of propensity
scores between the treatment and control groups. A high degree of
overlap is crucial to ensure that the groups are comparable. The balance
of covariates within the subgroups of observations also needs to be
evaluated, in order to guarantee that the subgroups are equivalent for
each covariate (Hair et al., 2019).
To define which observations are included in the control group, it is
possible to select a measure of proximity to the propensity score. In this
study, three algorithms are used to determine which provides the best
match between treated and control groups, ensuring that the observed
differences can be attributed to the treatment rather than pre-existing
characteristics: nearest neighbor matching with one neighbor, nearest
neighbor matching with five neighbors, and kernel matching, in which
each treated individual is compared with a weighted average of control
group observations, with the weights determined by their proximity in
the propensity score. Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) is also estimated to
provide a preliminary idea of the association between variables and to
validate the robustness of the results.
Dehejia and Wahba (2002) emphasize that when calculating the
propensity score, it is crucial to ensure that covariates are balanced
between groups. For this purpose, several tests should be performed. A
low pseudo-R2 reflects a lower dependence of observable variables in
determining treatment assignment, which indicates good balance quality. In addition, the likelihood ratio (LR) test is applied to verify whether
differences in covariates are statistically insignificant after matching.
To analyze the reduction in bias after matching, differences in means
and medians of covariates between treatment and control groups are
examined, as proposed by Stuart (2010). This also involves comparing
the averages of the covariates before and after matching. Table A2 in the
Appendix reports the descriptive statistics and standardized mean differences for all covariates before and after propensity score matching,
indicating a substantial reduction in covariate imbalance after
matching.

PSM is a widely recognized approach for estimating counterfactuals,
representing the outcome an individual would have obtained if they had
belonged to the opposite group (treatment or control). The propensity
score model aims to balance covariates between treatment and control
groups, and the appropriate selection of covariates is crucial to ensure
that counterfactuals satisfy the ignorability assumption (Hair et al.,
2019).

causal effects to be estimated from observational data when randomization is not feasible or practical. This assumption states that, conditional on the observed covariates, the assignment of treatment is
independent of potential outcomes. In other words, once covariates are

If selection bias is present, two individuals with similar observable
characteristics will have different probabilities of receiving treatment,
since the effect of μ is captured by γ which will be different from zero. If
there are no differences related to unobserved characteristics, no bias is
found (γ = 0) and the odds ratio for matched individuals receiving

extent to which the results obtained through PSM remain robust in the
presence of possible unobserved factors, thereby assessing the stability

3.3. Quantile regression
In addition to the Propensity Score Matching method, quantile
regression was used as another complementary method of analysis. This
technique makes it possible to estimate the conditional quantiles of the
response variable, allowing the assessment of how the explanatory
factors affect different points of the distribution. Thus, it becomes
possible to identify whether the impact of an intervention varies among
individuals with lower levels (lower quantiles) or higher levels (upper
quantiles) of financial literacy, providing a more detailed and robust
analysis of the heterogeneous effects of the treatment (Firpo et al., 2009;
Koenker, 2005).
In this study, the quantiles specifically estimated correspond to the
0.1 (10th percentile), 0.2 (20th percentile), 0.3 (30th percentile), 0.4
(40th percentile), 0.5 (median), 0.6 (60th percentile), 0.7 (70th
percentile), 0.8 (80th percentile), and 0.9 (90th percentile). The analysis
was conducted using the Unconditional Quantile Treatment Effects
(UQTE) methodology proposed by Firpo (2007), which allows for the
estimation of treatment effects along the entire outcome distribution,
rather than conditional on covariates.
The estimation was performed using Stata software,2 through the
ivqte command (instrumental variable quantile treatment effects). This
command facilitates the estimation of treatment effects at specified
quantiles, while adjusting for a set of relevant covariates.

these topics. This robust effect, confirmed even after Rosenbaum's
sensitivity test, indicates that school interventions can help broaden
young people's financial knowledge. These findings align with international studies that also observed a positive association between financial
education and literacy performance, such as Salas-Velasco et al. (2021)
and Cordero et al. (2019). Moreover, they reinforce the evidence from
Frisancho (2020), who found significant gains in a program in Peru,
suggesting that such policies have potential across different Latin
American contexts.
Another relevant point is that access to school-based financial education is not homogeneous. The logit model showed that students with
higher socioeconomic status, who attend extracurricular courses and
have not repeated grades, are more likely to be exposed to financial
content. Furthermore, boys were less likely than girls to have contact
with financial education. This finding echoes the literature discussing
access inequalities, such as Khusaini et al. (2022) and Mariatti et al.
(2024), and calls attention to the need for public policies that ensure
equitable access to this content, preventing financial education from
reproducing or exacerbating preexisting inequalities.
The disaggregated analysis revealed that Financial Planning and
Credit and Debt are the topics with the greatest impact, yielding gains of
nearly 50 points. This result indicates that content directly related to
young people's daily lives—such as expense control, understanding
wages, and responsible credit use—is more effective in improving literacy. This is consistent with Bottazzi and Lusardi (2021), who argue
that practical skills are essential to prevent excessive indebtedness and
promote sustainable financial behavior. Similarly, Messy and Monticone
(2016) identify planning as one of the main determinants of the ability
to make long-term financial decisions, underscoring the centrality of this
topic in curricula.
In particular, the strong and robust effects associated with the
Financial Planning and Credit and Debt topics are observed across both
the lower and upper ends of the financial literacy distribution. These
findings suggest that these contents play a central role in developing
foundational financial competencies while also supporting more
advanced financial understanding. This evidence is especially relevant
in the Brazilian context, where previous studies have documented high
levels of household indebtedness and low levels of retirement preparedness (Vieira et al., 2023), reinforcing the importance of emphasizing these topics within school-based financial education.
By contrast, the topics of Investments and Economic Concepts also
showed positive effects, though of smaller magnitude. These results
suggest that such content contributes to expanding students’ conceptual
understanding, even though it requires greater cognitive capacity to be
assimilated. This pattern is consistent with the analysis of Salas-Velasco
et al. (2021), who emphasize how socioeconomic and institutional factors condition the assimilation of more complex content, reinforcing that
inequalities can influence how different groups of students benefit from
financial education.
Quantile regression showed that effects are not uniform across the
proficiency distribution. High-performing students benefited more from
content on Investments, while lower-performing students experienced
greater gains in Financial Planning and Credit and Debt. This result
suggests that financial education can both broaden the repertoire of

more advanced students and reduce vulnerabilities among those at
greater risk of financial exclusion. This heterogeneity is compatible with
the arguments of Carneiro and Heckman (2003, 2006), according to
which the ability to absorb new content depends on skills accumulated
throughout the educational trajectory. Preston and Wright (2022) also
stress that social and gender inequalities influence how students benefit
from financial education, helping to explain the different impact patterns observed.
From a human capital perspective, these results reinforce the interpretation of financial literacy as a set of skills that can be accumulated
over time and that shape individuals' economic behavior throughout the
life cycle. In line with Schultz's (1961) concept of human capital and
Heckman's (2006) framework of skill formation, financial competencies
acquired during childhood and adolescence may generate persistent
returns by enhancing individuals' capacity to make informed financial
decisions in adulthood. Early mastery of these financial literacy topics,
particularly those related to financial planning and credit and debt
management, can contribute to more stable household financial trajectories, reducing vulnerability to excessive indebtedness and improving
preparedness for retirement. Thus, school-based financial education can
be understood not only as a short-term educational intervention but also
as an investment in long-term capabilities that influence family financial
stability and intergenerational economic outcomes.
From a public policy perspective, the findings underscore the
importance of consolidating financial education within the Brazilian
school curriculum, as envisioned in the BNCC. However, for this policy
to be effective, it is essential to invest in teacher training, the development of contextualized materials, and the adaptation of teaching practices to local realities (Bruhn et al., 2016; Lusardi & Messy, 2023;
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and, 2024). In this sense,
Iwatsubo et al. (2025) emphasize that financial education programs
must consider cultural and social differences in order to achieve significant results in diverse contexts.
6. Final considerations
6.1. Main findings and policy implications
This study analyzed the impact of school-based financial education
on the financial literacy of Brazilian students, using data from PISA
2022. Two complementary methodological approaches were employed:
Propensity Score Matching (PSM) and quantile regression. The combination of these techniques allowed for robust estimates and a more
precise understanding of the effects of financial education on student
performance.
The results obtained through PSM indicate that exposure to financial
education in school is associated with an average increase of 45.57
points in the plausible value of financial literacy (PVFL). This finding
confirms that the inclusion of financial content in the school environment contributes to the development of specific competencies in this
area.
The disaggregated analysis by topic showed that Financial Planning
had the largest average effect, with an impact of 49.99 points in PVFL,
followed by Credit and Debt (42.88 points), General Economic Concepts
(37.44 points), and Investments (35.40 points). These results highlight
that different topics generate specific effects, with some being more
9

N.M. Cassola et al.

Social Sciences & Humanities Open 13 (2026) 102748

effective in promoting financial learning—particularly topics related to
budgeting, retirement, interest, and debt, which are more accessible and
directly applicable to students’ daily lives.
Quantile regression revealed that the effects of school-based financial education are positive and significant across all quantiles, but with
varying intensity depending on the topic. Financial Planning stands out
in both lower and upper quantiles, suggesting a substantial impact on
students across different proficiency levels. Investment-related content
shows stronger effects in the upper quantiles, while Credit and Debt and
Economic Concepts exhibit consistent effects across the distribution,
with peaks at the lower and upper extremes. These patterns indicate that
financial education benefits all students, although gains tend to be
higher for those with better prior performance, reflecting the influence
of pre-existing inequalities in cultural capital and resource access.
The analyses also indicated that factors such as participation in
financial education courses outside school, gender, socioeconomic status, and academic history significantly influence the likelihood of a
student accessing financial education in school. Students with higher
socioeconomic status, for instance, are more likely to be exposed to
content, potentially exacerbating pre-existing inequalities in the absence
of public policies ensuring universal access.
In this context, the findings underscore the importance of financial
education as part of human capital development. By promoting knowledge that fosters more conscious decisions regarding consumption,
savings, and investment, financial literacy directly contributes to individual and collective well-being and strengthens economic citizenship.
From a public policy perspective, the results point to the need to
consolidate and expand financial education within the Brazilian school
curriculum. Effective implementation requires proper teacher training,
contextualized instructional materials, and evaluation mechanisms.
Special attention should be given to more vulnerable contexts to reduce
access inequalities and broaden the benefits of financial education for all
students.
Therefore, this study provides empirical evidence that can guide the
formulation of more equitable and effective educational policies. By
systematically integrating financial education into the school environment, it is possible to prepare young people for the challenges of the
contemporary world, promoting more responsible choices and contributing to a fairer, financially aware society.

Finally, the absence of a cost-effectiveness analysis limits the scope of
inferences for purposes of public policy formulation and evaluation.
Taken together, the limitations discussed above point to several
promising avenues for future research. In particular, future studies could
benefit from employing longitudinal or experimental designs to more
robustly identify the causal effects of school-based financial education
on students’ financial literacy over time. Such approaches would enable
the analysis of learning dynamics and the persistence of financial
knowledge and competencies beyond the immediate educational
context. Additionally, examining heterogeneous effects across student
subgroups, such as gender, socioeconomic background, and prior academic performance, would contribute to a more nuanced understanding
of distributional impacts and equity considerations.
Future research should further incorporate qualitative and contextual dimensions of program implementation. Greater attention to
instructional intensity, pedagogical approaches, teacher training, and
curricular integration may help identify which program characteristics
are most strongly associated with improved outcomes. Moreover,
comparative cross-country analyses using harmonized datasets could
shed light on how institutional and cultural factors condition the
effectiveness of financial education policies. Finally, the inclusion of
cost-effectiveness or cost–benefit analyses would provide valuable insights for policymakers seeking to design efficient and scalable financial
education interventions.

6.2. Limitations and directions for future research

Data availability statement

This study presents some limitations that warrant acknowledgment.
First, the use of cross-sectional data from PISA 2022 constrains the
ability to establish long-term or dynamic causal relationships between
school-based financial education and students' financial literacy.
Moreover, the measurement of exposure to financial education relies on
students’ self-reports, which may be subject to recall errors or misclassification, thereby introducing potential measurement bias.
Although PSM was employed to mitigate selection bias, the validity
of the estimates depends on assumptions, particularly the absence of
unobserved confounding factors, which cannot be fully verified with the
available data. In addition, the aggregation of financial education content into four broad thematic areas, while analytically convenient, may
obscure relevant variation in content, depth, and instructional focus
within each category.
The generalizability of the findings is also limited, as the institutional, socioeconomic, and educational context of Brazil differs substantially from that of other PISA-participating countries; therefore, the
results should not be interpreted as universally applicable. Furthermore,
the analysis does not explicitly explore heterogeneity across Brazilian
states, which may mask regional differences in the implementation and
effectiveness of financial education.
Additionally, the study does not incorporate information on qualitative aspects of financial education delivery, such as instructional
duration, teacher expertise, or the degree of curricular integration.

The data used in this study come from the OECD Programme for
International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022. We used the public-use
microdata files (Student Questionnaire, School Questionnaire, and
Financial Literacy cognitive assessment), as well as the corresponding
questionnaires and codebooks, all available through the OECD PISA
2022 data repository: https://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/2022database/
