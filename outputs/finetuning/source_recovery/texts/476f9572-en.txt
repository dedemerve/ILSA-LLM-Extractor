Abstract

This paper explores the state of teenage career development in Wales. It sets out findings
from the OECD Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), a comparative
international survey of young people in secondary education. PISA 2022 notably provides
considerable data on young people’s engagement in, and perceptions of, career
development, including information on occupational and educational intentions and
participation in career development activities. This paper compares student responses in
Wales to averages across the OECD, disaggregated by a range of student characteristics,
including gender, socio-economic background, academic proficiency and school type
attended (including by language spoken). Levels of career uncertainty in Wales, as across
the OECD, are now very high. Students in Wales who express an occupational expectation
are very strongly focused in one field of employment: the professions. They also
increasingly expect to complete tertiary education. The occupational expectations of young
people however are poorly aligned with actual patterns of labour market demand. In
comparison to OECD countries, opportunity exists to significantly increase the engagement
of students in Wales in career development. In terms of equity, the study shows evidence
of gender and social disparities being addressed.

Teenage career development in context

Concerns over the poor transitions of young people into the labour market are high in many
countries. In spite of young people leaving education more highly qualified and with more
years of schooling to their name than ever before, over the last generation the average ratio
of youth unemployment to adult unemployment has remained static (Covacevich et al.,
2021[1]). Societies turn to career guidance systems to help young people visualise and plan
their futures, enabling them to position themselves well to make informed and appropriate
investment decisions in their education and training and to convert their accumulated
human capital into successful employment. Growing international longitudinal evidence
demonstrates strong relationships between forms of teenage career development (typically
assessed at age 15) and employment outcomes ten years later (OECD, 2021[2]), illustrating
the preventative role that effective guidance can play in reducing the risk of poor outcomes
in the early labour market. Analysis of longitudinal data provides a framework for assessing
the relative strengths and weaknesses of guidance systems.
PISA 2022 provides the most comprehensive available international dataset on the state of
student career development at ages 15-16. Students from all OECD countries and the great
majority of participating non-OECD countries responded to questions related to their
participation in career development activities, including activities routinely associated in
longitudinal data with better employment outcomes. Data are also available on teenage
career thinking (for example, in relation to clarity of career vision and the alignment of
occupational and education plans) and attitudes towards the effectiveness of schools in
enabling career development (OECD, 2024[3]; [4]). Breaking down data by demographic
subgroups provides considerable insight into the extent to which the career development of
students can be seen as equitable.

Methodology

PISA 2022 collected data from nearly 690 000 students in 81 OECD member and partner
economies. In Wales, data was collected from 2 568 students in 89 schools. Additionally,
principals from each school provided questionnaire responses to complement the data. To
minimise the time burden on students, not all questionnaire items were presented to every
participant. Specifically, for the section on career development activities, students were
typically asked about four or five of the eleven available questions.

Insights from the analysis

The organisation of career guidance. How do schools in Wales manage career
guidance provision?
Overwhelmingly, school principals report that career guidance is formally scheduled into
students’ time in school, making it a mandatory requirement for 96% of students, much
higher than the OECD average of 58% (Table 1). Mandatory provision contributes to
equity in provision in that students from more socially disadvantaged backgrounds may be
less likely to voluntarily engage in school-mediated guidance (OECD, 2024[5]). In Wales,
the main responsibility for guidance is split between approaches where guidance
counsellors are either based in the school or visit regularly, or where responsibilities are
shared by all or specific teachers (Table 2). PISA data for Wales suggest that students in
schools where guidance counsellors hold responsibility for career guidance tend to
demonstrate more positive signs of career development. For example, students in these
schools are more likely to articulate clear occupational expectations (i.e., a well-defined
idea of their future job) (Table 4) and generally feel more confident that their educational
experiences will lead to successful transitions, compared to students in schools where
career guidance is handled by all or some teachers (Table 28).

Career certainty and uncertainty. Do students have a clear idea about their career
plans?
Teenage career uncertainty, defined as the inability or unwillingness to articulate an
occupational expectation at 15, is associated in national longitudinal studies with poorer
employment outcomes than would otherwise be expected (OECD, 2024[4]). In Wales, as
across OECD countries, levels of such career certainty have fallen steadily since 2015. In
PISA 2022, 44% of students in Wales (compared to 39% across the OECD) can be
categorised as uncertain in their career plans and 56% certain at the time of survey (61%
across the OECD) (Table 3 which illustrates levels of career certainty). In Wales, students
who are male, lower academic performers, rural inhabitants and in Welsh language
institutions are more likely to be uncertain about their job ambitions (Table 4). While it is
to be expected that many students will change their career plans over their duration of their
secondary education, the ability to express an occupation at any point suggests that students
are actively thinking about their plans for working life, providing opportunity to reflect on
their investments in education and training in light of typical professional requirements.

Occupational expectations. What are the jobs that students expect to have?
Where students do express an occupational expectation, their expectations tend to be
concentrated among a small number of possible professions (Table 5). In PISA, students
are asked to write in the kind of job that they expect to have around the age of 30. Responses
are then coded using the 2008 edition of the International Standardised Classification of
Occupations (ISCO). ISCO is based around ten major groups which are subdivided into
two-, three- and four-digit increasingly granular occupational codes. Coding is done on the
best match for the written in occupation. Between 2015 and 2022, using the three-digit
analyses, the proportion of students planning on working in one of the ten most popular
occupational choices shared by peers has remained steady at slightly under 50% (Table 5).
Coding at three digits provides consistency in comparison, but does risk oversight of

Student interest in becoming a teacher for example can be classified at two, three or four

Career signalling. How much do the occupational plans of students have in common
with actual patterns of labour market demand?
As in other OECD countries, the occupational expectations of teenagers in Wales are
strongly focused on the professions (Major Group 2 in the International Standardised
Classification of Occupations – ISCO). In Wales, 63% of students articulating a career plan
expect to work as a doctor, teacher, engineer, lawyer or another form of professional
occupation by around the age of 30. Data from Census 2021 (Office for National Statistics,
2023[6]) indicates however that only 22.5% of workers aged 30 to 34 in the actual labour
market are employed in such occupations (Table 13).

Occupational and educational ambitions. How ambitious are students for their
future careers?
Longitudinal studies commonly find that students who expect to work as senior managers
or professionals (ISCO Major Groups 1 and 2) go on to enjoy better employment outcomes
than comparable peers, even with similar levels of education achievement (Covacevich
et al., 2021[1]). As suggested above, students in Wales are ambitious for their futures. In
PISA 2022, 65% expected to work in an ISCO 1 or 2 profession, higher than the OECD
average and, but slightly lower on the 2015 figure for Wales (68%) (Table 15). In 2022,
majorities of students of all characteristics expect to work in professions which employ
only 30% of the total working population (and 32.1% of the labour force who are aged 3034). Occupational ambition among low performers on the PISA assessments in Wales has
increased, rising from 48% in 2015 to 51% in 2022. Disaggregating the data by Economic,
Social and Cultural Status shows a 9-percentage point ‘aspiration gap’ between high
performing students from the most and least socially advantaged quartiles (Table 16).
A similar, but stronger pattern is evident in the educational ambitions of students. As
illustrated in Figure 2, both in Wales and across the OECD, socially advantaged students
are far more likely to plan to complete tertiary education. Notably, the data reveals that low
performing students from the highest ESCS quartile are more likely to expect to complete
tertiary education than high-performing students from the lowest ESCS quartile.

Over the last decade, educational ambitions have grown considerably in Wales. Between
2015 and 2022 the percentage of students expecting to complete some form of tertiary
education grew from 46% to 63% (for students attending state schools this percentage rose
from 47% to 62.4%) (Table 19). Changing patterns of aspirations are strongest among boys
(growing from 39% to 57% over the period), socially disadvantaged students (31% to 49%)
and low performing students (30% to 52%). Among students attending state schools
between 2015 and 2022 the proportion expecting to complete some form of tertiary
education rose notably from 47% to 62.4%. While a smaller upward trend is apparent across
OECD countries, the trend is less dramatic than seen in Wales (Table 20).

Career alignment and misalignment. Are the educational plans of students
sufficient to achieve their occupational ambitions?
In Wales, as elsewhere across the OECD, majorities of students expect to work as senior
managers, professionals or technicians/associated professionals (ISCO Major Groups 1, 2
and 3) and intend to complete at least some level of tertiary education. However, many
students who expect to work in such occupations plan on leaving education after
completing no more than upper secondary. Such students can be categorised as being
misaligned in their aspirations and multiple longitudinal analyses provide evidence that
teenage misalignment can be linked with poorer ultimate employment outcomes than
would otherwise be expected. Researchers tend to view misalignment as an indication of
teenage confusion over how the labour market operates and how they might most
successfully engage with it (OECD, 2024[3]). As educational ambitions have risen,
misalignment levels have fallen in Wales (from 44% in 2015 to 25% in 2022), but still
remain above the OECD average at 21% (Table 22). In Wales, as across the OECD, it is
boys, the socially disadvantaged and low performing students who exhibit the highest rates
of career misalignment. Two out of five low performers in Wales expect to work as a senior
manager, professional or technician/associate professional, but do not expect to complete
any stage of tertiary education (Table 23).

Instrumental motivation. Do students think that their schools are helping them to
achieve their career plans?
PISA 2022 includes a range of questions relating to students’ perceptions of enablers and
barriers to their career progression. The results highlight that important minorities of
students express considerable anxiety about their career preparation. Results for Wales are
comparable to OECD averages (Table 27). A number of the statements to which students
react relate to the belief that engagement in education will be of value to long-term personal
development, including in work. These forms of instrumental (or extrinsic) motivation are
found in longitudinal analyses to commonly link with better actual employment outcomes
after the completion of secondary education (Covacevich et al., 2021[1]). Reviewing the
date in light of whether students are aligned or misaligned in their occupational and
educational plans reveals a correlation. The results show the need for some caution in
assessing the perspectives of students on their career planning. While students who are
aligned in their educational and occupational plans are more likely than their misaligned
counterparts to agree that they feel well-informed about possible paths after secondary
education, significant numbers in both groups disagree with the statement. In a similar
fashion, many students who say that they feel well-prepared for their future paths after the
completion of secondary education express future plans which suggest confusion
(Table 28).

Career development. To what extent are students engaging in career development?
Does career provision have a positive effect on the career thinking of students?
Career development takes place both within and outside of the school walls and a number
of development activities can be linked with better ultimate employment outcomes. These
include career conversations with parents and carers as well as with school staff
(Covacevich et al., 2021[1]). PISA 2022 data show that students in Wales speak with parents
and family members about their future education slightly more than is typical across the
OECD. However, a noteworthy trend emerges when comparing low-performing and
socially disadvantaged students to their more advantaged peers. Whereas 13.8% of low
performing students say that they speak to their family about their education plans no more
than once or twice a year, this applies to only 4.2% of high performers. Similarly, while
14.8% of the most socially disadvantaged students rarely speak about their education plans,
this applies to just 6.8% of their most socially advantaged classmates (Table 30).
Other career development activities that are most confidently associated with better longterm employment outcomes, based on OECD analyses of national longitudinal analyses,
include engaging with people in work through job shadowing or a work-site visit (where
students in Wales are much less likely to participate than on average across the OECD) and
attending a job fair (where the opposite is the case) (Covacevich et al., 2021[1]). Across the
OECD, on average students from the highest ESCS quartile are more likely to engage in
career development activities than their classmates from the lowest ESCS quartile. While
this pattern is replicated in Wales for important activities where students have the
opportunity to engage directly with people in work and employers in other areas, provision
in Wales goes against the OECD trend. Significantly from an equity perspective, the most
socially disadvantaged students are considerably more likely than their most advantaged
peers to have spoken with a career advisor on at least one occasion by the age of 15
(Table 32). Students in state schools are more likely to engage in career development than
their peers educated in private schools (Table 34).

Strategically important careers. How interested are students in occupational areas
of high strategic importance?
As the occupational expectations of young people taking part in PISA can be categorised
using the ISCO classification system, it is possible to explore over time the changing
interests of students in specific types of jobs. Analysis was undertaken in regard to three
occupational areas within Wales: creative and performing artists (ISCO 265); Software and
Applications Developers and Analysts ad Database and Network Professionals (ISCO 251,
252); Science and engineering associate professionals (ISCO 31). Between 2015 and 2022,
student interest in the first two fields has remained steady. In the third field, student interest
has doubled to 2.3%, but remains lower than the OECD average (2.8%) (Table 37). In this
field, interest growth has been driven by a tripling of the number of girls expressing their
expectation of working the occupation (0.4% to 1.5%) and a five-fold increase of interest
among the most socially disadvantaged students (0.3% to 1.6%). OECD analysis of data
from 32 country Survey of Adult Skills (PIAAC) enables assessments of the risk of
automation with regard to specific occupations. Breaking down the tasks within a job and
the extent to which such component parts are becoming automatable or digitalised allows
assessment of risks which students might face as they move into the labour market. Across

Comparative synthesis. How does the career development of students within the
jurisdiction compare with that in other relevant countries and territories?
Career development is a complex process through which children and young people learn
about themselves over long periods of time in light of growing understanding of their
interests, capabilities, potential educational pathways and possible futures in employment.
Career development is the result of many different episodes of learning in and out of school
whereby students gain access to new information and experiences and reflect upon them.
Research studies struggle to keep pace with such complexity of lived experience.
Longitudinal studies tend to focus for example on isolated forms or episodes of career
development in ways that rarely recognise that career development represents engagement
in multiple and diverse activities, experiences and encounters over time. Studies which
explore teenage engagement across a range of different guidance activities struggle at times
to create samples of sufficient size for analysis (Covacevich et al., 2021[1]). PISA data does
provide some opportunity to explore the extent to which students are engaged in different
forms of guidance. A comparative approach identifies the proportion of students who have,
by the age of 15, participated in three important guidance interventions. Combining
participation levels in activities that allow for career exploration and reflection (engaging
in job shadowing and/or work-site visits; a job fair; and speaking with a guidance
counsellor in school), shows that around one student in seven in Wales (15.1%) have
completed all three activities by the age of 15. A comparison with nine other jurisdictions
shows that such low levels of engagement are not unusual and higher than in Korea (14.3%),
Northern Ireland (13.2%), Scotland (11.1%) and Germany (7.5%), but lower than Denmark
(49%), England (22.4%), New Zealand (22.8%), Finland (19.3%) and the Netherlands
(15.3%) (Table 40).
