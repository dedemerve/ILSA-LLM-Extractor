REVIEW ARTICLE
Socioeconomic Status and Academic Achievement 
in Primary and Secondary Education: a Meta‑analytic 
Review
Juan Liu1 · Peng Peng2 · Baobao Zhao1 · Liang Luo3
Accepted: 14 June 2022 
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 
2022
Abstract
This study comprises two meta-analyses conducted to investigate relations between 
socioeconomic status (SES) and academic achievement, with a focus on macro-level, 
micro-level, and methodological moderating variables in primary and secondary 
education. The first meta-analysis is based on 326 empirical studies with 949,699 
students from 47 countries and areas, and the second is based on three international 
large-scale assessments (i.e., PISA, TIMSS, and PIRLS) with 1230 independent 
samples of 5,095,283 students from 105 countries and areas. We found moderate 
correlations between SES and academic achievement across the world, rs = .22 ~ .28. 
Moderation analyses revealed that (a) these relations have strengthened since the 
1990s; (b) GDP per capita and economic equality did not affect the relations; (c) 
higher net enrollment ratio and longer duration of compulsory education did not 
weaken these relations; (d) the relations stayed stable or even strengthened across 
grades in concurrent and longitudinal designs. Taken together, our findings suggest 
that educational expansion that focuses on increasing educational opportunities does 
not seem to reduce inequalities in academic outcomes between high- and low-SES 
school children in educational systems on the national level. Quality indicators for 
educational expansion, however, should be considered in setting educational policy 
to achieve inclusive, equitable education.

Academic achievement is closely related to students’ development, such that those 
with higher academic achievement have better employment after graduation, greater 
life satisfaction, and less behavioral or health risk (Crede et al., 2015; Whaley & 
Noel, 2013). Not surprisingly, in many countries, academic achievement is one of 
the most important indicators of educational quality on the national level (Tian & 
Sun, 2018). Given the importance of academic achievement, researchers in psychol-
ogy, education, and sociology have studied factors associated with students’ aca-
demic achievement, in the hope of finding important factors for educational policy 
and practice and incorporating them to provide equal opportunities for all students 
to be academically successful.
One of the important sociological factors associated with academic achievement 
is socioeconomic status (SES), which is often defined as the status or prestige of 
an individual or an individual’s family owing to the possession of certain social 
resources, capabilities, or wealth (American Psychological Association, 2007). A 
“common-sense” mechanism underlying the relation between SES and academic 
achievement is that high-SES families are more likely to invest in children’s learning 
(e.g., providing books, computers, tutors) and to have access to high-quality schools 
(see, e.g., Conger & Donnellan, 2007), both of which lead to better academic out-
comes. Families with higher SES also tend to show more knowledge of education 
and have greater expectations about what education provides, which promotes chil-
dren’s motivation to learn (e.g., Sohr-Preston et al., 2013). SES is even suggested 
to have indirect effects on academic outcomes through cognitive development. That 
is, children from high-SES backgrounds are more likely to fulfill their cognitive 
potentials (e.g., self-regulation and executive function skills), and this has a positive 
impact on their academic development (Finders et al., 2021; Peng & Kevit, 2020; 
Waters et al., 2021). Thus, the SES–academic achievement relations are often found 
to be an indicator of educational equity, such that the stronger such relations are in 
a given country, the less the educational equity is considered in that country (e.g., 
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2019).
Over the past several decades, research has been conducted to understand the 
relations between SES and academic achievement as well as factors that influence 
those relations, including individual studies (e.g., Thomson, 2018) and international 
large-scale educational assessments across countries (e.g., Chmielewski, 2019). Yet 
the size of such relations remains unclear. For example, small to large relations have 
been reported across different countries. Even within specific countries, great varia-
tion has been reported (e.g., Assadi et al., 2007; Fernández-Alonso et al., 2015).
Prior Meta‑analyses
Meta-analysis offers a powerful approach to examine the relations between SES 
and academic achievement, because of its advantages in statistical power, robust 
point estimation across different samples and measures, and correlational data with 
less publication bias (e.g., Chow & Ekholm, 2018; Lipsey & Wilson, 2001). To 
our knowledge, three meta-analyses of SES–academic achievement relations have 
been based on US and European samples (see Appendix Table S1). White (1982)

included 70 published and 31 unpublished articles from 1915 to 1975 and reported 
a mean effect size of 0.24 for student-level data. Sirin (2005) examined 58 journal 
articles from 1990 to 2000 and reported a mean effect size of 0.27. Harwell et al. 
(2017) included the 98 articles from White’s (1982) meta-analysis and 45 new arti-
cles from 1980 to 2010 and reported a mean effect size of 0.22 before 1980 and 0.25 
after 1980. Five meta-analyses focused on samples that included other countries. 
Çiftçi and Cin (2017) examined a global sample of 66 articles from 2000 to 2016 
(although countries were not specified) and found a mean effect size of 0.41. Liu 
et al. (2020) focused on samples from China from 1989 to 2016 and found a mean 
effect size of 0.24. Kim et al.’s (2019) meta-analysis included 49 empirical studies 
from 1990 to 2017 in 38 developing countries and found a mean effect size of 0.12. 
Kim (2019) included 19 empirical studies from 1990 to 2017 from seven countries 
in East Asia and found an average effect size of 0.22. Most recently, Rodríguez-
Hernández et al.’s (2020) study of 23 studies across 22 countries and areas showed a 
mean effect size of 0.06.
These meta-analyses have made significant contributions to our understanding of 
the relations between SES and academic achievement, but several questions remain. 
First, no meta-analysis has examined a comprehensive sample of countries, limit-
ing our understanding of the effects of moderator variables—especially macro-level 
(country-level) moderators that can provide implications for educational policies to 
achieve equity by addressing the negative effects of low SES on academic outcomes. 
Second, no meta-analysis of the relations between SES and academic achievement 
has been based on longitudinal data individually, which can provide more robust 
evidence on the possible changing effects of early SES on later academic outcomes. 
Third, there is a lack of meta-analysis and moderation analysis based on interna-
tional large-scale assessment databases, which are less influenced by the publication 
bias often criticized in meta-analyses based on empirical studies.
In the present study, we address the preceding issues with an up-to-date meta-
analytic review and includes data from both empirical studies and international 
large-scale assessments. We explore (a) SES–academic achievement relations 
among multiple countries and areas in primary and secondary education, and (b) 
how a comprehensive set of moderators may influence those relations.
Macro‑level Moderators
GDP Per Capita
Gross domestic product (GDP) per capita shows a country’s GDP divided by its 
total population, comparable to the average family SES in a country. Heyneman 
and Loxley (1983) have suggested that SES has a smaller effect on academic 
achievement in low-income countries (low GDP per capita), whereas such effects 
are larger in high-income countries (high GDP per capita). This is referred to as 
the “Heyneman-Loxley effect.” Kim et al. (2019) found that developing countries 
classified as low-income had the lowest effect size, r = 0.04, followed by lower-
middle–income countries, r = 0.18, and upper-middle–income countries, r = 0.19,

which supports the Heyneman-Loxley effect. But Kim et  al. focused only on 
developing countries. By including all possible samples from multiple countries, 
we investigate whether GDP per capita (a more objective and continuous vari-
able to represent the average family SES in a country) plays a significant role in 
SES–academic achievement relations, in order to further examine the Heyneman-
Loxley effect.
Net Enrollment Ratio
Net enrollment ratio refers to the percentage of school-age students at a certain level 
of education (UNESCO, 2020) in the same age population, which is a typical indica-
tor of educational expansion. It is expected that more students will have opportuni-
ties to access school, implying weaker SES–academic achievement relations with an 
increase in net enrollment ratio. However, in a large data assessment analysis from 
1964 to 2015, Chmielewski (2019) found that increasing school enrollment actu-
ally strengthened SES–academic achievement relations in some countries, implying 
that expanding enrollment revealed educational inequality that was hidden outside 
the school system. In contrast, for students in Hong Kong, China, where the GDP 
per capita and net enrollment ratio are both high, the SES–academic achievement 
relation was the weakest among countries participating in PISA 2012 (Kalaycıoğlu, 
2015). This finding suggests that students could benefit from educational expan-
sion and relatively high-quality education simultaneously in areas with high GDP 
per capita (Broer et al., 2019). Taken together, these mixed findings suggest that net 
enrollment ratio and GDP per capita are highly correlated and should be considered 
simultaneously in one model because of possible confounding effects. It is important 
to control for GDP per capita in order to reveal the unique effects of net enrollment 
ratio on SES–academic achievement relations.
Duration of Compulsory Education
Compulsory education refers to the legally required period of education when chil-
dren must attend school (UNESCO Institute for Statistics, 2020). The Incheon Dec-
laration and Framework for Action was adopted to ensure access to at least 12 years 
of primary and secondary schooling, of which at least 9 years should be compulsory 
and free by 2030. In reality, many countries have passed legislation for compulsory 
education (UNESCO, 2016). However, does the length of duration of compulsory 
education influence SES–academic achievement relations? Kirdar et al. (2016) have 
indicated that longer compulsory education forces every student to study in school, 
particularly low-SES students, weakening SES–academic achievement relations. 
Others have argued that extending the duration of compulsory education may lead to 
credential inflation (devaluation of educational or academic credentials over time), 
promoting competitive responses by affluent families to maintain relative superiority 
(Medeiros et al., 2020) and strengthening SES–academic achievement relations.

Economic Inequality
Economic inequality is the unequal distribution of income and wealth among individu-
als in a country, usually indexed as the GINI coefficient (Kirkland et al., 2019). Ample 
research has found a direct negative link between economic inequality and students’ 
academic achievement (Chiu & Chow, 2015). Growing economic inequality increases 
the disparities in parental investments in services for their children’s education (Dun-
can et al., 2017) and increases social segregation (Musterd et al., 2017), potentially 
widening the achievement gap between low- and high-SES students (Byun & Kim, 
2010; Reardon, 2011). However, cross-national empirical studies have found the oppo-
site. Studies using international education assessments have found that economic ine-
quality is not strongly related to SES–academic achievement relations (Marks, 2005). 
Thus, the role of economic inequality warrants further investigation via meta-analysis 
of both empirical studies and large international assessment databases.
Years
International education meetings have been held to promote educational equity (e.g., 
the UNESCO World Conference on Education for All in 1990 and the World Edu-
cation Forum in 2000). Most recently, over 1600 participants from 160 countries 
attended the World Education Forum 2015 in Incheon, South Korea. This dynamic, 
diverse international community rallied around a single shared vision of inclusive and 
equitable quality education (UNESCO, 2015) and set forth a new vision for education 
for the period from 2015 to 2030, embracing sustainable goals for educational devel-
opment. However, despite this global effort to address the challenge of educational 
equity, such effects may be offset by increases in income inequality over time (Rear-
don, 2011). In the present study, using time points suggested by the international edu-
cation meetings in 1990, 2000, and 2015, we explore possible changes in SES–aca-
demic achievement relations following each meeting during the past 30 years.
Micro‑level Moderators
Study Design
Prior meta-analyses evaluating the relations between SES and academic achievement 
have focused mostly on cross-sectional data. Longitudinal effect sizes (SES at early time 
points associated with subsequent academic achievement) arguably reflect SES–academic 
achievement relations with greater accuracy (Sirin, 2005). With longitudinal designs, one 
can explore whether students’ SES has (a) an accumulating effect on academic achieve-
ment over time, as suggested by the Mathew effect (growing inequality as a long-term 
social process; Caro et al., 2009), or (b) a diminishing effect on academic achievement 
over time, as suggested by the school compensation hypothesis (schooling may offset the 
negative effects of low SES on later academic achievement; Peng et al., 2019).

Grade Level
Grade level is another moderator to investigate in considering the Mathew effect and 
the school compensation hypothesis. Some studies have found that SES–academic 
achievement relations strengthen with grade level (e.g., Sirin, 2005), but some have 
found that SES–academic achievement relations weaken or remain stable with grade 
level (e.g., Harwell et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2020). Unlike previous studies, the pre-
sent study focuses only on primary and secondary education. During this basic edu-
cational stage, all students actively develop fundamental skills and accumulate aca-
demic knowledge (e.g., Curriculum Development Council, 2002), and investigating 
SES–academic achievement relations in this stage is therefore more meaningful for 
policies regarding educational equity.
Methodological Moderators
Measurement of SES
Indicators of economic, social, and cultural status as a composite SES variable are 
less biased and is a stronger predictor of academic achievement, and it has often 
been adopted in large international assessments such as PISA. In contrast, Kim 
et al.’s (2019) meta-analysis suggests that single SES measures are stronger predic-
tors of academic achievement than are composite measures. We categorize SES into 
five types (parental education, parental occupation, family income, family resources, 
and a composite index of those factors) in order to further investigate potential mod-
eration effects of SES measurement.
Subject of Academic Achievement
SES–academic achievement relations may also be moderated by achievement in 
specific academic subjects. Some studies have found a higher correlation in math 
achievement than in language achievement (e.g., Kingdon et al., 2017), but others 
have found the opposite (e.g., Dotterer et al., 2008). Among meta-analyses, Sirin 
(2005) revealed that SES–math was the strongest among all subjects, but Liu et al. 
(2020) found that SES showed that SES–language was stronger than science/math or 
general achievement. In the present study, we categorize students’ academic achieve-
ment into language, STEM, and general achievement in order to further investigate 
potential effects of subject of academic achievement.
Research Questions
To summarize, this meta-analysis addresses four major research questions: 
First, what is the overall magnitude of the relation between SES and academic

vary as a function of macro-level moderators such as GDP per capita, net enrollment 
ratio, duration of compulsory education, economic inequality (GINI coefficient), 
and years? Third, does the relation between SES and academic achievement vary as 
a function of micro-level moderators such as study design and grade level? Fourth, 
is the relation between SES and academic achievement affected by measurement of 
SES and subject of academic achievement? Our two meta-analyses address empiri-

SES–Academic Achievement Relations Based on Empirical Studies
Method
Literature Search
For this meta-analysis, the PsycINFO, ERIC, and Web of Science databases were 
searched for titles, abstracts, and keywords; search terms included socioeconomic 
status (SES) OR social class OR social status OR income OR education OR occu-
pation OR job OR disadvantaged and academic achievement OR performance OR 
academic outcome OR success OR GPA. Inclusion criteria for studies were as fol-
lows: (a) The study reported at least one correlation (r) between SES and aca-
demic achievement, including cross-sectional effect sizes and longitudinal effect 
sizes. (b) The study sample included typically developing students from primary 
to secondary school; studies that focused only on atypically developing samples 
were excluded. (c) The study provided r at the individual level. (d) The study was 
conducted in the years from 1990, when the World Conference on Education for 
All opened to December 2021. (e) The study was either published or unpublished 
(e.g., dissertations and conference papers, etc.). (f) The study was written in Eng-
lish. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) Studies with disability students (e.g., 
students with disease/learning difficulties, ADHD students). (b) Studies with pre-
primary and college students. (c) Longitudinal studies with r between academic 
achievement at an earlier point and SES at a subsequent point. (d) To avoid redun-
dancy between the two meta-analyses, 25 empirical studies (59 effect sizes) that 
were conducted using the PISA, TIMSS, or PIRLS databases. To ensure that we 
did not miss any related studies, we also searched the reference lists of extant sys-
tematic or qualitative reviews on SES and achievement and included all studies 
that met our inclusion criteria. When we were unable to obtain the full text of 
an article or could not find complete information, we sent email requests to the 
authors.
Finally, we included 326 articles with 348 independent samples; 1587 effect sizes 
(279 empirical studies with 838 cross-sectional effect sizes; 151 empirical studies 
with 749 longitudinal effect sizes); and 949,699 participants (range 24 to 157,814) 
from 47 countries and areas, with only one empirical study that did not report the 
national origins of samples (see Fig. 1). All studies were conducted from 1990 to

Analytic Strategies
First, we used the correlation coefficient r between SES and students’ academic 
achievement as the effect size. Because some studies reported multiple effect sizes 
for one independent sample, there were three different variance components: sam-
pling variance of the extracted effect sizes at level 1; variance between effect sizes 
extracted from the same study at level 2; and variance between studies at level 3 
(Cheung, 2015). To include all eligible effect sizes in each study, we used random 
effects robust variance estimation to explain statistical dependencies between effect 
sizes, which can be used with clustered data (i.e., effect sizes nested within sam-
ples) by correcting a study’s standard error to take into account correlations between 
effect sizes from the same sample (Hedges et al., 2010). This technique can be used 
to estimate a mean correlation (ρ) between all pairs of effect sizes within a clus-
ter and then calculate the between-study sampling variance estimate (τ2), which 
is an absolute measure of heterogeneity between studies. In finally performing all 
analyses, we estimated τ2 with ρ = 0.8, and sensitivity analyses suggested that the 
results were robust across different values of ρ (Hedges et al., 2010; Park & Beret-
vas, 2018).
Second, we converted all obtained original correlation coefficients r into Fisher’s 
Z-scores for subsequent analysis. Average effect sizes and confidence intervals were 
converted back to correlation coefficients for presentation (Lipsey & Wilson, 2001). 
Random-effects analysis was applied, because we assumed between-studies hetero-
geneity based on samples with varying study characteristics (e.g., different areas and 
countries). Otherwise, fixed-effects analyses would render our findings more suscep-
tible to type I error than would the random-effects analyses (Cheung, 2015; Hunter 
& Schmidt, 2000). Multilevel random-effects meta-analysis using Hedges et  al.’s 
(2010) corrections were performed with the ROBUMETA package in R to summa-
rize the overall weighted correlation coefficients and examine potential moderators 
(Fisher & Tipton, 2015).
We first used cross-sectional effect sizes to estimate the overall weighted mean 
correlation between SES and academic achievement. We conducted subgroup analy-
ses to examine the SES–academic achievement relations for each subgroup of each 
moderator. Meta-regression analyses were used to examine whether years, GDP per 
capita, GINI coefficient, net enrollment ratio, duration of compulsory education, and 
grade level moderated SES–academic achievement relations, and measurement of 
SES and subject of academic achievement were used as covariates. Second, we com-
pared cross-sectional effect sizes and longitudinal effect sizes to test whether there 
was an effect of study design in all empirical studies.
Finally, for moderation effects, a multiple moderator model was used. Hox (2010) 
has noted that many moderator variables are often interrelated, so it is better to add 
all significant moderating variables simultaneously in a single model to estimate the 
unique and true effect for each moderator. Thus, we entered all moderators simulta-
neously into one model to control for confounding effects among moderators. For

categorical moderators, we dummy coded variables to examine comparisons among

Publication Bias
The term publication bias refers to the problem of selective publication, whereby 
it is easier for significant findings to be accepted and published (Rosenthal, 1979). 
We examined publication bias in two ways. First, we used Egger’s regression test for 
asymmetry of funnel plots (Egger et al., 1997; Pustejovsky & Rodgers, 2019). Given 
the nested structure of our data, we added the standard errors of correlations as a 
moderator to predict correlations among studies, using robumeta in R (i.e., the alter-
native for the regtest function in the metaphor package in R; Viechtbauer, 2010). 
Second, we used contour-enhanced funnel plots to examine publication bias (Peters 
et al., 2008). When nonsignificant or uninteresting results are unpublished, funnel 
plots are asymmetrical. Contour-enhanced funnel plots can distinguish asymmetry 
caused by statistical significance related to publication bias or other factors, with 
the reference line set at 0 and additional contour lines set for different levels of sta-
tistical significance (e.g., < 0.001, < 0.01, < 0.05; two-tailed p values). If asymmetry 
is detected, we use trim and fill to explore the nature of “missing” studies (Duval 
& Tweedie, 2000). If the missing studies are in areas that are statistically nonsig-
nificant, the asymmetry may be caused by publication bias based on statistical sig-
nificance. Otherwise, the observed asymmetry is more likely to be caused by other 
factors.
In the present study, we examined publication bias for each data set using meta-
regression models for (a) cross-sectional effect sizes; (b) cross-sectional and lon-
gitudinal effect sizes for the effect of study design in all empirical studies; (c) all 
longitudinal effect sizes for the effect of time interval. With Egger’s test, we found 
no significant asymmetry for all empirical studies, β = 0.10, 95% CI = [− 0.05, 0.25], 
p > 0.05, or for longitudinal effect sizes, β = 0.16, 95% CI = [− 0.03, 0.34], p > 0.05. 
However, we found significant asymmetry for empirical studies with cross-sectional 
effect sizes, β =  − 0.46, 95% CI = [− 0.72, − 0.20], p < 0.001, which indicated that 
the correlations’ distribution was asymmetrical. With contours-enhanced funnel 
plots to examine possible factors leading to asymmetry (Appendix Figures S1, S2, 
and S3), we used the trim-and-fill algorithm to impute missing studies. Under the 
random-effects model, many missing studies were imputed in areas of statistical sig-
nificance (shaded areas) to the right of the mean effect size, yielding an increased 
adjusted effect size of 0.26 (95% CI [0.25, 0.27]; Appendix Figure S4). This indi-
cated, based on statistical significance that the asymmetry might be caused by fac-
tors other than publication bias, which was not surprising. Sirin (2005) has pointed 
out that most studies do not focus on the relationship between SES and academic 
achievement, but use it as a controlled variable. Thus, the significance level for the 
relation between SES and academic achievement did not necessarily contribute to 
publication bias in our study.

Results
Question 1: Overall Correlation between SES and Academic Achievement
For the empirical studies, there were 838 cross-sectional effect sizes; the overall cor-
relation between SES and academic achievement was r = 0.22, 95% CI [0.20, 0.23]. 
In addition, the average correlation for each subgroup of each moderator was signifi-

variable of study design into the regression model and found that the longitudinal

interval was coded as the amount of time between SES measurement and academic 
achievement measurement, with 749 longitudinal correlations from 123 studies, 
spanning from 4 months to 16 years. We calculated r for different longitudinal time

than r for cross-sectional effect sizes. Furthermore, we tested the time interval effect

achievement relation, β = 0.02, t = 2.32, p = 0.03, τ2 = 0.02: the longer the time inter-

longer significant, β = -0.004, t =  − 0.14, p = 0.89, τ2 = 0.02.

Method
Database Search
To test SES–academic achievement relations more adequately, we conducted the 
meta-analysis using three international large-scale assessments of math, science, and 
reading, including multiple waves of PISA (2000 to 2018), TIMSS (1995 to 2019), 
and PIRLS (2001 to 2016).
We calculated correlation r using the IEA’s International Database Analyzer Software 
(IDA, version 4.0), which enabled us to seamlessly take into account sampling informa-
tion and multiple imputed achievement scores to produce accurate statistical results for 
our meta-analysis. PISA provides sufficient data for each SES indicator, so to eliminate 
overlap for same indicators, we did the following: when a data set provided an overall 
indicator and sub-indicators, we calculated correlation r only for the overall indicator. 
For example, if the PISA database provided a father’s highest educational level, mother’s 
highest educational level, and parental highest educational level, we calculated the cor-
relation only between the parental highest educational level and academic achievement. 
Similarly, in some countries and areas, data from both parents and students are available 
for the same indicator (e.g., the number of books in a family), whereas data only from stu-
dents are available for all countries and areas. Therefore, we included only the data from 
students. Given our purpose to test moderation effects at the country level, we used coun-
tries and areas as a unit of analysis for SES–academic achievement correlations.
Finally, we obtained 1230 independent samples (PISA, 431; TIMSS, 600; PIRLS, 
199) and 9883 effect sizes for 105 countries and areas, representing 5,095,283 par-
ticipants (range 101 to 27,388). All of these studies were conducted from 1995 to 
2019.
Coding Procedure
We used the same coding procedure as in our meta-analysis of the empirical stud-
ies, with the exception that some variables were data specific (see Table 1).2 For 
example, in the international large-scale assessments, there were no variables for 
publication type or study design. Besides, to ensure the accuracy of coding, we cal-
culated r twice at an interval of 3 months. Cohen’s kappa for all the moderators was

Discussion
The overall SES–academic achievement correlation was r = 0.22, 95% CI [0.20, 
0.23] in empirical studies and r = 0.28, 95% CI [0.28, 0.29] in international large-

in the empirical studies. Furthermore, the present study revealed slightly more het-
erogeneity among empirical studies, τ2 = 0.02, than among international large-scale 
assessments, τ2 = 0.01.

Macro‑Level Moderators
One unique contribution of the present study is the investigation of moderators at 
a macro level based on both empirical studies and large assessments: years, GDP 
per capita, economic inequality, net enrollment ratio, and duration of compulsory 
education. Although substantial efforts have been made in recent decades by the 
global community to help improve equity in basic educational services across coun-
tries and education communities (UNESCO, 2020), we found that SES–academic 
achievement relations have strengthened since the 1990s, in line with other recent 
empirical studies (e.g., Byun & Kim, 2010; Chmielewski, 2019; Reardon, 2011). 
This may imply an increase in inequality in education globally.
We did not find that GDP per capita moderated SES–academic achievement rela-
tions, which is inconsistent with the Heyneman–Loxley effect (Heyneman & Loxley, 
1983). Heyneman and Loxley (1983) proposed that the relative effect of SES and 
school on academic achievement was related to a country’s national wealth—that in 
low-income countries, the effect of SES on academic achievement should be weaker 
than the effect of school, whereas in high-income countries, SES should play a more 
important role than school.
More recent findings have challenged the Heyneman–Loxley effect hypothesis. 
For example, Baker et  al. (2002) re-examined this hypothesis and suggested that 
SES predicted more academic achievement than did school variables across all sam-
pled countries, regardless of GDP per capita, and this finding stands even in coun-
tries in the Middle East and North Africa (Bouhlila, 2015). With regard to these 
new findings, Heyneman (2005) recently argued that earlier findings were correct 
but that the effect of SES on academic achievement may have strengthened with 
subsequent socioeconomic development in low-income countries; therefore, the rel-
ative effects of SES on academic achievement should be similar across countries, 
regardless of GDP per capita. Alternatively, Baker et al. (2002) argued that educa-
tion has expanded rapidly around the globe, reducing the extreme scarcity of school-
ing in low-income countries, so that GDP per capita may not be the most sensi-
tive predictor of students’ academic achievement globally. All this evidence, taken 
together, may highlight the important role of SES in predicting academic achieve-
ment, regardless of national wealth.
In addition, our findings suggest that income inequality has null effects on 
SES–academic achievement relations, consistent with prior empirical findings by 
Marks (2005). A possible explanation is that investment in education has yielded 
diminishing marginal returns (Chiu, 2010)—education has now reached a point 
where greater economic inequality produces disparities in parental investment 
(Duncan et al., 2017), but additional investment in education does not produce 
proportional increases in students’ academic achievement.
Net enrollment ratio and duration of compulsory education moderated 
SES–academic achievement relations in the international large-scale assess-
ments: the higher the mean net enrollment ratio and the longer the compulsory 
education, the stronger the SES–academic relations. These findings seem coun-
terintuitive, because low-SES families should benefit more from greater educa-
tional opportunities and longer compulsory education with education expansion.

1 3
However, it is also likely that inequities in academic achievement due to SES 
persist even with educational expansion. For example, Lucas (2001) proposed a 
model of effectively maintained inequality, in which student outcomes (e.g., aca-
demic achievement) have both quantitative and qualitative dimensions. Children 
in high-SES families are more likely receive qualitatively better education (e.g., 
private schools and tutors) to reproduce patterns of advantage, even if the quan-
tity of education is relatively equalized across high- and low-SES families (Lucas 
& Byrne, 2017). Thus, these findings may highlight the persistent issue of equal 
access to quality education. That is, the quantitative dimension of educational 
expansion (net enrollment ratio, duration of compulsory education) may not be 
an ideal indicator for educational policy regarding educational equity nowadays. 
Attention may be drawn to the fact that educational expansion should go hand in 
hand with increased educational quality (Lucas, 2001).
Although quality has also been highlighted by the global community, govern-
ments often guarantee quantitative expansion but compromise on educational 
quality in practice (UNESCO, 2016). Indeed, assuring educational quality while 
expanding education is a dual challenge, because large enrollments may increase 
the risk of lowering school quality (Medeiros et al., 2020) and may encourage 
students from high-SES background to seek a “better education” in various ways 
(e.g., with tutors, private schools; Alfonso, 2008). This inevitably creates educa-
tional inequity for disadvantaged students (Hannum & Buchmann, 2003).
We found that SES–academic achievement relations were not related to eco-
nomic development (i.e., GDP per capita, economic inequality) but were more 
related to educational development (i.e., educational enrollment, duration of 
compulsory education). These findings contribute to the debate between mod-
ernization theory and social reproduction theory. According to moderniza-
tion theory (e.g., DiPrete & Grusky, 1990; Marks, 2009, Marks and Mooi-Reci, 
2016), SES–academic achievement relations should have weakened over the past 
30 years and should weaken with higher GDP per capita, lower economic inequal-
ity, higher net enrollment ratios, and longer durations of compulsory education. 
In contrast, according to social reproduction theory (e.g., Casmar, 2004; Marks, 
2009), the relations between SES and academic achievement should maintain or 
strengthen regardless of the above-mentioned macro-level variables. Our find-
ings from both the empirical studies and the international large-scale assessments 
show the SES–academic achievement relations maintaining or strengthening, in 
support of social reproduction theory in general.
Micro‑level Moderators
With respect to study design, longitudinal effect sizes can help us test the authentic, 
accurate changes in SES–academic achievement relations as students develop (Sirin, 
2005) and help us understand the debate between the Matthew effect hypothesis and 
school compensation hypothesis. That is, is there a cumulative or a decreasing effect 
(compensated by schooling) of SES on academic outcomes from a developmental 
perspective? We found that with only study design in the meta-regression model,

the longitudinal SES–academic achievement relation was significantly stronger 
than cross-sectional ones, indicating a possible Matthew effect of SES on academic 
outcomes during development. However, after controlling for other moderators in 
the meta-regression, the moderation effect of study design was no longer signifi-
cant. We also did not find time interval to be a significant moderator of the relation 
between SES and academic achievement either, after controlling for other moder-
ators. Regarding grade level, based on the empirical studies, we did not find that 
grade level moderated SES–academic achievement relations; but based on the large 
assessments, the correlation for secondary education was stronger than that for pri-
mary education. Together, these findings may suggest that for individuals, SES–aca-
demic achievement relations may remain relatively stable or even strengthen dur-
ing primary and secondary education, highlighting the potential cumulative effect of 
SES with school age. Note that the school compensation hypothesis does not specify 
the type of schooling (e.g., private vs. public), and thus the effects of SES on edu-
cational quality may be one reason to explain the stable or even increasing effects 
of grade level on SES–academic relations. Future studies might look further into 
the schooling compensation hypothesis, taking into account the SES–schooling type 
interaction.
Methodological Moderators
Measurement of SES  Our results for the international large-scale assessments 
showed that the SES composite index had a stronger predictive effect on academic 
achievement than did single indicators, contrary to the findings of Kim et al. (2019). 
This might be because Kim et  al.’s meta-analysis focused mainly on developing 
countries, with the majority of studies in the sample published from 2000 to 2017. 
Our results with the control of GDP per capita and economic inequality suggest that 
in a more generalized setting, the SES composite score can be a better, more objec-
tive indicator to predict academic achievement.
Subject of Academic Achievement  We did not find a significant moderation effect 
for subject of academic achievement. One explanation may be that societies world-
wide have given equal attention to subjects in both humanities and science. Leach, 
for example, titled his 2013 lecture “STEM and the Humanities: A False Dichot-
omy.” Studying STEM can develop students’ technical skills for the labor market 
and economic development (Breiner et al., 2012; Gonzalez & Kuenzi, 2012). The 
humanities such as language provide tools for communication (Hines & Stern, 2019) 
and facilitate STEM learning (Peng et al., 2020). Thus, it is not surprising that SES 
(e.g., family investment) may have equal effects on the humanities and the sciences. 
That said, there is a debate about unbalanced performance between female and male 
students in the sciences and humanities as early as in elementary school (Pomer-
antz et al., 2002), so there may be an interaction between gender and academic sub-
ject on SES–academic achievement relations. However, we did not find a significant

interaction effect in the large assessments (see Appendix Table S4). Future studies 
should investigate this issue further.
Limitations
We note several limitations for the interpretation of our findings. First, our data from 
the empirical studies and the large assessments are correlational, suggesting caution 
with regard to causal inferences about SES–academic achievement relations. Well-
conducted quasi-experiments and experiments using random assignment of partici-
pants can provide stronger evidence of causality (Murnane & Willett, 2011). Other 
study designs such as regression discontinuity design (e.g., Barcellos et al., 2019) and 
cross-cohort comparison design (e.g., Castex & Dechter, 2014) may be a unique way of 
investigating causal effects of policy change. If the number of available studies is large, 
meta-analysis integrating such findings can play an important role in addressing the 
issue of causal inference regarding SES–academic achievement relations.
Second, our data were based mostly on students in school. Because no country’s 
net enrollment ratios reach 100%, especially in low-and middle-income countries, 
it is possible that some school-aged children are not enrolled in school (e.g., never 
schooled children, school dropouts, immigrants, refugee children). Although we 
used moderators related to educational expansion and indicated that SES–academic 
relations may be stronger with a more representative sample, our findings may not 
be generalizable to unschooled children. This might downwardly bias our meta-ana-
lytic estimates, particularly for low-and middle-income countries. Future research 
could consider sampling both school children and children out of school to compre-
hensively estimate SES–academic achievement relations.
Third, equity and quality are two primary indicators of assessment in educa-
tion, and high-performing educational systems always combine quality with equity 
(OECD, 2012). By focusing on SES–academic achievement relations, in the present 
study we have investigated the status quo of inequity in education. Future research 
could further investigate whether SES–academic achievement relations vary with 
the quality of education.
Implications for Practice
With the aforementioned limitations in mind, the results of this study provide up-
to-date knowledge for our understanding of SES–academic achievement relations 
and may have policy implications. First, SES–academic achievement relations have 
strengthened over time. This indicates that SES is closely associated with academic 
achievement, and educational equity remains a big challenge worldwide. Second, 
economic development variables (e.g., GDP per capita and economic inequality) did 
not affect SES–academic achievement relations, and educational expansion (e.g., 
duration of compulsory education, net enrollment ratio) had a limited impact on 
weakening SES–academic achievement relations. These findings suggest that coun-
tries should not overestimate the impact of educational expansion on educational

1 3
equity. Educational expansions may be limited mostly to low-quality schools or 
schools at risk as some research suggests (Sirin, 2005), and this may not alter the 
negative effects of low SES on academic outcomes on a macro level. Quality indi-
cators such as teacher–student ratios, trained teachers, the proportion of disadvan-
taged students, and supportive quality learning environments in educational expan-
sion should be taken into consideration for educational equity policies, not just the 
quantitative dimension of expansion (i.e., school enrollment, duration of compul-
sory education), because sustained high-quality education can minimize the nega-
tive impact of low SES on students’ achievement (Peng et al., 2019).
Finally, based on the international large-scale assessments, the correlation for 
composite SES index was stronger than that for single indicators. This might indi-
cate that in conducting cross-national comparative research on students’ academic 
achievement, composite SES index measures may need to be used as covariates or 
predictor variables. In addition, we found that subjects of academic achievement did 
not moderate SES–academic achievement relations. This may suggest that language 
support (e.g., language training) and STEM support at school should be equally 
important for reducing the impact of low SES on academic achievement.
