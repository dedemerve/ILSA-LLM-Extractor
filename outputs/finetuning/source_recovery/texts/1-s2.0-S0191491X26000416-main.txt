This study explores how student characteristics, the home environment, and the classroom environment relate to
the reading achievement of fourth-grade students in Türkiye. Grounded in Bronfenbrenner and Morris’s bioecological theory, the study conceptualizes reading achievement as an outcome that develops through proximal
processes occurring in home and classroom environments. It further posits that the effectiveness of these processes varies as a function of students’ individual characteristics, contextual conditions, and the time periods in
which they take place. Data were drawn from the 2021 cycle of PIRLS, collected during the COVID-19 pandemic.
Multilevel modeling was used to analyze hierarchically structured data, and the analyses were conducted using
the HLM 8 software package. Two-level models examined student and home contexts at the student level, while
the classroom level analysis focused on classroom/teacher-related variables. At the student level, gender, home
socioeconomic status, early literacy activities, reading confidence, students’ enjoyment of reading, digital selfefficacy, classroom engagement, and experiences of bullying were significantly associated with reading
achievement. At the classroom level, teacher experience, the availability of digital devices, language difficulties,
and the provision of reading materials were significant predictors. Factors such as the frequency of speaking the
test language at home, early literacy tasks, parents' interest in reading, and preschool attendance were not
significantly associated with reading achievement. These findings offer practical implications for schools and
education policymakers in addressing individual and contextual factors that enhance reading achievement.

According to PIRLS 2021 results, Türkiye scored 496 points, ranking
39th among 57 countries. Compared with 2001, Türkiye’s reading score
increased by 47 points; however, similar to 2001, it remained below the
international average in 2021. Additionally, the number of students
with advanced, high, and intermediate proficiency levels has increased
since 2001. Nevertheless, when Turkish students’ proficiency levels
were compared to international benchmarks, their percentages
remained below the global average across all levels.
Türkiye has a highly centralized education system in which decisionmaking authority is concentrated at the national level, while the autonomy of local and school-level actors remains limited. In this context,
pronounced socioeconomic disparities across regions and uneven access
to instructional and digital resources make Türkiye a distinctive case for
PIRLS-based multilevel research (Özçelik et al., 2022). Türkiye has
participated in PIRLS only twice (2001 and 2021), which has constrained the accumulation of national evidence on how home and
classroom conditions jointly shape fourth-grade students’ reading
achievement over time. Moreover, the post-pandemic educational
landscape has increased the relevance of digital competence and access
to technological infrastructure as potential sources of inequality. To
address these gaps, the present study adopts a theory-driven two-level
model grounded in Bronfenbrenner and Morris’s (2006) bioecological
theory and examines how student and home characteristics relate to
reading achievement, while also accounting for classroom-level conditions such as teacher qualifications, instructional resources, and access
to digital tools in a context marked by persistent socioeconomic and
digital inequalities. Thus, the study provides Türkiye-specific evidence
by identifying the most robust student/home predictors and the classroom features most strongly associated with reading achievement.
Within this framework, development is driven primarily by proximal
processes—recurring, reciprocal, and increasingly complex interactions
between an active learner and the persons, objects, and symbols in the
immediate environment. Importantly, the effectiveness of these processes is not uniform; rather, their strength and developmental impact
vary as a joint function of person characteristics, contextual conditions,
and the time periods in which they occur. In line with this perspective,
reading achievement in the present study is conceptualized as a developmental outcome shaped by the combined contributions of Process,
Person, Context, and Time (PPCT).
Within the PPCT model, the person component was operationalized
through student characteristics that may facilitate or constrain reading
development. Gender was treated as a demand characteristic, whereas
reading confidence, enjoyment of reading, and digital self-efficacy were
conceptualized as force characteristics reflecting motivational dispositions that can initiate and sustain literacy-related engagement
(Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006; Zimmerman & Kitsantas, 2014). In
addition, early literacy tasks and the frequency of speaking the test
language at home were included as resource characteristics, representing foundational skills and linguistic supports that may strengthen students’ capacity to benefit from literacy opportunities.
The process component represents developmentally meaningful interactions that occur on a regular basis and endure over time. In this
study, proximal processes were captured through students’ engagement
in reading lessons at school and early literacy activities in the home.
These indicators reflect sustained literacy-related experiences that support reading development across home and classroom settings, consistent with sociocultural perspectives emphasizing learning through
guided participation and interaction (Vygotsky, 1978).
The context component captures the nested environments in which

development unfolds. In this study, contextual influences were primarily
examined within the home and classroom microsystems. At home, socioeconomic status (SES) and parents’ interest in reading were used to
represent both structural resources and the involvement of significant
others in literacy development (Epstein et al., 2018). At the classroom
level, the microsystem was operationalized through teacher characteristics (teaching experience and formal education level) and instructional
conditions that shape opportunities for interaction with literacy-related
objects and tools (Nilsen & Gustafsson, 2016). Specifically, the availability of digital devices, classroom libraries, providing reading materials, and assessment practices using digital devices were treated as
classroom features that may support students’ engagement with texts
and instructional activities (Sanders & Scanlon, 2021). In addition,
teacher practices encouraging students to challenge ideas and develop
deeper understanding were included as indicators of a cognitively
activating instructional climate, which may strengthen students’ proximal processes by fostering more active participation during reading
instruction. Conversely, adverse classroom conditions—such as
bullying, instruction constrained by student readiness, and language
difficulties—were considered disruptive contextual forces that may
weaken proximal processes and hinder achievement (Bronfenbrenner &
Morris, 2006).
Finally, the time component emphasizes that development unfolds
across temporal dimensions. Although PIRLS 2021 does not include
direct indicators of the COVID-19 pandemic, the data were collected in
the post–COVID-19 period, which constitutes a macrotime condition
that may have altered learning environments and increased the relevance of digital learning factors (Bronfenbrenner & Evans, 2000;
Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006). Preschool attendance was included as
a temporal indicator reflecting earlier exposure to structured learning
contexts, continuity of learning experiences, and accumulated developmental opportunities over time.
Taken together, the bioecological framework enables a multilevel
analysis of both individual and contextual factors that relate to reading
achievement. Accordingly, the main goal of this study is to determine
the extent to which these variables are associated with reading
achievement and to assess the strength of these relationships (Neirouz,
2020). The following sections review the existing literature on how
home, student, and classroom factors show a significant association with
reading achievement.
1.1. The role of home-related factors in reading achievement
The home environment plays an essential role in shaping children's
early reading habits. Prominent international assessments, such as
PIRLS, PISA, and PIAAC, highlight several key contributors to reading
achievement, including socioeconomic status (SES) and the quality of
early parent-child interactions. Among these factors, SES emerges as a
particularly significant determinant (Hemmerechts et al., 2017; Leino
et al., 2022; Marˆ
oco, 2021; Martin et al., 2021; Solheim & Lundetræ,
2016; Strand & Schwippert, 2019; Van Damme et al., 2010).
It has been demonstrated that families with a higher SES have been
shown to engage more actively in early literacy practices and to ensure
access to educational resources, offering children a substantial advantage in their literacy development (Hemmerechts,2021; Hemmerechts
et al., 2017; Stancel-Piatak et al., 2013). However, the impact of these
advantages varies across countries. For example, using PIRLS 2016 data
from 48 countries, Marˆ
oco (2021) showed that while home resources
strongly influence reading achievement in Singapore, their impact is
weaker in high-performing systems such as Russia and Hong Kong,
suggesting that the effects of SES may depend on broader educational
and cultural contexts.
In addition to socioeconomic status, parental reading habits, language spoken at home, and access to diverse reading materials play
critical roles in shaping students' motivation and achievement. It is
evident that these factors, when considered collectively, foster a

Preschool attendance plays a critical role in shaping students'
reading achievements, although its effects are often mediated by socioeconomic status and the home literacy environment. Studies using
PIRLS data reveal mixed findings. For example, Hogrebe and Strietholt
(2016) reported that nonattendance in preschool does not significantly
impact reading literacy at the end of primary school for children from
similar socioeconomic backgrounds, except in certain countries.
Conversely, early literacy activities at home, such as storytelling and
reading together, significantly increase later reading achievement,
highlighting the importance of parental involvement during preschool
years (Geske & Ozola, 2020; Horvat, 2016).
Student bullying is another critical factor that negatively affects
students’ reading achievements. Bullying has been found to induce
stress, fear, and anxiety as these factors undermine students’ selfefficacy in reading and hinder their overall learning processes (Chen
et al., 2021). Rutkowski and Rutkowski (2016) emphasized that bullying
is a universal issue that adversely affects students’ performance
regardless of a country’s overall achievement level. Furthermore,
meta-analytic studies reveal that the effects of bullying vary depending
on factors such as students’ age, social context, and subject type. For
example, younger students often experience more severe impacts on
academic performance due to bullying, highlighting the vulnerability of
early learners (Özyildirim & Karada˘
g, 2024).
1.3. The role of the classroom environment in reading achievement
The classroom environment is a pivotal factor in the development of
reading skills, as it is the primary setting for instructional activities
(Mullis & Martin, 2019). Several key classroom-related factors include
teacher preparation (e.g., experience, formal education), the use of
technology (e.g., access to digital devices, digital assessments, digital
literacy), classroom reading practices (e.g., participation in reading
lessons, availability of classroom libraries, addressing language difficulties), and motivational strategies employed by teachers. One of the
most significant challenges for educators is maintaining students’
engagement with the subject matter, a key indicator of curriculum
quality (Leino et al., 2022; Mullis & Martin, 2019; Nilsen & Gustafsson,
2016). The extent to which pupils are engaged in their learning reflects
the effectiveness of a teacher's delivery of the curriculum (Martin et al.,
2013; Scherer & Gustafsson, 2015).
Teacher instructional practices that support reading development
are of particular importance. An analysis of PIRLS 2016 data reveals a
multifaceted relationship between teaching techniques and student
reading achievement (Marˆ
oco, 2021; Shiel & Eivers, 2009; Yan & Cai,
ˆ
2022). Maroco
(2021) highlights a lack of consensus on universally
effective practices for student-centered instruction. PIRLS 2021 further
investigates how teachers’ use of motivational strategies and engaging
materials influences students’ performance, although there remains
limited research (e.g., De Naeghel & Van Keer, 2013) on methods to
promote reading motivation in the classroom.
Teacher quality, often measured by education level and years of
experience, significantly impacts reading achievement (Goe, 2007;
Mohammadpour & Abdul Ghafar, 2014). Experienced teachers excel in
time management, classroom control, and diversifying educational
strategies (Cheung et al., 2009; Geske et al., 2021; Leino et al., 2022).
Additionally, increased teaching hours positively correlate with students’ reading achievements (Calero & Escardíbul, 2019; Karimi, 2012).
However, studies from Nordic countries suggest a weak relationship
between teacher preparation, curriculum quality, and student outcomes
(Leino et al., 2022).

construct understanding, challenge opinions, and engage with diverse
texts is essential to fostering reading literacy. Research informed by
PIRLS methodologies highlights the value of self-involving reading tasks

1.2. The role of student-related factors in reading achievement
Individual differences among students have a notable association
with reading achievement. Factors such as gender, attitudes toward
reading (Tse et al., 2013), digital skills (Zhang et al., 2024), participation in preschool (Normandeau & Guay, 1998), and experiences with
student bullying (Özyildirim & Karada˘
g, 2024) have all been identified
as significant contributors to reading achievement. For example, digital
skills significantly enhance students' ability to engage with reading
materials in online environments (Zhang et al., 2024), whereas early
participation in preschool has long-term benefits for literacy development (Normandeau & Guay, 1998). Understanding these factors is
essential for designing targeted interventions to improve reading literacy across diverse student populations.
Among these individual differences, gender has consistently
emerged as a significant factor. A substantial body of research has
indicated that girls tend to outperform boys in reading literacy
(Hofmeyr, 2022; Oberleiter et al., 2023; Schulz-Heidorf & Støle, 2018).
This gender difference is often linked to girls' exposure to more supportive literacy environments and their development of more positive
attitudes toward reading (Huang & Chen, 2019; Trong & Kennedy,
2007). Addressing these disparities is crucial for creating equitable
educational opportunities for all students.
Another variable frequently highlighted in the literature as influencing reading achievement is students’ attitudes. These attitudes,
encompassing feelings about reading and confidence in learning, are
crucial for developing motivation and interest in reading (Marˆ
oco, 2021;
Yang et al., 2018). Positive emotions toward reading, known as intrinsic
motivation and self-efficacy, or a belief in one’s ability to perform
reading tasks, are key predictors of achievement. Building on these
concepts, PIRLS 2021 assesses students’ attitudes through scales such as
'Students Like Reading', 'Students Confident in Reading', and 'Digital
Self-Efficacy'. Findings from ePIRLS 2016 expanded the focus to digital
reading skills, highlighting a positive correlation between computer use
and achievement in online informational reading tasks (Mullis, Martin &
Liu, 2019). To further explore this topic, PIRLS 2021 introduced the
Digital Self-Efficacy Scale, which measures students’ confidence in the

3.2. Sampling design and weighting
Differences in school or student participation rates, or the exclusion
of certain subgroups from the assessment, can compromise the representativeness of the sample (Addey et al., 2017). To address this issue,
the current study used sampling weights at both the student and classroom levels within multilevel analyses to ensure the sample truly represents the target population. According to PIRLS methodology, these
weights consider the probability of selection and participation for each
level, thereby reducing bias and improving the accuracy of population
estimates. In the study, student-level data were weighted using
TOTWGT (Total Student Weight), which integrates selection probabilities from the school, classroom, and student levels. Classroom-level data
were weighted with TCHWGT (Teacher Weight) as recommended for
teacher-level data analyses (Almaskut et al., 2023; Fishbein et al., 2024).
In addition to final sampling weights, PIRLS provides replicate
weights designed for design-based variance estimation (e.g., jackknife
repeated replication). Because the present study employed a modelbased multilevel framework implemented using HLM, where replicate
weight procedures are not directly supported, replicate weights were
therefore not incorporated into the multilevel estimations. Instead,
sampling weights were applied at both levels, and standard errors were
estimated within the plausible value framework using robust estimation
procedures. This approach is consistent with established practices in
multilevel analyses of PIRLS data (e.g., Leino et al., 2022; Nilsen &
Gustafsson, 2016).

2. Aim
The aim of this study is to investigate how student-, home-, and
classroom-level factors contribute to the reading achievement of fourthgrade students in Türkiye, using data from PIRLS 2021. Guided by
Bronfenbrenner and Morris’s bioecological theory, the study employs a
multilevel modeling approach to account for the nested structure of
students within classrooms, with a particular focus on the combined
effects of individual characteristics and classroom-level instructional
factors. Specifically, the study seeks to determine the extent to which
these variables explain variance in reading performance. To this end, the
following research questions were addressed:
1. To what extent do variables related to the student and home contexts
influence the reading achievement of fourth-grade students in PIRLS
2021?
2. To what extent do variables related to the classroom context influence the reading achievement of fourth-grade students in PIRLS
2021, after controlling for student and home context variables?

3. Methods and data

The dependent variables in this study are the five plausible values
representing students' PIRLS 2021 reading achievements. Plausible
values, a key component of the PIRLS scaling strategy, were derived via
multiple imputation methods to estimate reading competence scores for
all students, as each student completed only a subset of the assessment
items (Foy & Yin, 2017).
In order to capture the broader learning context, PIRLS 2021 gathered information through surveys targeting students, parents, school
principals, and teachers. This study conducted a secondary analysis
using publicly available data from these surveys, focusing on home,
student, and teacher factors.
Several background variables used in the analyses (e.g., home socioeconomic status, early literacy activities, and digital self-efficacy)
were derived from context scales pre-constructed and provided by the
IEA. These scales were constructed from multiple questionnaire items
using Item Response Theory (IRT) scaling procedures and are reported

This study employed a secondary data analysis of the PIRLS 2021
international assessment and adopted a correlational research design. It
employed a two-level multilevel modeling approach grounded in
Bronfenbrenner and Morris’s bioecological theory. The analysis
considered student and home-related variables at Level 1 and teacher
and classroom-related variables at Level 2, reflecting the nested structure of students within classrooms. This approach made it possible to
simultaneously examine the individual and contextual factors influencing reading achievement. The data were accessed through the official
PIRLS database (https://pirls2021.org/data). All multilevel analyses
were conducted using HLM 8 software.

The PIRLS assessment uses a two-stage random sample design. In the
first stage, a sample of schools is selected, and in the second stage, one or
more intact classes are chosen from those schools. Because of the nested
nature of the data (classes within schools and students within their
respective classes), a multilevel modeling approach was used. This study
employed two-level modeling, with the first level representing students
and the second representing classrooms. Students in the same class are
expected to exhibit similarities across various variables. In such cases, it
is difficult to claim that observations are independent of one another.
The assumption of independence is a key requirement in standard statistical tests such as linear regression. If this assumption is violated, the
estimates of standard errors in conventional statistical tests will be too
small, resulting in many incorrectly significant results (Hox, 2010).
Multilevel modeling addresses this issue by relaxing the assumption of
independence.
In line with the hierarchical structure of the dataset, classroom-level
analysis was deemed appropriate. While most Turkish schools in PIRLS
2021 included only one sampled class, some had multiple, justifying the
use of classroom (rather than school) as the second level. Each student
was matched with a single teacher using class identifiers (IDCLASS),
ensuring a clear and consistent hierarchical relationship. As Gustafsson
and Nilsen (2016) emphasize, modeling at the classroom level helps
avoid aggregation bias and more accurately captures instructional
differences.

5. Discussion
This study examined the influence of student, home, and classroomrelated factors on fourth-grade reading achievement in Türkiye using
PIRLS 2021 data. Consistent with Bronfenbrenner and Morris’s bioecological theory, the results suggest that reading performance is shaped
by the dynamic interplay of individual characteristics and contextual
influences. Multilevel analysis revealed that student-level variables
explained a larger proportion of the variance in reading achievement
than classroom-level variables (Aslano˘
glu & Kutlu, 2007; Lopes et al.,
2022).
Among the student-level predictors, reading self-confidence emerged
as the most significant positive predictor, while bullying was the
strongest negative predictor. These findings underscore the critical role
of socio-emotional dynamics in literacy development. In this sense,
bullying may undermine students’ sense of emotional safety and reduce
their willingness to participate in classroom literacy activities, thereby
limiting the learning opportunities that classroom instruction is intended to provide. In addition, gender, digital self-efficacy, classroom
engagement, and socioeconomic status were found to significantly influence reading achievement, reflecting both personal initiative and
environmental support structures. Interestingly, a statistically significant negative relationship was indicated between the enjoyment of
reading and reading achievement.
From a PPCT perspective, these student-level characteristics may
shape how students participate in literacy-related experiences and how
much they benefit from them, both at home and in the classroom.
At the classroom level, teacher experience, the availability of digital
devices, and language difficulties indicated notable association with
reading achievement. In particular, greater access to digital devices was
associated with higher student performance, underscoring the importance of technological resources in classroom environments. Interestingly, the provision of reading materials aligned with students’ interests
was negatively associated with achievement, suggesting a possible
mismatch between student preferences and instructional goals.

materials are not suitable, student motivation and reading achievement
may decrease. A lack of material diversity and a mismatch with students'
developmental levels may hinder skill development. This is consistent
with Bozkuş (2025), who found that materials aligned with students’
interests were negatively associated with reading achievement in
Türkiye. Additionally, textbook analyses by Açıkel and G¨
ok (2024)
indicate that current Turkish reading materials often lack cognitive
depth and fail to support higher-level comprehension, especially when
student preferences are prioritized without pedagogical alignment.
Teachers should better understand students' needs and preferences,
offering diverse reading options and guidance to improve overall performance. Bruggink et al. (2022), drawing on PIRLS 2016 and examples
from Chile, Chinese Taipei, England, Georgia, and Spain, recommend
providing an educational environment that encourages equal participation. A plethora of research has shown that the "learning through
playing and doing" strategy is both effective and productive. Accordingly, teachers should consider students' diverse abilities and preferences and provide personalized education.

Second, although the present study employed multilevel modeling to
account for student- and classroom-level dynamics, the analyses treated
these factors additively and did not examine potential cross-level interactions. However, from a bioecological perspective, home and
classroom environments are not independent influences; rather, they
operate as interconnected microsystems whose joint contribution to
reading achievement unfolds through reciprocal processes over time.
Accordingly, future research should explicitly test interaction terms to
examine whether supportive classroom conditions can mitigate disadvantages in the home environment and whether student characteristics
strengthen the association between instructional practices, classroom
resources, and reading achievement.
Third, the time component of the bioecological framework was
covered only to a limited degree in the present study. Although preschool attendance duration was used as a temporal indicator, it reflects
students’ early learning experiences only in a rather narrow way. In
addition, while it is recognized that PIRLS 2021 was implemented in a
post–COVID-19 context, the study could not examine how pandemicrelated shifts in learning conditions may have shaped reading achievement more directly. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted with
this limitation in mind.
Fourth, the study relied on cross-sectional data, which limits the
ability to establish causal claims. Therefore, the observed relationships
should be understood as correlational.
Fifth, in this study, missing data were handled by the listwise deletion approach. While multiple imputation methods provide an alternative for large-scale evaluation data, when sampling weights, hierarchical
structure, and plausible values are used simultaneously, variance and
standard error estimates may be biased if proper modeling and integration are not performed. Therefore, it is recommended to retest the
findings with design-weighted and multilevel loss data approaches in
future studies.
Finally, while some classroom-level variables such as digital access
or students’ readiness to learn may reflect broader school conditions,
these were reported by individual teachers and analyzed at the classroom level. Given that most Turkish schools in PIRLS 2021 were represented by a single sampled class and teacher, school-level (Level 3)
variables assessed via the principal questionnaire were not included.
Therefore, the analysis was limited to two levels. Future research could
integrate principal-reported school data and adopt a three-level hierarchical modeling approach to better account for school-wide influences
on reading achievement.
7. Conclusion
This study examined how various factors related to students, their
homes, and their classrooms influence the reading achievement of
fourth-grade students in Türkiye. The findings show that reading success
is influenced not only by personal traits, but also by the different environments and systems surrounding the student.
Among student-level variables, reading confidence, gender, digital
self-efficacy, and experiences of bullying emerged as significant predictors of reading achievement. Notably, bullying had the most substantial negative association with reading performance, highlighting the
critical need to promote emotionally safe and inclusive school
environments.
At the contextual level, home socioeconomic status and early literacy
experiences were linked to student success, while classroom-level factors such as teaching experience, access to digital tools, and language
difficulties also played vital roles. Notably, the availability of digital
devices was the strongest positive predictor at the classroom level,
underscoring the importance of equitable digital infrastructure, particularly in post-pandemic educational contexts.

materials was associated with lower reading achievement. This finding
suggests that providing materials alone is not sufficient; their

6. Limitations
This study, while offering valuable insights into the multilevel determinants of reading achievement among Turkish fourth-grade students, has several limitations. First, the data were derived from PIRLS
2021, an international large-scale assessment with standardized instruments. This meant that the analysis could only look at the factors
included in the PIRLS framework, which restricted the ability to explore
other psychological, teaching, or cultural factors that might affect
reading performance. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the data
limits the ability to draw causal inferences about the relationships
identified. Future research could benefit from longitudinal studies that
incorporate a broader range of variables to better understand the complexities influencing reading achievement.
