a b s t r a c t 
The integration of AI in archaeology poses several risks due to the oversimpliﬁcation of complex archae- 
ological data for computational ease. This reductionist approach fosters a deterministic view, treating 
provisional classiﬁcations as deﬁnitive truths and inﬂuencing subsequent interpretations. The reliance on 
legacy data and Big Data for AI training risks perpetuating outdated ideas and frameworks. As AI expands 
from automating tasks to interpreting and creating reconstructions, archaeologists must adopt a critical 
approach to avoid biased and harmful outputs. The deterministic view of AI hinders informed debate. 
Archaeologists should engage in discussions that address the classiﬁcatory, and ethical aspects as well 
as the materiality of AI. The accumulation of data in AI mimics storytelling but lacks the interpretative 
depth needed to understand historical human perspectives. Developing theories and narrative practices 
is essential to making archaeological data meaningful. The shift from a representational to a co-creative 
view of data is necessary to understand its re-use and the power dynamics involved. Finally, to normalise 
AI in archaeology, a critical and sceptical approach is needed to integrate AI into the real world and 
understand its implications and ethical considerations.

1. Introduction 
After conducting several studies on the application of Artiﬁ- 
cial Intelligence (AI) in Archaeology based on the experience of 
the ArchAIDE Project [ 1 , 2 ], I would like to investigate the broader 
role of AI within the discipline, starting from the concept of post- 
digitality. My focus will be on the computational landscape shaped 
by the interplay between digital and material apparatuses and the 
practices of training data classiﬁcation. This exploration will lead to 
an analysis of the ethical dimensions of AI, with particular atten- 
tion to its oracular [ 3 ] and “enchanted determinism" [ 4 ] aspects, 
delving into their wider implications for the ﬁeld of archaeology. 
This paper can also be seen as a starting point for the COST Action 
MAIA (Managing Artiﬁcial Intelligence in Archaeology, CA 23141), 
which commenced on September 20th, 2024 [ 5 ]. Therefore, I will 
begin by explaining key concepts, such as the general functioning 
of AI, to ensure that my point of view is understood. 
1.1. Post digital 
As expounded by Cascone [ 6 ], the concept of post-digital de- 
scribes a scenario in which, after a period of disruption, the radi- 
cal changes brought about by digitalisation have now become nor- 
malised. This means that once-disruptive technologies are now

seamlessly integrated into daily life, and the absence of digital 
technology is more noticeable than its presence. In this normalised 
state, digital technologies are deeply embedded in societal struc- 
tures and practices, leading to a reduced perception of technolog- 
ical innovation as inherently disruptive. The post-digital paradigm 
also critically views techno-positivism, rejecting a simplistic narra- 
tive of one-way technological progress. Instead, it recognises that 
technological development has both positive and negative aspects. 
This critical perspective encourages a reconsideration of the social 
and cultural impacts of technology, promoting a more nuanced un- 
derstanding of its effects. Rather than seeing analogue and digital 
as opposites, the post-digital perspective advocates for their inte- 
grated and hybridised coexistence. Today, in Archaeology, technolo- 
gies like GIS, 3D surface models, and spatial analysis are essential 
to archaeological practice and are no longer seen as disruptive. This 
approach fosters an environment that encourages creative synthe- 
sis and experimentation to generate new forms of expression and 
innovation [ 7 ]. Furthermore, integrating digital and conventional 
practices gives rise to new methods that defy easy categorisation. 
Looking to the more conventional digital methods, the understand- 
ing of technology, recognising the interplay between digitisation, 
societal norms, and cultural practices, the studies on the agency of 
instruments in mediating archaeological endeavours [ 2 , 8 ], and the 
availability of quantitative and qualitative complex data ([ 9–11 ], p. 
6), archaeology is fully post-digital. Nevertheless, what happens if 
we consider Artiﬁcial Intelligence?

2. Artiﬁcial Intelligence 
Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI) is a term coined in 1955 by Mc- 
Carthy [ 12 ] to denote an interdisciplinary ﬁeld that originated with 
the theories of Alan Turing [ 13 ]. Over 70 years, AI has under- 
gone cycles of expansion, such as the current one, characterised 
by great conﬁdence in achieving goals and signiﬁcant funding, as 
well as cycles of decline, known as AI winters, marked by scep- 
ticism due to disappointing technological developments and re- 
duced funding. Generally, AI involves the study and development 
of systems and technologies capable of emulating [ 14 ] speciﬁc hu- 
man abilities, such as reasoning, perception, learning, natural lan- 
guage processing, and pattern recognition, to automate complex 
tasks and make autonomous decisions. These processes generally 
require large amounts of data (Big Data), signiﬁcant computational 
resources, and specialised hardware such as graphics processing 
units (GPUs) and tensor processing units (TPUs). AI encompasses 
various areas of research and application, including Machine Learn- 
ing (ML), which is the broadest ﬁeld, Artiﬁcial Neural Networks 
(ANNs), Deep Learning (DL), and Generative AI (GenAI), which are 
subsets ( Fig. 1 ). 
2.1. Machine learning 
ML involves algorithms that recognise patterns in data and 
make predictions [ 15 ]. In ML, collected data is pre-processed 
(cleaned and normalised) to bring all features (the variables de- 
scribing the entities in the dataset) to a common scale. Then, the 
most suitable features are selected to describe the phenomenon 
under examination. This selection reduces model complexity, im- 
proves performance, and decreases variance. Low variance fails to 
capture all characteristics in the training data (underﬁtting), while 
high variance does not generalise correctly to new data, ﬁtting too 
closely to the training data due to its excessive speciﬁcity (over- 
ﬁtting). The most common ML models are linear regression, used 
to predict a continuous variable based on one or more indepen- 
dent variables [ 16 ]; logistic regression, which predicts the prob- 
ability that an instance belongs to a certain class, typically a bi- 
nary classiﬁcation [ 17 ]; Random Forest, based on decision trees 
trained on random data samples, used for classiﬁcation and regres- 
sion [ 18 ]; and Support Vector Machine (SVM), which creates one or

more hyperplanes in the feature space to separate data into classes 
[ 19 ]. These are supervised learning models, and their selection de- 
pends on the type and quantity of data and the complexity of the 
phenomenon to be analysed [ 20 ]. Training involves a dataset that 
teaches the model to relate input data to their respective output la- 
bels through an optimisation process to minimise a cost function. 
After training, the model is evaluated using an independent test 
dataset to estimate its performance on unknown data and assess 
its ability to generalise. If it does not generalise well, it can be re- 
ﬁned by adjusting parameters, adding more features, or using tech- 
niques that combine predictions from multiple models (ensemble 
learning). 
2.2. From artiﬁcial neural networks (ANNs) to deep learning (DL) 
ANNs are systems of algorithms inspired by studies on the 
structure of the human brain developed in the 1940s by McCulloch 
and Pitts ([ 3 ], p. 81) and the concept of the perceptron introduced 
by Rosenblatt [ 21 ] and revisited by Minsky and Papert [ 22 ]. ANNs 
consist of basic computational units called artiﬁcial neurons, char- 
acterised by activation functions, connected by weighted connec- 
tions, and organised into an input layer, at least one intermediate 
or hidden layer that processes data, and an output layer that pro- 
duces the result. Artiﬁcial neurons can accept any real input value, 
not just binary values, assign different weights to input values and 
deﬁne a threshold value that determines (or does not) their acti- 
vation. The threshold value can be ﬁxed or variable based on in- 
put received from other neurons. The variation of the threshold 
value and the weights of the various inputs allows the neuron to 
learn. With an arbitrarily deﬁned threshold value, a deterministic 
model is created. A probabilistic model is obtained by inserting 
a probabilistic activation function in which the neuron activates 
when the activation probability exceeds 50% ([ 3 ], p. 83). This in- 
troduces non-linearity into the network and improves the training 
process, enabling it to learn complex relationships. Training can be 
supervised with labelled data, unsupervised with unlabelled data, 
semi-supervised with mixed data, or reinforcement-based, relying 
on rewards and penalties. An error measure between predicted 
and desired output is calculated during supervised training. This 
error is then backpropagated through the network using a back- 
propagation algorithm, which calculates error gradients concern- 
ing the weights of the neural network and updates the weights to 
reduce the error gradually [ 23 ]. This process is iterated, dynami- 
cally modifying the weights within the network through reinforce- 
ment or weakening feedback until the network converges on a so- 
lution that generalises well to unknown data. The feedback can 
be entirely automatic, as in generative networks, or involve hu- 
man intervention (human-in-the-loop). DL models are ANNs that 
do not require manual feature engineering, are particularly suit- 
able when dealing with complex data not easily represented by ML 
algorithms, and can automatically learn hierarchical data represen- 
tations. In practice, the initial layers manage to extract low-level 
features, such as edges, lines, and colours of images, while deeper 
layers combine these features to form increasingly complex, high- 
level representations like objects or concepts [ 24 ]. Given the sys- 
tem’s autonomy in modifying weights and values, the hidden lay- 
ers’ level is particularly opaque and often referred to as a black 
box, which does not allow an understanding of the states of in- 
dividual neurons [ 25 ]. Overﬁtting is common in DL. To overcome 
this, techniques such as reducing the number of model parame- 
ters [ 26 ], adding dropout (randomly dropping some nodes during 
training) [ 27 ], cross-validation (repeating training and evaluation 
on different dataset portions) [ 28 ], early stopping (halting training 
when the validation error starts increasing) [ 29 ], data augmenta- 
tion (generating synthetic training data by manipulating existing 
data to create realistic variations of the original samples) [ 30 ], and

transfer learning [ 31 ] are used. The latter originates from a model 
that is pre-trained on a large, general dataset (such as ImageNet) 
and then ﬁne-tunes it on a speciﬁc dataset, taking advance of pre- 
viously acquired knowledge. These last two techniques were not 
speciﬁcally conceived to overcome overﬁtting but are often used 
for this purpose. Various types of deep neural networks exist, such 
as deep neural networks (DNNs), which are composed of multiple 
hidden layers [ 32 ]. Feedforward neural networks (FNNs) are used 
for classiﬁcation and regression, with information ﬂowing in one 
direction from the input to the output layer without loops [ 33 ]. 
Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) has cyclic feedback, allowing for 
sequential data processing and long-term memory retention, thus 
applied to time series and text generation [ 34 ]. Long Short-Term 
Memory (LSTM) networks are RNNs that retain and forget infor- 
mation for longer periods [ 35 ], while Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) 
networks are RNNs with a simpler architecture and fewer param- 
eters than LSTMs [ 36 ]. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) use 
convolutional layers that, by applying a ﬁlter (kernel) on small 
overlapping regions of the image, extract features from the input, 
followed by pooling layers to reduce the spatial dimension (width 
and height) of the output and the computational complexity of the 
model, thus reducing the risk of overﬁtting. CNNs are used for im- 
age analysis and pattern recognition within images [ 37 ]. Generative 
Adversarial Networks (GAN) comprise two competing networks: 
the generator produces synthetic data while the discriminator en- 
deavours to differentiate between real and synthetic data, with the 
goal of training the generator to generate increasingly realistic data 
[ 38 ]. Autoencoders allow dimensionality reduction. They include 
an encoder that converts the input into a lower-dimensional la- 
tent space and a decoder that reconstructs the input from the la- 
tent embedding [ 39 , 40 ]. Transformer neural networks utilise atten- 
tion mechanisms to process sequences of tokens and have revolu- 
tionised the ﬁeld of Natural Language Processing (NLP) [ 41 ]. 
2.3. Generative AI 
GenAI is used to generate texts, images, and videos. In textual 
models, the process starts with tokenising a text corpus, divid- 
ing it into small units (tokens) corresponding to individual words, 
morphemes, n-grams (groups of n characters), or single characters. 
Through unsupervised learning, the network adjusts the values as- 
sociated with the tokens and the weights of internal connections 
to predict the next token [ 42 ]. This operation generates the Large 
Language Model (LLM), known for these systems, a statistical- 
probabilistic correlation model among the tokens, associating to- 
kens with an abstract multidimensional space that numerically ex- 
presses (embedding) the usage contexts and relationships of the 
single token within the corpus (vectorisation) [ 43 ]. Often, after a 
phase of unsupervised learning, the model is reﬁned through su- 
pervised or reinforcement learning [ 44 ] or using self-supervised 
learning [ 45 ] or GANs. In the generative phase, the system gen- 
erates responses using a sequence-to-sequence mechanism [ 46 ], 
where a sequence of output symbols is obtained from a series of 
input symbols. Here, the transformer intervenes [ 47 ], not examin- 
ing tokens sequentially but considering the context through an at- 
tention mechanism [ 48 ] that weights the token values, considering 
the values of other tokens in the context and adding other val- 
ues (Query, Value, and Key) to the embedding vector. This method 
better disambiguates polysemous words, improves semantic mem- 
ory, and enhances performance with long inputs ([ 3 ], pp. 89–92). 
In practice, an encoder vectorises the input text, while a decoder 
generates the output text from the encoder’s representation. Ulti- 
mately, texts are produced predictively. Encoders and decoders can 
be paired or not. 
The generation of images, known as Text-to-Image, Text-Guided 
Diffusion Models [ 49 ], or Conditional Image Generation [ 50 ], works 
by assigning numerical values to each pixel and creating math- 
ematical relationships on which the model learns to distinguish 
images. This system uses a GAN (Generative Adversarial Network) 
where the generator creates images from the text while the dis- 
criminator evaluates whether the images are realistic [ 51 ]. Im- 
age generation occurs through diffusion [ 52 ]. By starting with 
a clear image, noise is progressively added, disturbing the im- 
age until it becomes pure noise (forward diffusion) [ 53 ]. Subse- 
quently, the noise is removed, making the image clear again (back- 
ward diffusion) [ 54 ]. Essentially, the generator produces random or 
noisy images, and as it is trained, it learns to generate increas- 
ingly realistic images [ 55 ]. The discriminator acts as a critic and 
is tasked with distinguishing between real and synthetic images. It 
is trained to become more able to differentiate between the two 
categories. These two training processes alternate iteratively un- 
til the generator can produce images that the discriminator can 
no longer recognise from real ones. Finally, a human text prompt 
guides the diffusion process, creating an image from a combina- 
tion of words and noise, integrating visual and textual components 
[ 56 ]. 
3. AI in archaeology 
Although it is diﬃcult to determine the exact number of pa- 
pers that speciﬁcally focus on archaeological AI—and AI tools of- 
fer little assistance in this regard—a search on Google Scholar (us- 
ing the query "Archaeology AND AI" while excluding medicine and 
health) returned 16,400 publications, including 837 journal papers 
produced in the last decade, reﬂecting a steadily growing interest 
in the topic. So far, AI has provided certain beneﬁts to archaeol- 
ogy by analysing large datasets and addressing specialised, time- 
consuming tasks. However, its accuracy often falls short, and much 
of the nuanced work still requires manual intervention, limiting 
the overall effectiveness of AI in this ﬁeld. Nevertheless, with the 
emergence of generative models such as ChatGPT, Claude, DALL- 
E, Gemini, Midjourney, and Stable Diffusion, it is easy to fore- 
see their increasing use even by non-domain experts. Up until 
now, AI has been applied mainly to classiﬁcation and identiﬁca- 
tion tasks in the study of landscapes, objects, texts, and recently 
in robotics. For the identiﬁcation of archaeological sites, for exam- 
ple, CNNs trained on satellite data [ 57 ], LiDAR data with transfer 
learning [ 58 ], or historical cartography [ 59 ] have been proposed, 
as well as Random Forest models with LiDAR data [ 60 ]. CNNs have 
also been used for the semantic segmentation of GPR anomalies 
[ 61 ], while Casini et al. [ 62 ] used a transformer with images an- 
notated by experts (human-in-the-loop) without transfer learning 
for detecting archaeological sites in Mesopotamian ﬂoodplains and 
exploring the potential of a collaborative workﬂow between hu- 
mans and AI. In this approach, archaeologists reﬁne the AI’s pre- 
dictions to enhance future analyses and surveys. The models were 
ﬁne-tuned using satellite imagery and site annotations, with sig- 
niﬁcant input from domain experts who helped build the dataset 
and evaluate the predictions, achieving an accuracy of around 80%. 
At the level of artefacts, we can cite examples of GANs for the 
virtual restoration of ceramics [ 63 ] and coins [ 64 ], while Tsigkas 
et al. [ 65 ] used a pre-trained CNN for the automatic identiﬁcation 
of rock art and Gualandi et al. [ 66 ] CNNs with transfer learning 
for recognising ceramics developing two machine-learning tools 
openly accessible through desktop and mobile applications: one 
for shape-based recognition using a novel deep-learning architec- 
ture and another for decoration-based recognition using standard 
image classiﬁcation methods, achieving 83% and 62 % accuracy, re- 
spectively. Instead, the method described by Zachariou et al. [ 64 ] 
addresses two key applications: improving the accuracy of auto- 
matic image-based coin type matching algorithms, which are af- 
fected by the coin’s condition, and providing a visualisation of

ﬁeld, but the recent RePAIR projects [ 77 ], dedicated to Roman fres-

the newly funded AUTOMATA project [ 78 ], aimed at the creation 
of a robotic AI-supported work cell for enhanced digitisation of 
ceramics and lithics, indicate that this will be a new line of ap- 
plied studies. The use of GenAI seems for now limited to the im- 
mersive reconstruction of landscapes [ 79 ], the palaeoenvironment 
based on pollen data [ 80 ] or entomological and environmental 
data. 
4. Challenges 
Archaeology must address challenges related to data availability, 
transparency, and ethical and technical issues that are inherently 
linked to the nature of the discipline. 
4.1. Data, data availability and Big Data 
4.1.1. All begins with data 
Even though methods that address data and label scarcity ex- 
ist [ 81 ] —such as approaches that allow neural networks to be 
progressively trained as data are gathered (e.g., incremental learn- 
ing) and multimodal [ 82 ] and self-supervision [ 83 ], including tech- 
niques like few-shot learning [ 84 ] and zero-shot learning [ 85 ]—AI 
algorithms typically require a substantial amount of training data. 
However, in archaeology, there is often a lack of suﬃcient training 
data for AI applications and standards for combining data. Unlike 
the hard sciences, where large groups typically conduct research 
and produce substantial volumes of data, archaeology relies on the 
work of individual researchers or small teams, making access to 
training data complex and fragmented. Digital collections that are 
openly available for re-use and comparison are scarce, and there 
are signiﬁcant barriers to access, such as restrictive copyright or 
poor metadata, and creating new data from scratch can be expen- 
sive and challenging. 
4.1.2. Even deﬁning archaeological data can be challenging 
Archaeological data are a proxy for interpreting and under- 
standing the intertwined relationships between humans and non- 
humans through materiality strongly affected by a fragmentary, 
partial, and incomplete nature ([ 86 , 87 ], p. 178; [ 88 ]). Archaeologi- 
cal data are not solely the result of human actions. It encompasses 
intentional and unintentional deposition by past and recent soci- 
eties and non-human factors that play a role in determining what 
materials have survived over time. Attitudes towards data depend 
on different theoretical approaches. I will use a simpliﬁcation to 
distinguish between different positions, even though they are ac- 
tually quite blurred, as we can see further. Realist, positivist, and 
modernist approaches view data as given (empirical and indis- 
putable), while constructivist, phenomenologist, and postmodernist 
approaches consider data as co-created (interpretive and nego- 
tiable). In the former perspective, data are seen as a direct piece of 
information, a pre-existing feature that can be captured neutrally 
and objectively, independent of philosophical thought. The role of 
archaeologists consists, therefore, in adopting representative means 
of capturing data and creating as complete a record as possible 
while minimising errors or biases. Consequently, data are under- 
stood as capable of providing a reliable and consistent framework 
for interpretation and are valuable because they can be used as ev- 
idence. In the second perspective, data do not exist independently 
but are created through archaeological practice. Factors such as 
“research agenda, technologies, physical and temporal constraints, 
conventions”, and learned behaviours make a difference in what 
data are created, resulting in “multiple possible datasets” [ 8 ]. Data 
are considered primarily interpretive [ 89 ], “situated contextually, 
culturally, socially, politically, technologically, and spatially” [ 8 ]. All 
this shapes the potential dataset available to archaeologists. Con- 
temporary recording practice is intertwined with established ex- 
pectations and procedures, including digital techniques, measure- 
ment and diagnostic instruments for investigation purposes [ 90 ]. 
This leads to a paradox (the blurring we cited before): while data 
are often perceived as an existing entity awaiting collection, they 
are also recognised as a product of human interpretation and ac- 
tion within the archaeological process ([ 89,91,92 ], p. 93). Archaeol- 
ogists call data collection a situated, partial knowledge production 
“constructed as an interpretation of the phenomenal world, not in- 
herent in it” ([ 93 ], p. 8), and the capability of data to serve as a 
source of knowledge arises precisely from their mobility from the 
speciﬁc conditions in which they were generated to different situ- 
ations of analysis and re-use. ([ 91 ], p. 31). Avoiding the oxymoron 
of the so-called raw data [ 94 ], we can distinguish between unpro- 
cessed data (typically instrumental quantitative measurement) and 
processed data (manipulated and/or cleaned data) and between 
primary, secondary, and tertiary data. Primary is the data from the 
direct observation of the archaeological evidence, i.e. the proxy for 
the original tangible and intangible evidence (unprocessed data) 
[ 95 ]; secondary is the data made available for re-use (processed); 
tertiary is the data derived from other researchers’ analysis and re- 
use of data (interpreted). These distinctions emphasise the travel- 
ling of data ([ 96 ], p. 27). The use of secondary or tertiary data has 
become more common, distancing us from the material evidence. 
This suggests that the concept of digital data exists at a high level 
of abstraction. Data are not a single entity, but rather, they embody 
multiple aspects simultaneously [ 95 ]. 
The increased accessibility of high-variety data and digital an- 
alytical tools encourages archaeologists to embrace the concept of 
Big Data and use extensive datasets from disparate origins despite 
their inherent approximation and complexity. Data can be repro- 
cessed several times, and their value does not decrease; if any- 
thing, it increases with aggregation. By accepting the use of high 
volumes of data collected at different times and in various ways 
from heterogeneous sources, archaeologists also accept data with 
varying levels of accuracy, approximation and messiness. Conse- 
quently, entropy is probably inevitable [ 10 ]. Additionally, in a Big 
Data perspective, data are preferably considered as given, as an ob- 
jective reproduction of the archaeological record, instead of nego- 
tiable data to be enquired just where they come from, “just what 
uncertainties, assumptions, classiﬁcations, and concepts [their]

4.2. Transparency 
For over two decades, the ethical issues surrounding AI have 
been widely debated, focusing on key principles [ 103 , 104 ], poten- 
tial risks [ 105 ], the governance of AI, and the ethics inherent in 
algorithms [ 106 , 107 ]. Interest in these topics has steadily grown, 
driven by the increasing impact of AI on society and the necessity 
for responsible and transparent AI practices. In archaeology, ethical 
dilemmas have often been examined from a theoretical perspec- 
tive (e.g., [ 73,108 ]). However, Anichini and Gattiglia [ 1 ] provide a 
practical example with the ArchAIDE project, where they involved 
professional archaeologists in the application’s design to reduce 
AI’s oracular role. This approach allowed users to choose from ﬁve 
possible outcomes while retaining ﬁnal decision-making authority 
in the recognition process. Transparency, therefore, emerges as a 
crucial ethical consideration in addressing the challenges brought 
about by AI in archaeology. Understanding any biases in the data 
and how the algorithms work is crucial. If archaeologists cannot 
trust and check that an AI algorithm has made a correct iden- 
tiﬁcation, then the results cannot be used in research. AI is of- 
ten likened to a black box clichè - it produces outcomes, but the 
process behind them remains hidden. This black box is seen as 
a mysterious, powerful entity associated with a data-driven algo- 
rithmic culture and the opacity of proprietary software, especially 
when using pre-trained networks from companies like Google, Mi- 
crosoft, and Meta. While using open-source software and making 
the code available could be a solution, building a new network 
from scratch is not always practical. Moreover, even if the source 
code and neural network models are made public, understanding 
the code requires high technical skills. Transparency would also 
involve replicating the process, clarifying different variables and 
threshold values, and sharing the training and testing datasets as 
open data. Unfortunately, this is often unachievable in archaeol- 
ogy due to national legislation on Cultural Heritage. Also, creating 
a training dataset is neither a neutral nor purely technical oper- 
ation. It is the crystallisation of a complex and varied world into 
discrete classiﬁcations (such as the archaeologically debatable cat- 
egories of site/off-site), a process that involves inherently politi- 
cal, cultural, and social choices, shaping the epistemic boundaries 
that govern how AI operates, deﬁning its worldview and its ac- 
tion on the world ([ 98 ], p.135). The scarcity of available data in ar- 
chaeology often leads to the use of transfer learning methods, par- 
ticularly ImageNet, to pre-train a network for classiﬁcation tasks. 
ImageNet is a repository of over fourteen million images organ- 
ised into over twenty thousand categories. It has been thought 
to be a general ontology of images, helpful in supplying critical 
training and benchmarks for object and image algorithms [ 109 ]. 
However, it is not a neutral tool. ImageNet was created without 
any ethical considerations, relying on the labelling work of un- 
derpaid crowd workers [ 110 ], and is based on a semantic struc- 
ture imported from WordNet, a word classiﬁcation database devel- 
oped in 1985 by Princeton University that has become an essential 
source for computational linguistics ([ 111 ], p. xviii). WordNet itself 
was modelled after the Brown Corpus, a list of one million words 
sourced from newspapers and a random sampling of books com- 
piled in the 1960s reﬂecting a very speciﬁc cultural space. These 
ethical and systemic issues recur for GenAI. On the one hand, mod- 
els are trained on millions of images without recognising copy- 
right, and on the other, we do not know the corpora used for train- 
ing, a lack of transparency that prevents us from understanding 
what biases are introduced into the model [ 112 ]. 
The need for supporting intelligible explanations of AI func- 
tioning is emphasised by Explainable Artiﬁcial Intelligence (XAI) 
[ 113 ] aiming to develop technological solutions such as Model- 
Speciﬁc explainability [ 114 ], Model-Agnostic explainability [ 115 ], 
Model-Centric explainability [ 116 ], and Data-Centric explainability 
[ 117 ]. All these solutions are diﬃcult and not straightforward. Deep 
learning and neural networks with thousands of layers and pa- 
rameters make these algorithms very hard to understand. The fun- 
damental question is whether these algorithms can be explained. 
Currently, they cannot provide understandable explanations; if 
they do, they are either uninterpretable or overly simpliﬁed. Using 
human semantic explanations raises ethical concerns due to mis- 
alignment with neural networks and the challenges of veriﬁcation. 
Moving towards a human-centred AI that involves human-in-the- 
loop models can help mitigate the risk of black-boxing by allowing 
for more human involvement beyond just providing inputs and in- 
corporating outputs in analyses. For instance, archaeologists could 
have the ﬁnal say at the end of a recognition process. 
4.3. Epistemology and hermeneutics 
When I ﬁrst used the ArchAIDE App ( https://archaide-desktop. 
inera.it/ ) on my mobile device, it felt like the smartphone was me- 
diating between me and the potsherd. I no longer needed to phys- 
ically handle the potsherd, examine its surfaces, and analyse the 
paste to interpret it. Instead, I could take a picture with my smart- 
phone and let the technology do the cognitive work for me. Using 
AI felt like magic and also brought a sense of disruption. 
While these devices are not yet part of everyday archaeological 
practice, they are likely to become so sooner than expected. There- 
fore, it is crucial to pay attention to epistemology, hermeneutics, 
technological agency, and competitive cognitive artefacts, as well 
as address the challenges presented by autonomous digital tools 
with technological agency before they are more widely used in ar- 
chaeology. 
In Ihde’s view ([ 118 ], p. 63), technology gives voice to things, 
but in an AI-driven world, it also enables information to commu- 
nicate. Algorithms engage in hermeneutics by interpreting data, 
and this virtual cognitive process is embodied in computational 
media. The algorithm interprets information and guides users on 
what to read. In essence, AI actively mediates the world, displaying 
autonomy and technological intentionality. Returning to the topic 
of training datasets that we previously discussed, it also appears 
clear how both human decisions and technology inﬂuence their 
creation. These datasets not only contain biases but also exhibit 
agency. Archaeology faces the challenge of acknowledging technol- 
ogy as an agent we depend on to uncover meaning and, at the 
same time, that reﬂects our hermeneutics. When we train a neu- 
ral network, we impart our knowledge and agency to algorithms, 
representing a shift towards post-anthropocentric hermeneutics. 
This autonomous digital technological intentionality creates infor- 
mation, interprets data, and guides archaeologists on what to anal- 
yse. Therefore, when we derive knowledge from algorithms, it is 
important to recognise that the algorithms mediate between us 
and the world [ 2 ].

4.4. Technical challenges 
Using AI in archaeology presents an array of technical chal- 
lenges derived from the aspects abovementioned - the discipline’s 
inherent complexity and the unique nature of archaeological data 
- especially when working in real-world scenarios. AI algorithms 
have several challenges due to the lack of data and its fragmented 
nature. These algorithms typically need large and well-organised 
datasets to work effectively. It is technically challenging to combine 
data sources, such as satellite images, LiDAR, geophysical surveys, 
and excavation records, into a single dataset for AI analysis. The 
data is often not labelled, and creating labelled datasets for train- 
ing AI models is labour-intensive and requires expert knowledge. 
In front of many technical solutions, such as multi-modal sys- 
tems, self-supervision per data and label scarcity, few-shot learn- 
ing, zero-shot learning, agnostic learning, and incremental and 
continual learning models [ 119–121 ], the choice of effective algo- 
rithms or their development represents an extraordinary challenge 
in archaeology. For example, archaeological sites and artefacts of- 
ten display intricate patterns that can be diﬃcult for AI algorithms 
to identify and categorise due to their complexity, which can be in- 
ﬂuenced by natural processes, human activities, and cultural prac- 
tices, making the development of accurate algorithms diﬃcult. As 
mentioned, AI models trained on speciﬁc archaeological datasets 
may struggle to generalise to new sites or regions. Transfer learn- 
ing techniques, which allow models to apply knowledge gained 
from one context to another, are still developing in archaeology. 
Understanding the temporal context of archaeological ﬁnds is cru- 
cial, and AI models must be capable of recognising objects and 
placing them within a chronological framework, which involves 
interpreting complex stratigraphic data. As previously discussed, 
AI models can inherit biases from their training data, leading to 
skewed interpretations of archaeological ﬁndings. Ensuring that AI 
tools provide balanced and accurate insights requires careful con- 
sideration of these biases. Moreover, processing and analysing large 
archaeological datasets require signiﬁcant computational resources. 
Access to the necessary hardware and software resources depends 
on economic, logistical, and technological factors, often related to 
inequalities, such as the existing digital divide among the archaeo- 
logical community, which AI can increase. Sustainability is another 
concern, as maintaining a neural network is crucial for research 
projects with budget limits. For example, the ArchAIDE neural net- 
works were initially developed using TensorFlow 1.0 but later mi- 
grated to PyTorch due to the challenges of switching to Tensor- 
Flow 2.0 [ 1 ]. Successful implementation of AI in archaeology also 
requires collaboration between computer scientists and archaeol- 
ogists to bridge the knowledge gap and develop robust tools and 
methodologies. 
4.5. Invisible materiality 
Behind AI, there are not only algorithmic aspects [ 2 , 8 ] but also 
material ones. AI is a mega-machine [ 122 ] represented by an in- 
tricate network [ 123 , 124 ] composed of a technical apparatus that 
depends on industrial infrastructures, data centres, submarine ca- 
bles, supply chains and human labour, personal devices and their 
raw components, transmission signals, datasets, continuous com- 
putation cycles, etc., of which the materiality can be seen. This ma- 
teriality is extractive and linked to the exploitation of the planet’s 
energy and mineral resources, low-cost labour, and data, as well as 
a large carbon footprint. Running a single (large) NLP model, for 
example, can produce as much CO2 as 125 round-trip ﬂights be- 
tween Beijing and New York [ 125 ], an impressive ﬁgure consider- 
ing that the computing power required to train a single model by 
OpenAI increases tenfold every year [ 126 ]. This terraformative ma- 
teriality [ 127 ] creates an archaeologically investigable global com- 
putational landscape [ 128 ]. It is a mega-machine that produces in- 
equality (digital and data divide) and maintains a strong colonial 
footprint. Expressions like data mining and metaphors like “data is 
the new oil” refer back to colonial rhetoric to semantically trans- 
form data through a representative vision ([ 91 ], p. 93) into a nat- 
ural resource waiting to be discovered, exploited and consumed 
[ 129 ]. If data are oil, AI becomes the reﬁning process, which, in 
line with a neoliberal vision, transforms it into capital, not a non- 
rival common good, as we had hoped [ 10 ]. In this way, a dataset 
can remain open, but its meta-value, i.e., the model created, be- 
comes private property. 
5. Discussion 
5.1. What risks are there for archaeology? 
The ﬁrst is strictly linked with the inherent nature of archaeo- 
logical data and the wanting to capture complexities by excessively 
simplifying what is complex in order to be easily calculable. This 
reductionist desire produces a deterministic enchantment since it 
assumes classiﬁcatory approximations as basic truths, assigning 
ﬁxed labels to ﬂuid complexity. Instead of trying to build new sys- 
tems capable of grouping expressions into machine-readable cate- 
gories, we should question the origins of the categories themselves 
and their social and political consequence ([ 98 ], p. 197). For exam- 
ple, AI that identiﬁes sites starts from a site- and anthropocentric 
classiﬁcation, which conditions subsequent interpretations. Due to 
the re-use of legacy data, the necessary use of Big Data for train- 
ing datasets also involves the risk of reiterating ideas and inter- 
pretations and maintaining colonialist schemes. The increasing use 
of GenAI in archaeology, which we will witness in the coming 
years, will involve a transition from the automation of archaeologi- 
cal practices, such as the automatic classiﬁcation of artefacts or the 
identiﬁcation of ’sites’, to interpretation and the creation of images, 
reconstructive movies or texts. Partially delegating these activities 
to AI must necessarily lead to a critical approach. The oracular as- 
pects of enchanted determinism, in its metaphysical utopian and 
dystopian twins (AI as a solution to every problem or as a source 
of every danger), preclude informed discussion, critical examina- 
tion or possible rejection. This is where we must start instead: 
from the informed discussion among archaeologists, who analyse 
the material, classiﬁcatory and ethical aspects of AI. With this 
type of classiﬁcation and data, the danger of proposing, through 
GenAI, harmful and biased (colonial) textual or graphic reconstruc- 
tions that can take root and be diﬃcult to overturn ([ 98 ], p.128) 
is particularly high, as it is increasingly directed towards general- 
ist rather than purely academic use. However, it is important to 
keep in mind that AI textual reconstructions should not be under- 
stood as true narratives. In the digital practices we have observed, 
we witness an accumulation process, which works by adding one 
piece of data to another, similar to storytelling, that is, the coun- 
terpart of the narrative ([ 130 ], p. 68). If we think of the accumu- 
lation of tokens that create a text, the context of connections that 
establishes the sense of things gives way to a being-next-to-each- 
other or a being-one-after-the-other of events emptied of mean- 
ing ([ 130 ], p.53). Data proceed by addition and accumulation; they 
fragment and do not carry meaning, while narrative creation oc- 
curs through selection and synthesis and produces a continuum in 
which meaning ﬂows ([ 130 ], p.10). AI emulates forms of thought 
but does not interpret, i.e., it does not allow us to understand 
how humans viewed things and acted in the past. To do this, it 
is necessary to develop theories, that is, forms of narrative that 
develop conceptual contexts capable of making things intelligible, 
even more so in this moment of digital acceleration. Paraphrasing 
Han ([ 130 ], p. 90), as archaeologists, we must have more courage 
for philosophy and theory, which means having more courage for

narrative practice if we want to maintain control over the archae- 
ological narrative. Especially because archaeological research does 
not occur in a vacuum, without any responsibility for the ideas it 
disseminates; on the contrary, it has a strong public inclination 
and cannot avoid ethical issues. We must, therefore, be aware of 
how, in addition to perpetuating narratives heavily compromised 
by colonial discourse, the more complex issues of context, con- 
ditioning, relationships and disciplinary factors are hardly recon- 
cilable with current disciplinary approaches in computer science 
or with the commercial ambitions of the technology sector ([ 98 ], 
p. 195) and risk maintaining consolidated academic power struc- 
tures and increasing the digital divide between Western research 
and that of the Global South. The privatisation of the meta-value 
produced by data should push us to reﬂect without prejudice on 
the open dissemination of our research data or the need to ensure 
its non-commercial use to guarantee a public use of the produced 
meta-value. 
Materiality illustrates the subjective, political, hegemonic and 
authoritarian aspects ([ 98 ], p. 19) of the digital apparatus and de- 
serves to be investigated archaeologically. In this scenario, archae- 
ology would be attentive to how culture materialises, starting from 
the gigantic databases, and how it is co-created by human ac- 
tors and the technical apparatus. For this reason, it is necessary 
to abandon a representative vision of archaeological data, which 
sees data as already existing elements, ready to be collected, and 
move to a co-creative [ 89 ] or relational ([ 91 ], p. 101) vision. The 
representative vision does not allow us to understand either the 
data or their re-use, obscures the operations of power and avoids 
the responsibility of consequences. Data are central in the classiﬁ- 
cation process, which, as we have seen, is conﬁgured as an act of 
power whose interactions and the social and political theories un- 
derlying it and supported by it must be taken into account ([ 131 ], 
p. 319). It is an AI-speciﬁc colonising impulse that determines how 
the world is measured and deﬁned and simultaneously denies its 
intrinsic political, objective, universal and neutral nature. 
Finally, we need to respond to our initial question. Is archaeol- 
ogy still post-digital in the AI age? The concept of post-digital en- 
compasses both the idea of a state in which the disruption caused 
by digitalisation has already occurred, where technology is nor- 
malised and not perceived as disturbing and the rejection of tech- 
nological innovation and techno-positivism, which, combined with 
the mixture of analogue and digital, digital and real world, be- 
comes the engine of innovation [ 7 ]. A decade ago, Jeremy Huggett 
[ 11 ] answered this same question negatively. Considering only the 
normalisation and the end of the disruption due to digital, he saw 
them as close (in some sectors), but not yet happened. In my opin- 
ion, we would not be wrong to deﬁne post-digital archaeology if 
we considered digital applications excluding AI. Nobody ﬁnds GIS, 
3D models, spatial analyses, etc., disturbing. In these ﬁelds, techno- 
positivism is surpassed, and the mixture of real, analogue and digi- 
tal worlds is daily. However, the introduction of AI has shuﬄed the 
cards. The disruptive aspects are not yet overcome, its introduction 
being too recent and still evolving, and in many researchers, the 
oracular aspects of enchanted determinism are still rooted. To en- 
ter a period of normalisation, we must cultivate more disenchant- 
ment and scepticism towards AI in order to humanise it and in- 
corporate it into the real world. Perhaps, however, paraphrasing 
Cramer [ 7 ], in an AI era, the question of whether something is al- 
gorithmic or not is no longer really important, “just as the ubiq- 
uity of printing, shortly after Gutenberg, made all debates about 
the ’printing revolution’ obsolete” [ 7 ]. 
6. Conclusions 
While AI holds signiﬁcant promise for advancing archaeological 
research, these challenges highlight the need for careful and con- 
sidered application. AI is starting to shape archaeological research, 
thanks also to the funding it receives. Addressing data quality and 
integration issues, developing robust and interpretable algorithms, 
and navigating ethical considerations are essential steps towards 
realising the full potential of AI in archaeology. The ﬁeld can over- 
come these challenges through interdisciplinary collaboration and 
ongoing reﬁnement of AI techniques. The resources needed to train 
and optimise models risk increasing inequalities, while the biases 
embedded in the datasets used deserve careful examination. Sig- 
niﬁcant time should be dedicated to assembling datasets. This in- 
volves careful and comprehensive documentation work that in- 
cludes the objectives, values and motivations behind the collec- 
tion, data selection and assembly, model creation, and analysis of 
potential users. AI research in archaeology, being interested in hu- 
man data and the creation of narratives with which humans inter- 
act in intense ways, should prioritise the well-being of people who 
may be negatively impacted by the resulting technology [ 112 ]. This 
means dedicating time to engaging stakeholders at the beginning 
of the design process, exploring multiple strategies to reach goals, 
paying attention to possible dual-use scenarios, and allocating ef- 
forts to mitigate harm. Approaches that are more effective when 
implemented at the beginning of the process rather than as post- 
hoc risk analyses.
