INTRODUCTION
The development of technology has greatly enriched data obtained from computer-­based educational 
assessments. Many items in innovative tests require test-­takers to complete tasks in computer-­simulated 
interactive environments. When test-­takers interact with the interface to solve the items, the actions 
they took (e.g., keystrokes and mouse clicks) are recorded in order in the computer log files. The re-
corded information depicts the entire response process of each test-­taker. Such process data provide in-
formation on not only whether or not the tasks were completed successfully but also how the problems

Abstract
Response process data collected from human–­computer in-
teractive items contain detailed information about respond-
ents' behavioural patterns and cognitive processes. Such data 
are valuable sources for analysing respondents' problem-­
solving strategies. However, the irregular data format and 
the complex structure make standard statistical tools dif-
ficult to apply. This article develops a computationally ef-
ficient method for exploratory analysis of such process data. 
The new approach segments a lengthy individual process 
into a sequence of short subprocesses to achieve complex-
ity reduction, easy clustering and meaningful interpretation. 
Each subprocess is considered a subtask. The segmentation 
is based on sequential action predictability using a parsimo-
nious predictive model combined with the Shannon entropy. 
Simulation studies are conducted to assess the performance 
of the new method. We use a case study of PIAAC 2012 to 
demonstrate how exploratory analysis for process data can 
be carried out with the new approach.

Research on process data has gained great prominence recently. Tools in natural language processing 
such as n-­grams have been adapted to distinguish response processes of different groups (He & von 
Davier, 2016; Liao et al., 2019; Stadler et al., 2019). Automated feature extraction methods have been 
proposed to facilitate the analysis of response processes through regression models (Tang et al., 2020, 
2021). Qiao and Jiao (2018) applied various machine learning techniques to process data to investigate 
strategies used by students within the same category or across different categories. Chen et al. (2019) 
proposed an event history model for predicting response duration and outcome. A latent variable model 
for response processes has been proposed by Chen (2020). Xiao et al. (2021) explored the latent states 
in problem-­solving processes using hidden Markov models.
Most applications of process data analysis focus on one or several features in response processes and 
explore their relationship with other variables of interest such as response outcomes, response time, and 
respondents' demographics. Little work has been done to reveal respondents' high level problem-­solving 
strategies. Identifying respondents' problem-­solving strategies can provide insights into problem-­solving 
behaviours and help design educational interventions to improve the skill. Given a response process, 
identifying the strategy for task completion requires a close examination of the process and a thorough 
understanding of the item design. Therefore, it is time consuming to obtain an overall picture of the 
strategies adopted in a large set of response processes.
The major obstacle to developing analytical tools for identifying problem-­solving strategies is the 
great amount of variation and noise in response processes generated by flexible computer simulation 
environments. As a result, there is often no easy-­to-­check criterion. In this article, we analyse problem-­
solving strategies in response processes through a subtask point of view inspired by the observation that 
a complex problem-­solving task can often be decomposed into several subtasks to accomplish.
To illustrate this subtask view, we present here a sample item in the 2012 Programme for the 
International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC). A screenshot of the item interface is shown 
in Figure 1. According to the item description in the left-­hand panel, respondents are instructed to select 
as many music files as possible from the spreadsheet in the right panel for copying to a music player 
with 20 MB storage. An additional requirement is that only jazz and rock music should be selected. A 
possible (efficient) strategy for solving the item involves first sorting the files in an ascending order of 
the file sizes and then selecting the first several files of genre jazz or rock until the total size of selected 
files (indicated at the bottom of the interface) reaches 20 MB.
There are three possible subtasks for completing the required task: EXPLORE, SORT, and SELECT. 
Subtask SORT involves rearranging the music files by sorting. Subtask EXPLORE involves an explo-
ration of the menu bar and the buttons in the interface. Subtask SELECT involves selecting files by 
ticking boxes. Each response process consists of completing some or all of the three subtasks in a 
certain order. Consider the response process ‘Start, Toolbar_Sort, Sort_A_2, Sort_OK, Menu_Edit, 
Menu_Help, Menu_Data, Toolbar_Help, Toolbar_Save, Tick_9, Tick_12, Tick_19, Tick_13, Tick_14, 
Next’ as an example. In this response process, the subprocess ‘Toolbar_Sort, Sort_A_2, Sort_OK’ is 
related to sorting the files by clicking the sort button in the toolbar. In particular, action ‘Sort_A_2’ 
means to sort the music files in an ascending order of the values in the second column. This subprocess 
corresponds to subtask SORT. After sorting, the respondent explores the interface and learns how to 
select files by performing ‘Menu_Edit, Menu_Help, Menu_Data, Toolbar_Help, Toolbar_Save’, which 
belongs to subtask EXPLORE. The final subprocess ‘Tick_9, Tick_12, Tick_19, Tick_13, Tick_14’ is 
related to subtask SELECT.
Using the subtask point of view, a response process can be decomposed into several subprocesses, 
each of which corresponds to a subtask. Although different subprocesses completing the same subtask 
still vary, they often share a similar pattern and are less variable and easier to work with than the original 
response processes. Given such subtasks, one can decompose the total variation among response pro-
cesses into within-­subtask variation and between-­subtask variation and study them separately.

Identifying the sequence of subtasks could help us understand respondents' problem-­solving strate-
gies in practice. In the example item, if SORT as a subtask appears in the response process, it indicates 
that the respondent made use of the efficient sorting strategy instead of purely visual examination 
when deciding which music files to import. Besides whether a subtask is used or not, in which order 
the subtasks were performed could also be informative about the strategies. Knowing respondents' 
differences in performing subtasks would guide the understanding of respondents' problem-­solving 
skills.
A crucial step of subtask analysis is to segment the response process into a series of subprocesses. 
Observed processes are not naturally segmented. Although subtasks and strategies can be identified by 
domain experts based on their understanding of the item design and human cognitive processes, such 
an approach is time consuming and not scalable. Completing a subtask usually requires taking a short 
sequence of actions in order. Concluding that a subtask was completed when one or several related ac-
tions were used is likely to produce false detections. For example, sorting a spreadsheet involves opening 
the sorting window by clicking the menu item or the toolbar icon, choosing the variables to sort by, 
confirming the sort, and closing the window. A respondent may open the sorting window when explor-
ing the interface and close it right away. Although a sorting related action (open the sorting window) 
was used, sorting was not performed. Also, due to the flexibility of the interface, it is often difficult to 
list all possible subsequences completing a subtask and check whether a response process contains one 
of these subsequences.
To the authors' best knowledge, there is no data-­driven method particularly designed for segment-
ing response processes. In psychometrics, there exists work on detecting the phase transitions of scale 
scores or students' test behaviours (Lee & von Davier, 2013; Shao et al., 2016). However, these meth-
ods are not applicable to our problem as (1) they were designed for sequences of continuous scores or 
binary item responses instead of categorical time series and (2) their aim is to identify the only or the 
first transition point instead of finding the boundaries of multiple phases. Various segmentation meth-
ods for categorical sequences have been proposed in other contexts, mostly for text segmentation and

In this article, we propose a novel data-­driven procedure to decompose each response process 
into several subprocesses, thereby identifying the corresponding subtasks. The procedure is moti-
vated by observing that actions within a subtask are more predictable than those at the transition 
between two subtasks. In the procedure, we first train an action prediction model that utilizes a 
recurrent neural network (RNN; Bengio et al., 1994) to account for the long-­term dependence in 
action sequences. The predictability of an action is then quantified by the entropy of its predictive 
distribution. If an action is highly predictable (with low entropy), then it probably belongs to the 
same subtask as the preceding actions; if an action is not predictable (with high entropy), then it 
suggests a transition to a different subtask. This predictability-­based segmentation turns a long and 
highly variable action sequence into a short and simple sequence of problem-­solving subtasks that 
are easy to visualize and analyse.
The performance of the proposed procedure is examined in both simulation and a case study of 
the PSTRE items in PIAAC 2012. The dataset contains response processes from more than 40,000 
respondents. The lengths of the processes range from several dozen to a few hundred, depending on 
the task complexity and individuals' problem-­solving strategies. We demonstrate in the case study how 
results produced by the proposed method are used for exploratory analysis. We also show that the sub-
task sequences are much easier to visualize than the original response processes. An overview of the 
problem-­solving strategies used for the item can be obtained from the visualization. The association 
between respondents' problem-­solving behaviours and a variety of variables such as response outcome 
and response time is also examined. It is worth pointing out that the proposed method is generally 
applicable to response processes other than those demonstrated in the case study. Response processes 
with more sophisticated problem-­solving patterns than those in the PIAAC dataset could reveal more 
benefits of the method.
The rest of this article is organized as follows. Subtask analysis section describes the proposed 
procedure for segmenting processes and identifying subtasks. In Simulations section we evaluate 
the proposed method on simulated data. The case study of response processes from PIAAC 2012 is 
presented in Case study section. Concluding remarks section contains some concluding remarks.
SUBTASK ANALYSIS
In this section, we develop a data-­driven method to identify subtasks. The underlying rationale is that 
the subtasks are usually simpler and their response processes are more homogeneous. From a techni-
cal viewpoint, short-­term action predictions are more accurate within the same subtask than those at 
the transition between subtasks. Therefore, we begin with an action predictive model, followed by the 
predictability-­based segmentation or subtask identification method.

Sequence segmentation
The action prediction model provides us, for each t, with an estimated predictive (conditional) prob-
ability density pt = (pt1, … , ptM
). We do not use this model to actually predict future actions but 
rather to assess the intrinsic uncertainty. To this end, we propose to use the Shannon entropy (Cover & 
Thomas, 2006),
which is used in information theory to quantify the uncertainty of a distribution. For instance, a point mass 
distribution has entropy equal to the minimum value zero, whereas the maximum entropy is achieved at the 
uniform distribution.
Figure 3 presents the entropy process h = (h1, … , hT−1
) of a response process as a function of time 
t. As the figure shows, a response process usually starts at a relatively high entropy since little informa-
tion is available in the first action. Then the predictability of subsequent actions gradually increases and 
the entropy decreases accordingly as a subtask begins. As the problem-­solving process evolves, entropy 
rises to a relatively high level again, suggesting that the person has accomplished a subtask and is about 
to explore the system for the next subtask. The entropy fluctuates several times until the process reaches 
an end. The entire entropy process h consists of several U-­shaped curves. We observe that the processes 
are often very predictable within the same subtask. On the other hand, when a subtask is accomplished, 
it is generally more difficult to predict the subsequent actions as there are usually several options and the 
test-­taker might take any of them at random. This forms our basic understanding of a response process. 
Each U-­shaped curve of the entropy process corresponds to a subtask accomplishment as marked in 
Figure 3. Our process segmentation algorithm presented in the following partitions a response process 
through identifying the U-­shaped curves in the corresponding entropy process.
Based on the above understanding, we segment a response process in two steps. First, we identify 
all local maxima of the corresponding entropy process h that are the potential endpoints of U-­shaped 
curves. Then, we filter the set of local maxima and keep those that form ‘deep’ U-­shaped curves in the 
entropy process. A U-­shaped curve is considered to be deep if the entropy change within the curve is 
significant relative to the entropy fluctuation in the entire sequence. Technically, a subsequence hi:j of

We would like to make a few remarks before concluding the section. First, we use the action fre-
quency profile of a subprocess for clustering. This way of summarizing subprocesses could potentially 
obscures the differences among subprocesses, as it ignores the order of actions. However, in our data, 
subtasks are not often distinguished by different orders of performing actions, so we use the action 
frequency profile for simplicity. In cases where the order of actions within a subtask does matter, one 
could use the frequency profile of action pairs or more advance feature extraction methods such as 
those proposed by Tang et al. (2020) and (2021) to summarize subprocesses into vectors for clustering.
Second, a common method for determining the number of clusters W in k-­means clustering is the 
elbow method (Thorndike, 1953). In this method, clustering is performed for a series of values of W. 
The mean squared distance between the objects and the centroids of clusters decreases as W increases. 
In a plot of the mean squared distance against W, one looks for where the curve starts to flatten out 
(i.e., the elbow of the curve) and selects the corresponding W. In practice, the method may not produce 
interpretable clusters and may not even be feasible when the elbow is not obvious. Therefore, domain 
experts' knowledge of items is often needed to determine the number of subtasks (clusters) in the pro-
cedure. Also, using a slightly larger number of clusters than needed for clustering and then combining 
clusters with similar interpretations often leads to more meaningful results.
Third, a common way of interpreting clusters is to examine the elements (subprocesses) close to the 
centroid of each cluster and find their shared characteristics. Moreover, computing the action frequency

Results
Figure 5 summarizes the results for different subtask identification methods. For the proposed SIP, the 
five measures of subtask estimation accuracy defined in Table 1 are plotted against the values of 휆 in the 
left-­hand panel of the figure. When 휆 is small, Precision and Precision+ are relatively low since the pro-
cesses are overly segmented and unnecessary partitions are produced; when 휆 is too large (greater than .8), 
Recall and Recall+ decrease since the action sequences are undersegmented and many subtasks are not 
captured. Overall, the performance is robust to the choice of 휆 when its value stays away from 0 and 1.
The middle and right-­hand panels of Figure 5 display the boxplots of the five measures for the estimated 
subtask sequences obtained from HMM and eHMM on the 100 datasets. For HMM, the Precision, Precision+, 
Recall+ and Overlap are all below .6. Recall is generally higher than Precision, indicating that HMM tends to 
identify unnecessary subtask transitions. For eHMM, the relatively high Overlap but low Precision and Recall 
suggest that the model performs reasonably well on identifying the subtasks involved in transitions but poorly 
on pinpointing where the transitions occur. It is clear that the proposed SIP performs substantially better than 
HMM and eHMM, noting that the vertical axes of the two panels are on the same scale.

sequence length is approximately 25. We also examine the effect of sample size, number of actions and 
average process length by altering one of these quantities and controlling the others in the data genera-
tion process. In general, the accuracies would increase with the sample size and the number of actions 
and would decrease with the average process length. The detailed results are included in Appendix C.

CONCLUDING REMARKS
Process data have been a recent focus in the psychometrics literature, due to the increased use of 
problem-­solving items in large-­scale testing as well as the recognition of potential value in educational 
assessment. Exploration of process data has been hindered by the lack of effective statistical and psy-
chometric tools. In this article, we develop a new approach to conducting exploratory analysis of pro-
cess data. The new approach, using the concept of action predictability, transforms long and complex

response processes into shorter and more interpretable subtask sequences. Our case study of PIAAC 
2012 data shows that the subtask sequences can be used to visualize and identify respondents' problem-­
solving strategies. By decomposing variations in the observed response processes, subtask analysis can 
be used to explore the relationship between problem-­solving behaviours and variables of interest.
The computational cost of applying the proposed procedure is mainly the time spent on training the 
RNN-­based action prediction model, which is typically within two hours on a laptop with two cores 
and 16 GB memory for the dataset in our case study. Given the trained action prediction model, the 
remaining steps in the procedure can complete within a minute. The computational complexity of the 
entropy-­based segmentation step is linear in sequence length.
In the action prediction model, we set the dimension of the action embeddings x t to be the same as the 
dimension of the latent states 휽t. In a more general formulation, the two dimensions do not have to be the 
same. Let K1 and K2 denote the two dimensions. In principle, one would fit the action prediction model 
with different combinations of K1 and K2 and select the one producing the lowest validation loss as the final 
model for computing prediction uncertainty. We found in our experiments that the segmentation results are 
fairly robust to the choice of K1 and K2, so we set K1 = K2 = K for simplicity. Similarly, we use K = 20 for in 
both the simulation experiments and the case study, as we observe that the segmentation results are robust 
to the choice of K when K exceeds several dozen. One could fine tune the dimension for each item by con-
sidering a range of K and choosing the one producing the lowest validation loss. For item U19a, we consider 
K = 10,20,40,100,200 and 400. The current choice K = 20 gives a validation loss of .114 and K = 200 pro-
duces the lowest validation loss, .107. The state sequences obtained from the two choices have Overlap .943.
The SIP procedure is automatic in terms of segmenting response processes, but it still requires crit-
ical human input in the subtask identification step to determine the number of clusters (subtasks) and 
to interpret clusters as subtasks. Comparing with the traditional method of identifying problem-­solving 
strategies by examining response processes one by one, the proposed procedure requires less human 
effort and is scalable to large datasets.
The proposed approach allows a great deal of flexibility, which enables possible refinement and 
improvement. First, there is no restriction on the action prediction model as long as it characterizes 
the progress of the response processes reasonably well so that the unpredictability of an action is not 
mainly due to lack of fit. For instance, we can consider high-­order Markov chains as an alternative to 
RNN, or add additional information such as the timestamps of the actions to further improve model fit. 
Also, errors in the estimated action prediction distributions are not taken into account when computing 
entropies and performing segmentations in the current procedure. Developing a coherent probabilistic 
model and statistical framework for error analysis is a potential future direction.
The proposed procedure provides a tool to systematically transform long and complex response 
processes into shorter and less variable subtask sequences that are easier to work with. The resulting 
subtask sequences can be used as input in subsequent analysis to obtain a deeper understanding of the 
difference in problem-­solving strategies among different respondent groups and the relationship be-
tween problem-­solving strategies with other variables of interest. The subtask sequences could also be 
connected with latent variable models to investigate what and how respondents' latent characteristics are 
related to problem-­solving strategies.
