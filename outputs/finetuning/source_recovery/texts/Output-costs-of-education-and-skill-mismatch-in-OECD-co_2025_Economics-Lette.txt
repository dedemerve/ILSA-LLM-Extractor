A B S T R A C T
We quantify the output costs of education and skills mismatch for 17 OECD economies, using a calibrated 
model of vertical mismatch. Eliminating the frictions generating mismatch would raise output by 3% to 
4% on average, varying between 0.5% to 9% across countries. Although the education and skill mismatch 
measures are constructed using different methods and differ in size, the output costs are similar between the 
two measures.
1. Introduction
Education and skill mismatch refers to workers employed in occu-
pations that require different education or skills. Workers are classified 
as over- or under-employed, depending on whether their education or 
skill is below or above the occupation average requirement.
We use cross-country data from the Survey of Adult Skills (PIAAC), 
for 17 OECD economies, to quantify the output costs of mismatch. 
We analyse the data through a model of vertical mismatch, proposed 
by Garibaldi et al. (2025). They used it to study mismatch in the US 
and how much it contributed to the evolution of the college premium, 
as well as compare the output costs across US states. We calibrate 
two versions of the model to fit empirical measures of education and 
skill mismatch, a prominent distinction in the literature. Eliminating 
education (skill) mismatch completely would raise output by 2.9% 
(3.8%) on average, with large cross-country differences ranging from 
0.4% to 7% of output (1% to 9%). Although the education and skill 
mismatch measures are constructed using different methods and differ 
in size, the correlation between their output costs is 0.87.
We confirm the finding of Garibaldi et al. (2025), that the main 
determinant of the output cost of mismatch is not the percentage of 
mismatched workers, but their wage relative to well-matched workers.

3. Education and skill mismatch in OECD countries
The OECD Survey of Adult Skills is part of the Program for the 
International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC). The data 
were collected between 2011 and 2015 and have more information 
on mismatch than country-specific labour surveys, including: socio-
demographic information, labour market status and the proficiency of 
adults aged between 16 and 65 in literacy, numeracy and problem 
solving. We only include respondents currently employed.
Definition of education and skill mismatch. Finding an appropriate mea-
sure of mismatch is a debated issue (Flisi et al., 2017). Some authors use 
subjective approaches based on self-reported mismatch, while others 
rely on objective measures from the characteristics of the majority of 
workers in a given occupation. We use a combination of both.
For the subjective approach, we use two questions asking workers 
to compare their skill level and that required for their job: ‘‘Do you feel 
that you have the skills to cope with more demanding duties than those 
you are required to perform in your current job?’’ and ‘‘Do you feel 
that you need further training in order to cope well with your present 
duties?’’. Workers who feel they have the skills and do not need further 
training are considered well-matched.
For the objective education mismatch approach, we consider high-
type workers as college worker (educational attainment of ISCED 5B) 
and higher. On average across countries, it corresponds to 40% of 
workers. We then compute its share within each 2-digit occupations. 
A college (non-college) worker is classified as under-employed (over-
employed) when working in an occupation that is a majority non-
college (college).
For skills, we analyse the distribution of literacy and numeracy 
proficiency scores. We consider as high-type (low-type) workers above 
(below) the median proficiency scores (implying a share of high-skilled 
workers, 𝑛 of 0.5). We then compute their share within each 2-digit 
occupation. A high-skilled (low-skilled) worker is classified as under-
employed (over-employed) when working in a majority low-skill (high-
skill) occupation.
Workers are only categorized as mismatched if found mismatched 
by both subjective and objective approaches.
As shown in Fig. 1, mismatch is sizable, with an average of 20% 
based on education (30% based on skills), consistent with Quintini 
(2011). Educational under-employment (13% on average) is larger than 
over-employment (7%), also for skill under-employment (17%) that is 
higher than skill over-employment (13%). While the skill-based mea-
sure reveals higher mismatch, the correlation between the two mea-
sures is low (0.22 for under-employment, 0.03 for over-employment), 
echoing the differences across mismatch measures documented in the 
literature (Flisi et al., 2017).
Wage. The wage refers to gross hourly earnings. Earnings data for 
Germany, Sweden, and US are reported in deciles. We use (Hanushek 
et al., 2015) procedure to assign the decile median to each respondent 
corresponding to the decile of the country-specific wage distribution. 
We trim the bottom and top one percent to limit the influence of 
outliers. We compute average gross hourly earnings for each worker 
group (high/low type, under- over-employed).
The average college or skill premium of well-matched workers 
hovers around 80%, and has a correlation of 0.9 across mismatch 
measures. The well-matched premium for college (skilled) workers is 
large: they earn 50% (60%) more than under-employed college (skilled) 
workers. This variable 𝑤ℎ

Norway (education-based) to 90% in the US (both education or skill).

While the two indicators of mismatch differ in the size of mismatch 
across countries, they yield similar wage outcomes, namely for the 
cost of under-employment and gain of over-employment (a correlation 
across measures of 0.91 and 0.67).

4. Output costs of mismatch and discussion
We quantify how much output would increase if we eliminate 
frictions behind mismatch to zero (Fig. 2). The output costs are on av-
erage 2.9% for education mismatch (3.8% for skill mismatch), varying 
substantially across countries, with low costs in Belgium and Nordic 
countries, to around 6 to 9% in the US, Spain and Korea.
The two measures deliver similar predictions regarding output costs. 
While having different magnitudes of under- and over-employment 
and shares of high-type workers, the correlation of output costs across 
measures equals 0.87.
Confirming the findings in Garibaldi et al. (2025), output costs are 
not driven by the percentage of mismatched workers per se. Instead, 
they are closely related to wage premium of well-matched college 
workers, with correlation of 0.98 in both education and skill measures.
Let us compare US and Belgium. The US output cost of mismatch are 
between 6% and 7%, while in Belgium they are below 1%. Focusing on 
education, over-employment in the US is 9% (7.5% in Belgium), while 
13% of US workers are under-employed (10% in Belgium). The extent 
of educational mismatch is relatively similar, while output costs are 
different, so it cannot be their primary driver. The key difference is the 
wage differentials between mismatched and well-matched workers.

Countries like the US, with sizable wage losses of under-employment 
(of more than 90%), would benefit from eliminating mismatch, while 
countries like Belgium, with more moderate wage costs of under-
employment (20%–25%) would not. It is easier to understand why this 
variable is so related to output costs if we compute the derivative of 
output with respect to under-employment: 𝜕𝑌
𝜕𝑢= −𝑤ℎ+𝜁𝑤𝑙. This partial 
derivative tell us that reducing under-employment at the margin would 
raise output by 𝑤ℎ−𝜁𝑤𝑙, which dividing by 𝜁𝑤𝑙 is exactly the wage 
loss of under-employment. Reducing under-employment, at the margin, 
always raises output.
Our estimates suggest that the cut-off value is around 𝑤ℎ
𝜁𝑤𝑙= 1.4. 
Whatever the mismatch measure, output costs start building up (start-
ing at 2 to 3% of output loss) in countries where the wage premium 
of well-matched high-type workers relative to under-employed coun-
terparts is larger than 40%. Policy makers in those countries should be 
concerned that too few educated/skilled workers are actually working 
on well-matched jobs. This scarcity of labour away from productive jobs 
is likely to be costly. In contrast, countries with moderate wage cost of 
under-employment should be less concerned about mismatch.
