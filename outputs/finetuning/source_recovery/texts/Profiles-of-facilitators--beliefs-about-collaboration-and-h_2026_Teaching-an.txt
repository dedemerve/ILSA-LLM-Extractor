A B S T R A C T
Collaboration among teachers can enhance the impact of professional development (PD). However, little is 
known about how the facilitators providing teacher PD view collaboration. This study explores facilitators’ 
beliefs about collaboration and how these relate to their experience, job satisfaction, self-efficacy, and willing­
ness to innovate, as variables linked to the implementation of innovations. 190 German facilitators for mathe­
matics teacher PD participated in an online survey. Latent profile analysis revealed three profiles of beliefs: 
Selective Collaborators, Moderate Collaborators, and Collaboration Enthusiasts. Collaboration Enthusiasts 
showed higher self-efficacy and willingness to innovate than Moderate Collaborators, highlighting how positive 
beliefs about collaboration may support the transfer of innovation.
1. Introduction
Collaboration among teachers is a key factor in enhancing the suc­
cess of professional development (PD) programmes and supporting the 
implementation of innovative teaching practices. Research has shown 
that teacher collaboration can improve student outcomes, increase 
teacher self-efficacy, and facilitate the sustainable transfer of new 
teaching approaches (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Goddard et al., 
2007; Sancar et al., 2021). For instance, teachers who collaborate with 
their colleagues provide more cognitively activating instruction than 
teachers who collaborate less (OECD, 2020).
While the impact of teacher collaboration on PD outcomes is well- 
documented, there is limited understanding of how the individuals 
responsible for delivering PD programmes – namely facilitators – view 
collaboration (Thurm et al., 2024). This research gap is particularly 
noteworthy considering that facilitators serve as agents of transfer in the 
cascade model of PD. This model refers to a structured, multi-level 
approach in which innovative teaching approaches are disseminated 
across different levels of the education system (Stahnke et al., 2025). 
Ideally, facilitators are first prepared for their role in providing teacher 
PD with respect to the content, i.e., an innovative teaching approach at 
hand. If the cascade model functions as intended, teachers participating 
in facilitator-led PD learn about the new teaching approach and 
implement it successfully in their classrooms, which may then improve 
student learning (Roesken-Winter et al., 2021; Stahnke et al., 2025). In 
this model, facilitators are assumed to play an important role in shaping 
transfer processes, and their beliefs have been identified as a potentially 
relevant factor (Borko et al., 2014; Prediger et al., 2019). Specifically, 
facilitators' collaboration beliefs may influence how they value collab­
oratively preparing PD with other facilitators or teachers, how they 
conceptualize effective teacher PD in terms of collaborative activities, 
and what they expect regarding teachers' collaboration within PD set­
tings (Borko et al., 2014; Prediger et al., 2019; Roesken-Winter et al., 
2021). However, research suggests that educators' beliefs do not 
necessarily translate into enacted practice; discrepancies between re­
ported beliefs and professional actions have been documented, often due 
to contextual, institutional, or systemic constraints (Saadati et al., 
2019). Accordingly, facilitators’ collaboration beliefs should be under­
stood as an important, but not sufficient, condition shaping PD enact­
ment, rather than as direct indicators of PD quality, transfer 
effectiveness, or student learning outcomes (Barzel & Selter, 2015; 
Borko et al., 2014).
In a cascade model of transfer, facilitators' beliefs about collabora­
tion can have three foci: their collaboration with other facilitators of 
teacher PD, the collaboration among teachers in the PD provided by the 
facilitators, and the collaboration of the facilitator with teachers outside

could inform about their needs in terms of professional growth and

Teachers' beliefs about collaboration have positive effects on various 
aspects such as job satisfaction, self-efficacy, or willingness to innovate 
instructional methods (Duyar et al., 2013; Richter & Pant, 2016; Tor­
opova et al., 2020; Vangrieken et al., 2015). These factors, in turn, in­
fluence instructional quality and student learning as well as teacher 
retention (Hu & Duyar, 2024; OECD, 2020). However, it remains un­
clear whether these relationships also apply to facilitators. Learning 
more about such connections could be crucial in evaluating whether 
fostering positive beliefs about collaboration among facilitators is 
worthwhile, as it may enhance facilitators’ job satisfaction, self-efficacy, 
and willingness to innovate in a similar way and could consequently 
improve both PD quality (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Roesken-­
Winter et al., 2021) and retention among facilitators (Scherer and Rolka, 
2024).
In summary, given the crucial role facilitators play in shaping PD 
quality and supporting the implementation of innovations through 
teacher PD, it is vital to understand (1) their beliefs about collaboration 
and (2) how these relate to their experience as well as their job satis­
faction, self-efficacy, and willingness to innovate. This study addressed 
this research gap by following a person-centred approach (Woo et al., 
2024) and examining profiles of beliefs about collaboration among fa­
cilitators of mathematics teacher PD in Germany. The aim of this study is 
twofold: first, to explore profiles in facilitators' beliefs about collabora­
tion using latent profile analysis; second, to examine how these profiles 
relate to facilitators’ experiences in teaching and delivering PD, as well 
as their job satisfaction, self-efficacy, and willingness to innovate. By 
investigating these research questions, the study seeks to explore facil­
itators' views of collaboration and how they relate to other factors that 
may enhance PD outcomes and support the sustainable transfer of in­
novations into classroom practice.
1.1. The important role of facilitators for the transfer of innovations
Teacher PD can successfully initiate the transfer of innovative 
teaching approaches into schools (Borko, 2004). The quality of such PD 
programmes affects the extent to which teachers implement innovative 
teaching approaches in their classrooms (Darling-Hammond, 2017; 
Maass et al., 2019). In addition to the design of the PD programmes, the 
facilitators who deliver them play a crucial role in PD quality and thus in 
the professionalisation of teachers and the transfer of innovations 
(Borko et al., 2014; Prediger et al., 2019; Roesken-Winter et al., 2021). 
Therefore, facilitators need to be prepared for this challenging task, 
enabling them to acquire the necessary knowledge and skills, but also to 
develop productive beliefs (Borko et al., 2014). Programmes aiming to 
prepare facilitators need to consider the nested structure of the content 
they have to learn: They require expertise regarding subject-specific 
content at classroom level (e.g. an innovative teaching approach), but 
also expertise in supporting teachers' learning of this specific content (i. 
e. on the PD level; Luft & Hewson, 2014; Prediger et al., 2019; Roe­
sken-Winter et al., 2021). This includes pedagogical content knowledge 
at two levels: first, knowledge about how students learn a specific con­
tent, and second, how teachers learn the respective PD content, as both 
are relevant when facilitating teachers’ learning (Prediger et al., 2019; 
R¨osken-Winter et al., 2015).
In a cascading model, the transfer of new teaching approaches can be 
successfully supported at scale (Borko, 2004; Roesken-Winter et al., 
2021; Stahnke et al., 2025; Thurm et al., 2024). In such a model, facil­
itators are responsible for providing teacher PD (Maass & Artigue, 2013; 
Roesken-Winter et al., 2021). They are ideally prepared for the impor­
tant task of providing teacher PD through facilitator PD courses. After­
wards, they independently conduct PD courses in their respective 
regions where teachers then implement the new teaching approaches in 
their classrooms (Maass & Artigue, 2013; Stahnke et al., 2025). This 
structure enables the systematic and scalable dissemination of educa­
tional innovations. If teachers participating in PD additionally are 
encouraged to collaborate with their colleagues, the content of PD can 
be further disseminated, supporting the implementation of innovative 
approaches in schools (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Richter et al., 
2022; Sun et al., 2013; van Es et al., 2014).
1.2. Effects of teacher and facilitator collaboration
Collaboration between teachers can have many positive conse­
quences for students, teachers themselves, and overall school develop­
ment (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Goddard et al., 2007; Vangrieken 
et al., 2015). In general, collaboration can be defined “as joint interac­
tion in the group in all activities that are needed to perform a shared 
task” (Vangrieken et al., 2015, p. 23). Such collaboration can have 
different foci and depths, ranging from merely sharing ideas to working 
together on materials and teaching approaches (Little et al., 2010; 
Sancar et al., 2021). Teacher collaboration is mostly investigated within 
schools or in PD courses involving teachers from different schools 
(Sancar et al., 2021; Vangrieken et al., 2015). In contrast to collegiality, 
collaboration does not focus on the relationships between teachers and 
their emotional benefits, but is rather concerned with the way in­
dividuals work collaboratively towards a shared goal (Kelchtermans, 
2006).
Similarly, collaboration among facilitators is assumed to play a 
crucial role in cascading PD models since they are responsible for 
disseminating new teaching approaches to teachers. We understand 
facilitator collaboration as facilitators working together, jointly plan­
ning PD sessions, and reflecting on their facilitation strategies, which 
allows them to align their understanding of the PD content and to 
effectively support the development of teachers' professional compe­
tencies. Depending on the context, such collaboration most likely takes 
place across schools (e.g. in Germany) or within the same school (e.g. in 
China; Thurm et al., 2024). Through collaboration, facilitators can 
ensure consistent PD implementation across different contexts and 
support one another in refining their approaches. Moreover, collabora­
tion contributes to facilitators’ own professional learning by fostering 
shared responsibility, mutual feedback, and the development of a col­
lective expertise necessary for scaling educational innovations effec­
tively (Prediger & Selter, 2024). In the German context, facilitators 
report regularly collaborating with other facilitators when providing PD 
as a team, designing PD together or exchanging ideas and information 
(Thurm et al., 2024). Proximal to their collaboration practices are their 
beliefs, which are their “understandings, premises, or propositions” 
(Philipp, 2007, p. 259) about collaboration. For facilitators, these beliefs 
can refer to collaborating with other facilitators, to the collaboration of 
teachers in the PD provided by the facilitator, or to their collaboration 
with teachers outside of the PD context, as many facilitators also hold 
teaching positions (Thurm et al., 2024).
A first positive effect of collaboration is that students attending 
schools with high levels of teacher collaboration show higher achieve­
ment in mathematics and reading (Goddard et al., 2007). Accordingly, 
findings from the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 
indicated that such differences in achievement might be grounded in 
different teaching practices: Teachers who collaborated with colleagues 
often provide more cognitively activating instruction (Methlagl, 2022;

OECD, 2020). Establishing collaboration structures can also have a 
positive impact on teachers themselves: Collaboration with colleagues at 
their school improves teachers’ self-efficacy and job satisfaction 
(Clement & Vandenberghe, 2000; Duyar et al., 2013; Meredith et al., 
2023). Moreover, the possibility to collaborate and interact with others 
is a motivating factor for teachers to engage in PD programmes within 
and across schools (Richter et al., 2022). Going beyond effects on indi­
vidual teachers and their classes, collaboration among teachers and 
other actors in educational systems is beneficial for disseminating the 
content of PD programmes (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Sun et al., 
2013; van Es et al., 2014), and facilitating the sustainability of education 
reforms and innovations (Prenger et al., 2022).
Consequently, stimulating collaboration and establishing collabora­
tive structures is consistently viewed as an essential design principle of 
high-quality PD measures (Barzel & Selter, 2015; Darling-Hammond 
et al., 2017; Desimone, 2009; Prediger & Selter, 2024; R¨osken-Winter 
et al., 2015). However, providing time and resources for collaboration 
alone does not ensure PD success in terms of teacher learning or student 
achievement. Teachers must also be supported in processing and 
reflecting new teaching approaches and their impact on student learning 
(Timperley et al., 2007). Thus, facilitators’ role in fostering impactful 
teacher collaboration is crucial, especially when teachers from different 
schools are participating in a PD course. Yet, little is known about how 
facilitators themselves view collaboration, i.e. what beliefs about the 
role of collaboration they hold.
Considering the research on teacher collaboration, it is reasonable to 
assume that facilitators' beliefs about collaboration could affect both the 
effectiveness of PD programmes and the dissemination of new teaching 
approaches through PD. Firstly, positive beliefs about the collaboration 
with other facilitators could lead to facilitators engaging in more dia­
logue with other facilitators about how they provide teacher PD courses, 
seeking more feedback from others and thus improving the quality of PD 
courses (Thurm et al., 2024). Furthermore, positive beliefs about the 
benefits of collaboration among teachers in PD courses could motivate 
facilitators to implement opportunities for such collaboration in their PD 
courses and to support similar positive beliefs in the teachers that 
participate in their PD courses (Roesken-Winter et al., 2021). Thus, 
learning more about facilitators’ beliefs about collaboration can inform 
not only the design of programmes preparing future facilitators but also 
support the transfer of innovative teaching approaches through teacher 
PD.
1.3. The relationship between facilitators’ beliefs about collaboration and 
their experiences, job satisfaction, self-efficacy, and willingness to innovate
Research on teacher collaboration suggests that beliefs are related to 
personal characteristics such as experience, job satisfaction, self- 
efficacy, and willingness to innovate (for a review, cf. Vangrieken 
et al., 2015). However, little is known with respect to facilitators' beliefs 
and related, potentially important factors. To identify such factors, we 
draw on research from the teacher level. We leverage the structural 
analogies between the teacher and the facilitator levels, focusing on the 
analogous aspects of teaching and learning. First, we transfer and adapt 
theoretical frameworks and constructs originally developed for teachers 
to the context of facilitators. Then, we investigate whether analogous 
findings can be observed for facilitators. This approach is referred to as a 
lifting strategy by Prediger et al. (2019). Accordingly, we draw on 
findings on teacher collaboration and lift them to the facilitator level, 
proposing similar relationships between facilitators’ collaboration pro­
files and their experience, job satisfaction, self-efficacy, and willingness 
to innovate. In the context of PD, these views may influence PD quality 
and teacher growth (Prediger & Selter, 2024) as well as facilitator 
retention (Scherer & Rolka, 2024).
Experience is often used as an indicator of expertise, but years of 
experience do not automatically lead to the acquisition of relevant 
knowledge, skills or beliefs (Palmer et al., 2005). Teaching experience 
was found to impact student achievement (Johansson et al., 2023), 
although experience teaching the same grade appears more important 
than general experience (Huang & Moon, 2009). Experience has also 
been linked negatively to teacher judgements (M¨oller et al., 2022) and to 
more intensive forms of collaboration (e.g. working together on tasks; 
Richter & Pant, 2016). Yet, experienced teachers can still benefit from 
collaboration and further develop their teaching practices with col­
leagues (Goddard et al., 2007; Richter et al., 2011). When considering 
experience at the current school, teachers with more experience tend to 
collaborate more, likely because they have established routines and 
structures (OECD, 2020).
With regard to facilitators, studies indicate that the combination of 
teaching experience and experience as a facilitator leads to more 
effective PD (Borko, 2004), as experienced facilitators acquire addi­
tional knowledge and skills for providing PD, including methods for 
stimulating collaboration (Day & Gu, 2007). However, to our knowledge 
there is no empirical evidence on how facilitators’ beliefs about 
collaboration relate to their experience as teachers and facilitators.
Job satisfaction is the satisfaction and fulfilment with one's career 
choice (Ainley & Carstens, 2018) and includes well-being and content­
ment with professional activities (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2017). Job 
satisfaction can reduce the risk of burnout and the intention to leave the 
profession (Madigan & Kim, 2021; OECD, 2020). Satisfied teachers are 
more engaged, show better teaching performance, and have lower 
burnout rates (OECD, 2020). Job satisfaction also positively affects the 
transfer of PD content into practice (Vigerske, 2017). Collaborative work 
environments can contribute to job satisfaction by providing social 
support and opportunities for collaboration (Drossel et al., 2019; Ron­
feldt et al., 2015) and collaboration is a significant predictor of teacher 
job satisfaction (Duyar et al., 2013; Klassen & Tze, 2014; Skaalvik & 
Skaalvik, 2011, 2017; Toropova et al., 2020). Additionally, willingness 
to innovate and job satisfaction can improve collaboration quality 
(Thurlings et al., 2015).
Regarding facilitators, there is no evidence on their job satisfaction 
and its relation to collaboration beliefs or other characteristics. How­
ever, qualitative findings suggest that dissatisfaction with the facilitator 
role may lead to drop out from qualification programmes (Scherer & 
Rolka, 2024). Given the resources invested in facilitators' preparation (e. 
g. Prediger & Selter, 2024), it is important to examine whether positive 
collaboration beliefs are linked to job satisfaction and whether sup­
porting collaboration may reduce dropout.
Self-efficacy can be defined as a teacher's “judgment of his or her 
capabilities to bring about desired outcomes of student engagement and 
learning” (Tschannen-Moran & Woolfolk Hoy, 2001, p. 782). It is 
associated with better teaching practices and student outcomes (Zee & 
Koomen, 2016). Teachers with high self-efficacy are more committed 
and show higher job satisfaction (Darling-Hammond, 2007; Ng et al., 
2010; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2014). Self-efficacy develops through 
mastery experiences, vicarious experiences, and social persuasion 
(Bandura, 1977; Gale et al., 2021). Accordingly, collaboration is posi­
tively related to self-efficacy, as it provides opportunities for shared 
learning and feedback (Duyar et al., 2013; Richter & Pant, 2016; Sehgal 
et al., 2017). Collaborative activities can also enhance self-efficacy 
(Schipper et al., 2018), and teachers with higher self-efficacy are more 
willing to collaborate and try new approaches (Collie et al., 2012; 
Goddard et al., 2004; Kolleck, 2019).
Evidence on facilitators' self-efficacy is still limited. However, it is 
likely to be particularly important, as facilitators must adapt PD ap­
proaches to different contexts (Prediger & Selter, 2024). Facilitators' 
self-efficacy refers to their ability to provide high-quality PD that sup­
ports teacher learning. High-quality PD can be described through six 
design principles (Barzel & Selter, 2015; Prediger & Selter, 2024; Roe­
sken-Winter et al., 2015): Competence-orientation means focusing on 
productive practices for typical instructional demands. Case focus means 
working on cases from classrooms and experimenting with new prac­
tices. Participant-orientation refers to working with teachers' perspectives

and needs. Reflection stimulation means reflecting on instructional 
practices. Experiment model describes that inputs, teaching experiments 
and reflections are connected within the PD programme. Finally, 
collaboration stimulation encompasses initiating teacher collaboration 
within the PD programme. There is limited empirical evidence on fa­

follows these principles. With respect to the last design principle, facil­
itators in the German context report only moderate self-efficacy for 
supporting teacher collaboration (Thurm et al., 2024). Initial findings 
suggest that positive beliefs about the benefits of collaboration with 
other facilitators and teacher collaboration within PD programmes are 
predictors of facilitators' self-efficacy (Hansen et al., 2024). Drawing on 
teacher research, facilitators’ self-efficacy may also affect PD quality and 
teacher learning, and may reduce dropout in cascade models.
Willingness to innovate describes the readiness to introduce and to 
implement new ideas and methods in teaching (Rogers, 2003; Thurlings 
et al., 2015). Teachers’ openness to change can predict student 
achievement (Hu & Duyar, 2024). In Rogers theory on the diffusion of 
innovations (2003), persons who are more open to change are assumed 
to adopt innovations early-on. Empirical data confirms this assumption, 
with teachers who are willing to innovate also developing more creative 
teaching approaches and adapting more easily to new educational 
standards (Fullan, 2016). Research also indicates that teachers who are 
willing to innovate exhibit higher job satisfaction and effectiveness 
(Thurlings et al., 2015). Furthermore, innovative teachers are often 
more active in cooperative networks and share their ideas with col­
leagues (Vangrieken et al., 2015). For facilitators, little is known about 
their willingness to innovate and its relation to collaboration beliefs. 
However, a positive relationship seems likely, as openness to new PD 
approaches may foster collaborative idea exchange and joint develop­
ment of PD designs, potentially enhancing PD quality and teacher 
learning.
In summary, research shows that teacher collaboration is associated 
with job satisfaction, self-efficacy, and willingness to innovate, which in 
turn support the transfer of innovations. However, little is known about 
facilitators' collaboration beliefs and how they relate to these factors. 
Following a lifting strategy, it is reasonable to assume a similarly central 
role of facilitators’ beliefs about collaboration.
1.4. The present study
Facilitators play an important role in the cascade model of transfer 
(Stahnke et al., 2025). How they conduct teacher PD and support 
teacher learning is crucial to whether innovative teaching approaches 
are implemented effectively and thus improve student learning (Borko 
et al., 2014; Stahnke et al., 2025). In this model, facilitators' beliefs 
about collaboration are crucial, as they are likely related to whether 
facilitators collaborate with others, how they design and conduct 
teacher PD courses, and how they support collaboration among teach­
ers. However, little is known about facilitators’ beliefs about collabo­
ration and how these relate to other views that may support the 
effectiveness of transfer in a cascade model.
Addressing the research gap, we explore facilitators’ beliefs about 
collaboration and how these relate to their experience as well as their 
job satisfaction, self-efficacy, and willingness to innovate. The study 
pursues two research questions. 
(1) What profiles of beliefs about collaboration can be identified 
among facilitators?
Since facilitators are often teachers themselves, their beliefs with 
regard to three foci of collaboration are of interest. First, how they view 
collaboration with other facilitators in order to e.g. prepare PD material. 
Second, how they view teacher collaboration among teachers in the PD 
courses that they provide. Third, how they view collaboration with other 
teachers outside of PD, since they are often working as teachers, too. 
Following a person-centred approach, we acknowledge that beliefs 
regarding these three foci may not relate to one another in the same way 
for every facilitator but form distinct profiles (cf. Woo et al., 2024). To 
our knowledge, there is no research on this topic available yet. Thus, this 
research question is explorative and we do not formulate specific ex­
pectations on which profiles emerge. 
(2) How are the profiles of beliefs about collaboration related to fa­
cilitators' experience as a teacher and as a facilitator, their job 
satisfaction, their self-efficacy, and their willingness to innovate?
Following a lifting strategy, i.e., proposing similar relationships on 
the facilitator level as observed on the teacher level (Prediger et al., 
2019), we hypothesize that profiles characterized by positive beliefs 
about collaboration are associated with more experience, higher job 
satisfaction, higher self-efficacy, and a greater willingness to implement 
innovative PD approaches.
2. Method
2.1. Context of the study
The present study was conducted in the context of the QuaMath 
programme (Developing Quality in Mathematics Classrooms and Teacher 
Professional Development), which is funded by the Standing Conference of 
the Ministers of Education and Cultural Affairs (KMK) in Germany 
(Prediger & Selter, 2024). The programme aims to improve mathematics 
education through a cascading PD programme that follows established 
high-quality principles for mathematics education (Prediger et al., 
2022) and high-quality teacher PD (Barzel & Selter, 2015; Prediger & 
Selter, 2024; Roesken-Winter et al., 2015). In a cascade model, the 
programme prepares facilitators in a year-long facilitator PD pro­
gramme, conducted by the DZLM (German Centre for Mathematics 
Teacher Education). Afterwards, facilitators will lead teacher PD in 
tandems, meaning that two facilitators deliver teacher PD as a team. 
Through this multi-level structure, PD concepts can be systematically 
disseminated across several levels of the educational system, enabling 
the large-scale reach of teachers while maintaining conceptual coher­
ence and quality of implementation. The facilitator preparation pro­
gramme began with the start of the school year 2023/2024. Facilitators 
were recruited and selected by the QuaMath state representatives and 
state coordinators, who are appointed by the respective federal states.
The study does not aim to evaluate the QuaMath programme but 
utilized it as a data collection opportunity to examine how facilitators 
who start this programme but already have experience in providing 
various PD courses view collaboration, and how their beliefs relate to 
other factors. However, the previously described design of the QuaMath 
programme places a great emphasis on collaboration: For instance, fa­
cilitators are going to work in teams, collaboration stimulation is a core 
design principle for the teacher PD courses and by creating teacher 
networks, QuaMath aims to implement sustainable collaborative struc­
tures where teachers work together on implementing new teaching ap­
proaches. Considering this important role of collaboration in the 
QuaMath programme, facilitators’ beliefs about collaboration may be 
particularly relevant to the effectiveness of the programme.
2.2. Sample
The data were collected prior to the start of the facilitator prepara­
tion programme in August 2023. A total of 373 facilitators were invited 
to participate in the survey. Of these, 23 participants did not respond 
and an additional 18 participants were excluded due to missing data. 
This resulted in a final sample of 332 facilitators from 15 of the 16 
federal states in Germany, including both experienced and inexperi­
enced participants. Since the present study focuses on facilitators’ be­
liefs about collaboration in the context of prior experience with PD, only

facilitators attended PD courses as participants, and all of them have 
conducted PD programmes as facilitators themselves.
2.3. Measures
A standardized 38-item questionnaire measured facilitators’ de­
mographic background, their experiences as a facilitator and a teacher, 
beliefs about collaboration, job satisfaction, self-efficacy, and willing­
ness to innovate. Since research on facilitators is an emerging area of 
educational research, there are few measures available that were 
developed specifically for this group. Instead, measures developed for 
teachers are often used or adapted (i.e., lifted to the facilitator level; 
Prediger et al., 2019). Accordingly, we drew on several teacher mea­
sures for the facilitator level.
Beliefs about collaboration were measured with three foci, using a 
four-item scale for each level: (1) In Germany, facilitators are often 
teachers themselves, and are still involved in teaching. Participants re­
ported beliefs about collaboration with other teachers and the extent to 
which they themselves have collaborated as teachers (e.g. “It is impor­
tant for my work as a teacher to exchange ideas with colleagues.”; scale 
from Meudt et al., 2020). (2) We assessed beliefs about collaboration with 
other facilitators. For this purpose, we adapted the scale by Meudt et al. 
(2020; e.g. “It is important for my work as a facilitator to exchange ideas 
with other facilitators.”). (3) Since facilitators need to foster collabora­
tion among teachers, we also measured how they perceive collaboration 
among teachers in their PD courses. Again, we adapted Meudt et al.’s 
(2020) scale for teacher collaboration (e.g. “It is important for teachers 
in PD to exchange ideas with their colleagues.”). Since the three scales 
referred to different professional roles, facilitators were asked to adopt 
the respective perspective associated with each scale and reflect on how 
they view collaboration in that role. This instruction was necessary 
because many facilitators in Germany hold multiple professional roles 
simultaneously (e.g. as teachers and PD facilitators). Participants 
responded on a five-point Likert scale from “does not apply” to “applies”. 
The reliability analysis indicated good internal consistency for all three 
scales (ω1 = 0.78; ω2 = 0.75; ω3 = 0.80).
Job satisfaction as a facilitator was measured using four items that 
were adapted from Ditton and Merz (2013). On a five-point Likert scale, 
participants stated their satisfaction with their role as facilitators (e.g. “I 
am very satisfied with my role as a facilitator”). The reliability analysis 
indicated good internal consistency (ω = 0.89).
Self-efficacy regarding the six design principles for high-quality PD 
(Barzel & Selter, 2015; Prediger & Selter, 2024) was measured using six 
subscales with three items each: competence orientation, case focus, 
participant orientation, reflection stimulation, experiment model, 
collaboration stimulation (ω between 0.75 and 0.88 for subscales). The 
items were adapted from the scale for teacher self-efficacy by Schwarzer 
and Schmitz (2002), an example item for collaboration stimulation is “I 
am confident in my ability to encourage teachers to collaborate on 
lesson ideas even between PD sessions.” Participants rated their 
self-efficacy on a five-point Likert scale from “does not apply” to “ap­
plies”. Since we were interested in facilitators’ overall self-efficacy 
regarding the implementation of high-quality PD, we formed a com­
posite scale based on the six subscales. The overall scale showed 
excellent reliability (ω = 0.93).
Willingness to innovate was assessed with four items adapted from 
Meudt et al. (2020). One item is: “With regard to my role as a facilitator, 
I am open to external suggestions”. Participants rated their openness to 
try out innovative suggestions and to continuously develop profession­
ally on a five-point Likert scale. The scale's reliability (ω = 0.52) in­
dicates low internal consistency, suggesting that the items may not 
consistently capture the same underlying construct. Due to this low 
reliability, the results regarding willingness to innovate should be 
interpreted with particular caution and considered exploratory in 
nature.
2.4. Data analysis
Data were analysed using SPSS (Version 28) and RStudio. SPSS was 
used to analyse background characteristics as well as for the exploratory 
data analysis.
To answer Research Question 1, a latent profile analysis (LPA) was 
conducted. LPA is based on structural equation modelling (SEM) and is 
used to identify latent profiles of continuous variables within a sample of 
individuals (He & Fan, 2020; Woo et al., 2024). To identify these distinct 
profiles or groups within a dataset, LPA uses probabilistic models (He & 
Fan, 2020). It determines the optimal number and characteristics of 
profiles by relying on objective model fit indices. These indices help to 
ensure that the identified profiles best represent the underlying profiles 
in the data (Meyer et al., 2013).
When conducting an LPA, there are generally five steps (He & Fan, 
2020). 
(1) Identifying LPA indicators: After the data were examined for 
multicollinearity, normal distribution, and linearity, which are 
assumptions for the LPA, LPA indicators were identified. The 
indicators used to perform the LPA were facilitators' beliefs about 
collaboration (with other teachers, with other facilitators, among 
teachers in PD sessions).
(2) Specifying LPA models: To refine the specification of the LPA 
models, an exploratory factor analysis was conducted to identify 
the underlying factor structure. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) 
measure confirmed the sampling adequacy for the analysis (KMO 
= 0.662), and the Bartlett's test was significant (p < .01), indi­
cating that the assumption of sphericity was met and the sample 
size was sufficient for the analysis. A preliminary analysis was 
conducted to obtain the eigenvalues for each factor in the data. 
Three factors had eigenvalues above the Kaiser criterion of 1 and 
explained 58.4 % of the variance. A scree plot was used to 
determine the best factor structure pattern. The results of this 
analysis supported a three-factor solution.
(3) Estimating LPA models: Subsequently, the LPA was performed in 
RStudio using the packages tidyLPA (Rosenberg et al., 2018) and 
mclust (Scrucca et al., 2016) to identify profiles in collaboration.
(4) Evaluating LPA models: The best-fitting latent class model was 
determined by examining a combination of absolute and relative 
fit indices. These statistics, along with considerations of class size 
and interpretability, determined which model was selected. As 
there was no underlying theoretical basis to determine the 
number of classes suitable for the sample, three different models 
comprising between two and five classes were tested. To evaluate 
the LPA models, the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and 
Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) were used to choose the 
appropriate number of classes.

3. Results
3.1. Descriptive results
The descriptive results are presented in Table 1. Overall, facilitators 
held positive beliefs about collaboration with other teachers (M = 4.2) 
with scores showing a wide range from 1.50 to 5.00 and a moderate 
variability (SD = 0.7). Facilitators' beliefs about collaboration with other 
facilitators had a slightly higher mean of 4.6 (SD = 0.5). Facilitators’ 
beliefs about collaboration among teachers during PD were the highest 
among all three beliefs (M = 4.7; SD = 0.4).
In terms of experience, facilitators reported a wide range of teaching 
experience, from 3 to 40 years with an average of 16.5 years (SD = 8.5), 
highlighting the substantial teaching background of the sample. Expe­
rience as a facilitator also varied widely, ranging from 1 to 34 years (M 
= 6.6; SD = 5.4). Job satisfaction (M = 4.6; SD = 0.5), facilitators’ self- 
efficacy (M = 4.2; SD = 0.4), and willingness to innovate (M = 4.7; SD =
0.4) were high, suggesting that on average the facilitators were satisfied 
with their role, had moderate to high self-efficacy and showed a strong 
inclination towards innovation.
Table 2 presents the correlations between variables. The three sets of 
beliefs about collaboration show only small correlations with each 
other, indicating that facilitators hold distinct beliefs regarding the 
different foci of collaboration. Facilitators’ beliefs about collaboration 
with other facilitators and about collaboration among teachers during 
PD sessions correlate moderately with both self-efficacy and willingness 
to innovate. It is also worth noting that self-efficacy, willingness to 
innovate, and job satisfaction show positive relationships with each 
other.
3.2. Research Question 1: facilitators’ beliefs about collaboration
To reveal profiles in facilitators’ beliefs about collaboration, we first 
conducted an exploratory factor analysis which identified a three-factor 
structure. Thus, the LPA was performed using three variables: beliefs 
about collaboration with other facilitators, beliefs about collaboration among 
teachers during PD, and beliefs about collaboration with other teachers. The 
three-class model had the best fit indices (AIC = 759.80; BIC = 805.26; 
entropy = 0.82), indicating a clear assignment of participants to profiles. 
The minimum class membership probability was 0.84, and the 
maximum class membership probability was 0.95, suggesting that most 
facilitators were accurately assigned to their profiles. The proportion of 
facilitators in the smallest class was 0.14, while the proportion in the 
largest class was 0.66. The bootstrap likelihood ratio test was significant 
(p < .01), supporting a three-class model.
Table 3 provides an overview of facilitators' beliefs about collabo­
ration in the three profiles. In Profile 1, the Selective Collaborators (n = 26 
[13.7%]), participants show highly positive beliefs about collaboration 
with other facilitators and about collaboration among teachers from a 
facilitator's perspective, but are less positive with respect to collabo­
rating with other teachers. Profile 2, the Moderate Collaborators (n = 38 
[20.0%]), represents participants who hold moderately positive beliefs 
about collaboration across all three levels. They consider collaboration 
less positively both with teachers and facilitators and also view collab­
oration among teachers within their PD courses as less important. Profile 
3, the Collaboration Enthusiasts (n = 126 [66.3%]), includes participants 
holding strongly positive beliefs about collaboration with both teachers 
and facilitators, and placing high importance on teacher collaboration 
during PD.
3.3. Research Question 2: relationship of collaboration profiles and 
facilitators’ experiences, job satisfaction, self-efficacy, and willingness to 
innovate
Table 3 shows the means and standard deviations for facilitators' 
experiences in the different profiles, their job satisfaction, self-efficacy, 
and willingness to innovate. To reveal the relationship between the 
profiles of beliefs about collaboration and facilitators' experiences, job 
satisfaction, self-efficacy, and willingness to innovate, we conducted a 
MANOVA. The homogeneity of error variances between groups was met 
according to Levene's test for teaching experience, facilitator experience, 
self-efficacy, and job satisfaction (p > .05), but not for willingness to 
innovate (p = .042). The homogeneity of covariance matrices was 
confirmed by Box's test (p = .180). The MANOVA showed a statistically 
significant difference between the collaboration profiles for the com­
bined dependent variables (F(16, 356) = 39.506, p < .001, partial η2 =
0.64, Wilk's Λ = 0.13).
Post-hoc, a one-way ANOVA was conducted for each dependent 
variable to examine for which variables the profiles differed. Statisti­
cally significant differences were found for self-efficacy (F(2, 187) =
7.637, p < .001, partial η2 = 0.08) and willingness to innovate (F(2, 
187) = 125.8, p < .001, partial η2 = 0.07). Both effects were of moderate 
size but absolute differences between profiles were rather small with 
means between 4.0 and 4.3 for self-efficacy and between 4.5 and 4.7 for 
willingness to innovate. Due to the low reliability of the scale for will­
ingness to innovate, the respective results also need to be interpreted 
with caution and treated as exploratory findings. Finally, no effects were 
found with respect to teaching experience, facilitator experience, and 
job satisfaction.
Tukey-HSD post-hoc tests revealed which of the three profiles 
differed in self-efficacy: The two profiles – Moderate Collaborators and 
Collaboration Enthusiasts – differed significantly (MDiff = −0.31, 95% CI 
[-0.49, −0.12] p < .001; Cohen's d = −0.75), whereas the other profiles 
did not differ significantly. Due to the non-homogeneity of variances for 
willingness to innovate, the Games-Howell test was used. The Games- 
Howell test showed a significant difference for willingness to inno­
vate, again between the Moderate Collaborators and the Collaboration 
Enthusiasts (MDiff = −0.23, 95% CI[-0.34, −0.08]; p < .05; Cohen's d =
−0.67), but not between the Selective Collaborators and the Moderate 
Cooperators or the Selective Collaborators and the Collaboration Enthusi­
asts. In summary, the Moderate Collaborators show significantly lower 
self-efficacy for providing high-quality PD and lower willingness to 
innovate their PD practices than the Collaboration Enthusiasts.

4. Discussion
The aim of this study was, first, to identify profiles of beliefs about 
collaboration among facilitators and, second, to reveal how these are 
related to their experiences as teachers as well as facilitators, job satis­
faction, self-efficacy, and willingness to innovate. Importantly, the study 
was not designed to evaluate the effectiveness of the QuaMath pro­
gramme or the cascade model. Rather, QuaMath provided the context for 
data collection. Accordingly, the Discussion focuses on interpreting the 
identified belief profiles, their relations to the investigated facilitator 
characteristics, and implications for future research. We identified three 
profiles of beliefs, providing new insights into facilitators’ beliefs about 
collaboration with three foci: collaboration with facilitators, among 
teachers during PD, and with teachers outside of PD. Facilitators in the 
Collaboration Enthusiasts profile hold profoundly positive beliefs about 
collaboration in all three facets and thus consider collaboration highly 
valuable. Given the central role of facilitators in the cascade model of 
PD, the finding that two-thirds of the investigated facilitators for 
mathematics education were identified as Collaboration Enthusiasts 
highlights the prevalence of such beliefs in this facilitator population. 
The remaining one-third of facilitators were equally assigned to the two 
profiles Moderate Collaborators with moderate levels of collaboration 
beliefs across all three levels and Selective Collaborators with positive 
levels of collaboration beliefs except for the collaboration with teachers 
outside of PD. The Collaboration Enthusiasts show comparatively higher 
self-efficacy for providing high-quality PD and higher willingness to 
innovate than those in the Moderate Collaborators profile. However, it is 
important to note that the overall levels of self-efficacy and willingness 
to innovate were relatively high across all profiles (see Table 3). Thus, 
these differences should be interpreted as relative rather than as an 
indication of low levels of these constructs in any profiles.
Building on these descriptive findings, the profiles identified in this 
study offer valuable insights into how facilitators' beliefs about collab­
oration are embedded in facilitators' dual role, as they often serve 
simultaneously as practicing teachers and PD facilitators (Thurm et al., 
2024). The identification of a distinct profile characterized by consis­
tently positive collaboration beliefs for the majority of facilitators 
highlights the relevance of these beliefs within this facilitator popula­
tion. Given prior research at the teacher level indicating that 
collaboration-related beliefs are associated with professional learning 
processes (cf. Methlagl, 2022; OECD, 2020), it is plausible that such 
beliefs relate to how facilitators approach collaborative preparation and 
reflection of teacher PD. At the same time, research has repeatedly 
shown that stated beliefs do not necessarily translate into enacted 
practices, as contextual, institutional, and systemic constraints may 
hinder implementation (e.g. Saadati et al., 2019). Thus, collaboration 
beliefs should not be interpreted as direct indicators of facilitators' 
actual collaborative behavior or of PD quality. Rather, they can be un­
derstood as one potentially relevant antecedent that may interact with 
contextual factors (e.g. time, organizational support, program struc­
tures) to shape practice. Since the present study did not assess facilita­
tors’ actual collaboration or the quality of teacher PD, future studies – 
ideally including observational or longitudinal data – should examine 
whether and under which conditions these belief profiles translate into 
collaborative practices and specific design choices when facilitating 
teacher PD (Barzel & Selter, 2015; Prediger & Selter, 2024).
Facilitators in the Moderate Collaborators profile reported less posi­
tive collaboration beliefs across all three facets and may therefore view 
collaboration as less beneficial or more demanding. In the cascade 
model of transfer, less positive beliefs regarding collaboration with other 
facilitators may be associated with perceiving fewer benefits or higher 
costs of collaboration, potentially leading to less emphasis on joint 
preparation, peer feedback, or collective reflection. However, as the 
present study did not assess facilitators' actual collaborative practices, 
such interpretations remain speculative. Within the facilitator tandems 
in QuaMath, differing collaboration beliefs may be reflected in varying 
approaches to joint work, ranging from task-oriented coordination to 
more collaborative forms of engagement. Similarly, less positive beliefs 
about collaboration among teachers may be associated with differing 
expectations regarding collaborative activities within teacher PD set­
tings. Whether and how these beliefs relate to opportunities for collab­
oration provided during teacher PD, and subsequently to teachers’ 
implementation processes, remains an open empirical question war­
ranting further investigation (cf. Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Prenger 
et al., 2022).
While facilitator preparation programmes usually include opportu­
nities for meaningful collaboration, thereby modelling the design prin­
ciple they are expected to promote, Moderate Collaborators may need 
additional support. Identifying facilitators in this profile at the begin­
ning of large-scale PD programmes through a short screening might 
allow targeted support rather than spending limited resources on all 
facilitators. In programmes using tandems of facilitators providing PD 
together (as in QuaMath), it may be advisable to pair Moderate Collab­
orators with Collaboration Enthusiasts, to foster more collaboration and

Selective Collaborators hold less positive beliefs about collaborating 
with teachers outside PD than their colleagues, while holding positive 
beliefs regarding collaboration among facilitators and among teachers 
within PD. In the cascade model as implemented in QuaMath, collabo­
ration between facilitators and teachers outside PD is not considered 
central (cf. Prediger & Selter, 2024). In our data, Selective Collaborators 
do not significantly differ from the other profiles in terms of experience, 
job satisfaction, self-efficacy, or willingness to innovate. This supports 
the idea that for facilitators, such beliefs may not be as important as 
beliefs focused on collaboration in the PD context.
Thus, in cascade models disseminating innovative teaching ap­
proaches, collaboration within levels (i.e., among facilitators and among 
teachers) may be more important for transfer than collaboration be­
tween facilitators and teachers. However, further research is needed to 
explore if beliefs about collaboration with other teachers indeed do not 
affect how facilitators provide PD, particularly regarding fostering 
collaboration among teachers (Barzel & Selter, 2015; Darling-Hammond 
et al., 2017; Desimone, 2009; Prediger & Selter, 2024; Roesken-Winter 
et al., 2015). Such beliefs may become relevant if PD and teaching ap­
proaches need to be developed through co-construction, rather than 
dissemination and adaptation of ready-for-use approaches (Barzel & 
Selter, 2015; Prediger & Selter, 2024). Moreover, qualitative research is 
needed to understand facilitators’ reasons behind these less favourable 
beliefs for example whether facilitators identify strongly with their 
facilitator role and perceive limited value of feedback from other 
teachers.
Interestingly, we found no significant differences between the pro­
files in terms of facilitators’ prior experience as teachers or as facilita­
tors. This may reflect that most facilitators across all profiles have 
considerable experience in both roles, hence, only few beginning facil­
itators and no “pre-service” facilitators where included. Previous find­
ings suggest that experienced teachers tend to cooperate less intensively 
(Richter & Pant, 2016). We did not find such tendencies, possibly 
because all facilitators already demonstrated moderate to high collab­
oration beliefs. This finding may indicate that collaboration beliefs are 
not simply a result of accumulating years of experience. Instead, quali­
tative differences in professional trajectories (e.g. opportunities for 
collaborative work, institutional norms, prior PD) and participation in 
specific qualification programmes may be more strongly related to 
collaboration beliefs than years of experience alone. Further research 
could therefore focus on indicators of expertise and learning opportu­
nities rather than on experience alone.
Similarly, no differences in job satisfaction were found between 
profiles. A promising finding is that the facilitators in all profiles are 
highly satisfied with their role, which might support facilitator reten­
tion. Across facilitators, job satisfaction and positive beliefs about 
collaborating with other facilitators are correlated significantly. This 
aligns with teacher-level findings indicating that positive beliefs about 
collaboration contributes to job satisfaction (Klassen & Tze, 2014; 
Ronfeldt et al., 2015; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2011, 2017). Supporting 
collaboration among facilitators may therefore improve their job satis­
faction, and prevent dropout, which is particularly desirable given the 
resources often invested in preparing these facilitators for their role in 
the cascade model (e.g. Prediger & Selter, 2024). The observed corre­
lations suggest that collaboration beliefs co-vary with facilitators’ 
evaluations of their role. Importantly, the data do not allow conclusions 
about directionality. Both pathways (collaboration beliefs fostering 
satisfaction versus satisfied facilitators perceiving collaboration more 
positively) and third-variable explanations (e.g. organizational support) 
remain plausible. Moreover, job satisfaction correlated moderately with 
self-efficacy beliefs. Future research could explore whether in­
terventions aimed at strengthening self-efficacy also enhance job satis­
faction and, through interviews, provide more detailed insights into 
which design principles facilitators feel confident about and how this 
relates to job satisfaction.
In contrast to job satisfaction and experience, we did find differences 
in self-efficacy and willingness to innovate between the profiles 
Collaboration Enthusiasts and Moderate Collaborators. Facilitators who 
held positive beliefs about collaboration with other teachers and facili­
tators, as well as positive beliefs about collaboration among teachers in 
the given PD, exhibited higher self-efficacy and a higher willingness to 
innovate their PD practices. These findings are consistent with previous 
research on teacher self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997; Collie et al., 2012; 
Goddard et al., 2004). Just as for teachers, facilitators with positive 
views of collaboration might have had more opportunities for vicarious 
experience and social persuasion, fostering their self-efficacy (Gale 
et al., 2021). Similar to teachers, facilitators with positive views of 
collaboration are more open to innovations (Vangrieken et al., 2015). 
However, while the observed differences in self-efficacy and willingness 
to innovate between profiles were statistically significant, the effect 
sizes were moderate. This suggests that beliefs about collaboration 
explain a meaningful, yet only moderate proportion of variance in these 
constructs. Moreover, given that mean levels of self-efficacy and will­
ingness to innovate were generally high across all profiles (see Table 3), 
these differences reflect variations within an overall high level of 
self-efficacy and willingness to innovate rather than substantial differ­
ences between facilitators. Thus, although facilitators' positive beliefs 
about collaboration appear to come with greater confidence and open­
ness to innovation, other factors such as facilitators' expertise or insti­
tutional support, as well as contextual factors are also likely to 
contribute to these outcomes. Moreover, given the relatively low inter­
nal consistency of the scale for willingness to innovate, results regarding 
this variable must be interpreted with caution. Nonetheless, the 
observed effects align with the assumption that positive beliefs about 
collaboration can be an important source of facilitators’ self-efficacy and 
readiness to implement innovative PD practices within the cascade 
model.
In the context of the cascade model of PD, the present findings point 
to potential associations between facilitators' collaborative beliefs, self- 
efficacy, and willingness to innovate. In particular, the identified re­
lationships suggest that collaborative structures at the facilitator level 
may shape facilitators’ perceptions of their professional role, including 
their confidence in facilitating PD and their openness to adapting PD 
practices. Facilitators characterized as Collaboration Enthusiasts, for 
example, may perceive more opportunities for shared reflection and 
exchange when trying new approaches due to their openness to 
collaborating with others on implementing these approaches. This 
openness may also be associated with a higher reported willingness to 
innovate.
Collaborative structures such as tandems of facilitators providing PD 
together or networks of facilitators supporting each other should not be 
interpreted as effective per se. Rather, they should be viewed as po­
tential contexts whose role for facilitators’ beliefs warrants further 
empirical investigations. However, more research is needed, especially 
longitudinal and mixed-methods studies, to examine the directionality 
of the observed relationships. For example, it is unclear whether more 
positive beliefs about collaboration lead to higher self-efficacy or if 
developing greater self-efficacy makes facilitators more likely to seek 
collaboration with colleagues. Building on evidence from the teacher 
level suggesting links between self-efficacy, willingness to innovate and 
professional learning (e.g. Fullan, 2016; Zee & Koomen, 2016), future

4.1. Limitations
Several limitations of this study need to be considered. First, while 
the sampling process aimed for breadth, achieving full representative­
ness was not possible. Nonetheless, the sample is notably large for the 
group of facilitators and includes a substantial proportion of all facili­
tators working in mathematics education across Germany, providing 
new and valuable insights. Additionally, by only focusing on facilitators 
who have at least some experience, this study cannot provide any in­
sights into the role of beliefs about collaboration for “pre-service” fa­
cilitators. Further research could investigate the impact of such beliefs 
on early career facilitators' self-efficacy as well as their retention and 
professional growth. Second, the scales used in this study were adapted 
from existing instruments addressing teachers and have not been spe­
cifically validated for the group of facilitators, indicating the need for 
further refinement and validation in future research. Third, the scale 
measuring willingness to innovate demonstrated low reliability, 
whereas the remaining scales showed acceptable to strong reliability, 
calling for a revision of this instrument. The low reliability of the scale 
could be due to ceiling effects. Additionally, willingness to innovate may 
mean something different in relation to the level of teachers from which 
the instrument was adapted and for facilitators, whose practice may 
generally be characterised by innovating one's practice. More qualitative 
research is needed to better understand what it means to be willing to 
innovate as a facilitator. Fourth, as this study focused exclusively on 
facilitators in the field of mathematics, it remains uncertain whether 
facilitators in other subject areas exhibit different beliefs about collab­
oration. Moreover, the study did not measure actual collaborative 
behaviour but facilitators' beliefs. Finally, while teachers mostly 
collaborate with colleagues at their schools, facilitators often also 
collaborate across schools and even with other institutions such as 
universities with teacher education programmes. We did not account for 
this complexity of collaboration among facilitators. Further research is 
needed in this regard.
5. Conclusions
In summary, the findings show that facilitators who hold less positive 
beliefs about collaboration with other facilitators also have lower self- 
efficacy for providing high-quality PD and a reduced willingness to 
innovate their PD courses, as Collaboration Enthusiasts in our data 
demonstrated higher self-efficacy and greater openness to innovative PD 
approaches than Moderate Collaborators. Overall, these results confirm 
the idea that collaboration may be a key factor in the effective transfer of 
PD content, not only for teachers but also for facilitators (cf. Prediger & 
Selter, 2024). Thus, building collaborative structures for facilitators by 
establishing professional networks and promoting teamwork during PD 
preparation and delivery may positively impact these factors and in turn 
support the dissemination of new teaching approaches. The identified 
profiles of beliefs about collaboration also suggest that some facilitators 
may need additional support regarding their views on collaboration. 
Thus, facilitator preparation programmes should address not only 
knowledge and skills but also professional beliefs, and may even seek to 
identify those individuals in need of additional support. Further research 
within QuaMath will provide more in-depth insights.
