Abstract
In this study, we applied elastic-net logistic regression to explore critical predictors of
students’ mathematics and science levels using data from eighth-grade students in the
2019 Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS). The elastic-net
approach, a machine learning based approach combining Ridge and Least Absolute
Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, addresses multicollinearity
while enabling variable selection, offering a robust methodology for analyzing largescale educational datasets. Key ﬁndings highlight the critical role of home educational
resources and student conﬁdence in predicting outcomes in both subjects.
Additionally, reduced disruptive behaviors during math lessons emerged as signiﬁcant
contributors to mathematics proﬁciency level. The models achieved high accuracy
(up to 77.91% for mathematics and up to 70.74% in science) and balanced sensitivity
and speciﬁcity, underscoring their practical relevance in educational contexts. This
research demonstrates the potential of elastic-net logistic regression to provide
insights into selection of predictors for student science and mathematics proﬁciency
levels.

Introduction
Predicting student outcomes has long been a central focus in educational research
because of its potential to inform policies and practices. However, this task is inherently complex, involving a web of interrelated factors such as family background,
teacher inﬂuence, and school-level variables. Machine learning, a subset of artiﬁcial
intelligence (AI) that teaches machines to learn with data, has emerged as a valuable
tool for uncovering these dynamics by using its unparalleled computational power
and ability to analyze complex relationships within the data, from student achievement
scores and psychosocial factors to resource availability (Jordan & Mitchell, 2015;
Yang, 2022). Machine learning enables systems to learn patterns from data without
being explicitly programmed for each task. By leveraging machine learning algorithms, education researchers can identify the most inﬂuential variables affecting
student outcomes and, in turn, might develop more effective predictive models.
Recent advancements in machine learning have spurred its application across
various ﬁelds, including healthcare, ﬁnance, manufacturing, and education. In healthcare, for instance, machine learning models are used for diagnosing diseases and predicting patient outcomes (Kumar et al., 2024; Simon & Aliferis, 2024). Similarly, in
ﬁnance, these algorithms aid in fraud detection and risk assessment (Yuan, 2024).
Within education, machine learning has been employed to develop personalized learning platforms, predict student performance, and enhance retention rates (e.g., Hussain
et al., 2019; Iatrellis et al., 2021; Musso et al., 2020; Ofori et al., 2020). Despite its
transformative potential, however, the ﬁeld of gifted education has been slow to
adopt machine learning techniques (Hodges & Mohan, 2019). This lag might represent
a missed opportunity to address pressing issues in our ﬁeld, such as the underrepresentation of racially and socioeconomically diverse populations in gifted programs
(Hodges et al., 2018; Yoon & Gentry, 2009).
Traditional identiﬁcation methods for gifted students, such as standardized tests
(e.g., Naglieri Nonverbal Ability Test (NNAT) and Cognitive Abilities Test
(CogAT)), have been criticized for their inability to mitigate disparities among racially
and socioeconomically diverse students. Lee et al. (2020) and Ozen et al. (2025) suggested that widely used intelligence quotient (IQ) tests often suffer from measurement
bias, speciﬁcally allegiance bias. Allegiance bias may arise when researchers afﬁliated
with a test unintentionally report more favorable outcomes (Zimmerman & Marcus,
2024). For example, in a meta-analysis of studies using the CogAT (Lohman, 2013)
for gifted identiﬁcation, Ozen et al. (2025) found that if the test creator was involved
in a study as an author, the test showed a signiﬁcantly higher correlation with other
existing metrics. A similar result was found for the NNAT (Lee et al., 2020). Both ﬁndings escalate the concerns about the commonly used IQ tests’ concurrent validity,
which both authors describe as the correlation of the IQ tests with other metrics.

Additionally, in a meta-analysis of gifted and talented identiﬁcation practices, Hodges
et al. (2018) found that Black and Latino/a/x1 students are 34% less likely to be identiﬁed for gifted programs compared to their White and Asian American peers. This disparity is often attributed to systemic issues such as unequal access to resources or
contextual factors such as family income (Aladangady & Forde, 2021; Peters,
2022). Reinvestigating the predictors of student success and the magnitude of these
impacts might be an important early step towards enabling educators and researchers
to recognize and eliminate factors that limit student success. For example, after selecting variables, counterweighing or minimizing the effect of the variables that students
cannot control, such as home educational resources, might reduce disparities and foster
inclusivity in gifted education programs.
The literature on predictors of science and mathematics achievement in the Trends
in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) data highlights several key
themes. Affective factors, such as self-conﬁdence and attitudes towards mathematics
and science, are relatively strong predictors of student achievement within the
created models (Butakor, 2016; Khine et al., 2015). Instructional quality and school
characteristics are critical as well, with inadequate school resources negatively impacting student outcomes (Coskun & Karadağ , 2023; Ruthven, 2011; Wiberg, 2019). In
meta-analytic research, Ruthven (2011) underscored the importance of pedagogical
development aimed at improving student attitudes and achievement, indicating that
instructional strategies play a signiﬁcant role in educational success. Additionally,
home resources have a signiﬁcant relationship with achievement in science and mathematics, with disparities linked to students’ backgrounds (Geesa et al., 2019; Topçu
et al., 2016). In a comparative study, Geesa et al. (2019) found that more home
resources result in higher mathematics achievement in South Korea, Türkiye, and
the United States, because additional resources at home increase students’ access to
the scholastic knowledge that supports their achievement.
The TIMSS dataset is a robust foundation for exploring machine learning applications in education. Previous studies have utilized TIMSS data to predict student performance in mathematics and science (Nadaf et al., 2022; Yoo, 2018), automate scoring of
open-ended questions (von Davier et al., 2023), and compare the performance of different machine learning models (Park et al., 2023). These efforts highlight the dataset’s
suitability for machine learning research, given its scale and breadth. Predicting students’ high and advanced levels in TIMSS data is fundamentally a grouping
problem in the context of machine learning, where the goal is to group students into
two categories: High-advanced or non-high-advanced. Yoo (2018) used all variables
provided in TIMSS 2011 for mathematics to create an elastic-net model to deﬁne
the models that predict if a student would reach the advanced level in South Korea,
and Yoo’s model reached an accuracy level of approximately 76%. Based on our literature research, we have not observed any similar studies using US data.
Selecting and creating a model requires analyzing diverse variables, such as
individual-level factors (e.g., attitudes toward learning) and teacher level and schoollevel inﬂuences (e.g., resource availability). Selecting and combining these variables
in a single model, such as a multi-level model, is a difﬁcult task and machine learning

The following research questions guided the study:
1. What are the key predictors of achieving high or advanced proﬁciency levels
(Levels 4 and 5) in the 2019 TIMSS mathematics and science benchmarks?
2. How well can elastic-net logistic regression predict students’ placement in high
or advanced (Levels 4 and 5) versus non-high or advanced proﬁciency levels
based on selected variables by elastic-net regression?

Method
Data Characteristics
The data for this study were drawn from the 2019 Trends in International Mathematics
and Science Study (TIMSS) dataset. TIMSS is an international assessment that measures trends in mathematics and science achievement worldwide (Mullis et al.,
2021). First administered in 1995, TIMSS is conducted every four years, assessing students in Grades 4 and 8 across approximately 70 countries. Our study focused on
8th-grade students from the United States who participated in TIMSS in 2019.
Eighth grade corresponds to eight years of schooling since the start of primary education. The dataset includes information about student achievement and such contextual
variables as demographic data, students’ self-concept and attitudes, instructional
quality, and resource availability. The breadth and depth of the TIMSS dataset
makes it ideal for machine-learning models that can identify and utilize trends and patterns and build models that will help educators predict student success in science and
math.

Data Preparation and Variables
We used and adapted Yoo’s (2018) data preparation and analysis steps. Initially, we
merged and processed the 2019 TIMSS data using the International Association for
the Evaluation of Educational Achievement’s International Data Analyzer (IEA’s
IDB; Version 5.0) to combine data ﬁles shared on the TIMSS website. Then, we completed the rest of the analysis on R Studio (Version 2024.09.1 + 394) with glmnet
package (Friedman et al., 2023).
Dependent Variables
TIMSS provides ﬁve plausible variables for each students’ science and mathematics
achievement. These plausible variables are random values from the posterior distributions and multiple imputed proﬁciency estimates (Jewsbury et al., 2024). Additionally,
from each plausible value (PV), TIMSS created categorical benchmark variables
valued from Levels 1 to 5. Level 1 represents Below Low, which has PVs below

400. Level 2 indicates Low, which has PVs between 400 and 475. Level 3 is
Intermediate, which has PVs between 475 and 550. Level 4 represents High, which
has PVs between 550 and 625. Lastly, Level 5 represents Advanced, which has PVs
above 625. We used benchmark variables to create our dependent variable. Students
in Level 4 and 5 were classiﬁed as high or advanced (coded as 1). All others were classiﬁed as non-high or advanced (coded as 0).
Since each student has ﬁve benchmark scores, we applied a majority vote method
(Breiman, 1996; Yoo, 2018) separately for each mathematics (BSMIBM01 to
BSMIBM05) and science (BSSIBM01 to BSSIBM05) score. A student was classiﬁed
as high or advanced if the majority (at least 3 out of 5) of their benchmark values were
at Levels 4 or 5. For example, if a student’s benchmark values were 4, 5, 4, 4, and 3, the
majority value (4) placed them in the advanced category. In the case of a tie between
values with the highest frequency (e.g., 4 and 3 each appearing twice), the higher value
was selected (e.g., 4) instead of dropping the student to avoid sample reduction. Table 1
shows the majority vote results for science and mathematics benchmark scores.
Independent Variables and Missing Data Handling
As independent (predictor) variables, we included any shared survey scores by TIMSS
in the elastic-net logistic regression model. A complete list of the variables that have
been included in the analysis is provided in Table 4. Since our binary dependent variable was derived from benchmark scores created from PVs, we excluded the raw PVs
for mathematics and science from the analysis. Regarding missing data, we followed
these steps: (1) Exclusion of cases with missing benchmark scores to ensure accurate
classiﬁcation, (2) Removal of variables with more than 10% missing data, leading to
the exclusion of four variables and (3) Application of listwise deletion to eliminate
cases with missing values across the remaining predictor variables. We acknowledge
that listwise deletion may not always be an ideal approach and acknowledge this as
a limitation for our study. However, we chose this approach due to the computational
complexity of modern missing data techniques (e.g., multiple imputation) for our
elastic-net logistic regression, which has already 10-fold cross-validation.
Additionally, since imputations would use entire data set to impute missing data,
this would result in data leakage from the test data to training data and from
one-fold to another, which is not an ideal approach for training and testing a model
in machine learning (Apicella et al., 2024).

Given the multitude of variables in international assessment tools like TIMSS, ﬁnding
the optimal model or combining relevant variables can feel like a nearly impossible
task for researchers. In this study, we used elastic-net logistic regression for both independent variable selection and model creation. Elastic-net combines Ridge regression
(L2 penalty) and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO; L1
penalty), enabling variable selection and addressing multicollinearity in datasets
with highly correlated predictors (Friedman et al., 2023). Logistic regression is often
used when the dependent variable is categorical. In our study, it is dichotomous; one
is used for high or advanced students and zero is used for the rest (G = 1). Therefore, a

The elastic-net logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify key predictors
while addressing multicollinearity and ensuring effective variable selection. The tuning
parameter, α, which was set to .5 in this study, as this value provides a balance between
Ridge and LASSO penalties, making it suitable for handling correlated predictors
(Hastie & Qian, 2014). To determine the penalty parameter, λ, we employed 10-fold
cross-validation procedure, which was suggested by creators of glmnet R packet
(Friedman et al., 2023). For binomial target variables, the cross-validation process
evaluated the model following these performance measures: (1) misclassiﬁcation
error, (2) area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), (3) binomial
deviance, (4) mean squared error (MSE), and (5) mean absolute error (MAE).
Misclassiﬁcation error uses the proportion of incorrectly classiﬁed observations,
reﬂecting the model’s overall accuracy in distinguishing between classes. AUC is a
measure of the model’s ability to distinguish between classes, with higher values indicating better discriminatory performance. Binomial deviance is a metric that quantiﬁes
the likelihood of the model predictions given the observed data, with lower values indicating a better ﬁt. MSE uses the average squared difference between predicted and
actual values, assessing the overall prediction error. MAE utilizes the average absolute
difference between predicted and actual values, providing a straightforward measure of
prediction accuracy (for more information, see Yoo, 2018). For each performance

Discussion
The ﬁndings of this study provide meaningful insights into the factors inﬂuencing
science and mathematics performance, as explored using elastic-net logistic regression
models on the TIMSS 2019 dataset. By leveraging two penalty parameters, λ1SE and
λmin, we were able to optimize the balance between model complexity and generalization, conﬁrming the utility of both penalty parameters. While λ1SE provides a simpler,
more interpretable model by excluding less critical variables, λmin offers a more inclusive approach, retaining variables that might otherwise be over penalized. This balance
ensures a comprehensive analysis of predictors.
The performance metrics indicated consistent results across both science and mathematics models. Accuracy remained the highest for mathematics (77.91%), with
science models achieving around 70% accuracy. Sensitivity, a critical metric for detecting rare cases such as high or advanced level students, was slightly lower but still
acceptable for mathematics models (68.23%) outperforming science models (64%).
Speciﬁcity, reﬂecting a models’ ability to identify non-high or advanced students correctly, showed a higher range in mathematics (80–90%) compared to science (70–
80%), indicating that mathematics models might be more robust in distinguishing
between student performance levels. Notably, the inclusion of both λ1SE and λmin demonstrated that these penalty parameters do not cause signiﬁcant variability in performance metrics within each cross-validation measure (e.g., AUC), further supporting
the robustness of the modeling approach.
The analysis of regression coefﬁcients in predicting student performance in science
and mathematics revealed signiﬁcant insights into the factors inﬂuencing students’ proﬁciency levels. The home educational resources index consistently was a positive,
strong predictor for both subjects, indicating that access to educational materials and
support at home plays a crucial role in facilitating student achievement. This ﬁnding
is supported by research indicating that the availability of resources, such as books
and internet access, signiﬁcantly correlates with improved academic outcomes in
both mathematics and science (Gao, 2023; Ker et al., 2023; Kim & Padilla, 2020).
Furthermore, the strong prediction of educational resources in the models aligns
with the ﬁnding that students with better access to educational materials tend to
perform better academically, as highlighted in studies examining the impact of home
environments on learning (Gao, 2023).
In addition to home educational resources, student conﬁdence was identiﬁed as a
particularly strong predictor, especially in mathematics, where it was the most inﬂuential factor across various models. This aligns with existing literature that emphasizes
the foundational role of self-efﬁcacy and conﬁdence in academic performance, particularly in challenging subjects like mathematics (Aş kın & Öz, 2020; Chan & Liem,
2023). The relationship between conﬁdence and achievement is well-documented, suggesting that students who believe in their abilities are more likely to engage with and

Ozen et al.

707

succeed in complex tasks (Aş kın & Öz, 2020). However, it is important to note that the
interplay between self-conﬁdence and academic performance in mathematics and
science is complex and multifaceted. High-performing students often demonstrate elevated self-efﬁcacy, which not only contributes to their success but also encourages
them to take on more challenging tasks. As students experience success in these subjects, their self-conﬁdence grows, further reinforcing their academic achievements. An
important aspect to consider is the reciprocal nature of the relationship between students’ high conﬁdence and their high performance; do students perform well and
then gain conﬁdence as a result, or does students’ conﬁdence result in high performance? The data cannot determine which is the case, but this dynamic relationship
highlights the need for educational strategies that promote self-efﬁcacy to enhance
student performance in mathematics and science.
The variable of disorderly behavior during math lessons, which was only available
in the mathematics models, showed positive coefﬁcients, indicating a negative impact
of students’ disruptions in classroom on learning outcomes. This ﬁnding underscores
the signiﬁcance of classroom management of students’ disruptive behaviors, which
have been shown to directly affect student engagement and achievement (Chan &
Liem, 2023; Chen, 2023). The presence of students’ disruptive behavior can hinder
their learning process and is reﬂected in their mathematics achievement. The exclusion
of variables such as instructional clarity and students’ value of science or mathematics
due to penalization raises important considerations regarding their predictive value.
While these factors are undoubtedly important, their effects may be overshadowed
by more dominant predictors such as home resources and student conﬁdence. This
observation is consistent with the idea that certain educational factors may interact
in complex ways, where the inﬂuence of some variables is mediated by others
(Chen, 2023). The nuanced nature of predictive modeling in gifted education highlights the need for targeted interventions that consider the interplay of various
factors affecting student performance.
These ﬁndings emphasize the multifaceted nature of student learning environments,
where access to resources, the cultivation of conﬁdence, and the maintenance of positive student behavior are critical for enhancing their performance in science and mathematics. Future research could further explore the interactions among these variables
and expand the analysis to include diverse datasets, thereby validating and enriching
our understanding of the predictors of academic success in these subjects.
Traditional statistical methods, such as Multi-Level Model and Generalized Linear
Models, have long served as the foundation of quantitative research by allowing
researchers to test hypotheses and establish relationships between variables. After
selecting the theory-based variables, researchers create their models and interpret the
results. Although these methods are robust for small-scale and well-structured data,
they might encounter challenges when applied to large-scale, high-dimensional datasets, as they require simplifying assumptions and may fail to account for complex relationships or interactions among variables. With the advent of machine learning
techniques, particularly regularization methods such as elastic-net in this study,
researchers now have powerful tools for analyzing large-scale data like TIMSS

708

Journal of Advanced Academics 36(4)

2019. These techniques enable variable selection and shrinkage, addressing issues of
multicollinearity while retaining important predictors. By combining predictive accuracy with interpretability, elastic-net offers a bridge between traditional statistical
approaches and advanced machine learning methodologies, making it particularly
well-suited for exploring nuanced educational phenomena, such as identifying
factors contributing to gifted education identiﬁcation. The present study demonstrated
the potential of these methods with large-scale educational datasets in gifted education,
providing insights beyond the capabilities of conventional approaches.
The use of machine learning to analyze data and predict students’ success in science
and math courses is a powerful and exciting method that holds great promise in helping
educators and administrators to identify and eliminate obstacles in students’ paths.
Multiple studies have shown that teachers’ evaluations of students are marred by
biases that result in the underrepresentation of minoritized students (Hodges et al.,
2018; Peters & Engerrand, 2016; Siegle et al., 2016).
There remain, however, limitations and downsides to machine learning in the
context of analyzing student data and predicting their outcomes. First, the use of historical data that reﬂect biases or inequities will teach the program to incorporate those
biases into its algorithms, thus perpetuating rather than eliminating disparities. Second,
there can be a black box problem, that is, the processes that the machine learning
program uses to come to its conclusions may not be fully clear or able to be explained
by the researchers. The inability to understand how results are reached will limit the
program’s usefulness for future iterations and research questions. Finally, a decrease
in human judgment and review and an overreliance on qualitative data and analysis
in the process of predicting student success risks the introduction of errors. For
example, the accurate interpretation of qualitative factors, such as teacher evaluations
or student self-ratings of their own self-efﬁcacy or psychosocial skills, may be difﬁcult
for a machine learning program. Further, analysis of student potential, creativity, or
achievement in a non-academic domain (e.g., leadership or the arts) may be challenging for the program. The impact of these errors on students’ self-perceptions, performances, and availability of opportunities could be signiﬁcant. A student who is
predicted to perform poorly may be discouraged by the results of the analysis. If a
model erroneously shows that students are likely to struggle in an above-level class,
they may be denied access to the class, a decision that will change the student’s academic trajectory and, ultimately, their career plans and possibilities.
Additionally, this study has several limitations. The cross-sectional nature of the
TIMSS 2019 dataset restricts the ability to infer causal relationships between predictors
and student achievement, suggesting the need for future research using longitudinal
data to explore temporal dynamics. The study’s focus on U.S. 8th-grade students, as
well as the limited set of variables used in the dataset, restricts the generalizability
of the ﬁndings to other grade levels, countries, or educational contexts. Self-reported
survey responses for key variables, such as student conﬁdence and classroom environment, are prone to social desirability bias and may not fully capture the complexity of
these constructs. Another limitation is the reliance on variables available only within
the TIMSS dataset; therefore, potentially inﬂuential factors not captured by TIMSS

Ozen et al.

709

could not be included in the analysis, which may limit the comprehensiveness of the
ﬁndings. Another limitation is the use of listwise deletion for handling missing data,
which we preferred to avoid potential data leakage from the model test to the training
data. Listwise deletion can lead to signiﬁcant reductions in sample size in large-scale
datasets like TIMSS. To limit that, we dropped the variables that had more than 10%
missing data; however, missing responses were particularly common in the teacher
questionnaire variables, and, therefore, we ended up dropping those four variables
from our analyses due to our decision to eliminate variables with over 10%
missing data.

Conclusion
Our ﬁndings have important implications for research, policy, and practice. The identiﬁcation of home educational resources or similar factors as critical predictors that are
beyond students’ control underscores the need for incorporating these factors into
gifted identiﬁcation protocols to identify more students from diverse backgrounds.
For example, by controlling for variables that students cannot inﬂuence or adding a
counterbalance for these factors, the identiﬁcation process can account for disparities
resulting from socioeconomic differences, ensuring that students with fewer resources
are not unfairly disadvantaged. It is important to emphasize that this suggestion does
not imply lowering the scores of students from higher socioeconomic backgrounds
but rather seeks to level the playing ﬁeld by addressing factors outside the students’
control. Moreover, the study demonstrates the potential of predictive analytics, such
as elastic-net models, to inform personalized learning interventions and improve
student outcomes. However, in every model created, this approach gave the strongest
coefﬁcient to a variable beyond students’ control. This is concerning, as the selection of
predictors from a large pool of variables should not be left entirely to automated
methods, particularly when the model is intended to serve a practical purpose, such
as selecting students for gifted programs. If, in the future, machine learning or AI
models are used to determine students’ proﬁciency levels, this could result in substantial ethical issues. Policymakers, educators, and researchers should exercise caution
when using these kinds of automated models in the context of human subjects.
Close supervision of these models is very crucial in cases in which the model is
used for such practical purposes as selecting high and advanced students, to
examine what variables are included in the models and their impact on the target variable. On the other hand, outright dismissal of these automated models is not advisable.
These models can still provide valuable insights, enabling policymakers to identify students at risk of being overlooked for gifted programs by penalizing or counterbalancing
variables beyond the students’ control. Policymakers can allocate resources effectively
and design targeted strategies to support these students with low home resources to
close the gap. Similarly, integrating different machine learning approaches into educational research could also help address systemic inequities in gifted education. Future
research should explore and expand on these methodologies to broader contexts and
datasets, enabling tailored recommendations for diverse educational systems.
