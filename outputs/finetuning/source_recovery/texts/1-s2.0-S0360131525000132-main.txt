A B S T R A C T
In recent decades, log-based process data has been increasingly used in computer-based assess­
ments to examine test-takers’ response patterns and latent traits. This study provides a systematic 
review of the use of log-based process data in computer-based assessments. Following the PRISMA 
(Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guideline, we identified 
2548 publications, of which 330 were finally included in this study after careful screening and 
full-text review. The results of this study can assist researchers in better understanding: (1) what 
are the trends in using log-based process data in computer-based assessments, (2) which process 
indicators have been constructed from raw log files, (3) what latent constructs have been inferred 
from process indicators and at what inferential levels, and (4) what are the benefits, challenges, 
and future recommendations for using log-based process data. By examining these questions, we 
conclude that the use of log-based process data in computer-based assessment shows many po­
tentials for enhancing the assessment. Therefore, more study using log-based process data in 
various fields is encouraged to better understand test-takers’ underlying response processes 
during assessments. Additionally, there is also a considerable demand for validating process in­
dicators and the generalizability of findings.
1. Introduction
Computer-based assessment has grown in popularity in recent decades due to its advantages over traditional paper-based 
assessment. One of the significant advantages is that it not only records the response product of each item but also provides the po­
tential to observe or infer test-takers response process based on log files generated by assessment systems (Goldhammer, Hahnel, 
Kroehne, & Zehner, 2021; Maddox, 2023). By analyzing these log-based process data, researchers could further examine test-takers’ 
behavioral patterns (Eichmann, Greiff, Naumann, Brandhuber, & Goldhammer, 2020; Hahnel, Ramalingam, Kroehne, & Goldhammer, 
2022) and even draw inferences about their abilities (Reis Costa, Bolsinova, Tijmstra, & Andersson, 2021; Stadler, Hofer, & Greiff, 
2020), motivations (Lundgren & Ekl¨of, 2020; Soland, 2019), and engagements (Azevedo, 2015; Nagy, Ulitzsch, & Lindner, 2023). 
Obtained findings not only could provide insight into whether learning has been achieved at a certain time in a summative function, 
but it also can be formatively used to enhance the learning and teaching process (Perry, Meissel, & Hill, 2022). In light of these new 
potentials, many studies have used log-based process data to examine test-takers’ behaviors and latent traits and develop advanced 
measurement models. However, no systematic review has been conducted to investigate a crucial question — how log-based process

1.1. Literature review
Researchers in the fields of educational and psychological measurement are usually interested in inferring latent constructs based 
on observable measures (DeVellis, 2017; Edwards & Bagozzi, 2000; Embretson, 2010). The term latent construct typically represents a 
postulated latent attribute of people (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955), while the observable measure refers to an observed score gathered 
through self-report, interview, observation, or some other means (Edwards & Bagozzi, 2000; Lord & Novick, 2008). In the traditional 
paper-based assessment, the observable measures are mainly obtained from test-takers’ answers or solutions to an assessment task or 
item, which can be seen as the “product” of their work (Zumbo, Maddox, & Care, 2023), and are further used to infer test-takers’ latent 
attributes. It is important to note that relying solely on product measures may lead to gaps in inferring latent constructs. This is because 
product measures do not provide information about the response processes that test-takers used to arrive at their answers or solutions. 
Therefore, it can be challenging to determine whether test-takers approached the tasks in the intended ways, or if different test-takers 
used different strategies to arrive at the same solution (Ercikan, Guo, & He, 2020). Hence, response processes have been valued as 
evidence for interpreting the fit between the construct and product measures in recent decades. The Standards for Educational and 
Psychological Testing (shortly, Standards) has highlighted response processes as one of five sources of validity evidence in its recent two 
editions (AERA, APA, & NCME, 1999; 2014).
Despite the significance of the response process, there is no widely accepted definition yet. The Standards do not provide a clear 
conceptual or operational definition of response processes but only mention them as psychological processes and cognitive operations 
in the Standards for Validity section (AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014, p. 26; Hubley & Zumbo, 2017). In recent years, researchers have 
attempted to define it in several ways. For example, Ercikan and Pellegrino (2017) stated that response processes refer to test-takers’ 
thought processes, strategies, approaches, and behaviors when they read, interpret, and formulate solutions to assessment tasks. In 
light of this definition, Hubley and Zumbo (2017) further expanded the response processes as the mechanisms that underlie what 
people do, think, or feel when interacting with and responding to the item or task and are responsible for generating observed test score 
variation. This latest definition of response processes as underlying mechanisms extends beyond the cognitive realm to include 
emotions, motivations, and behaviors (Hubley & Zumbo, 2017). Given that these underlying mechanisms can be considered as latent 
attributes of people, in line with the definition of latent constructs, Zumbo et al. (2023) introduced two distinct categories of con­
structs: test constructs and process constructs. Specifically, test constructs are theoretical constructs targeted by the assessment and 
reflected in the product-oriented test-taker response data to the items or tasks in the test; process constructs are underlying response 
processes and reflected in the process data recorded by the various sensors as test-takers perform actions while completing the task 
(Zumbo et al., 2023). In sum, the relationship between different constructs and measures can be depicted in Fig. 1; process constructs 
can serve as validity evidence when using product measures to infer test constructs, and process constructs can be inferred from process 
measures, such as think-aloud protocols, cognitive interview, eye-tracking, and computer log files.
Process measures, or process data, can be collected from various sources. These sources include think-aloud protocol, cognitive 
interviews, ethnographic/anthropological observations (e.g., stand, position, glance, gesture, and talk), video recording, screen 
capturing, eye tracking, physiological measures, post-assessment questionnaires, and computer log files (Azevedo, 2015; Goldhammer 
& Zehner, 2017; Hubley & Zumbo, 2017; Lindner & Greiff, 2023; Maddox, 2017, 2023). Among them, the process data from computer 
log files has received growing attention in recent years due to its several advantages. One of the significant advantages is that the data 
collection mechanism of computer log files is relatively less intrusive or even completely unobtrusive (Goldhammer et al., 2021). The

Due to these advantages, process data from computer log files has been increasingly collected in recent decades. For example, 
several well-known international large-scale assessments, including the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), the 
Programme for the International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC), the Trends in International Mathematics and Science

other small-scale online course assessments embedded in learning management systems. Based on these collected data, researchers 
have conducted many studies to examine students’ latent traits and test-taking processes, such as problem-solving processes (Ren et al., 
2019), reading and writing strategies (Hahnel et al., 2022; Tate & Warschauer, 2019), test-taking engagements (Azevedo, 2015), or 
test navigation patterns (He, Borgonovi, & Su´arez-´Alvarez, 2023).
Despite the increasing use of log-based process data in empirical studies, to the best of our knowledge, there has not been a sys­
tematic review that has summarized which process constructs were investigated, what data sources are commonly used, how to pre- 
process and analyze data, and what are benefits, challenges, and recommendations for using log-based process data in computer-based 
assessments. Only one study has systematically reviewed the ethical and regulatory issues of using process data in educational 
assessment (Murchan & Siddiq, 2021). In addition, another non-systematic review (Reis Costa & Leoncio Netto, 2020) summarized the 
use of process data in international large-scale assessments, mainly in the PISA and PIAAC. The main focus of their review was to 
organize the analysis level of process data under a six-layered ecological framework, including (1) examining item characteristics, (2) 
examining test characteristics, (3) linking process data with personal characteristics, (4) linking process data with classroom contexts, 
(5) linking process data with family/other outside-of-school ecology, (6) linking process data with educational system and state/­
country context. Based on this ecological framework, the author categorized all 36 identified papers, revealing that the process data 
were mainly analyzed in layers 1, 2, 3, and 6.
In addition to these review papers, several conceptual papers discussed the use of process data in educational assessment and 
proposed important frameworks for the use of process data (Bergner & Von Davier, 2019; Goldhammer et al., 2021; Hubley & Zumbo, 
2017; Lindner & Greiff, 2023; Maddox, 2023; Zumbo et al., 2023). For example, Bergner and Von Davier (2019) examined how NAEP 
collected and analyzed process data over the past two decades to better understand the historical trends in NAEP’s use of process data. 
The authors proposed an ordered five-level framework to describe the relative importance of process data in relation to outcomes. The 
structure of levels is as follows: (1) Level 1: process data is irrelevant or at least ignorable, and only the outcomes are considered; (2) 
Level 2: process data is auxiliary to understanding the outcome, and process data may be understood through independent measures; 
(3) Level 3: process data is essential to understanding the outcome, and process data are measured in the same task that generates 
outcomes, also the outcome scores account for process features; (4) Level 4: process data is the outcome, and process scores are derived 
from an expert rubric, in the sense of a holistic rating; (5) Level 5: process data is the outcome, and process scores are derived from a 
measurement model that accounts for dependencies in sequential data (Bergner & Von Davier, 2019; Jiang, Gong, Saldivia, 
Cayton-Hodges, & Agard, 2021). Based on this framework, the authors indicated that the majority of NAEP process data was used at 
level 2 or level 4, whereas levels 3 and 5, which could potentially provide more valuable insights to support the assessment arguments, 
have been largely overlooked.
Another important conceptual paper was based on research conducted using the PIAAC and PISA process data. Goldhammer et al. 
(2021) have suggested that computer log data and the indicators derived from it can be used to deduce different cognitive or

motivational attributes of the work process. To validate constructed variables from log files, they have proposed a four-level evi­
dence-centered design framework, which is shown in Fig. 2. The framework consists of two reasonings to link raw log data with latent 
constructs: levels of extraction (data-driven reasoning) and evidence-centered design (theory-driven reasoning). The data-driven 
reasoning is typically based on data-mining techniques to explore patterns or regularities in low-level features (i.e., the smallest 
unit of meaningful actions or states, such as delete or backspace action in the keystroke logs) derived from original log files, and further 
constructs process indicators (i.e., aggregated features, such as the total number of delete or backspace action in the keystroke logs) to 
infer high-level latent constructs (e.g., test-takers’ writing propensity). In the data-driven reasoning, two intermediate inferential steps 
were included between raw log data and latent constructs. This is because raw log files are mainly generated and stored for system 
monitoring and debugging, so they may not be meaningful or useful from an assessment standpoint (Hao & Mislevy, 2018). In addition, 
the format of log files is platform-specific, making it difficult to compare and reproduce findings when using different assessment 
systems. By constructing observable low-level features and process indicators, more meaningful and generic representations can be 
used to infer test-takers’ latent attributes (Goldhammer et al., 2021). The theory-driven reasoning, on the other hand, typically starts 
with a theoretical assessment framework, defines latent attributes to be measured (i.e., student model) and correspondence empirical 
evidence (i.e., evidence model), designs situations or tasks eliciting desired behaviors (i.e., task/activity model), and measures the 
work process based on computer log files.
1.2. The present study
Drawing on existing literature, the current study aims to fill the gap by conducting a systematic review of the use of log-based 
process data in computer-based assessments. Given that most studies used log-based process data in a data-driven way, this study 
intends to investigate the following research questions based on data-driven reasoning in the evidence-centered design framework 
(Goldhammer et al., 2021). Firstly, at the raw data level, we aim to examine the trends in using log-based process data in 
computer-based assessment, including the number of publications over the years, the data sources that have been used (e.g., PISA, 
PIAAC, NAEP, TIMSS, or other assessments), and the distribution of sample demographics (e.g., preschool, primary, middle, high, 
postsecondary students, or other adults) involved in the data collection. Secondly, at the low-level feature and process indicator level, 
our primary goal is to summarize the types of process indicators constructed and the approaches used to construct them. This involves 
pre-processing procedures of transforming raw log files and low-level features into the process indicators. It is important to note that 
this review does not cover the types of previously constructed low-level features, as their construction is highly platform-specific and 
offers limited generalizable insights.
Thirdly, at the latent construct level, we aim to synthesize the types of latent constructs inferred from process indicators and their 
operationalizations. In addition, we also intend to summarize the inferential levels that have been used to infer latent constructs based 
on process indicators. In our study, we categorize the inferential levels into four distinct levels. Specifically, (1) Level 1: process in­
dicators are used as observed variables to predict task performance or being predicted by other variables. For instance, the number of 
actions is used to predict the success of problem-solving; (2) Level 2: process indicators are used to infer latent constructs based on 
some rules or criteria. For example, item response time is used to filter rapid guessing and solution behavior based on the normative 
threshold approach; (3) Level 3: process indicators are used to infer latent constructs by being incorporated into holistic approaches. 
For example, the action sequence is incorporated into a coding scheme or network model to infer holistic problem-solving strategies; 
(4) Level 4: process indicators are used to build latent constructs by being incorporated into measurement models. For instance, item 
response time is incorporated into the lognormal response time model to build a latent speed construct.
These levels of inference applied in this study are mainly based on Bergner and Von Davier’s (2019) five-level framework with two 
modifications. The first modification is to remove the original level 1 (i.e., process data is irrelevant or at least ignorable, and only the 
outcomes are considered) from the inference levels, since all empirical studies identified in this review include the log-based process 
data. As a result, there will be four inferential levels in total. The second modification is to replace the term “outcome” with “latent 
construct”. In other words, instead of examining the relative importance of process data in relation to outcome, we intend to identify 
the relative inference level of process data in relation to latent construct for each empirical study. The reasoning behind this is that the 
original framework was proposed to understand the historical trends or evolution in NAEP’s use of process data. Therefore, in the 
original framework, the relative importance of process data in relation to outcome was mainly considered for the scoring purpose. 
However, in practice, process data was used for various purposes, such as defining latent test-taking speed (van der Linden, 2007), 
detecting rapid guessing (Wise, 2017), identifying aberrant responses (Gorgun & Bulut, 2022), or examining web-navigation strategies 
(Gao, Cui, Bulut, Zhai, & Chen, 2022). Only using the term “outcome” may not be enough to cover all these uses, so the term “latent 
construct” will be used to refer to a broader use of process data. Finally, this study also intends to summarize the benefits, potential 
challenges, and recommendations for using log-based process data from multiple perspectives.
In summary, this study aims to investigate the following four key questions regarding the use of log-based process data in computer- 
based assessments: (1) What are the trends in using log-based process data? (2) Which process indicators have been constructed? (3) 
What latent constructs have been inferred from process indicators, and at what inferential levels? (4) What are the benefits, challenges, 
and future recommendations for using log-based process data?

3. Results
3.1. Trend in using process data
The descriptive analysis of reviewed papers showed that the number of publications using process data in computer-based as­
sessments has increased exponentially over the past two decades (see Fig. 4). At the dawn of the 21st century, researchers began to 
explore response processes during computer-based assessments, leading to a steady but persistent increase in related studies. This 
growth trajectory reached a pivotal point in 2015, largely fueled by the availability of data from international large-scale assessments 
such as PISA and PIAAC. This pivotal moment marked the beginning of a considerable increase in research activity, witnessing a 
notable count of 64 studies using process data in 2023.
In all publications, around 40% of research used process data from international large-scale assessments, including PISA (25%), 
PIAAC (9%), NAEP (5%), and TIMSS (1%). The remaining research used process data from testing companies (e.g., Educational Testing 
Service, Pearson, and Duolingo; 9%), formative course assessments embedded in learning management systems (10%), or other 
sources (41%), such as game-based assessment and various large-scale or small-scale assessments. Furthermore, the distribution of 
sample demographics in these publications is also diverse. A large proportion of studies obtained data from high school students (34%), 
followed by undergraduate and graduate students (18%), middle school students (17%), other adults (15%), primary school students 
(8%), and preschool students (1%). Approximately 7% of publications did not specify sample demographics.
3.2. Process indicators
After collecting the data, various pre-processing procedures were applied to clean the data and extract meaningful information. 
According to the evidence-centered design framework from Goldhammer et al. (2021), two types of meaningful information can be 
extracted: low-level features and process indicators. Low-level features are the smallest unit of meaningful process components. It

includes two kinds: action (behavior occurs at a certain time point) and state (sub-process or a fraction of the behavioral stream at a 
certain duration of time). Low-level features are typically platform-specific and task-specific. For instance, one commonly used task is 
the Traffic task from PISA 2012 (see task screenshot from Fig. 5; https://www.oecd.org/pisa/test-2012/testquestions/question2/). In 
the task, test-takers were required to find the quickest route from Diamond to Einstein which took 31 min in total. From the raw log 
files, at least 5 low-level actions can be identified, which are start-item, click, double-click, choose specific route, and end-item (see 
more from Lundgren & Ekl¨of, 2020). Unlike the Traffic task, which only relies on mouse clicks, writing-related tasks typically record 
both mouse clicks and keystrokes, resulting in different types of low-level features. For example, Zhu et al. (2019) identified several 
keystroke actions, including insert, delete, copy, paste, jump, and replace. Guo, Zhang, Deane, and Bennett (2020) identified four types 
of low-level states, including long-pause state, text-production state, local editing state, and jump editing state. Different from the 
abovementioned tasks, tasks from game-based assessments included even various types of low-level features. Taking 3-D Immersive 
Virtual Assessments from Jiang, Clarke-Midura, Baker, Paquette, and Keller (2018) as an example (see task screenshot from Fig. 5), 
test-takers were asked to figure out two questions (i.e., why a frog has grown six legs and why the bees are dying) by walking around a 
village, talking to NPCs, reading information pages, making observations, and conducting laboratory tests. In this task context, 
low-level actions are talk, inspect, read, test, look, take note, etc. Given that low-level features are highly context-specific, it’s difficult 
to summarize all low-level features identified in previous literature.
Process indicators, however, as aggregations of low-level features, are more comparable across different studies and can therefore 
provide more generalizable insights. This study categorized process indicators into three types: time indicators, count indicators, and 
sequence indicators, considering that computer log data is the time-stamped action sequence data composed of time, actions, and 
sequence information (Goldhammer et al., 2021). Time indicators are constructed only based on time-related information, count 
indicators are constructed via counting the number of actions or states, and sequence indicators are constructed by considering the 
sequence of actions or states. In the empirical study, these three indicators can be used separately or together with others to represent 
underlying response processes. According to our review, the most common way to use process indicators is only using time indicators 
(51%), followed by the combination of time and count indicators (19%), only sequence indicators (11%), the combination of time and 
sequence indicators (6%), the combination of all three indicators (6%), only count indicators (5%), and the combination of count and 
sequence indicators (2%). Overall, time indicators are the most often used indicators, being included in 82% of studies, followed by 
count indicators (32%), and sequence indicators (25%).
Previous research has constructed various time, count, and sequence indicators. Table 1 provides a summary of process indicators, 
and references for each. Specifically, time indicators include time spent on specific items, tasks, tests, pages, locations, states, or 
actions. It also includes time spent from the start to the occurrence of specific actions or responses, time spent from the occurrence of 
specific actions or responses to the end, and time between two actions, words, sentences, or paragraphs. In addition, there are also time 
of individual test-takers or synchronized time of grouped test-takers, timestamps of specific actions, and the proportion of time spent 
on specific items, pages, tasks, or actions. All these time indicators can be the total, mean, medium, or log-transformed time. Log- 
transformed time indicators are commonly used since the distribution of response time typically takes values in the positive reals 
and has a long tail (van der Linden, 2006). Count indicators, on the other hand, usually include the count of all actions in tasks or tests, 
count of specific actions, counts of specific items, pages, states, or tasks that test-takers have visited, count of actions per minute or in a

specific duration period, and counts of attempts or submission have been made. In addition, based on low-level keystroke actions, 
researchers also constructed the length of the burst, which counts the continuous keystrokes without any pauses. Finally, count in­
dicators can also include the proportion of specific actions over the total number of actions. Similar to time indicators, all these count

Compared to time and count indicators, the construction of sequence indicators is much more complicated and involves more 
advanced techniques. Based on the order of low-level features, previous studies constructed the sequence of duration time, sequence of 
actions or events, and sequence of states or subtasks. In addition to these basic sequence indicators, previous studies also constructed 
sequence distance indicators based on sequence mining techniques. These sequence distance indicators describe how action sequences 
differ either from each other or with respect to pre-defined sequences (He, Borgonovi, & Paccagnella, 2021). Commonly used sequence 
distance indicators include edit distance, longest common subsequence distance, and dynamic time wrapping distance. The edit 
distance, also known as the Levenshtein distance, calculates the minimum number of editing operations (i.e., deletions, insertions, and 
substitutions) required to transform one sequence into another. The longest common subsequence (LCS) distance identifies the longest 
subsequence common to two sequences and calculates the degree of closeness between two sequences as its length. The dynamic time 
wrapping distance calculates the optimal warping path between two sequences based on dynamic programming (see more description 
about three distance indicators from He et al., 2021, 2023). According to these sequence distance measures, previous studies also 
defined four indexes: similarity (i.e., the ratio between the length of LCS and the length of the reference sequence), efficiency (i.e., the 
ratio between the length of LCS and the length of the actual sequence), consistency of similarity (i.e., similarity for each person across 
items), and consistency of efficiency (i.e., the standard deviation of the efficiency for each person across items)(He et al., 2021; Jiang, 
Cayton-Hodges, Nabors Ol´ah, & Minchuk, 2023). Moreover, other sequence indicators include n-gram sequences (He, von Davier, & 
Han, 2019) as well as vectors generated from multidimensional scaling (Tang, Wang, He, Liu, & Ying, 2020) and recurrent neural 
network-based sequence-to-sequence autoencoders (Tang, Wang, Liu, & Ying, 2021). To be specific, n-gram sequences are generated 
based on n-gram language modeling, which treats an action sequence as a sequence of integers or symbols in the language context (e.g.,

3.3. Latent constructs and inferential levels
After constructing process indicators, previous research used them mainly in four different levels to infer latent constructs. At the 
first level, process indicators were used as observed variables to independently relate with other variables, without inferring any 
latent constructs. Therefore, at this level, process indicators can be analyzed based on any statistical or machine-learning approach. At 
the second level, process indicators were used as indicators to infer latent constructs based on some rules or criteria. These rules or 
criteria are explicit, clear-cut, and crafted, and typically operate on sets of “if-then” logic. At the third level, process indicators were 
used to infer latent constructs in the sense of a holistic perspective, based on methodologies such as qualitative coding schemes, 
sequential pattern mining, process mining, network analysis, and clustering. At this level, the inferred latent constructs primarily 
concern patterns and profiles of response processes. At the fourth level, process indicators were used as indicators to build latent 
constructs by being incorporated into measurement models. Constructed latent constructs at this level typically refer to test-takers’ 
specific propensity, which can be modeled as continuous or categorical latent variables. These propensities might represent underlying 
abilities, attitudes, or other psychological traits that are not directly observable but are inferred from test-taking processes. Previous 
studies applied these inference levels either individually or in combination to investigate various latent constructs. The most prevalent 
inference level is level 4 (36%), followed by level 1 (29%), level 2 (22%), and level 3 (13%). However, over half of the publications that 
applied level 4 inference focus on constructing latent response speed based on the log-normal response time model or its variants. If 
these papers are not counted, the proportion of papers employing four inference levels follows the order of level 1, level 2, level 3, and 
level 4. All inferred latent constructs in each level and the number of publications that include them can be seen in Table 2. Given that 
the operationalization methods vary across each inference level, the following sections provide detailed descriptions of the types of 
latent constructs inferred and their operationalizations separately for levels 2, 3, and 4.
3.3.1. Latent constructs at the inference level 2
Commonly inferred latent constructs at level 2 are test-taking engagement-related constructs. As shown in Table 2, these constructs 
include cheating, quitting, rapid guessing, test-taking effort, persistence, and wheel-spinning. The definition and operationalization of 
these constructs are as follows. (1) Cheating is operationalized based on the Bayesian residual index ( Wang, Xu, & Shang, 2018), 
Guttman error-time index (Alexandron, Wiltrout, Berg, Gershon, & Ruip´erez-Valiente, 2023), or Kullback-Leibler Distance index (KL) 
(Man, Harring, Ouyang, & Thomas, 2018). For example, in Man et al. (2018), cheating is defined as having pre-knowledge of some 
items and the real-time copying of answers from other test-takers. If a person produces a high KL value, it means that the person has an 
erratic pattern that differs substantially from the behavior of the group, denoted as cheating. (2) Quitting is defined as test-takers not 
reaching the end of test, or test-takers’ total response time falls below the time limit (Ulitzsch, Matthias, & Pohl, 2020). (3) Rapid 
guessing is identified based on several approaches, including the normative threshold method (NT; Wise, 2006), the change in in­
formation and accuracy method (ChIA; Wise, 2019), the cumulative proportion method (CUMP; Guo et al., 2016), Bayesian residual 
index (C. Wang et al., 2018), Guessing Behaviors Index (GBI; Chang, Plake, & Kramer, 2011), and visual inspection approach (Nagy 
et al., 2023), etc. For instance, in the normative threshold method, responses with an item response time less than 10% (or 5%, 15%, 
20%) of the mean item response time are classified as rapid guessing, while others are considered solution behaviors. A comprehensive 
review of identifying rapid guessing based on item response can be seen by Rios and Deng (2021). (4) Test-taking effort is mainly 
operationalized as response time effort (RTE) and response behavior efforts (RBE). RTE is the proportion of items for which test-takers 
exhibited solution behavior, and an examinee exhibiting solution behavior on all items would attain the maximum RTE score of 1.0. 
Using RTE, a student was identified as unmotivated for a given test if he or she did not spend enough time on at least 90% of a test’s 
items to read those items. RBE is a more general measure of test-takers’ effort than RTE by considering three types of test-taking 
behaviors as non-effortful (i.e., rapid guesses, rapid omits, or rapid perfunctory). RBE is calculated by 1- (total number of responses 
classified as non-effortful/total number of presented items), with higher values indicating higher test taker effort. In addition, Chang 
et al. (2011) proposed the test-taking effort index (TEI) and individual test-taking effort index (ITEI) to operationalize test-taking 
effort. Specifically, TEI is an index designed to quantify the response time difference between correct and incorrect items for each 
item/item block for different individuals (or different groups of examinees). ITEI is an index designed to measure the magnitude of the 
response time difference ratio between correct and incorrect items for each item/item block for an individual (or a group of exam­
inees). (5) Persistence is operationalized either based on time spent on unsolved items (Ventura & Shute, 2013) or counts of attempts 
more than once on an item (Yang & Ogata, 2023). Wheel spinning, however, is operationalized as attempts more than once on an item 
without making substantial progress (i.e., failed in around 33.3% of attempts).
In addition, previous studies also inferred test-taking strategy-related constructs. The definition and operationalization of these 
constructs are as follows. (1) Planning is mainly measured by planning time, and operationalized by three indicators, which are the 
duration of the longest interval between two successive actions, the delay of the longest interval between any two successive actions, 
and the variance of interval between each two successive actions (Eichmann, Goldhammer, Greiff, Pucite, & Naumann, 2019). (2) 
Sourcing is defined as attending to, evaluating, and using available or accessible information about the sources of documents, 
including proactive sourcing, repeated sourcing, task-related sourcing, and general sourcing (Hahnel et al., 2019). These sourcing 
strategies are operationalized based on four dichotomous variables. Specifically, proactive sourcing is operationalized by whether the 
source was accessed within the first 10% of the document processing time; repeated sourcing is operationalized by whether the source

instance, Arslan et al. (2020) operationalized the source-focused problem-solving strategy in drag-and-drop items as action sequences 
that the sources are selected and dragged sequentially (e.g., an action sequence, where the first source, second source, third source, 
fourth source, and fifth source were dragged and dropped into the corresponding targets, in that order). Whereas target-focused 
problem-solving strategy is operationalized as action sequences that the targets are filled sequentially (e.g., an action sequence of 
filling the first target, second target, and third target, in that order). In addition, vary one thing at a time (VOTAT; also known as 
control of variables strategy) and vary nothing at a time (NOTAT; also known as noninterfering observation strategy) strategies are also 
two commonly examined problem-solving strategies in scientific reasoning and knowledge acquisition. VOTAT strategy implies that 
one variable is systematically varied at a time, while all other variables remain constant so that problem solvers can isolate the variable 
that causes an effect. NOTAT strategy refers to actively observing how the problem environment behaves by itself without any 
interference instead of actively exploring the problem environment (Greiff, Niepel, Scherer, & Martin, 2016). Both strategies can be 
operationalized by test-takers action sequences. In addition, the NOTAT strategy can also be operationalized based on the relative 
number of tasks across the total number of tasks in which a student at least once applied an intervention classified as noninterfering 
observation (Greiff et al., 2016; Lotz, Scherer, Greiff, & Sparfeldt, 2022). (5) Self-regulated learning refers to the self-directed and 
self-regulated strategy that test-takers apply to enhance their learning. It was operationalized as orientation, planning, evaluation, 
monitoring, first reading, rereading, and elaboration components based on test-takers’ action sequences. For example, the evaluation 
process is the process that has the actions sequence of reading – (navigation) – general instruction/rubric – (navigation) – re-reading 
(Rakovi´c et al., 2023). Other inferred latent constructs at level 2 are test-taking speed-related constructs. Specifically, speed is usually 
operationalized based on item response time. For example, Coomans, Hofman, Brinkhuis, van der Maas, and Maris (2016) defined fast 
responses as those given before half of the allotted time has expired and considered all other responses slow responses. In addition, the 
pace is defined as a constant tempo established and maintained by test-takers while they progress through an exam, which also refers to 
test-takers’ feeling of knowing (Lee & Haberman, 2016; Thompson, Yang, & Chauvin, 2009). For example, Thompson et al. (2009)
operationalized pace as the fraction of theoretically available time not taken to answer the question.
3.3.2. Latent constructs at the inference level 3
Latent constructs inferred at level 3 can be organized into three categories. (1) The first category is skill-related constructs, 
including collaborative problem-solving skills (Li, P¨oys¨a-Tarhonen, & H¨akkinen, 2023; Stoeffler, Rosen, Bolsinova, & von Davier, 
2020; Zhu & Todd, 2019), computational thinking skills (Rowe et al., 2021), and scientific skills (Cui, Chu, & Chen, 2019). These 
constructs are primarily operationalized based on qualitative coding of log-based process data. For example, Stoeffler et al. (2020)
inferred four levels of effectiveness in collaborative problem-solving perspective-taking skills based on the combination of test-takers’ 
response, clickstream, and interaction data. These inferred skill levels are also used for scoring purposes to rate test-takers’ perfor­
mances. (2) The second category is test-taking profile-related constructs, identified mainly based on various clustering techniques. 
These constructs include profiles or clusters of specific behavioral patterns, including using support functions (Hicks, Circi, & Li, 2019), 
reviewing and revisiting items (Zhang, Andersson, & Greiff, 2023), searching (Tenison & Sparks, 2023), time allocation (Guo, 2022), 
internet cheating (Garg & Goel, 2023), writing (Bennett, Zhang, Sinharay, Guo, & Deane, 2022; Tate & Warschauer, 2019), reading 
instructions and responding to items (Hahnel et al., 2022; He et al., 2023). For example, using k-means clustering techniques, Garg and 
Goel (2023) identified two profiles: cheating and non-cheating profiles. The cheating profile has a high tab-switch count, implying that 
students in this profile have frequently left the assessment window to switch to other browser tabs. However, the non-cheating profile 
is primarily characterized by very low values of tab-switch count, indicating that students in this profile stick to the assessment window 
and do not switch to other browser tabs. In addition to profiling specific behavioral patterns, some profiles detail test-takers’ strategies 
for solving problems and approaching the overall test. This includes profiles for goal pursuing strategies for multiple goal-balancing

3.3.3. Latent constructs at the inference level 4
Latent constructs at level 4 can be classified into five categories: latent ability, latent speed, latent engagement, latent behavioral 
tendency, and latent class-related constructs. These latent constructs are defined by incorporating process indicators into various 
measurement models, including the item response theory (IRT) model and its variants, structural equation models, finite mixture 
models, and latent class or latent profile models. A detailed explanation of latent constructs in each category is as follows. (1) Inferred 
latent constructs in the first category are ability-related constructs, including generic latent ability theta parameter (e.g., Tang, 2023), 
latent knowledge state (Ranger & Kuhn, 2014), planning ability (Zhang, Andersson, & Greiff, 2023), collaboration ability (Andrews 
et al., 2017), and collaborative problem-solving ability (Scoular & Care, 2020). For instance, as illustrated in Fig. 6, the generic latent 
ability θ parameter can be better estimated by incorporating item response time (middle image; Reis Costa et al., 2021) or action 
sequence (right image; Tang, 2023) into the IRT models. (2) Latent constructs in the second category are speed-related constructs, 
including general response speed to answering items (e.g., van der Linden, 2006, 2007), speed of computer operating (Goldhammer, 
Naumann, & Keßel, 2013), speed of word recognition (Goldhammer et al., 2013), and speed of performing first action in the task 
(Klotzke & Fox, 2019). These speed-related constructs are typically defined by various time indicators constructed in Section 3.2. 
Response speed is the most commonly investigated latent speed construct, typically defined by speed parameter τ in the log-normal 
response time model proposed by van der Linden (2006) (see left image in Fig. 6). (3) Latent constructs in the second category are 
engagement-related constructs, including latent engagement (e.g., Bulut, Gorgun, & Karamese, 2023), test-taking persistence (e.g., 
Nagy et al., 2023), test endurance or tendency to discontinue (Ranger & Kuhn, 2014; Ulitzsch et al., 2020), and level of certainty 
(referring to how certain the students want to be before answering a question; Papamitsiou & Economides, 2017). For example, latent 
engagement refers to test-takers’ tendency to maintain their effort throughout the test. It is typically constructed by modeling be­
haviors indicative of rapid disengagement (e.g., rapid guessing, rapid perfunctory responses, and rapid omissions) along with solution 
behaviors in measurement models (Bulut et al., 2023). (4) Constructs in the fourth category are behavioral tendency-related con­
structs, including latent actions or traces (De Boeck & Scalise, 2019; Liang, Tu, & Cai, 2023), latent exploration tendency (Costa & 
Chen, 2023; M. Zhang, Andersson, & Greiff, 2023), revisit propensity (Bezirhan, von Davier, & Grabovsky, 2021) and writing pro­
pensity (e.g., Bennett et al., 2022). Among them, latent action or trace indicates the intensity of actions and is usually measured by 
incorporating the number of actions into the measurement models. In addition, writing propensity includes various fine-grained 
writing-related constructs, such as between-word speed, writing fluency, writing productivity, paraphrasing, etc. These 
fine-grained constructs are mainly created based on conducting exploratory factor analysis on a large amount of keystroke count 
indicators (Bennett et al., 2022). (5) The final category is latent class-related constructs, including the latent class of VOTAT strategy, 
work pace, web navigation patterns, test-taking engagement, writing, problem-solving, and collaborative problem-solving. They are 
constructed as latent categorical variables to indicate various classes of latent strategies, engagements, or test-taking patterns. 
Commonly used measurement models for constructing them include latent class model, latent profile model, latent space model, and 
mixture IRT model.

3.4. Benefits, challenges, and recommendations
Drawing on previous research on the opportunities, challenges, and ethical considerations in using process data (Lindner & Greiff, 
2023; Murchan & Siddiq, 2021), this study summarized the benefits and challenges of using process data from four perspectives: 
theoretical grounding, data collection and analysis, practical implication, and ethical consideration (see Table 3). In addition, four 
aspects of future recommendations were given for the better use of process data.
3.4.1. Benefits
Regarding the benefits of using process data, from the theoretical perspective, process data could provide more information than 
product data for examining response processes and revealing why and how test-takers differ in response products. Findings regarding 
response processes may facilitate the development of process-oriented theories, which have gained increasing importance in educa­
tional psychology (D¨ornyei, 2000; Janssen, Kirschner, Erkens, Kirschner, & Paas, 2010; Kimmerle, Moskaliuk, Oeberst, & Cress, 2015). 
In addition, process data also could provide validity evidence for other inferences (Cui et al., 2019; Engelhardt & Goldhammer, 2019; 
Soland, 2019). For example, item response time was used as the validity evidence for students’ motivation and self-efficacy (Soland, 
2019) and test score interpretation (Engelhardt & Goldhammer, 2019). Second, from the data collection and analysis perspective, 
compared to other data collection approaches, process data is more cost- and time-efficient, easier to collect huge volumes of 
fine-grained data from a large number of participants, and unobtrusive to performance or thinking process (Biswas et al., 2017). Third, 
from the practical implication perspective, process data is helpful to item development, test design, and scoring, which is especially 
useful in pre-testing and field-testing situations (He et al., 2021; Zhu, Shu, & Davier, 2016). For instance, when analyzing the process 
data from PIAAC, He et al. (2021) observed that Japanese test-takers had unexpectedly lower efficiency than anticipated. After further 
investigation, they found a typo in the Japanese version of items; a space between two words that are optional in real-life settings in 
Japanese was in fact considered mandatory by the system for respondents to be able to proceed in the context of the assessment items. 
Moreover, micro-behavioral information obtained from process data can be displayed to instructors, who can then provide students 
with more precise formative feedback or real-time interventions (Bennett et al., 2022; Harindranathan & Folkestad, 2019). For 
example, Papamitsiou and Economides (2015b) clustered students’ test-taking behaviors, built profiles for each student, and presented 
profiles to instructors to enhance their instruction. Finally, from ethical considerations, test-takers would have a pleasant participation 
experience since their data are collected in the background, and the unstructured nature of computer log files may promote data 
confidentiality as its difficult to draw meaningful inferences about an individual response.
3.4.2. Potential challenges
Although process data has many benefits, some challenges remain to be addressed. First, from the theoretical perspective, the main 
issue is the lack of theoretical groundings for validifying process indicators and linking process indicators with latent constructs such as 
test-taking engagement or other higher-order thinking processes (Ren et al., 2019; Stadler et al., 2020; Xiao, Veldkamp, & Liu, 2022). 
The construction of process indicators should be based on theoretical foundations or evidence-centered design (ECD; Goldhammer 
et al., 2021). However, most previous studies identified process indicators mainly based on data-driven or human labeling, which may 
be subjective or lack validity. Several studies have applied the ECD (Chen, Cui, & Chu, 2020; Hao & Mislevy, 2018), but the number of 
studies remains limited. One potential reason is the lack of micro-level or process-oriented theories that could link fine-grained 
behavioral indicators with high-level latent constructs. An example of such a theory is the micro-level self-regulated learning (SRL) 
processes model (Siadaty, Gasevic, & Hatala, 2016). In this model, event logs were linked to micro-level SRL processes such as task 
analysis, goal setting, and making a personal plan. These micro-level constructs were further used to infer the macro-level constructs, 
such as planning. Second, from the data collection and analysis perspective, it is ideal to have a pre-designed system with specific plans 
for what types of process data to construct. The construction of these systems is intricate and costly, and the collected log data may also 
be system- or context-specific, complicated, and unstructured, making data analysis challenging. Previous studies mainly applied 
advanced statistical and machine learning techniques to pre-process and analyze the data, but this may be challenging for researchers 
who lack relevant skills (Qiao & Jiao, 2018). In addition, some advanced artificial intelligence or sophisticated process data analysis 
methods may have potential fairness and bias issues (Mehrabi, Morstatter, Saxena, Lerman, & Galstyan, 2022). Third, from the

practical implication perspective, the most mentioned issue is the generalizability of findings (Bennett et al., 2022; Cui et al., 2019; 
Guo & Ercikan, 2021; Wang, Salehi, Arseneault, Nair, & Wieman, 2021). Due to the large volume of log files and limited computational 
power, previous research has typically focused on specific subject tests (e.g., math, science, language, or problem-solving), tasks, items, 
and samples. However, the results obtained from specific subgroups may not be applicable to others. For example, there are two 
distinct frameworks for designing CPS tasks: finite state automata (FSA) and linear structural equations (LSE) (Eichmann, Gold­
hammer, Greiff, Brandhuber, & Naumann, 2020). In FSA-based tasks (e.g., Ticket in PISA), test-takers solve problems by using a set of 
operators to switch between several finite numbers of pre-defined states. Whereas, in LSE-based tasks (e.g., Climate Control in PISA), 
test-takers solve problems by manipulating several interrelated inputs and observing the change of output variables. Previous research 
has used different logic and methods to examine these two types of tasks, which may lead to incompatible results. In addition, cultural 
differences may also contribute to limited generalizability. For instance, Ercikan et al. (2020) revealed that students who used 
character-based languages, such as Chinese and Japanese, had much more time but fewer actions than Canadian students, even though 
they had comparable overall competencies and test response time. Finally, from the ethical consideration, the collection of process 
data can be perceived as invasive, and informed consent may affect response processes. Moreover, making any diagnostic inference 
based on process data should be extremely cautious and responsible (Lindner & Greiff, 2023). Additionally, the potential for bias and 
fairness issues in process data analysis must be acknowledged. These challenges stem from two key aspects: the application of so­
phisticated machine learning and artificial intelligence techniques, which may inherently carry biases and fairness concerns (Mehrabi 
et al., 2022), and the possibility of introducing bias at the bottom-up inference stage if researchers lack robust and comprehensive 
theoretical foundations.
3.4.3. Future recommendations
Based on this review, we identified four gaps in the literature that can be addressed in the future. First, there is a limited proportion 
of data collected from younger students; only 8% of the data is from primary school students, and less than 1% is from preschool 
children. This disparity may be attributed to the relatively low proportion of younger students who use computers and the simpler tasks 
they engage in, which typically involve fewer possible actions or events. Therefore, the current distribution of studies reflects the 
natural variation in access to computer-based tasks among different age groups. However, the behavioral and unobtrusive nature of 
log-based process data has great potential for inferring preschool children’s cognitive and motivational attributes. Traditionally, 
cognitive and motivational response processes in younger children were primarily examined using qualitative methods such as 
observation, video ethnography, or parents’ reports. These methods have limitations in terms of subjectivity, time-consumption, and 
cost. In today’s digital age, it is inevitable for younger children to be exposed to various digital devices, such as smartphones, tablets, 
and computers. We can use these devices to collect process data and investigate younger children’s behavioral patterns, cognitive 
strategies, and engagement more objectively.
Secondly, regarding process indicators, most studies (82%) have used time indicators to examine the test-taking process. Relatively 
fewer studies (32%) have used count indicators, while even fewer studies (25%) have used sequence indicators. One potential reason is 
that response time data has a long history of being utilized to investigate cognitive processes in disciplines like education and psy­
chology. For instance, Hermann Ebbinghaus employed response time measures to understand memory processes in the late 19th 
century (Ebbinghaus, 1913). Therefore, the theory and methods associated with analyzing response time data are far more mature than 
those for the count and sequence indicators. Another reason is that constructing count and sequence indicators, especially sequence 
indicators, may be more complicated. Specifically, the approaches for constructing sequence indicators still need to be improved. 
There are only two commonly used approaches, multidimensional scaling and autoencoder, to develop latent vectors that retain as 
much information from the original action sequences. Two other approaches either break down the entire sequence into 
mini-sequences (n-gram modeling) or develop similarity indexes by comparing the similarities of action sequences (sequence distance 
measures). However, in reality, the amount of obtainable action is enormous, and the stored information is even greater. Therefore, 
developing more advanced methods to extract indicators that retain as much standardized and meaningful information as possible will 
be a promising area of research.
Thirdly, regarding the inferred latent constructs, as seen in Table 2, the commonly examined latent constructs include response 
speed, latent ability, test-taking engagement, and problem-solving strategy. However, research is lacking on other important latent 
constructs, such as reading navigations, writing patterns, decision-making, and goal-pursuing. Hence, future studies could explore 
various latent response processes based on process data. Moreover, most of these latent constructs were inferred mainly based on time 
indicators. Therefore, future studies could explore more ways to infer latent response processes by incorporating count and sequence 
indicators into either holistic statistical approaches or measurement models. Finally, very few studies have discussed how the results of 
log-based process data have been validated. Researchers have proposed several ways for validation. The first approach is evidence- 
centered design, a theory-driven framework comprising the students, task model, and evidence models (Chen et al., 2020; Gold­
hammer et al., 2021; Hao & Mislevy, 2018). With the use of ECD, process indicators were conceptually formulated before data 
collection, the collected data were then used to examine whether the results could validate the theoretical assumptions. Another way 
for validating the log-based process data is to triangulate it with other sources of process data, such as eye-tracking, think-aloud, and 
self-report questionnaires, etc. (Maddox, 2023). This kind of multimodal multichannel approach has been increasingly applied in 
learning analytics research to validate the use of different types of process data (Molenaar et al., 2022). Other validating approaches 
include associating results from log-based process data with other constructs or conducting traditional experimental design to examine 
the causal inference between latent constructs and process indicators (Goldhammer et al., 2021). Future studies could focus more on 
examining the use of different methods for the validation of log-based process data analysis.

4. Discussion and conclusion
This review provides a comprehensive survey of the use of log-based process data in computer-based assessments, centered around 
one key question— how log-based process data has been used to infer various latent response processes in computer-based assess­
ments. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review examining the use of log-based process data in computer-based 
assessments. Findings from this review can help researchers and practitioners better understand (1) trends in the use of log-based 
process data in computer-based assessments, (2) types of process indicators that have been constructed from raw log files, (3) 
latent constructs that have been inferred from process indicators and the inferential levels applied, as well as (4) the benefits, chal­
lenges, and directions for using log-based process data.
Despite these advantages, this study may have three limitations. Specifically, four inference levels applied in this review are 
adapted from the framework proposed by Bergner and Von Davier (2019). The original framework was proposed to outline the his­
torical trends of using process data in relation to outcomes. Although the modified framework categorizes empirical studies effectively, 
it can still be further enhanced. For example, the various holistic approaches included in level 3 could be subdivided for clearer 
differentiation. Another limitation of this review is that it did not provide a detailed summary of pre-processing procedures and data 
analysis methods. Although these procedures and methods are varied and challenging to summarize, they are important topics. Future 
studies could summarize them by finding an appropriate framework to outline their applications. Finally, this review is limited to 
studies that only used computer log files as a source of process data. Given that other data sources, such as think-aloud and eye-tracking 
data, are also beneficial for examining response processes, future research could further undertake reviews of the use of these data 
types.
