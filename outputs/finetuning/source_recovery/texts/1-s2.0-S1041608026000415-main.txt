ABSTRACT
International assessments consistently document variation in achievement between countries, but even more
striking is the substantial within-country variation across different domains and subdomains, the sources
of which remain poorly understood. This study draws on the opportunity-to-learn (OTL) framework, which
conceptualizes students attained achievement in assessments as shaped by the extent to which the tested
content is covered in the intended curriculum and in the learning opportunities implemented in classrooms. Using
TIMSS data from 108 countries and territories, we examine how content expectations and classroom coverage
predict student performance. Exploiting within-country, between-domain variation, we estimate fixed-effects
mediation models for mathematics and science, separately. Findings indicate that content specified in the
intended curriculum influences student achievement, and that this relationship is partially mediated by what
is taught in classrooms. These results hold across subjects and grades, underscoring content coverage as key
lever for improving learning outcomes.

Relevance and the implications of your research
Our study shows that what countries put in their official curriculum—and what teachers actually cover in class—shapes how well
students do in math and science. Exploiting data from many countries and years, we find that content coverage is linked to higher
achievement, in part because teachers are more likely to teach those
topics. Practically, this means systems can boost learning by aligning
standards, textbooks, assessments, and classroom instruction, and by
supporting teachers to cover priority content well. Policymakers and
school leaders can act by streamlining curricula, providing aligned
materials, and offering targeted professional learning so the intended
curriculum becomes the taught and learned curriculum.

position in most educational systems across the world. As statements
of what students should learn at each stage of their schooling, they
define the specific learning objectives to be mastered in each grade
and subject area, serving not only as a guide for teachers’ instructional
planning but also to communicate expectations to several educational
stakeholders (e.g., students, parents, teachers) (Porter, 2002; Schmidt

et al., 2001). While throughout much of the world, national curricula
determine such expectations, within the United States context, states
are pivotal in defining the learning experiences for students within
their public schools. However, the adoption of the Common Core
State Standards (CCSS) reflects a move towards a more centralized
curriculum, involving an attempt to articulate a coherent set of learning
objectives that transcend state boundaries and shape learning across

Given that centralized curricula determine what students are expected to learn in public schools, the content can be a highly contested issue and continually evolves to support preparing students for a
rapidly changing world (Gouëdard et al., 2020). As a result, curricular
or standards-based reforms are common, yet vary in their frequency.
Given the wide-ranging effects curricular reforms can have on student
learning experiences, as well as the relatively low-cost to implementing
such changes, curricular or standards-based reform can be viewed as a
relatively cheap, high-leverage policy lever for influencing educational
system performance (see Boser et al., 2015; Polikoff, 2015; Steiner,
2017). However, much of the success of such reforms relies on the
coherence and alignment across different levels of the educational system (Polikoff, 2012; Porter, 2002; Schmidt & Prawat, 2006; Stringfield
et al., 2000).
The curriculum can be understood to inhabit three distinct levels
of the educational system (McDonnell, 1995; Mullis & Martin, 2017;
Schmidt et al., 1997, 1997). The intended curriculum is determined at
the national, state, or local government levels, defining the expectations for student learning across different content domains and grade
levels. Curricular or standards-based reform occurs at this level. The
intended curriculum shapes the broader instructional system through
its influence on teaching practices, textbook contents, and assessments
used within the classroom which encompasses the implemented curriculum or the content that is taught by teachers and covered in the
classroom. Finally, at the student-level, educational outcomes represent
the attained curriculum which captures what students have learned. It
is important to realize that each level influences the next, but does
not dictate what must be taught. Alignment in the content covered
across the three levels is indicative of a coherent educational system
and the presence of effective teacher practice (Daus et al., 2019; Rolfe
et al., 2021, 2022). Consistency across the intended and implemented
curriculum levels can also ensure the reduction of inequalities as all
students are given the opportunity to learn the same content.
The content covered by centralized curricula (the intended curriculum) varies by country as well as over time which is linked with
what is being taught in classrooms (Isaacs et al., 2020; Johansson &
Yang Hansen, 2018). There is a wealth of evidence, mainly conducted
in the US context, linking what is being taught in the classrooms
with student achievement (Lamain et al., 2017). Studying international
trends in content coverage has the potential to help us better understand how changes in a country’s content coverage across the intended
and implemented levels is related with student educational outcomes to
better understand the potential impacts of curricular or standards-based
reform. However, developing strong evidence on the link between
curriculum content with student outcomes is a difficult task as the
relationships are complex and may not be direct and correlations are
likely biased by omitted variables or reverse causality.
This study seeks to overcome these methodological challenges by
using country-by-domain dataset from multiple cycles of the Trends
in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) to better
understand how content listed in the intended curriculum gets covered
in the classroom and eventually makes its way to influence the learning
of students.

Current conceptualizations of OTL view curricula as functioning on
three levels: (1) the intended curriculum, (2) implemented curriculum,
and (3) attained curriculum (McDonnell, 1995; Mullis & Martin, 2017;
Schmidt et al., 1997, 1997) (see Fig. 1). The intended curriculum
refers to the system-level, or ‘‘official’’, curriculum setting the academic
grade-level expectations of students throughout a country or state.
The implemented curriculum, also called the ‘‘enacted curriculum’’,
refers to the taught content within a classroom. Finally, the attained
curriculum captures student learning within the classroom. While the
intended curriculum outlines the full scope of learning expectations for
students, only a subset is typically enacted in classrooms (implemented
curriculum), and an even smaller portion is ultimately learned and
retained by students (attained curriculum). As a result, the intended
curriculum’s impact on student learning is likely mediated by the
implemented curriculum (Daus et al., 2019; McDonnell, 1995; Petty &
Green, 2007; Schmidt et al., 1997, 1997).
The OTL framework considers content exposure or content coverage
as the match between taught content (or intended to be taught content
as dictated by national or centralized curricula) and tested content
(Luyten & Scheerens, 2022). Due to OTL’s importance in supporting
student learning, a substantial body of literature studying the effects
of OTL on student learning has focused on the content coverage as
a key component of learning (Lamain et al., 2017; Schmidt et al.,
2015, 2001). Operationally, OTL is often measured through different
indicators of content coverage across varying levels of the educational
system. At the centralized level, document reviews of national curricula
can reveal the content standards that are expected to be learned at
certain grade levels. Moving to the lower level, in the classroom, common instruments include student or teacher questionnaires as well as
classroom observations that can quantify the extent to which students
are exposed to content through instruction or classroom materials such
as textbooks (e.g., Polikoff, 2012, 2015).
The choice of what topics are covered by a curriculum is influenced
by a variety of factors and continues to evolve over time through global
discourse. Acknowledging the clear relevance of national curricula in
what students learn, the international assessment, TIMSS, has followed,
since its inception, a ‘‘curriculum-based approach’’ (Hastedt & Rocher,

framework which establishes that learners will succeed if they spend
the time and have the opportunity to learn the task (Carroll, 1963,
1989; Husén, 1967). Initially conceptualized by Husén (1967) in the
context of international assessments, OTL has been widely employed

Some experimental or quasi-experimental designs have been utilized
in studies of the relationship between curricular choices (or content
choices) and student achievement clearing pointing towards a positive
relationship (Steiner, 2017). For instance, the What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) managed by the US Department of Education’s Institute
of Education Sciences (IES) presents evidence from several studies
that have found experimental or quasi-experimental evidence of large
effects of particular curricula on student achievement (e.g., Borman
et al., 2008; Hirschhorn, 1993; Zucker et al., 2008). Furthermore,
studies have identified positive effects of curricula tied to the use of
related textbooks on student performance (Koedel et al., 2017; Koedel
& Polikoff, 2017). A key predictor of curriculum content coverage is
instructional alignment with centralized standards, suggesting that curricula will have greater impact if it is taught in the classroom (Polikoff,
2012; Polikoff & Porter, 2014). Furthermore, defining ‘‘business-asusual’’ practices has been a challenge (Steiner, 2017). Large effects
may be driven by weaker curricula used within the control group
meaning that studies may come to differing conclusions depending
on the counterfactual situation used. Another consideration is how
aligned the assessments are to the intended curriculum. A positive
effect may be found on one assessment, but not on another depending
on alignment with content standards (Polikoff, 2010). Using TIMSS as
a data source has the potential to overcome some of these concerns
over counterfactuals as the assessment is tied to national curricula and
its possible to make comparisons within-countries allowing for one to
study whether different curricular focuses are tied with better performance in certain subject domains. Furthermore, the TIMSS database
includes large representative samples from a multitude of countries
expanding the generalizability of the typically small-scale experimental
studies.
In the international realm, studies have mainly been contained
to analysis of the cross-sectional data provided in TIMSS or PISA
limiting the potential quantitative designs that can be applied. Correlational evidence points to a positive relationship between curriculum
content coverage or coherence and student achievement (Schmidt &
Houang, 2007; Schmidt et al., 2001, 2005). However, this can be influenced by the country-level confounding variables mentioned above.
Furthermore, few studies have empirically examined the potential for
curriculum ‘‘attrition’’ or the fact that the effect of intended curriculum
may be mediated through the implemented curriculum (Kuger, 2016).

2.2. OTL and student achievement
Initially, measures of OTL were used as a way to check the validity
of comparisons in international assessments (Husén, 1967). That is,
OTL was used to explain differences in performance across countries,
so that countries would not be penalized for not covering certain topics
from an international assessment (Polikoff et al., 2021). However, this
purpose evolved as countries, including the US, saw OTL as a construct
worth studying to improve standings on international assessments (McDonnell, 1995; Porter, 1995). Providing a clear motivator for curricular
or standards-based reforms as high-leverage policies to improve student
performance, this shift in focus of OTL as a predictor of achievement
led to research aiming to understand the link between curricula content coverage and student performance. Several research studies have
explored the link between content coverage (across varying levels of
curricula) and student achievement. In a comprehensive review of 51
studies, Lamain et al. (2017) find that 44% of the reported effects were
statistically significant and positively related with student achievement.
However, the review notes some limitations of the current research on
the effects of content coverage on student achievement.
First, the predominance of research has been carried out in the
United States (37 out of 51 studies reviewed), taking just a national
and mostly westernized view of the question. Evidence has shown
clear variation in the content covered by centralized curricula across
countries meaning that national findings may not be generalizable to
other contexts (Schmidt et al., 2001). Furthermore, analyses conducted
within single countries are often constrained by the limited withincountry variation in curricular content. Even when they do, such as
in the United States across states, variation between countries is likely
a bit larger (Schmidt et al., 1997, 1997). Some international studies
have emerged to fill this gap (e.g., Barnard-Brak et al., 2018; Daus &
Braeken, 2018; Luyten, 2016; Schmidt et al., 2015). Mainly, these studies make use of international assessments like IEA’s TIMSS or OECD’s
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA). However, one
limitation of studies using PISA is that it does not follow a curriculumbased approach and relies on student-reports about content coverage in
the classroom which has been critiqued as being conflated with other
factors, such as the quality of instruction, students’ learning aptitudes
and motivation (Luyten & Scheerens, 2022; Yang Hansen & Strietholt,
2018). TIMSS is particularly suited for curriculum research because
it was intentionally designed to assess content defined in national
curricula and includes viewpoints from teachers about implementation
(Yang Hansen & Strietholt, 2018). A second limitation of the current
research base is that most work looking at the relationship between
content coverage and student achievement has been correlational (44
out of 51 in Lamain et al. (2017)’s review) not being able to rule out the
influence of potential confounders. Most studies have utilized control
approaches (e.g., student-level controls like SES or prior achievement)
with very few utilizing experimental or quasi-experimental designs.

3. Data

In the following sections, we outline how, in line with this differentiation, each aspect of the curriculum – intended, implemented, and
attained – was measured at the content domain level.

3.1. Sample
The present study uses international data from the TIMSS assessment, which provides information on the intended, implemented, and
attained curriculum in mathematics and science for Grades 4 and 8.
TIMSS is a repeated cross-sectional study conducted on a four-year cycle; in this paper, we use data from the 2003, 2007, 2011, 2015, 2019,
and 2023 cycles (for further information, see https://timssandpirls.bc.
edu/timss-landing.html).
TIMSS employs a two-stage sampling approach. In the first stage,
a random sample of schools is drawn, and in the second stage, one
class is randomly selected within each sampled school. All students in
the selected classes are assessed, and their mathematics and science
teachers are surveyed. In addition, national curriculum experts provide
information on the official curricula for Grades 4 and 8 in each participating country. The combined dataset includes information from 108
countries, 153,964 mathematics teachers and 188,060 science teachers,
and 3,226,717 students. Detailed information on sample sizes by grade
level and cycle can be found in Table 1, while information by country
can be found in Appendix Table A.1 and Table A.2.

3.2.1. Intended curriculum (curriculum questionnaire)
To measure the intended curriculum, we used data provided by
national curriculum experts in mathematics and science. For each content domain, these experts indicated whether specific subdomains were
included in the national curriculum for the corresponding grade level.
For example, experts were asked whether the Pythagorean Theorem
is expected to be taught to all or almost all students by the end of
Grade 8, as an indicator of the intended curriculum in the Geometry
content domain. For an average number of subdomains within each
content domain, see Table A.3, Table 2, Table A.4, Table A.5. Based on
these responses, we calculated the percentage of subdomains covered
within each content domain for each country, resulting in three to four
indicators for the intended curriculum depending on the subject and
grade level (one value for each content domain). Separate measures
of the intended curriculum were computed by content domain, grade,
country and cycle.

3.2.2. Implemented curriculum (teacher questionnaire)
The teachers of the classes assessed in TIMSS reported in the teacher
questionnaire whether they had taught the subdomains mostly before
this year, mostly this year, or whether they had not yet taught the
subdomain or had only introduced it. As with the curriculum questionnaire, teachers were also asked, for example, whether they had taught
the Pythagorean theorem (mostly before this year, mostly this year, or
whether they had not yet taught the subdomain or had only introduced
it). As an indicator of the coverage of the implemented curriculum
for each content domain, grade level, country, and cycle, we calculate
the average percentage of the subdomains that teachers reported had
been taught mostly before this year OR mostly this year. If there was
information from more than one teacher per class (as might be the case
in science), the subdomain is considered to have been taught as soon as
one teacher stated that they had taught it mostly before this year or in
this year. We averaged the percentage of the subdomains that teachers
reported had been taught mostly before this year or mostly this year
to the country level to determine average coverage of subdomains for
each content domain.

4. Analysis strategy
To understand our analytical approach, it is useful to distinguish
between the estimand and the estimate. We use mediation modeling
(Baron & Kenny, 1986) to define the estimand as the effect of the
intended curriculum on the attained curriculum and whether this is
mediated through the implemented curriculum and we use countryfixed effects models to estimate it (Allison, 2009). Because we rely
on observational data, conventional OLS regressions cannot identify
the mediation parameters due to unmeasured confounding (Imai et al.,
2010). Country-level factors such as cultural attitudes toward mathematics and science or teacher qualifications systems may bias coefficients (Schmidt et al., 2001). Fixed effects models help address
these biases, enabling a more credible estimation of the mediation
parameters. We explain our approach in greater detail below, outlining
the mediation framework, the fixed-effects specification, and how these
are integrated in our analysis.

3.2.3. Attained curriculum (student achievement data)
In addition to overall achievement in mathematics and science,
TIMSS measures achievement in the content domains. TIMSS comprises of approximately 90 to 100 items per subject and grade level,
distributed across the various content domains in slightly different
proportions. In the content domain Geometry, for example, students are
tested on whether they can solve the Pythagorean theorem in a task.
The plausible values of students’ content domain-specific achievement
were established with a mean of 500 and a standard deviation of
100. We aggregated student achievement at the country level for each
content domain, grade level, and cycle using sampling weights. The
country average of content domain-specific achievement represents the
attained curriculum, i.e., the extent to which students have learned the
intended and implemented curriculum topics.

5.3. Robustness to country units and weighting
Our analyses combine participating education systems as defined
by TIMSS, which include both nation-states and, in a small number
of cases, subnational systems (e.g., U.S. states or Canadian provinces).
To assess whether results are sensitive to this composition, we conducted two complementary robustness checks. First, we re-estimated
all models excluding single participating federal states while retaining
their corresponding countries, and second, we estimated the models
including single participating states while excluding the corresponding
countries. As shown in Tables A.12 and A.13, the synthesized total,
direct, and indirect effects are highly comparable to the main results
in both magnitude and statistical significance.
In our main analyses, we weighted each country equally, even
though participating education systems differ substantially in size. An
alternative approach would be to assign larger weights to countries
with larger student populations and smaller weights to countries with
smaller populations. We prefer equal weighting because the primary
level of analysis is the country (or education system) level. Nevertheless, to assess whether this choice affects the results, we additionally
replicated all analyses using country-weighted specifications, where
weights correspond to the actual student population sizes of the respective countries. The resulting estimates were nearly identical to
the unweighted results, indicating that our findings are not driven
by an overrepresentation of smaller education systems (cf. Fig. A.1).
Taken together, these sensitivity analyses demonstrate that the main
conclusions are robust to alternative weighting choices.

6.2. Limitations
While our findings are robust and methodologically innovative,
several limitations should be acknowledged.
First, there are measurement limitations. We operationalize the
intended curriculum through a questionnaire completed by the national
experts (National Research Coordinator) in each country. Although
these experts are well-positioned to provide authoritative information,
relying on a single respondent introduces the risk of measurement
error. In countries with decentralized or regional curricula, these experts may not have complete information, and in rare cases there may
be incentives for strategic overstatement of content coverage. These
factors could attenuate the observed relationships.
Second, country- and domain-fixed effects remove much of the
stable, country-level heterogeneity. However, country-domain-specific
shocks remain a potential confound. For example, an unrelated major
textbook introduction in Grade 8 biology could coincide with curriculum revision and influence both teacher coverage and student
performance, creating bias in mediation estimates. However, due to the
curriculum revision, the textbooks would inevitably have to be revised
to cover the new content so such textbook revisions would be a part of
the estimated effect of the curriculum content.
Third, and related to the previous point, our design mitigates
some threats to validity but cannot eliminate all concerns about reverse causality. In particular, low achievement may prompt curriculum
change, as in the case of Germany’s ‘‘PISA shock’’ (Davoli & Entorf,
2018), where disappointing results triggered standards reform. If such
reforms occur within the same TIMSS cycle, part of what we estimate
as the effect of curriculum coverage could actually reflect a response
to prior low performance.
Fourth, the scope and generalizability of our analyses has limitations: Our analyses focus on TIMSS mathematics and science domains.
While these subjects are central to most national curricula, effects
might differ in other areas such as reading literacy, ICT, or citizenship education. Moreover, TIMSS content frameworks may not capture
all nationally valued outcomes, particularly in countries emphasizing
competencies beyond the tested domains.
Fifth, although our analyses combine large, nationally representative samples from about 100 countries, these systems are not randomly
selected from the global population of countries. Participation in TIMSS
is voluntary and tends to be higher among high- and middle-income
countries, with the Global South comparatively underrepresented. Importantly, the two largest countries in the world, China and India,
do not participate in TIMSS, which limits the extent to which our
findings can be generalized to the global student population. As a
result, while our conclusions are robust within the set of participating
systems, they should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to
non-participating or underrepresented contexts.
Sixth, our findings are based exclusively on within-country variation
and therefore should be interpreted at that level of analysis. While
we observe that greater coverage is associated with higher student
achievement within countries, this relationship cannot automatically
be generalized to differences between countries. Associations may differ
across levels of aggregation, and cross-national patterns may reflect additional factors, such as differences in curricular depth or instructional
focus. Any implications for between-country variation should therefore
be considered cautiously and require direct empirical investigation at
the country level.

6. Conclusion
6.1. Main findings and contributions
This study contributes to a deeper understanding of how the intended curriculum influences what students learn. Our results show
an effect of the intended curriculum on student achievement, which is
partly mediated through the implemented curriculum. That is, while
national curriculum frameworks set the foundation for learning, the
extent to which teachers cover the prescribed content plays a key
role in determining learning outcomes. The robustness of our findings
(i.e., replicating across mathematics and science, across grades 4 and 8,
and across multiple TIMSS cycles) provides strong empirical support for
the Opportunity to Learn (OTL) conceptual framework. It strengthens
the case that curriculum policy is not merely symbolic but exerts
measurable influence on learning outcomes across diverse educational
contexts.
To contextualize the magnitude of the estimated effects, the mean
total effect of the intended curriculum across grades and subjects is
𝛾 = 0.19. This coefficient implies that a 10-point increase in content
coverage is associated with an improvement of roughly 2 points on
the TIMSS achievement scale. Although this effect may appear modest
at first glance, it becomes more meaningful when compared with the
typical learning gains observed over one school year. Evidence from the
TIMSS Longitudinal Study indicates that average achievement growth
from Grade 4 to 5 is approximately 25 points, and from Grade 8 to 9
about 10 points (von Davier et al., 2025). Against this backdrop, variation in curriculum content coverage is associated with performance
differences that represent a nontrivial proportion of expected annual
learning gains. This suggests that curricular decisions have the potential
to yield notable differences in student achievement.
In addition to these substantive insights, the study advances methodological approaches in curriculum research. We adopt a within-country, between-domains design, which enables us to reduce unobserved
heterogeneity across countries which is a common limitation in earlier
cross-sectional studies. By exploiting variation within countries, we
minimize the influence of contextual factors that differ across countries
but remain constant within them.

6.3. Policy implications

6.5. Concluding reflection

Our results have several implications for both national education
policy and international assessment design.
For national policymakers and curriculum developers, the robust
association between content coverage in intended and implemented
curricula and student achievement suggests that curriculum policy
is a meaningful lever for improving educational outcomes. Previous
research has shown that performance profiles across content domains
vary between countries and that these profiles can change over time
within countries (Johansson & Strietholt, 2019). Our study provides a
possible explanation for these observed variations in student outcomes
by linking them to differences in the intended and implemented curricula. Reform efforts should ensure that priority content domains are
explicitly included in the intended curriculum and that mechanisms
are in place to support their implementation, such as professional
development, aligned teaching resources, and formative assessment
tools.
For international assessment organizations, our study raises concerns about constructs measures in comparative assessments. The
TIMSS framework does not perfectly align with all national curricula,
and our findings show that students from countries with lower content
cover may be disadvantaged in international comparisons. Notably,
some of the highest-performing systems do not fully cover the TIMSS
framework, suggesting that their measured performance may underestimate their potential. This calls for exploring modular or adaptive
assessment designs that better accommodate curricular diversity while
preserving comparability.
This construct representation issue is likely exacerbated when international large-scale assessments are not explicitly designed to reflect
national curricula, but instead adopt independent assessment frameworks. For example, in PISA, OECD experts define what they consider
to be key skills for the future, rather than aligning the framework
with the curricular intentions of participating countries. From the
perspective of the OTL framework, this limits the construct validity of
such assessments, as they may measure content and skills that are only
partially represented in national curricula. Consequently, the validity of
these tests for informing national education policy is reduced, particularly in systems where the assessment framework diverges substantially
from the intended curriculum (Meyer & Benavot, 2013; Meyer et al.,
2017).

In a global education landscape increasingly driven by evidencebased reform, our study offers large-scale and methodologically robust
evidence on the pathways from curriculum policy to student learning.
By combining a mediation framework with fixed-effects estimation, we
show that national curricular intentions do not remain on paper, but
they shape classroom practice and student achievement in measurable
ways. The takeaway from our findings for policymakers is that curriculum matters, but only if it is both well-designed and well-implemented.
One pathway to achieve improvements in student outcomes is coherence between curriculum policy, instructional practice, and assessment
design.
