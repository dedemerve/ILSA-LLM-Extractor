This study aimed to examine the emotional and cognitive dimensions of teachers to elucidate how various
clusters manifested their emotions and perceived their self-efficacy within the context of curriculum reform.
Employing a survey design, we analyzed responses from 624 upper secondary school teachers in Taiwan. The
results revealed three teacher clusters distinguished by varying levels of emotions and self-efficacy. Regression
analyses indicated that self-efficacy, in comparison to emotions, emerged as a stronger predictor for teacher
collaboration and learner-centered teaching practices. These findings hold implications for designing tailored
professional development courses.

1. Introduction
Teaching occurs in challenging contexts, demanding that a teacher
be perceived as a whole person possessing skills and values (Fullan &
Hargreaves, 2002). Recognizing teachers as "total teachers" involves
delving into both their emotional and cognitive dimensions. Despite the
paramount role of emotions in teaching, scholarly attention has predominantly focused on cognitive aspects, with emotions only receiving
increased attention starting in the late 1990s (Sutton & Wheatley, 2003).
Emotions are not purely psychological processes; they are socially constructed and individually enacted modes of existence that stem from
both conscious and unconscious evaluations of perceived achievements
within social-historical contexts (Schutz et al., 2006). This sociocultural
perspective suggests that emotions are shaped by the interplay of social,
cultural, and political factors (Fried et al., 2015; Schutz et al., 2006). The
emphasis on contextual influences leads our study to explore emotions
within the framework of curriculum reform in an Asian society. Several
layers of contexts were utilized to interpret teachers’ emotional expressions and reactions to reforms in this study.
At the outermost layer lies the sociocultural context. In collectivistic
societies like Taiwan, individuals tend to balance positive and negative
emotions to maintain harmonious relationships, contrasting with individualistic societies where extreme emotional expressions are more
common (Guo, 2019; Miyamoto & Ryff, 2011). Cultural scripts aid in
interpreting the expression of emotions among Taiwanese teachers.

Moving to the meso layer, we encounter the reform context. In Taiwan, a
new wave of curriculum reform has been underway since 2019 with the
introduction of Curriculum Guidelines for 12-year Basic Education. This
reform advocates for a learner-centered pedagogy rooted in social
constructivism (Pan & Chen, 2024a), marking a shift in the teaching
paradigm. It poses a considerable challenge for teachers, particularly in
a Confucian heritage society where traditional teaching methods such as
direct instruction and memorization have long been prevalent. Additionally, the Curriculum Guidelines grant schools a certain degree of
autonomy to develop their curriculum, promoting collaborative course
design among teachers, which adds an extra demand to their daily lives.
The emotional responses of teachers to these reforms were assessed in
this study. Delving deeper into the inner layer, we scrutinized "content
as context," a concept proposed by Grossman and Stodolsky (1995). As
school subjects possess histories, norms, and status that influence
teacher work, we examined how school subjects, as arenas for teachers’
practices, interpret the variations manifested by teachers.
Emotions, being a multi-componential construct, encompass the
critical element of appraisal. Teachers’ emotional experiences originate
from their judgment of external incidents; they are rooted in cognitions,
inseparable from perception or judgment (Nias, 1996). Therefore,
emotions can be perceived as “interpreted feelings” (Ortony et al.,
2005). Apart from recognizing the intertwining of cognitive processes
with emotions, there are other cognitive aspects, such as
self-understanding or self-efficacy, which correlate with emotions

Evolutionary theorists suggest that emotions function to initiate actions that have shown adaptability over time. Positive emotions are
associated with inclinations toward exploration and engaging with the
unknown, whereas negative emotions often prompt individuals to avoid
certain behaviors (Frenzel et al., 2009). Hence, we hypothesized that
teachers with more positive emotions and higher self-efficacy would
exhibit more innovative professional practices. Prior relevant studies
have provided some empirical support for these hypotheses. Relationships between emotions and teaching behaviors have been identified
(Chen, 2019; Kunter et al., 2008; Saunders, 2013), although research in
this area remains relatively scarce compared to studies on the relationship between self-efficacy and teaching practices (e.g., Evers et al.,
2002; Nie et al., 2013; Suprayogi et al., 2017; Zee & Koomen, 2016).
Regarding the connections with teacher collaboration, research is
limited, with only indirect investigations into collegial relationships
(Chen, 2016). Further exploration of the roles of emotions and
self-efficacy in the learner-centered teaching approach and collaborative
engagement is warranted.
To address the aforementioned research interests, this study
employed a survey design to collect data from upper secondary school
teachers in Taiwan. Cluster analyses were used to discern distinct
teacher clusters characterized by varying levels of emotions and selfefficacy. This classification formed the basis for subsequent regression
analyses investigating the effects of emotions and self-efficacy. Given the
relevance of demographic factors such as age and school subjects in
explaining variances in teacher performance, as indicated by previous
literature (Grossman & Stodolsky, 1995; Hargreaves, 2005), we have
included them in our investigation.
Overall, our study seeks to contribute in three ways. First, it assessed
teacher emotions for curriculum reform in an Asian society, offering
insights different from studies focusing on teacher interactions with
students, colleagues, and parents, particularly in Western settings (e.g.,
Becker et al., 2014; Hargreaves, 2001; Koenen et al., 2019). Second, the
study adopted a “total teachers” perspective by embracing both aspects
of emotion and cognition (i.e., self-efficacy). This approach distinguishes our study from previous ones that often concentrated exclusively
on either emotions or cognition. Third, we intended to provide a deeper
understanding of the interconnected attributes of emotions and
self-efficacy in different teacher clusters and their effects on teachers.
This understanding can inform the design of tailored professional
development courses focused on enhancing teachers’ emotional regulation skills and self-efficacy, ultimately facilitating a smoother adaptation to new pedagogical approaches.

scaffold and respond to students’ learning, promoting autonomy and

importance on the interconnectedness between individuals, the sociocultural context, and development through collaboration and imitation
(Vygotsky, 1978). Constructivist pedagogy asserts that the primary goals
of teaching are centered on students developing a deep understanding of
their areas of interest and nurturing habits of mind (Richardson, 2003).

and practical experiences, (c) promoting collaborative learning, and (d)

2024a; Richardson, 2003; Yilmaz, 2008). Based on these components,
we assessed learner-centered teaching across four dimensions: situated
learning, inquiry learning, experiential learning, and collaborative
learning. Effective strategies include the use of real-life materials to
spark student engagement and facilitate the comprehension of concepts
(Hendricks, 2001; Reyes et al., 2019), encouraging students to ask
questions and engage in investigations (Aditomo & Klieme, 2020;
Constantinou et al., 2018; Grant et al., 2017), organizing hands-on activities (Bada & Akinbobola, 2018; Powell & Wells, 2002), and facilitating group discussions and collaborative learning (Abubakar &
Arshad, 2015; Hmelo–Silver et al., 2013). These strategies are all
instrumental in helping students acquire the competencies outlined in
the Curriculum Guidelines.
2.2. Teacher collaboration
As the new curriculum in Taiwan allows schools a degree of freedom
in curriculum governance, the promotion of learning communities is
encouraged, wherein teachers can collaboratively engage in curriculum
development and course design. Teacher collaboration stands as a
linchpin for the effective implementation of curriculum reform. It has
gained recognition for its pivotal role in fostering ongoing reflection and
improvement in teaching practices, often materializing as professional
communities or teacher teams (Vangrieken et al., 2015).
Ongoing research has sought to categorize teacher collaboration into
various typologies to understand its contributions to teacher development. For instance, Little (1990) distinguishes collaboration based on
levels of interdependency and collegiality, leading to four distinct typologies: storytelling and scanning, aid and assistance, sharing, and joint

To empirically examine teacher emotions, researchers have

2.3.2. Teacher self-efficacy
Bandura (1997) characterized self-efficacy beliefs as individuals’
assessments of their capacity to strategize and carry out particular behaviors. When applied to teaching, teacher self-efficacy can be described
as the belief in one’s ability to bring about desired outcomes in terms of
student engagement and learning (Holzberger et al., 2013; Tschannen-Moran & Hoy, 2001). These beliefs, as suggested by Tschannen-Moran and Hoy (2001), are context-specific and closely tied to
instructional capabilities and tasks. Teacher self-efficacy is a multidimensional concept (Guskey & Passaro, 1994; Soodak & Podell, 1996;
Tschannen-Moran & Hoy, 2001). It can be broken down into three
fundamental factors: classroom management, instruction, and student
engagement (Klassen et al., 2011; Tschannen-Moran & Hoy, 2001).
Classroom management self-efficacy is linked to teachers’ confidence in
their ability to establish a structured learning environment and effectively manage disruptive student behavior. Instructional self-efficacy
concerns teachers’ faith in their competence to employ innovative
teaching methods and assessment strategies. Lastly, self-efficacy in
student engagement encompasses teachers’ convictions regarding the
emotional and cognitive assistance they can offer students and their
capacity to inspire student learning. These are the three domains of
teacher self-efficacy employed in TALIS 2018; Ainley & Carstens, 2018;
OECD, 2020).
When analyzing the effects of teacher self-efficacy, research has
shown that it is positively associated with occupational commitment
(Klassen & Chiu, 2010), teacher engagement (Durksen et al., 2017),
collegial collaboration (Çoban et al., 2023), and job satisfaction (Caprara et al., 2006). Teacher self-efficacy also influences instructional
behavior and practice (Evers et al., 2002; Nie et al., 2013; Suprayogi
et al., 2017; Zee & Koomen, 2016). Nie et al.’s (2013) study, which
focuses on constructivist practices, is the most related to this study. In
contrast to the extensive exploration of teacher self-efficacy, the investigation into the connections between teachers’ emotions and professional practices of collaborative engagement and teaching behaviors has
received relatively less attention. However, existing relevant studies
have suggested that positive emotions are closely associated with
student-focused instruction (Chen, 2019; Saunders, 2013).
Furthermore, the literature has examined how demographic factors
elucidate variations in teachers’ self-efficacy. Notably, age emerges as a
significant factor, with career teachers rating themselves higher on
overall self-efficacy compared to novice teachers (Tschannen-Moran &
Hoy, 2007). Another factor is teaching experience. Self-efficacy increases as teachers accumulate years of experience but declines after 23
years (Klassen & Chiu, 2010).

the significance of investigating teachers’ emotions and self-efficacy is
particularly pronounced in the context of educational reform. Viewing
emotions as socially constructed, this study considered the contextual
influences of socioculture, education reform, and school subjects.

2.3. Teacher emotions and self-efficacy as influential factors
2.3.1. Teacher emotions
Teacher emotions have garnered significant attention in educational
studies since the late 1990s (e.g., Hargreaves, 1998; Marshake, 1996;
Nias, 1996). In the educational context, emotions are understood as
deeply personal interpretations of events stemming from individuals’
subjective evaluations (Oatley, 2000). They are inherently subjective in
nature. Moreover, when viewed through a sociocultural lens, emotions
are perceived as products of social construction, interwoven with their
environment, and molded by the interaction of cultural, social, and
political influences (Fried et al., 2015; Schutz et al., 2006). Emotions are
contingent upon the context in which they occur.
Regarding the sociocultural contextual influence on emotional responses, Miyamoto and Ryff (2011) observed that Americans tend to
exhibit a predominantly positive outlook, emphasizing the amplification
of positive emotions. Integrating both positive and negative emotions is
less common among Americans. On the other hand, East Asians, characterized by a moderate dialectical emotional style, are inclined to
embrace contradictions and impermanence in their emotions. When
confronted with simultaneous positive and negative emotions, they
often seek to balance them dialectically, reflecting the influence of East
Asian culture, which values balance and moderation.
Examining teacher emotions within the context of educational reform has crucial implications for policy implementation. However,
many educational reforms focus on encouraging teachers to accept new
policies, often prioritizing their "buy-in" while overlooking how teachers
emotionally react to these changes (Datnow, 2018). In the limited
research on teacher emotional responses to reform, Hargreaves (2005)
has highlighted the significance of age and career stage as pivotal determinants. His research indicated that younger teachers often approach
educational reform enthusiastically but may grapple with insecurity and
self-doubt regarding their abilities. Middle-career teachers, on the other
hand, show diminished enthusiasm but tend to have more confidence in
their professional competence, maintaining an open-minded stance towards educational reform. Nevertheless, older teachers, despite their
extensive experience and decision-making authority, may become
increasingly resistant to change as they progress in age and approach the
twilight of their careers.
Additionally, school subjects serve as specific contexts within which
secondary teachers work (Grossman & Stodolsky, 1995). Different
subjects entail shared beliefs concerning the potentials and limitations
influencing teachers’ responses to educational reform. Math teachers,
for instance, have been observed to exhibit reluctance towards instructional changes, possibly due to their perception that math follows a
well-defined and sequential structure (Gamoran & Weinstein, 1998;
Stodolsky & Grossman, 1995). This arouses their concern that

3.2.2. Teacher self-efficacy scale
When assessing self-efficacy, we utilized the teacher self-efficacy
scale from TALIS 2018; Ainley & Carstens, 2018; OECD, 2018, p. 19).
This scale comprises 12 questions, divided into three domains: classroom management, student engagement, and instruction as proposed by
Tschannen-Moran and Hoy (2001). In this study, we specifically focused
on two domains: student engagement and instruction. To suit our
research, we modified the TALIS four-point scale into a six-point scale.
In total, we employed eight items, which included sample questions such
as "Get students to believe they can do well in school work" to gauge
student engagement and "Use a variety of assessment strategies" to
measure instruction. In the reliability analysis, we scrutinized the
sub-construct level, which encompassed student engagement (α = 0.87)
and instruction (α = 0.88). We also computed a composite score for all
self-efficacy items (α = 0.91).

3. Method
3.1. Participants
With a survey design, this study utilized a method of stratified cluster
sampling to enlist participants from upper secondary schools across
various geographical regions and sizes in Taiwan. Following the school
selection process, teachers were invited to voluntarily complete online
questionnaires in June 2023. The research team reached out to each
school for assistance with our survey before disseminating the online
survey website and monitored the response rate throughout the process.
Upon observing lower-than-expected response rates from some schools,
we contacted them to encourage their teachers to fill out the questionnaire. Subsequently, we obtained 624 responses out of the 640 teachers
who were invited to participate. Among the survey participants, 36.4%
were male, 63.3% were female, and 0.3% indicated their gender as
“other.” A significant majority of the respondents held master’s degrees,
comprising 83.5% of the sample.

3.2.3. Teacher collaboration scale
The items used to assess teacher collaboration in this study were
adapted from the teacher questionnaire in the TALIS 2018 survey
(OECD, 2018, p. 18). Teacher collaboration was measured using two
subscales: "professional collaboration" and "exchange and coordination
for teaching," each comprising four items. We revised two items to
address our contextual needs, which involved joint lesson planning and
discussing learner-centered teaching with colleagues. Respondents used
a six-point scale to indicate the frequency of their engagement, ranging
from 1 = never to 6 = once a week or more. These two subscales aimed
to evaluate the extent of teacher involvement in collaborative activities,
including activities like sharing teaching materials and engaging in
co-teaching scenarios. Sample items for "professional collaboration" and
"exchange and coordination for teaching" included statements like
"Observe other teachers’ classes and provide feedback" and "Engage in
discussions about the learning development of specific students,"
respectively. The scale’s Cronbach’s α was 0.89.

and underwent a pilot test involving the participation of 113 upper

to analyze the data collected from the formal survey. This analysis

The first cluster comprised 245 teachers demonstrating the highest
scores for emotions related to joy and self-efficacy, along with the lowest
levels of fatigue. This group can aptly be labeled as "high enthusiasm and
efficacy." The second cluster, consisting of 270 individuals, exhibited
"moderate enthusiasm and efficacy." Their scores fell between the lowest

When analyzing the demographic composition of these teacher
clusters, significant differences were observed in terms of duties (χ 2 =
28.20, p < 0.001), teaching subjects (χ 2 = 22.83, p < 0.05), and age (χ 2
= 13.85, p < 0.05) (Table 4). In comparison to Cluster 3, Clusters 1 and 2
exhibited a higher proportion of teachers with administrative responsibilities and those teaching subjects in Sciences, as well as other
subjects such as Arts, Integrative Activities, Technology, Health, and
Physical Education. Conversely, more teachers in Cluster 3 were tasked
with teaching English and Social Studies. Moreover, both Clusters 1 and
2 had a lower percentage of teachers aged over 50 years old compared to

This study employed cluster analysis to identify distinct groups of
teachers based on levels of joy, fatigue, and self-efficacy. These three
variables were standardized using z-scores. Both hierarchical and nonhierarchical cluster analyses were conducted as recommended by
many researchers (Hair et al., 2019). Initially, hierarchical clustering
was performed utilizing the Ward method and squared Euclidean distance as the similarity measure. This approach aimed to determine the
optimal number of clusters representing the dataset. The hierarchical

5.1. Three teacher clusters and their demographic composition
Emotions persist as a constant presence in teachers’ lives and work,
and the incessant influx of numerous demands into their workspaces
profoundly impacts every facet of their existence. In an era marked by
nearly unprecedented policy activism, teachers grapple with substantial
challenges amidst heightened accountability. The ongoing curriculum
reform in Taiwan has ushered in a new agenda. The most significant
changes occur at the upper secondary school level, necessitating school
curriculum development and the design of elective courses (Ministry of
Education, 2014, 2022). Therefore, we anticipated that the reform
might influence the emotions of high school teachers. However, contrary to our initial predictions, teachers exhibited moderately high levels
of positive emotions. Plausible explanations for these findings may
include the government’s support in course design, which enhanced
teachers’ preparedness for change, and the fact that course design
typically does not extend to every teacher, thereby maintaining their
moderately high degree of joy and mitigating their perceptions of
overall workload fatigue. Additionally, cultural orientation could serve
as another explanatory factor. In collectivistic cultures, the emotional
rule places significant importance on attending to the needs of others
and regulating their emotions for relational maintenance (Guo, 2019).
Seeking a middle way entrenched in the cultural script, East Asians tend
to strike a balance between positive and negative emotions (Miyamoto
& Ryff, 2011).
Although the aggregated data reveal the above findings, it is essential
to acknowledge that they may not fully encapsulate the nuances of
teachers with different characteristics. Our study has identified three
teacher clusters, each demonstrating varying levels of joy, fatigue, and
self-efficacy, suggesting an interdependent relationship between emotions and cognition (Pitkäniemi, 2017). Cluster 1 exhibited the highest
levels of joy and self-efficacy, coupled with the lowest fatigue, reflecting

During this wave of curriculum reform in Taiwan, the shift to
learner-centered teaching and collaborative course design has placed
considerable strain on teachers. Understanding teacher emotions and
self-efficacy is essential for supporting their professional growth and
improving teaching effectiveness. This study specifically targeted upper
secondary school teachers in Taiwan to investigate their emotional reactions to the national curriculum reform and their perceptions of self7

teachers through change, generally held more positive perceptions of

In Cluster 3, characterized by "low enthusiasm and efficacy," unlike
Clusters 1 and 2, neither joy nor fatigue showed a significant connection
to teacher engagement in collegial collaboration. To explore the reasons
behind this finding, we analyzed the demographic factors. While the
regression analyses indicated that teaching subjects were not significantly related to teacher collaboration in Cluster 3, the finding might be
limited by our choice of reference group. The demographic composition
revealed information for reference. Cluster 3 had fewer subjects not
covered in the General Scholastic Ability Test and Advanced Subjects
Test (college entrance examinations), such as arts, integrated activities,
technology, and health and physical education. School subjects, which
are the focus of upper secondary school teaching, are featured by
different cultures and norms (Grossman & Stodolsky, 1995). Teachers of
subjects not included in the college entrance examinations enjoyed more
leeway in sharing and discussing with colleagues and designing lessons
jointly. However, teachers responsible for subjects counting toward
college entrance exams would experience pressure to ensure student
success in the tests, particularly in Confucian heritage societies with a
high academic emphasis (Pan & Chen, 2024b). The stress placed on
teachers and peer competition to achieve better student performance
might hinder them from reaching out to other colleagues.
Regarding learner-centered teaching, self-efficacy emerged as a significant predictor across the three teacher clusters. When teachers were
confident in their instructional abilities and felt capable of fostering
student development, they were more inclined to adopt learner-centered
teaching, even among those in Cluster 3. This finding reinforces previous
studies that have established a connection between self-efficacy and
instructional practices (Holzberger et al., 2013; Nie et al., 2013),
underscoring the crucial role of teacher confidence in their competence
when implementing innovative teaching methods. However, teacher
emotions were not significantly associated with learner-centered
teaching in any cluster except for fatigue, which revealed its effect in
Cluster 3. Fatigue dampened the inclination for the innovative teaching
approach in teachers categorized under the ’low enthusiasm and efficacy’ cluster. Although joy was initially linked with the adoption of
learner-centered teaching, this relationship lost significance when
self-efficacy was factored in. This suggests that implementing
learner-centered practices requires not only positive emotions but also
competence in guiding students to explore, collaborate, and construct
knowledge from practical experiences. Notably, self-efficacy emerged as
a stronger predictor compared to the positive emotion of joy when both
were included in the same regression equation.
Furthermore, fatigue is defined as "an affective state of tiredness,
physical and emotional exhaustion" (Burić et al., 2018, p. 331). This
study found a moderately low degree of fatigue among upper secondary
school teachers. The inclination to suppress negative emotions by
adhering to the collectivistic culture (Hofstede, 2011) seems to apply to
most teachers in our sample. This cultural tendency, combined with a
dialectical emotional style that seeks to balance positive and negative
emotions (Miyamoto & Ryff, 2011), may have helped mitigate the
negative effects of fatigue on teachers’ professional practices. While the
existing literature has examined the effects of teachers’ negative emotions, such as students’ negative emotions (Becker et al., 2014) and the
traditional teaching approach to knowledge transmission (Chen, 2019),
it mainly focuses on negative emotions like anger, anxiety, sadness, or
fear. Therefore, further research on the effects of teachers’ fatigue is
warranted.

5.2. Teacher clusters with higher disposition of emotions and self-efficacy
tended to enact professional practices of collaboration and learnercentered teaching
Following the identification of teacher clusters, we conducted analyses to examine the differences in teacher collaboration and their
adoption of learner-centered teaching practices. It was discovered that
Clusters 1 and 2 demonstrated a higher degree of collaboration with
colleagues compared to Cluster 3. Although Cluster 1 exhibited more
engagement in collaboration than Cluster 2, the disparity was not statistically significant. The most pronounced differentiation among the
three clusters was evident in their practices of learner-centered teaching.
Cluster 1, distinguished by the highest levels of joy and self-efficacy,
emerged as the group of teachers most dedicated to promoting situated,
inquiry-based, experiential, and collaborative learning, followed by
Clusters 2 and 3. These results highlight the connection between teacher
emotions and self-efficacy with their professional practices in implementing the new curriculum. Previous studies directly investigating the
relationships between teacher emotions and teaching behaviors have
suggested that positive emotions are associated with a student-centered
pedagogical approach (Chen, 2019; Saunders, 2013). Indirectly related
studies, such as the work of Kunter et al. (2008), Frenzel et al. (2009),
and Becker et al. (2014), also offer supporting evidence for our findings.
Kunter et al. (2008) confirmed positive relationships between teachers’
self-reported enthusiasm for teaching and students’ perceptions of
cognitive challenge, social support, and discipline levels during instruction. In Frenzel et al.’s (2009) research, teacher and student
enjoyment were found to be positively correlated, a result corroborated
by Becker et al. (2014).
5.3. Teacher emotions and self-efficacy had differential effects on
professional practices across three clusters
While the analysis of cluster differences provides insights into the
role of emotions and self-efficacy in teachers’ professional practices for
the reform, we further quantified the relative magnitude of their effects.
The findings of regression analyses indicated that joy and self-efficacy
were significant predictors of teacher collaborative behaviors in Clusters 1 and 2, highlighting the importance of positive emotions and
teaching confidence for teachers in the two clusters to work with their
peers. The literature has documented the positive outcomes of teacher
enjoyment in terms of instructional behavior and student emotions (e.g.,
Becker et al., 2014; Chen, 2019; Kunter et al., 2008; Saunders, 2013),
albeit without a specific focus on collaborative practices. Our study
contributes further evidence to substantiate these positive outcomes. As
explored in earlier research, self-efficacy has been linked to teacher
collaboration (Çoban et al., 2023), aligning with our findings. Additionally, it was unexpected to find that fatigue was positively related to

6. Conclusion and implications
While previous studies often delved into either the emotional or
cognitive aspects when examining their connections to teacher development and outcomes, our study adopted the "total teachers" perspective, encompassing both dimensions. The findings revealed three
distinct teacher clusters characterized by varying levels of emotions and
self-efficacy within the Taiwanese context of implementing a new national curriculum. Emotions were strongly related to self-efficacy, with
the degrees of emotional experiences aligning with those of self-efficacy
in each of the teacher clusters. Teachers with higher levels of positive
emotions and self-efficacy were able to demonstrate more professional
practices of collaboration and learner-centered teaching. Although
emotions and self-efficacy were interdependent, they exhibited different
strengths when examined together. Self-efficacy emerged as a more
robust predictor than emotions for teacher professional practices.
Moreover, teachers’ emotional reactions to the curriculum reform unveiled the operation of emotional rules embedded in the sociocultural
context of Asian societies. Balancing positive and negative emotions
reflects the social norm of the middle way (Miyamoto & Ryff, 2011).
Viewing “content as context” (Grossman & Stodolsky, 1995), math
teachers tended to show less dedication to reform actions due to their
value on the sequential dependency nature of math content.
These findings hold significant implications for policymakers and
school leaders. First, they underscore the heterogeneity among teachers,
with diverse emotional and efficacy profiles categorized into different
clusters. Tailored strategies should be developed to effectively support
teachers in implementing curriculum reform to accommodate these
varying characteristics. Second, in high-stakes accountability environments, teachers face heightened pressure. Despite prior literature suggesting that teachers in Asian societies tend to regulate their emotions
(Miyamoto & Ryff, 2011), our study still observed a moderately low
level of negative emotions, such as fatigue. This indicates the need for
school leaders to be attuned to teachers’ emotional well-being. Third,
the study found that the emotion of joy is conducive to teacher collaboration for most teachers, suggesting that schools should consider
exploring ways to help teachers derive enjoyment from their work by
providing both administrative and emotional support. Fourth, math
teachers were inclined toward less learner-centered teaching. Policy
implementation and professional development activities must consider
teachers’ conceptions of subject matter, which may affect their responses to reforms. Lastly, self-efficacy emerged as a significant predictor of professional practices in this study. Enhancing teacher
self-efficacy is a fundamental prerequisite for expected reform actions
initiated by teachers. Professional development programs can be
designed to foster teachers’ confidence in their teaching profession.
Limitations exist in this study, which are outlined along with suggestions for future academic inquiry. While we employed cluster analysis to uncover differences in reform behaviors among the clusters, we
did not elucidate the reasons behind the existence of three distinct
clusters. An alternative approach could involve discriminant analysis to
identify the influential variables that differentiate these clusters.
Moreover, our statistical analyses only captured the associations of
emotions, self-efficacy, and professional practices. To understand the
causal mechanisms, employing structural equation modeling would be
beneficial. This technique can help untangle the intricate relationships
and causal pathways among these variables. Additionally, we solely
relied on self-reported data, which might not accurately reflect the
