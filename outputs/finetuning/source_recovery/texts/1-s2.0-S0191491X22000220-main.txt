A B S T R A C T  
This study aims to fill in the gap of the accumulating literature on the paradoxical relationship between students’ 
non-cognitive variables and their academic achievement. Previous studies either focus on a single item or didn’t 
check the measurement invariance of the non-cognitive variables. This study first checked the measurement 
invariance of two important non-cognitive variables including students’ confidence and interest using TIMSS 
2015 data and found that both scales are metric invariant and thus the comparison of the correlation coefficients 
across different countries are possible. Correlational analysis indicates that the paradox exists at country level for 
all countries and at class level only for a few countries.   
1. Introduction 
Both students’ academic self-concept (ASC) and their interest in 
school subjects are non-cognitive factors that function as a driving force 
for students’ school engagement and their academic performance 
(K¨oller, Baumert, & Schnabel, 2001; Pintrich & Schunk, 1996; Viljar­
anta, Tolvanen, Aunola & Nurmi, 2014). 
1.1. Academic self-concept (ASC) 
Students’ ASC refers to students’ perceptions and attitudes toward 
their capacities and performance (Areepattamannil & Freeman, 2008; 
Lent, Brown, & Gore, 1997). It is an essential component of studies on 
school psychology as it impacts students in a variety of aspects including 
their motivation, school engagement and achievement, course/major 
selection in schools and even their future career path and accomplish­
ments (Marsh, Abduljabbar, Parker, Morin, Abdelfattah & Nagengast, 
2014). The association between students’ ASC and their academic per­
formance has been studied by many scholars (Areepattamannil & 
Freeman, 2008; Cokley, 2007; Marsh, 1990; Marsh, Trautwein, Lüdtke, 
K¨oller & Baumert, 2005; Valentine, DuBois, & Cooper, 2004; Viljaranta 
et al., 2014), and it has been found that students with higher ASC tend to 
perform better in school (Cokley & Patel, 2007; Marsh, 1990; Marsh 
et al., 2005; Pintrich & Schunk, 1996). In addition, researchers have 
identified a reciprocal relationship between students’ ASC and their 
academic performance. Specifically, students’ prior achievement in 
school can positively affect their ASC, subsequently a positive ASC is 
associated with better academic performance even after controlling for 
previous performance (Marsh et al., 2014). Therefore, students’ ASC can 
both be a cause and effect of their academic achievement (Eccles et al. 
1983; Marsh et al., 2005; Viljaranta et al., 2014). 
Students’ ASC is not only shaped by their previous performance but 
also by the achievement of their counterparts within the same class or 
school (Marsh et al., 2014). Adopting different frame of reference can 
lead to disparate ASC for the equally able students measured by objec­
tive standards (e.g., standardized tests). For example, students enrolled 
in selective schools or gifted education programs tend to underestimate 
their true academic capacity if they compare themselves with their 
classmates who are mostly exceptional students; however, if the same 
students were placed in less selective or even low-performing environ­
ment, their self-concept would be more positive. This contextual effect 
on students’ ASC is often referred to as “Big-Fish-Little-Pond-Effect” 
(BFLPE) (Marsh & Parker, 1984; Marsh et al., 2014). 
1.2. Academic interest 
Students’ general academic interest is regarded as a salient intrinsic 
motivational determinant for success in school (K¨oller et al., 2001). 
Normally, people’s motivation to exercise something can be either 
intrinsic or extrinsic. Intrinsic motivation derives from the subject or the 
activity itself (Eccles et al., 1998; Gottfried et al., 2001). Specifically in 
school, it is the pleasure or enjoyment of the learning process that

1.3. Paradoxical relationship in the field of international large-scale 
assessments 
The paradoxical relationship between non-cognitive factors, espe­
cially ASC and students’ academic performance using International 
Large-Scale Assessments (ILSAs) has been of interest of educational re­
searchers for nearly two decades (Liou, 2014; Marsh et al., 2014; Marsh, 
Abduljabbar, Parker, Morin, Abdelfattah & Nagengast, 2015; Shen & 
Pedulla, 2000; Shen & Talavera, 2003; Shen & Tam, 2008; Seaton, 
Marsh, & Craven, 2009; Wang, 2015; Wang & Bergin, 2017; Wang, 
2020). Specifically, the paradoxical relationship refers to a phenomenon 
that at the individual level, there is a positive association while at a 
higher aggregated or ecological level, the association turns to be 
negative. 
The most frequently used ILSAs data in such paradox studies are 
either from Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study 
(TIMSS) or from the Organization for Economic Co-operation and 
Development Program for International Student Assessment (PISA). 
1.4. Studies using TIMSS data 
Shen and his colleagues’ research is among the earliest paradox 
studies using ILSAs datasets. Shen & Pedulla (2000) analyzed TIMSS 
1995 data and found out that at individual level, there is a positive as­
sociation between students’ mathematics and science achievement and 
their self-perception in the two subjects (measured by two items: stu­
dents’ self-perceived competence in the subjects and students’ perceived 
easiness of the subjects); however, at country level, the association turns 
to be negative. And this paradox exists for all age cohorts (3rd, 4th, 7th, 
and 8th graders). In Shen’s following studies (Shen, 2002; Shen & 
Talavera, 2003; Shen & Tam, 2008), he and his colleagues continued to 
analyze TIMSS 1999 and TIMSS 2003 data and claimed consistent 
findings for 8th graders for both mathematics and science. Shen and 
Talavera (2003) also adopted the school as the unit of analysis to check 
the student-school level paradox and identified a mixed picture: the 
paradox is not present for how much students like the subjects and how 
well they think they are doing with the subjects but quite pronounced for 
students’ perceived easiness of the two subjects. They proposed that the 
rigor of the curriculum leads to such paradox. Specifically, less rigorous 
curriculum and loose scoring standards lead students to falsely believe 
that their academic capacity is high or the subject matter is easier than it 
actually is (Shen & Pedulla, 2000; Shen, 2002; Shen & Talavera, 2003; 
Shen & Tam, 2008). 
The series of Shen’s studies progressed along the time by including 
more educational systems, analyzing more self-perception items, con­
trolling covariates (Shen, 2002; Shen & Talavera, 2003; Shen & Tam, 
2008), and taking the complex survey design into consideration (Shen & 
Talavera, 2003; Shen & Tam, 2008). It is noted that these studies use 
single items rather than multi-item scales to measure student 
self-concept. This is probably due to the fact that the self-perception 
scale is only available till 2011 for TIMSS. It is a long debated topic as 
to whether multi-item measure outperforms single item measure 
(Bergkvist & Rossiter, 2007; Diamantopoulos, Sarstedt, Fuchs, Wilc­
zynski & Kaiser, 2012; Sarstedt & Wilczynski, 2009). Generally, using 
single item to measure a construct is regarded as skeptical or even a 
grave error due to its lack of reliability and validity (Sarstedt & 
Wilczynski, 2009). Another limitation of Shen’s studies is that the ma­
jority of them focused on eighth graders (Shen, 2002; Shen & Talavera, 
2003; Shen & Tam, 2008). 
Later, Marsh and his colleagues used TIMSS 2007 data to test the 
cross-cultural generalizability of Big Fish Little Pond Effect (BFLPE) and 
tried to earn its place as a pan-human theory (Marsh et al., 2014; Marsh 
et al., 2015). BFLPE is a theory proposed by Marsh (1987). Basically, 
BFLPE suggests that students tend to use their peers’ ability as a frame of 
reference to form their own academic self-concept and thus students 
with the same capacities tend to underestimate themselves when 
attending schools where students’ average performance is high while 
overestimate themselves when attending schools where students’ 
average performance is low. Compared with Shen’s studies, Marsh’ 
studies are methodologically more advanced. They adopted Doubly 
Latent Models to check the BFLPE across countries. Such method is a 
combination of Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) and Multilevel 
Modeling (MLM) to account for measurement and sampling error 
(Marsh et al., 2009). Both studies conducted measurement invariance of 
the self-concept construct and their analyses supported that the 
self-concept construct reached metric invariant, indicating that the as­
sociation between the latent variable and the observed items are the 
same across groups. However, the number of groups involved in the two 
studies are small (two countries and 26 countries, respectively). It is 
noted that Marsh et al. (2015) study also include primary school stu­
dents (fourth graders) and is the first study to use class as the unit of 
analysis. Wang (2015) is another study to analyze BFLPE using TIMSS 
2007 data. It also adopted Doubly Latent Model and the measurement 
invariance of the self-concept construct across 49 countries was tested at 
both within level and between level. 
Only two studies have been found using TIMSS 2011 data to explore 
BFLPE. One is Liou (2014), which only focused on Taiwai while another 
one is Wang and Bergin (2017), which examined BFLPE for eighth grade 
mathematics across 59 countries and regions. Wang and Bergin (2017) 
suggested that BFLPE existed in 58 out of 59 countries. The measure­
ment invariance of students’ math self-concept was tested, however, 
only configural measurement invariance has been reached. Therefore, 
the association between students’ mathematics achievement and their 
mathematics self-concept could only be analyzed in each country and 
both the magnitude and the direction of the association could not be 
compared across countries. Besides, compared with Marsh’s studies, 
Wang used 3 items (“I usually do well in mathematics”; “mathematics is 
not one of my strengths”; and “I learn things quickly in mathematics”) to 
measure students’ mathematics self-concept while Marsh used 4 items, 
the additional item is “mathematics is more difficult for me than for 
many of my classmates”. Thus, there is inconsistency on how to measure 
the self-concept construct among the paradox studies. 
The latest paradox study using the most updated TIMSS data (TIMSS 
2015) is Wang (2020). This study is an extension of Wang and Bergin 
(2017) and include also fourth graders. 
1.5. Studies using PISA data 
Paradox studies have also been conducted using Program for Inter­
national Student Assessment (PISA) data. Marsh and Hau (2003) used 
PISA 2000 data to analyze BFLPE across 26 industrialized western 
countries and obtained consistent findings with TIMSS data that the 
school average achievement (including mathematics, science, and 
reading) is negatively associated with students’ self-concept. Later 
studies using PISA 2003 data (Seaton, Marsh, & Craven, 2009; Seaton 
et al., 2010) increased the sample size of the educational systems to 
include both developed and developing countries. The findings of these 
studies support the universality of BFLPE. However, two limitations 
exist in those studies. First, the invariance level of the confidence scale 
was not checked beforehand; second, the cultural generalizability of 
BFLPE is based on school-aggregated results since the sampling unit in 
PISA is the whole school; which can cause concern because BFLPE is

1.6. The construct of the self-concept should be consistently measured 
across studies 
In Shen’s studies, self-concept is measured by single items; in 
Marsh’s studies, self-concept is measured by four items (see Table 2); 
and in Wang’s studies, self-concept is measured by three items (see 
Table 2). If the self-concept is not measured by the same items at the first 
place, we should be very careful to compare the findings from these 
studies. 
1.7. The construct of the self-concept should be invariant across different 
groups in each study 
Measurement non-invariance is listed as one of the major pitfalls in 
cross cultural studies (Rottig, 2009). If the construct in one cultural 
group differs from that of another cultural group, the analysis results 
based on these non-invariant constructs could be “at best ambiguous and 
at worst erroneous” (Steenkamp & Baumgartner, 1998, p. 78). Specif­
ically for the paradox studies, if the construct is different conceptually 
(e.g., the construct refers to different underlying factor or is composed of 
different items), there is no common ground to discuss any observed 
differences of the construct; if the construct is the same conceptually but 
different numerically (e.g., the association between the items and the 
construct is different across groups), statistical analysis could still be 
done but only within each group and cannot be compared with each 
other. For paradox studies, two kinds of measurement invariance of the 
self-concept scale should be taken into consideration. One is multilevel 
measurement invariance, which indicates that the self-concept scale 
should be measured the same both at student level and ecological levels 
(including class level, school level, and country level); another type of 
measurement invariance is that at each level, the scale should be 
invariant across different groups (e.g., countries, regions, or educational 
systems). In the existing literature, the issue of measurement invariance 
of the self-concept scales has just been raised up and taken care of in 
recent years. This is probably due to the fact that there is no self-concept 
scale in TIMSS or PISA dataset until recently. before that, single items 
were used as self-concept measure. Marsh and his colleagues’ BFLPE 
studies (Marsh et al., 2014, 2015) are pioneering studies which have 
checked the invariance of the self-concept scale before further exam­
ining the paradoxical association between this scale and students’ aca­
demic achievement. However, these studies didn’t check multilevel 
measurement invariance of self-concept scale and the countries/regions 
involved in the invariance test is limited. Marsh et al. (2014) has only 
two countries (United States and Saudi Arabia) involved and Marsh et al. 
(2015) has 13 countries involved. Wang & Bergin (2017) and Wang 
(2020) checked both the multilevel measurement invariance of 
self-concept within each country/region and the invariance of 
self-concept across different countries/regions, and the coun­
tries/regions involved is Wang’s studies are much more than Marsh’s 
study. However, it is noted that in Wang’s studies, the self-concept scale 
is only composed of three items. More details on different invariance 
levels and the appropriate analysis associated with each level is dis­
cussed in the methodology section. 
1.8. Purpose of the study 
Taken all these into consideration, this study aims to first check the 
measurement invariance of the non-cognitive variables including stu­
dents’ confidence in mathematics and science, and students’ interest in 
mathematics and science across different countries/regions before pro­
ceeding with further analysis. It is noted that students’ interest in 
mathematics and science has never been investigated for its invariance 
in the paradox studies. Next, the study conducts correlational analysis to 
explore whether the paradox exists using TIMSS 2015 data. If such 
paradox exists, is it because of East Asian countries? Previous interna­
tional assessments have identified two groups of high achieving coun­
tries/regions: East Asian Countries and European Countries (Lee, 2009; 
Stankov, 2010; Wilkins, 2004). However, compared with their European 
counterparts, who are also high achievers, East Asian students normally 
scored low in self-concept. This phenomenon has become known as the 
East Asian Learner Paradox (Stankov, 2010). Some researchers are sus­
picious of the actual existence of the East Asian Learner Paradox. Some 
argued that the discrepancy of students’ self-evaluation of these 
non-cognitive variables is the result of measurement issue that the 
non-cognitive indicators are only measured by very few items or even a 
single item (Stankov, 2010). Some other researchers proposed that the 
achievement gap observed between East Asian students and European 
students are exaggerated and thus does not represent the real differences 
of school performance between the two cultural groups (Hogan, 2008). 
Their reasoning is that East Asian pedagogy emphasizing memorization 
and repetition helps to produce high performers for TIMSS, but that is 
not the case for PISA since PISA requires more problem-solving skills for 
real life (Hogan, 2008). Despite the challenges of the existence of the 
East Asian Learner Paradox, Stankov (2010) suggests that the Confucius 
culture shared among the East Asian countries could be the driving force 
behind the East Asian Learner Paradox. The Confucius culture empha­
sizes academic achievement, competition, and collectivism and thus 
students tend to be more anxious and self-doubting (Wilkins, 2004; Lee, 
2009; Stankov, 2010). 
Therefore, we would like to explore whether the paradox is unique to 
East Asian countries or universal? However, it is noted that, this study on 
the paradox is only based on bivariate correlational analysis and thus 
exploratory. Hopefully, the results provided in this study could serve as a 
starting point to further explore the paradoxical association between 
non-cognitive factors and students’ academic achievement using more 
advanced methodology like the doubly latent models.

2. Data and methodology 
2.1. Data 
The data used in this study comes from the TIMSS 2015 dataset. 
TIMSS is an ILSA of mathematics and science for fourth and eighth 
graders which has been conducted every four years since 1995 (Mullis, 
Martin, Foy & Hooper, 2016). TIMSS 2015 includes data collected from 
57 countries and 7 benchmarking entities, which are additional educa­
tional systems within a country . The sample size of the study is above 
580,000 students (Mullis et al., 2016). 
2.2. Variables 
Non-cognitive scales included in this paper are: Students Like Learning 
Mathematics (fourth and eighth graders), Students Like Learning Science 
(fourth and eighth graders), Students Confidence in Mathematics (fourth and 
eighth graders), and Students Confidence in Science (fourth and eighth 
graders). The items measured by these scales appear below in Table 1 
and Table 2. As shown in Table 2, more items are included into the self- 
concept scale in this study than in previous studies (Marsh et al., 2014, 
2015; Wang, 2015, 2020; Wang & Bergin, 2017). 
2.3. Measurement invariance analysis 
Measurement invariance describes the comparability of a scale 
across different groups. In the CFA framework, a hierarchy of 
measurement invariance is defined as configural invariance to metric 
invariance/weak factorial invariance (Meredith, 1993) to scalar invar­
iance/strong factorial invariance (Meredith, 1993), where each type of 
invariance allows for greater comparability of the scale among groups 
(Milfont & Fischer, 2010). In the configural model, we allow both the 
factor loadings and intercepts to be freely estimated across countries. If 
the scale reaches configural invariance, we can conclude that the 
configuration of the scale is the same for each group and thus the scale 
can, at least, be compared conceptually across groups. In the metric 
model, we constrained the factor loadings to be the same across groups, 
while allowing intercepts to be freely estimated. When a scale reaches 
metric invariance, we can assume that not only the scale is composed of 
the same items, but also the association between the scale and items are 
the same across countries. To put it another way, one unit of change in 
the items will result in the same units of change in the scale for all 
groups. When metric invariance is reached, correlation and regression 
analysis are possible for such scales. In the scalar model, we constrain 
both the factor loadings and the intercepts to be the same across coun­
tries. If a scale exhibits scalar invariance, not only are associations be­
tween the scale and items the same for groups, but also the intercepts. 
Therefore, the mean values of such scales are comparable across groups. 
Therefore, a scale has to be at least metric invariant in order to compare 
the correlation or regression coefficients (both the magnitude and the 
direction) across different groups (either across different education 
systems or across different levels). Specifically for paradox studies, 
metric invariance is a prerequisite since we need to compare the direc­
tion of the association at different levels. 
However, the hierarchy of measurement invariance discussed above 
is developed based on continuous CFA modeling wherein the items to 
measure the unobserved factors are continuous variables. In our study, 
the items of all the eight non-cognitive scales are four-point likert scale 
(“Agree a lot”, “Agree a little”, “Disagree a little”, and “Disagree a lot”), 
therefore, categorical CFA seems to be a more appropriate modeling 
approach. The measurement invariance of categorical CFA is a little bit 
different from that of continuous CFA and the literature on the extension 
of categorical CFA to multiple population is still limited and relatively 
new (Millsap & Yun-Tein, 2004; Muth´en & Asparouhov, 2002; Rut­
kowski, Svetina, & Liaw, 2019; Svetina, Rutkowski, & Rutkowski, 2020; 
Wu & Estabrook, 2016). Similar to continuous CFA modeling, configural 
model is first tested, followed by equal slopes model, and then followed 
by more restrictive model: equal slopes and equal thresholds. The three 
models are analogous to configural, metric, and scalar models within 
continuous CFA modeling (Rutkowski & Svetina, 2017). Multiple Group 
Confirmatory Factor Analysis (MGCFA) was conducted using Mplus 8 to 
check the measurement invariance of the eight non-cognitive scales in 
the study. 
We compared the three models through both the chi-square test and 
through comparing model fit indices (e.g., CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and 
WRMR). The chi-square test, in our case, is not very informative since it 
is very sensitive to sample size while ILSA studies usually have big 
sample size (Bagozzi, 1977; Bentler & Bonett, 1980; Rutkowski & Sve­
tina, 2014); therefore, we only referred to the absolute change of the 
model fits to decide which measurement invariance level the scale has 
achieved. To speak in another way, if the model does not deteriorate 
significantly after being imposed with more constraints, we would claim 
that the scale reaches a higher level of measurement invariance.

invariance and ΔCFI ≥−0.1 for scalar invariance; ΔTLI ≥−0.15 for 
both metric and scalar invariance; ΔRMSEA ≤0.03 for metric invariance

invariance and ΔSRMR ≤0.01 for scalar invariance. For configural 
models, CFI and TLI should exceed.9, RMSEA should be less than.08 and 
WRMR should be less than.09. It is suggested that WRMR should not be 
used in isolation but rather be used together with CFI and TLI. If there is 
contradiction between model fit indices, CFI and TLI should be preferred 
(Rutkowski and Svetina, 2014).

3.2. Country level correlation analysis between students’ interest and 
math and science achievement 
In this section, the detailed description of the country level correla­
tion results is presented for Students Like Learning Mathematics (Fourth 
Graders) and then results for the other seven scales are discussed 
together. 
The results for Students Like Learning Mathematics (Fourth Graders) 
show that the student level correlations are positive for all countries, 
suggesting that there is a positive association between Students Like 
Learning Mathematics and mathematics performance at individual level 
for fourth graders. However, the country aggregated correlation shows a 
negative association (r_cnt level = −0.621, p < .01). As shown in Fig. 1, 
East Asian education systems including Singapore, HongKong, Korea, 
Taiwan, and Japan all appear at the upper left corner, indicating that the 
fourth graders in these countries tend to like mathematics less despite 
their high performance. Since also some researchers found that students 
in Asian countries have higher anxiety because of high stake tests 
(Amorin & Lam, 2013) and in general lower self-confidence and 
self-esteem (Ho, E., 2007), researchers assumed that these correlations 
could be attributed to the East Asian countries participating in TIMSS. 
Consequently, we also calculated the correlations for non-East Asian 
countries only. After excluding the East Asian countries from the figure, 
a negative country level correlation is still obvious (r_cnt level = −0.538, p 
< .01), suggesting that this educational paradox is a global phenomenon 
and cannot be attributed to cultural differences between East Asian and 
non-East Asian countries.

out to what extent such paradox exists and what are the reasons behind 
such paradox. Despite the accumulating literature on this topic, more 
studies are still needed to either complement what is missing in the 
literature or cross-validate what has been studied. This study aims to 
serve the first purpose. As mentioned in the introduction, few studies 
have ever checked the measurement invariance of the non-cognitive 
scales before analyzing their association with students’ academic per­
formance, which likely jeopardizes the validity of the statement that 
such paradox exists at the first place if the non-cognitive scales are not 
comparable among different groups. Our study fills this gap by checking 
two important non-cognitive scales: students’ confidence and interest in 
the subject matter. Our results suggest that all the non-cognitive scales 
are measurement invariant across educational systems. However, it is 
noted that the self-concept scales in this study includes more items into 
the scales compared with previous studies and some residual correla­
tions were added to improve the performance of the self-concept scale 
before proceeding with measurement invariance analysis. It is suggested 
that researchers should agree on what items should be included to 
measure students’ ASC. So far, the ASC scale in the filed of paradox 
studies is not measured consistently. 
The findings related to students’ interest is more promising since this 
scale performs relatively better. Based on correlational analysis, some 
potential research questions emerge such as whether the paradox is 
more obvious for younger students than older students and for mathe­
matics than science. In addition, based on what was found in our study, 
further research is needed to explore country specific explanations for 
the paradox where it exists.

5. Limitation and future studies 
As mentioned before, except for the measurement invariance test of 
the two important non-cognitive variables using most recent TIMSS 
data, the remaining part of the study is correlational analysis, which 
limits our capacity to make any statistical conclusions. It is mostly due to 
the fact that the variation across different levels (individual level, class 
level, and country level) was not partialed out and analysed separately; 
MLM should be adopted to distinguish within variation and between 
variation and will thus produce more accurate results. 
Since we found the evidence that there is paradoxical relationship 
between students’ non-cognitive scales and their mathematics/science 
achievement, it would be very interesting to investigate the differences 
in mathematics and science curricula. Will a curriculum analysis reveal 
that high achieving countries have demanding curricula, which nega­
tively affect students’ enjoyment in learning and their self-concept? Is 
teaching in higher achieving countries more strict or are there more 
drills included in teaching that create a positive impact on students’
