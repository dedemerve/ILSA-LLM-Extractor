ABSTRACT

ARTICLE HISTORY

The role of epistemic beliefs in science (EBS) and socio-economic
status (SES) on mathematics and science test results on both student- and school-level data was investigated via a secondary analysis of Estonian Programme for International Student Assessment
(PISA) 2015 survey data. The effective sample comprised 3991 students (52% girls, 48% boys) from 81 schools. Complementing
bivariate correlation analysis, two-level regression models were
computed where mathematics and science test scores were predicted from student- and school-level EBS as well as SES.
Mathematics and science test scores had a medium-sized correlation with both EBS as well as SES on the student-level data.
These correlations were larger on the school-level data. Multilevel
analyses showed that both higher mathematics and science
scores were predicted by male gender, higher student-level SES
and EBS, and higher school-level SES. Higher school-level EBS significantly predicted better science test scores, but this was not
the case with mathematics.

Introduction
Researchers and educators alike have developed an increased interest in understanding the diverse elements of the learning process. One area in particular that has
gained significant attention is how students’ beliefs about knowledge and knowing
(epistemic beliefs) are a part of the process of learning, and how these beliefs are
related to the knowledge-acquisition and knowledge-construction process (Hofer,
2001). Several studies have demonstrated the significant role of epistemic beliefs in
academic performance in the domains of mathematics as well as science (Cano, 2005;
Schommer, 1993). Additionally, epistemic beliefs are also especially relevant in the age

of information, e.g., when dealing with misinformation (Kerwer & Rosman, 2020; Torres
et al., 2018), as well as in the context of environmental and climate change topics
(Tasquier et al., 2016). While there are studies focusing on student-level epistemic
beliefs and academic achievement, little is known about the role of school-level
epistemic beliefs on students’ mathematics and science performance. The aim of the
present work is to investigate the association between students’ epistemic beliefs,
socio-economic status, and mathematics and science test performance on both student- and school-level data.
Epistemic beliefs (also known as personal epistemology, epistemological beliefs;
Kampa et al., 2016), and epistemic beliefs in science, or EBS (see Schiefer et al., 2022),
are a collection of beliefs of a particular individual about knowledge and its nature,
origin, and source (Hofer & Pintrich, 1997; Schommer, 1993). Epistemic beliefs have
been studied within a wide variety of frameworks, such as developmental, multidimensional, or integrated view (Merk et al., 2018). The developmental perspective refers to
beliefs about knowledge changing from being absolute and certain to forming an
understanding about the subjectivity of knowledge (i.e., it is based on subjective opinions), and learning that there is no absolute truth (and that knowledge depends on
argumentative strength of competing statements) in the final stage of this process
(Kuhn et al., 2000; Perry, 1970). The multidimensional approach posits that beliefs
about knowledge and its formation can be described by properties such as simplicity
and certainty (Hofer & Pintrich, 1997; Schommer, 1990). Finally, the integrative view
brings together the previous frameworks by assuming that several epistemic belief
dimensions (e.g., how a student believes how certain knowledge is) may independently develop over time (i.e., from thinking of knowledge in absolute terms to forming
more flexible and complex beliefs; Bendixen & Rule, 2004; Peter et al., 2016). Relatedly,
epistemic beliefs can be described as a continuum starting from naïve and ending
with more mature beliefs. Naïve beliefs refer to the beliefs that knowledge is certain
and unchanging, while mature beliefs imply to beliefs about knowledge as more complex and tentative (Buehl & Alexander, 2005; Mohamed & El-Habbal, 2013). Students
who hold more developed (mature) epistemic beliefs tend to have a deeper level of
understanding as opposed to students with more naïve (or less developed) belief systems (Greene et al., 2010).
Research has demonstrated the positive link between (more mature) epistemic
beliefs and academic achievement (e.g., see a meta-analysis by Greene et al., 2018,
based on 132 studies). Epistemic beliefs are positively associated with final course
grades and grade-point-averages (Hofer, 2001; Schommer-Aikins et al., 2005), and epistemic beliefs influence students’ cognitive processes of thinking, reasoning, and
knowledge acquisition (Hofer, 2001; Lee, 2019; Mason et al., 2013; Peters & Kitsantas,
2010). More mature EBS are positively related to students’ motivation and higher levels of self-efficacy (Buehl & Alexander, 2005; Chen, 2012; Mason et al., 2013).
What could explain the relationship between epistemic beliefs and academic
achievement? It has been hypothesised that beliefs affect achievement through
self-regulated learning (Hofer & Pintrich, 1997; Schommer, 1998). Self-regulated
learning implies a process where a learner actively monitors, manages, and regulates
their actions for expanding knowledge and to improve information acquisition

(Paris & Paris, 2001), and it is associated with better academic outcomes in itself, too
(see the meta-analysis by Jansen et al., 2019). Epistemic beliefs play an important role
in self-regulated learning, as they affect task definition, goal-setting, applying tactics
or strategies chosen for task execution, as well as success or failure attribution upon
completing the task (Muis, 2007). Therefore, epistemic beliefs play a significant role in
the formation of self-regulated learning which, in turn, affects academic achievement
(Muis & Franco, 2009; Trevors et al., 2016).
It has also been shown that individual epistemic beliefs are related to students’
socio-economic status (SES). For instance, it has been found that students with more
educated parents were less likely to believe in the simplistic nature of knowledge, and
the beliefs about quickness to which knowledge is obtained were also linked to
parents’ education (Schommer, 1990). Another study demonstrated that elementary
school students with lower SES had an overall less refined belief regarding knowledge
and produced lower scores in each of the four belief scales (Conley et al., 2004). In
addition, Ozkal et al. (2011) showed that family SES is positively correlated to tentative
views, whereas fixed views were negatively associated with family SES. In other words,
students from families with a high SES tend to embrace a more sophisticated belief
system, such as accepting that knowledge is changeable and not necessarily handed
down from an authority figure, compared with students with low SES family backgrounds. One possible hypothesis to explain these findings is that more educated
parents may provide a home environment that fosters an opportunity for children to
experience independence, resulting in the development of a more refined belief system (Ozkal et al., 2011).
SES effects on achievement and development have been noted already in early
stages of schooling (D’Angiulli et al., 2004). Students’ SES has also been shown to be
associated with academic achievement, with small-to-medium effect sizes (Jimerson
et al., 2000; Sirin, 2005; White, 1982). Not only is students’ SES a predictor of their academic achievement – it has also been demonstrated that higher SES of school as well
as students peers’ family SES are related to better academic outcomes (Caldas &
Bankston, 1997; Perry & McConney, 2010). Attending a school with classmates who
come from higher SES backgrounds does tend to positively affect one’s academic
achievement. In fact, the correlation between school average SES and school average
academic performance has been shown to be stronger than these correlations on the
individual level (Sirin, 2005).
Thus far, the role of a class or school’s average levels of EBS on students’ academic
achievement have received little or no attention in research. While the results of PISA
2015 survey (please see the Materials and Methods section for details) showed that
EBS are positively correlated with better science achievement test scores on a countrylevel analysis (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD],
2016, p. 48), these results could be explained by between-school differences in teaching, found in PISA 2015 (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development,
2016, p. 38). However, the school-level differences in SES and epistemic beliefs have
not been considered while predicting achievement test results in the PISA 2015 survey. Schools and classrooms are environments that not only facilitate learning, but
may also be responsible for the development of students’ attitudes towards learning

(Ryan, 2001). In addition to peers and their attitudes, teacher beliefs are also an
important factor in the formation of instructional context and students’ EBS (Hofer,
2001; Muis & Duffy, 2013; Pajares, 1992). Of relevance, Hattie (2017) has found that
school-level factors have a considerable effect on students’ achievement. Importantly,
however, school-level self-regulated learning can be effectively improved at schoollevel (Dignath & B€

by including both school-level SES and epistemic beliefs in explaining differences in

As students are nested into classes and schools, it is generally reasonable to implement multilevel analyses on educational data. Program for International Student
Assessment (PISA) meets many of the standards set for educational research, namely,
rigorous sampling and study design and well-developed objective measures of student
achievement (Anderson et al., 2009). Within the PISA survey, the data are collected on
student- as well as school-level. Therefore, the data are hierarchical in structure, which
means that multilevel modelling is a suitable analytic approach enabling to clarify the
role of student- vs school-level factors. In the current study, we aim to leverage on
these possibilities.
The main aim of the current work is to investigate how epistemic beliefs and SES
are associated with mathematics and science test scores from PISA 2015 survey. In
addition to student-level analyses, we study how these variables are inter-related in
the school-level data. Based on previous findings, we posed the following hypotheses:
H1: Higher epistemic belief scores (reflecting more mature epistemic beliefs) are positively associated with mathematics and science test scores. This hypothesis is rather confirmative, as the link between epistemic beliefs and academic outcomes has been
previously demonstrated (Greene et al., 2018).
H2: Higher socio-economic status (SES) is positively associated with mathematics and
science test scores. This hypothesis, too, is in line with previous findings demonstrating
the positive link between SES and academic achievement (Jimerson et al., 2000; Sirin,
2005; White, 1982).
H3: School-level socio-economic status and epistemic beliefs are positively associated
with student-level mathematics and science test scores. It has previously been shown
that school-level SES is associated with academic achievement (Perry & McConney,
2010). Although the associations between school-level epistemic beliefs and studentlevel academic achievement have received little attention, based on previous research,
it is reasonable to assume that school-level factors affect academic outcomes (Hattie,
2017; Muis & Duffy, 2013). Since the link between epistemic belief and academic
achievement has been established on student-level data (Greene et al., 2018), it is reasonable to hypothesise that this may also be the case on school-level data.
The present work could have both theoretical and practical implications.
Specifically, the results of the current study could further shed light not only on some
of the relatively robust findings (e.g., those of academic achievement and SES, as well
as STEM-subjects and epistemic beliefs on student-level data), but also outline the role
of school-level factors that may influence students’ mathematics and science results.
One may consider school-level clustering of achievement, SES, and epistemic beliefs as
somewhat analogous to the clustering of causes of risk factors of health, e.g., the

neighbourhood effect (Halonen et al., 2012). It is necessary to study the clustering of
predictors of academic achievement to better understand the causes and mechanisms
of inequality in education which may involve differences in SES, prestige of schools,
etc. It does not mean, however, that school-level effects are confined to differences in
teaching methods and/or quality; rather, the results may show that a significant part
of school differences stem from the tendency of children with similar SES background
to go to similar schools. Similarly, school-level epistemic beliefs may indicate to the
role of parents’ supportive attitude towards learning science, and not only the role of
school itself. Before these hypotheses can be tested, however, the present work aims
to show whether and how school-level SES and epistemic beliefs are associated with
students’ academic results.

Materials and methods
Sample and procedure
The PISA is a triennial international survey since 2000, which aims to evaluate education systems worldwide by testing the skills and knowledge of 15-year-old students in
primarily three domains: science, mathematics, and reading comprehension. More specifically, PISA covers students who are aged between 15 years, 3 months, and 16 years,
2 months at the time of assessment, who are enrolled in a school (in grades 7 and
higher) and have completed at least 6 years of formal schooling (Organisation for
Economic Co-operation and Development, 2017a). PISA uses a two-stage stratified
sampling method where, firstly, schools are sampled according to the school type and
demographic composition of a given region. Then, the students are sampled within a
school. Due to non-random sample selection where units are selected with differing
probabilities, sampling weights are used (Rutkowski et al., 2010). In case of nested
models (e.g., multilevel, or hierarchical models) that use student- (Level 1) and schoollevel (Level 2) data, using only school-level weights has been advised due to higher
accuracy (Mang et al., 2021).
In 2015, or the sixth wave of the PISA, there was a deeper focus on science (but, as
mentioned, mathematics and reading competencies were assessed as well). Computerbased tests were used, with assessments lasting for a total of 2 hours for each student.
In addition to the competencies test, students filled out a background questionnaire
which took approximately 35 minutes to complete. The questionnaire gathered information about the students themselves, their homes, and their school and learning
experiences (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2017a).
Importantly, each student only responded to a subset of all available items of reading, mathematics, and science competencies, with the rest being imputed to form
plausible values (PVs) that are multiple imputations of unobservable latent achievement for each student (Wu, 2005). The PISA student achievement dataset provides 10
PVs for reading, mathematics, and science test scores (Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2017a). It is advised to report the results of statistical
analyses (e.g., estimates) by using all PVs where estimates across all PV-dependent variations of models are combined by following the Rubin’s (1987) multiple imputation
approach (Laukaityte & Wiberg, 2017; Rutkowski et al., 2010).

Analytic sample
In the current study, we focused on the PISA 2015 Estonian sample. Data for all of the
participating countries is available on the OECD website (Organisation for Economic
Co-operation and Development, 2017a). We chose the Estonian sample because the
Estonian students were among the highest-scoring in the PISA 2015 survey. In addition, as also stated in the OECD’s earlier, PISA 2012 report, the between-school variation in achievement scores in Estonia is among the lowest, meaning that the
Estonian school system is comprehensive and does not differentiate by program,
school, or student ability (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development,
2013, pp. 46–47). In other words, ‘no matter what school [the students] attend, [the
students] are likely to achieve at high levels’ (Organisation for Economic Co-operation
and Development, 2013, p. 46). While we acknowledge that it would be possible to
include other countries’ and economies’ educational systems comparatively, we also
need to note that these kind of comparisons would be out of the scope of the present
study. Additionally, because high-level sampling procedures are utilised during the
PISA 2015 on a country level, providing representability of the student population, the
present data should be clearly suitable for addressing the aims of the present study.
The data for the Estonian survey comprised n ¼ 6147 15-year-old respondents from
n ¼ 207 different schools. Only 9th grade students were included for comparability;
furthermore, data of participants without achievement test scores and of participants
who had missing values on more than two epistemic beliefs in science scale items
were excluded. After these procedures, there were less than 0.5% missing data which
were imputed by using predictive mean matching. Finally, we excluded the participants from schools with less than 10 students. This resulted in the effective sample of
n ¼ 3991 students (52% girls, 48% boys) from n ¼ 81 schools (students per school
M ¼ 49.27, SD ¼ 26.63, Min ¼ 10, Max ¼ 115).
Measures
The PISA 2015 mathematics and science test scores
The PISA 2015 survey mathematics and science tests reflect the literacy regarding
these domains; in other words, these tests aim to assess the students’ capacity to formulate, employ and interpret mathematics and science concepts, procedures, facts
and tools to describe, predict and explain the phenomena (Organisation for Economic
Co-operation and Development, 2016).
As mentioned earlier, each student responded to a subset (i.e., not all) available
items to tests of mathematics and science competencies. The achievement test scores
are normed (using item response theory) on the scale from 0 (lowest score) to 1000
(highest score).
Studies have demonstrated that PISA achievement scores are associated with future
academic outcomes, providing evidence for validity (Fischbach et al., 2013; Rozgonjuk
et al., 2021).
The PISA 2015 epistemic beliefs in science (EBS) index
The epistemic beliefs in science (EBS) index is based on the model of EBS conceptualised by Conley et al. (2004). The original model contains four dimensions of EBS:

source (five items), certainty (six items), development (six items), and justification
(nine items). However, the PISA 2015 survey used three items from two of the dimensions: development (Ideas in science sometimes change; Sometimes scientists change their
minds about what is true; The ideas in science books sometimes change) and justification
(A good way to know if something is true is to do an experiment; Good answers are
based on evidence from many different experiments; It is good to try experiments more
than once to make sure of your findings). The response scales ranged from 1 ¼ Strongly
disagree to 4 ¼ Strongly agree.
The average (M ¼ 0) is estimated across the scores of OECD countries following the
item response theory approach. For the Estonian sample, EBS index M ¼ 0.02
and SD ¼ 0.89.
Although several works (J. Guo et al., 2022; L. Guo, 2022; Winberg et al., 2019) have
demonstrated the sound psychometric properties of the EBS index, we also checked
the internal consistency of the EBS on item-level data in the present analytical sample.
For that, we used the omega() function from psych v2.1.9 (Revelle, 2021) package.
Since the scale comprises the items from two subscales of the original EBS (Conley
et al., 2004), the number of expected factors was set to two. The results of this function include Cronbach’s alpha for the unidimensional scale, as well as McDonald’s
omega total (for the entire scale) and hierarchical (for each subscale). Hierarchical
omega is estimated using a Schmid–Leiman transformation and maximum likelihood
estimation. The results showed that the scale has good psychometric properties, as
Cronbach’s a was .87, McDonald’s xhierarchical was .76, and McDonald’s xtotal was .89
(xtotal ¼ .81 for justification subscale, and xtotal ¼ .82 for development subscale). Of
note, Schmid–Leiman factor loadings were higher for the unidimensional solution
(ranging from .63 to .73) in comparison to factor loadings of the subscales (ranging
from .33 to .45 and .27 to .44 for justification and development subscales, respectively).
Additionally, the correlation between subscale scores was r ¼ .88.
Although the items of the scale are drawn from two subscales of the original EBS
(Conley et al., 2004), recent works (J. Guo et al., 2022; L. Guo, 2022; Winberg et al.,
2019) as well as our additional analyses (mentioned above) with the present analytical
sample showed that the use of a unidimensional EBS index is justified. It should also
be noted that the subscales of EBS in the original PISA analyses (Organisation for
Economic Co-operation and Development, 2017b) were combined and validated (e.g.,
a correlation around r ¼ .50 across different countries), demonstrating very good
internal consistency, e.g., Cronbach’s a ¼ .875. Altogether, therefore, in the present
study, we used the unidimensional EBS weighted likelihood estimates (WLE) score
computed by the PISA 2015 team in the current work as a measure of EBS.

The PISA 2015 index of economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS)
Over the past PISA survey cycles, the PISA index of economic, social and cultural status
(ESCS) has been used to reflect the ‘students’ access to family resources (financial capital, social capital, cultural capital and human capital) which determine the social position of the student’s family/household’ (Avvisati, 2020, p. 3). The index is a composite
score of the following variables: 1. The socio-economic Index of Occupational Status;
2. The highest level of education of the student’s parents, (converted into years of

schooling); 3. The PISA index of family wealth (information about rooms at home, cars,
etc.); 4. The PISA index of home educational resources; 5. The PISA index of possessions
related to ’classical’ culture in the family home.
While there has been discussion on the validity of the scale (Rutkowski &
Rutkowski, 2013), it has been suggested that the ESCS index can be used as a ‘highlevel descriptive indicators of inequality of opportunity and of outcomes in education,
or as a “control” variable to account for the possible confounding effect of pre-existing
individual differences on the outcome of interest’ (Avvisati, 2020, p. 4). Therefore, in
the current work, while mindful about the limitations discussed by Rutkowski and
Rutkowski (2013), we use the ESCS as an indicator of socio-economic status (SES).
The average of ESCS (M ¼ 0) is a result of the scaling procedure, while countryspecific means are estimated following the item response theory approach. For the
Estonian sample, the ESCS index M ¼ 0.08 and SD ¼ 0.76.

Analysis
Data analysis was conducted in R v4.1.3 (R Core Team, 2022). Analyses including the
10 mathematics and 10 science plausible values were conducted by following the
Rubin’s (1987) multiple imputation approach. We used the misty v0.4.4 package
(Yanagida, 2022) for multilevel descriptive statistics and intraclass coefficients (ICCs).
Weighted Pearson correlation analysis was conducted with the stats v4.1.3 (R Core
Team, 2022) package. In addition, we computed regression models predicting mathematics or science test scores. Two models – one with mathematics and the other
with science achievement test scores as the outcomes – included age and gender,
and student- and school-level SES and EBS indices as predictor variables. The models
were computed with the lmer() function from the lme4 package version 1.1-23 (Bates
et al., 2015), using the restricted maximum likelihood estimator. In all models, school
variable was treated as random effects, while gender, socio-demographic index, and
epistemic beliefs were treated as fixed effects. We used the non-response adjusted
school base weights in analyses, as suggested in Mang et al. (2021). The package
mitml v0.4.3 (Grund et al., 2021) was used for pooling the model estimate results and
computing the total R2 statistics (Snijders & Bosker, 2012, p. 112).

Multilevel analysis models
In addition to student-level results, we also investigated if students’ mathematics and
science test scores are associated with school-level EBS and SES. In order to meet that
goal, a series of multilevel models were computed for each plausible value of mathematics and science test scores; the model estimates were then pooled by using
Rubin’s (1987) multiple imputation approach (Laukaityte & Wiberg, 2017; Rutkowski
et al., 2010). For each model, the fixed effects were students’ gender, EBS, and SES
index scores; additionally, school-level EBS and SES scores were included. School was
introduced as random effects variable. The non-response adjusted school base weights
were used, as recommended by Mang et al. (2021).
The results of these models are presented in Table 2. Both models show that boys
have higher scores than girls in mathematics and science tests. Additionally, in both
cases, higher student-level SES and EBS predicted higher test scores. Higher schoollevel SES was also positively associated with higher mathematics and science test
scores, whereas higher school-level EBS scores were significantly predicting higher science test scores, but not mathematics test scores.

Discussion
The aim of the present study was to investigate, on both student- and school-level,
how epistemic beliefs in science (EBS), and socio-economic status (SES) are associated
with student-level mathematics and science test scores. To meet this goal, we used
the PISA 2015 survey data and implemented both bi- and multivariate (including
multilevel) modelling. We posed several hypotheses.
First, we expected that EBS is positively associated with mathematics and science test
scores (H1). This hypothesis found support from the data, coherent also with previous

support from the data, in line with previous findings showing the positive correlation
between SES and academic achievement (Jimerson et al., 2000; Sirin, 2005; White, 1982).
Finally, we investigated how school- and student-level EBS and SES are associated with
student-level mathematics and science test scores. We hypothesised that these variables
are positively correlated (H3). We found that this was, indeed, the case. In fact, schoollevel variables had a stronger correlation with student-level academic outcomes than
student-level EBS and SES. Furthermore, school- and student-level EBS jointly predicted
both science, but not mathematics test scores. One potential explanation for this discrepancy could be in the potential overlap between the EBS questionnaire with science
test items. Specifically, science achievement test includes an epistemic knowledge area
in knowledge subcategory which addresses a student’s knowledge about the nature, origin, and understanding of knowledge. Therefore, it could be that due to this partial overlap there is a stronger association between EBS and science achievement test scores. On
the other hand, one should not overestimate this partial overlap, since the results of
mathematics achievement test suggest that although the correlations with EBS and SES
are lower (than science test’s associations with EBS and SES), the correlations of these
two achievement domains are in quite similar magnitude.
The results of the present study outline the importance of EBS for mathematics and
science achievement, at least on the student-level data. While SES, too, is a significant
predictor of academic achievement, it is more difficult to change one’s SES than fostering epistemic beliefs. The findings also suggest that when it comes to education, there
seem to be grounds for the so-called ’Matthew effect’ that goes by the adage ’the rich
get richer and the poor get poorer’ (Kerckhoff & Glennie, 1999; Rigney, 2010). High SES
on school-level tends to go together with better academic achievement in students.
The main contribution of the current study was the investigation of associations
between school- and student-level epistemic beliefs and SES on student-level

academic achievement. We demonstrated both student- and school-level epistemic
beliefs are highly relevant for better academic achievement. These results suggest that
it may be useful to emphasise the relevance of fostering epistemic beliefs in teacher
training if the goal is to improve the mathematical and science skills of students. The
students’ SES background is necessary to consider, as it may define whether the students have access to resources necessary for both developing epistemic beliefs as well
as improving their academic achievement. In addition, these results may have implications on educational policy – especially if the aim is to reduce gaps in educational
achievement. In case higher SES does foster better epistemic beliefs in science, policymakers may find it useful to support lower-SES schools with additional resources.
While this may not necessarily change a given individual student’s socio-economic
situation, the student may benefit from additional resources that support studying
and improving epistemic beliefs.
Some limitations need to be addressed. First, even though the PISA survey has
high standards and the data quality, in general, is high, this study is cross-sectional.
Therefore, even though there may be indications towards causal relationships, we
are careful in the current study with regards to causal inferences. While it seems
more plausible that school-level factors may influence a student’s academic achievements (not vice versa), there may be other causal variables that influence both
school-level as well as individual factors. Second, it may also be relevant to consider
not only school-level, but also higher level, e.g., regional, variables. It may be that a
certain region (e.g., a city) attracts wealthier families whose children attend the
schools in those regions. Our analyses were restricted to Estonian context; however,
future studies could also replicate the findings in other educational systems or
implement a cross-cultural approach. Third, the indices used for SES and EBS did
not cover all the facets of those constructs. Fourth, although studies have used PISA
2015 epistemic beliefs index as a reflection of a student’s epistemic beliefs (J. Guo
et al., 2022; Karakolidis et al., 2019), the implemented scale might not capture all
the dimensions of epistemic beliefs, it is limited in covering the different dimensions
of epistemic beliefs, and is limited to epistemic beliefs only in the context of scientific inquiry. Encompassing other dimensions and beyond scientific enquiry provide
more extended insights into the associations between mathematics and science
achievement, socio-economic status, and epistemic beliefs between students
and schools.
In conclusion, our study demonstrated that students’ mathematics and science
scores are positively associated with epistemic beliefs and SES; in addition, students
who go to schools that seem to foster more epistemic thinking (reflected by higher
epistemic beliefs in science scores) and are generally with a higher SES get higher
mathematics and science test scores. These results outline the relevance of epistemic
thinking in mathematics and science education.
