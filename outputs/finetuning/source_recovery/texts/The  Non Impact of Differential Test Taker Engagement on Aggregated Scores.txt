The (Non)Impact of Differential Test
Taker Engagement on Aggregated Scores
Steven L. Wise, James Soland, and Yuanchao Bo
NWEA, USA
Disengaged test taking tends to be most prevalent with low-stakes tests. This has
led to questions about the validity of aggregated scores from large-scale inter-
national assessments such as PISA and TIMSS, as previous research has found
a meaningful correlation between the mean engagement and mean performance
of countries. The current study, using data from the computer-based version
of the PISA-Based Test for Schools, examined the distortive effects of differential
engagement on aggregated school-level scores. The results showed that, although
there was considerable differential engagement among schools, the school means
were highly stable due to two factors. First, any distortive effects of disengage-
ment in a school were diluted by a high proportion of the students exhibiting no
non-effortful behavior. Second, and most interestingly, disengagement produced
both positive and negative distortion of individual student scores, which tended to
cancel out much of the net distortive effect on the school’s mean.
Keywords: computer-based testing, test motivation, test-taking engagement
During the past two decades, there has been a growing appreciation of
the impact of disengaged test taking on test score validity. It has been well
documented that disengaged test takers tend to perform, on average, lower
than their engaged peers, indicating that disengagement has a distortive effect
on scores (Wise & DeMars, 2005). This problem, moreover, has been long
recognized as an important threat to test score validity (Cronbach, 1960;
Thorndike, 1904), as disengaged test taking constitutes a serious construct-
irrelevant factor (Haladyna & Downing, 2004; Messick, 1984). Disengaged
test taking tends to be most prevalent with low-stakes tests (i.e., when test
takers do not perceive personal consequences associated with their test
performance).

Score interpretations, however, are not made only about individual scores.
There are numerous assessment contexts in which inferences are made on
aggregations of individual test-taker scores (e.g., means), and it has been
found that comparisons among these aggregations may be distorted if differen-
tial levels of engagement are present (Wise & DeMars, 2010). In practice, this
issue has led to questions about the validity of interpretations from large-scale
international assessments such as the Programme for International Student
Assessment (PISA) and the Trends in International Mathematics and Science
Study (TIMSS). In these assessment programs, samples of students from
different countries are administered assessments, the scores from which have
no consequences for the students (such as affecting grades or graduation). It is
therefore sensible to question how many students are disengaged when taking
these assessments, and the impact of such disengagement on the aggregated
scores for a country. Moreover, it is natural for someone to value the educa-
tional attainment of one’s own country relative to other countries, which
can lead to speculation regarding the degree to which the students from
higher-performing countries simply tried harder on the assessment than
students from one’s own country. Such questions shift the focus away from
the impact of disengagement on individual scores to the degree to which
differential disengagement between groups might distort country rank orderings
based on aggregated scores from international assessments (Elk€of, Pavesic, &
Grønmo, 2014; Hopfenbeck et al., 2017; Zamarro, Hitt, & Mendez, 2016).
This concern has led to research on the relationship between engagement
and aggregated test performance. One of the challenges inherent in this
research has been how to measure test-taking engagement. A diverse set
of measures has been used with data from international assessments. Butler
and Adams (2007) used the “effort thermometer” administered during the
PISA assessment that asks test takers to self-report their levels of engagement.
Several other studies investigating PISA have measured engagement by assess-
ing student performance on items that appear at different positions on different
test forms. Borghans and Schils (2012) and Debeer, Buchholz, Hartig, and
Janssen (2014) used the decline in item performance across item position as
an indicator of diminishing engagement. Similarly, Cosgrove and Cartwright
(2014) and Zamarro and colleagues (2016) used both declining performance
on items and increases in the numbers of omitted responses. Two studies
used engagement indicators based on how completely or carefully students
completed separate student surveys that accompanied the PISA or TIMSS
cognitive assessments (Boe, May, & Boruch, 2002; Zamarro et al., 2016).
Finally, Goldhammer, Martens, Christoph, and L€udtke (2016) measured
engagement based on response time-based identification of rapid-guessing
behavior (Schnipke, 1995; Wise & Kong, 2005).

Despite differences in the methods used to measure engagement, the studies
mentioned above have almost always found sizable correlations between
engagement and achievement. Boe and colleagues (2002) reported that more
than 50% of the national mean differences in achievement on TIMSS could be
explained by their engagement indicator (which they termed persistence).
Debeer and associates (2014) reported a 0.36 correlation between average
engagement and national reading scores. Borghans and Schils (2012) found
that engagement could explain 19% of the variation in average PISA test
scores between countries. Zamarro and coauthors (2016) reported correlations
between engagement and country performance on PISA ranging from 0.43 to
0.64. Similarly, Goldhammer and associates (2016), studying engagement on
the Programme of International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC),
found correlations between mean engagement and mean performance across
all countries ranging from 0.33 to 0.61.
The one study that did not find a relationship between engagement and per-
formance was Butler and Adams (2007), who reported that the expenditure
of effort on PISA by students was fairly stable across countries. The absence
of a relationship in the Butler and Adams study, however, may be due to chal-
lenges in using the PISA effort thermometer. Eivers (2010) noted that students
with poor reading achievement may have had difficulty understanding what
the effort thermometer was asking them to do, an observation supported by
the fact that 17.5% of the students did not complete the effort thermometer.
In addition, Butler and Adams treated those nonresponses as missing data,
although they may alternatively could have been interpreted as indicating
disengagement.
Hence, apart from the Butler and Adams (2007) study, sizable positive
correlations have been found at the country level between engagement and
performance. This has led to questions regarding the extent to which differen-
ces observed in performance between countries are due to differential test-
taking engagement—which would threaten the validity of inferences based on
the rankings of country performance scores. However, although observing
a correlation between engagement and performance is important, it does not
provide information about the nature of the relationship and the degree to
which country performance scores are distorted by disengaged test taking.
Having such information would be useful in gauging how concerned we
should be about the impact of disengagement. If, for example, the relative

METHOD
Instrument
The data set analyzed in this study came from the pilot administration of
a computer-based version of the PISA-Based Test for Schools (PBTS; OECD,
2016). The PBTS provides schools information about their 15-year-old
students by assessing their skills and creative application of knowledge in
mathematics, reading, and science. PBTS scores are linked to the international
PISA scale, and are used by schools to support research, benchmarking,
and school improvement. Because there were no consequences for students
associated with their PBTS performance, the test administration would be
characterized as low stakes.
The PBTS administers 140 scored cognitive items distributed across seven
test forms, with approximately 60 items per form. The PBTS uses multiple
item types, about half of which are selected response items, with the remainder
being short answer or brief constructed response. Each PBTS form is
administered within a relatively unspeeded 120-minute testing session that
is followed by the PISA self-report effort thermometer, and an online student
questionnaire. The PBTS is scored using the multinomial logit model (Adams,
Wilson, & Wang, 1997), and additional information regarding the PBTS and
the data set used in this study can be found in Wise and Gao (2017).
Participants
The data set analyzed in this study was the one used by Wise and Gao (2017).
In their study, the online version of the PBTS was pilot tested on 5,091
15-year-old students from 84 schools in the United States.
Measurement of Test-Taking Engagement
When computer-based tests (CBTs) are used, item response time can be used
to identify rapid-guessing behavior, which is characterized by responses
occurring so rapidly that test takers did not have time to fully consider
the item (Wise & Kong, 2005). With low-stakes tests, rapid guesses have been
60

shown to represent disengaged responses from unmotivated test takers. Each
item response in a test event can be classified as either a rapid guess or a solu-
tion behavior (i.e., the engaged counterpart to a rapid guess). Overall engage-
ment can be measured by response time effort (RTE), which is the proportion
of a test taker’s responses that were solution behaviors.
Response time-based methods for measuring engagement have several
advantages over other engagement measures. First, these methods classify
effort on each item, which permits a finer-grained assessment of a test taker’s
engagement. Second, the identification of rapid guessing provides a behavioral
link that can be validated between item response time and disengagement.
Rapid guesses have been shown to have accuracy rates similar to those from
random responding (Wise, 2015), and rapid guessing appears to represent a
response process markedly different from that underlying solution behavior
(Wise & Kuhfeld, in press). Moreover, rapid guessing and other types of rapid
responses do not appear to contribute to measurement, because they provide
little to no psychometric information (Goldhammer et al., 2016; Wise & Gao,
2017; Wise & Kuhfeld, in press).
The third advantage of response time-based methods is that they can be
used to adjust scores for the presence of rapid guessing. The initial example of
this was the effort-moderated model (Wise & DeMars, 2006), in which rapid
guesses are excluded from scoring. Effort-moderated scoring has been shown
to yield scores with higher convergent validation evidence (Wise & DeMars,
2006), and effort-moderated scoring has been found to be effective at remov-
ing rapid guessing-based score distortion (Wise & Kingsbury, 2016).
Wise and Gao (2017) developed a new measure of test-taking engagement
suitable for use with the PBTS. Response behavior effort (RBE) extended the
RTE engagement index developed for selected-response items (Wise & Kong,
2005), and can be applied to responses from both selected response and con-
structed response items, as well as for omitted answers. Similar to RTE, each
item response is classified as either a solution behavior or a non-effortful
behavior. In RBE, however, three distinct types of test-taking behaviors are
considered non-effortful: rapid guesses, rapid omits, or rapid perfunctory
responses1 (used with constructed response items). The presence of each of
these behaviors was identified using response time thresholds based on the
10% normative threshold method (Wise & Ma, 2012). RBE equals the propor-
tion of a test taker’s responses that were classified as solution behaviors.
Hence, as with RTE, RBE equaled 1.0 if no non-effortful responses occurred,

This method for creating a response time-based measure of test-taking
engagement resembled that described by Goldhammer and colleagues (2016).
Although Goldhammer and associates used different rules for establishing
time thresholds, they classified each response as effortful or non-effortful in
a manner similar to that used by Wise and Gao (2017). There was, however,
one minor difference: Goldhammer and colleagues classified rapid omits as
“not reached/not attempted” while Wise and Gao classified such responses as
non-effortful.
Previous Validity Evidence for RBE.
Wise and Gao (2017) validated
the RBE engagement index using five validation hypotheses. First, RBE was
found to be correlated with test taker self-reported engagement. Second, none
of over 2,500 responses classified as non-effortful had been flagged for later
review by test takers. Third, non-effortful responses were correct at a rate
much lower than that from solution behaviors. Fourth, the correctness of
non-effortful was consistent with that expected from random responding.
Fifth, non-effortful responses were uninformative, in that their correctness was
unrelated to overall test performance. Collectively, these findings validated the
three response behaviors as indicators of non-effortful responding.

For each student in the current study, each item response was classified as
either solution behavior or non-effortful, and RBE was calculated for each test
event. In addition, both standard PBTS scores and effort-moderated scores
were estimated. The difference between these two scores provided an add-
itional score that represented the degree of score distortion attributable to rapid
guessing. Distortion was calculated as follows:

RESULTS
Table 1 shows descriptive statistics for the school measures of PBTS performance
and engagement. There was a substantial degree of variability in mean perform-
ance. Mean RBE in each subject exceeded 0.98, indicating that a high proportion
of the students exhibited no non-effortful responses. An alternative way to inter-
pret RBE would be in terms of the percentage of students in a school that were
meaningfully disengaged. Wise and Gao (2017), citing previous research on RTE,
suggested that test scores are likely to be meaningfully distorted once RBE falls
below 0.90. Table 1 shows that the percentages of RBE scores less than 0.90

varied substantially across schools, ranging from 0% up to nearly 28%.
This result indicates that there were material differences across schools in the
percentages of disengaged students during the PBTS administration.
Scatterplots showing the relationships between mean engagement and mean
performance for the 84 schools are shown in Figure 2. In each subject, a sub-
stantial positive correlation is seen, with the lower levels of mean engagement
being associated with lower mean performance. It is noteworthy that the mag-
nitudes of these school-level engagement-performance correlations (0.56–0.72)
are consistent with those reported at the country level in previous research
studies (Boe et al., 2002; Borghans & Schils, 2012; Debeer et al., 2014;
Goldhammer et al., 2016; Zamarro et al., 2016). The scatterplots in Figure 2
illustrate the type of relationships that generated the concerns described earlier
regarding the extent to which observed differences in mean performance are
due to differences in mean engagement. Stated another way, to what extent
are the country (or, in our case, school) rank orderings due to differential
engagement, rather than true achievement differences?
One way of addressing this question is to compare the school-level standard
and effort-moderated PBTS scores, for which rapid guesses are either included
or excluded during scoring. The scatterplots in Figure 3 show that, for each
subject, the two types of scores exhibited a nearly perfect positive (r > 0.999)
relationship. This suggests that the presence of rapid-guessing behavior, and
its associated distortion, had little net effect on the relative positions of
the school means. Thus, even though the schools exhibited substantial
variation in the proportions of students showing meaningful disengagement,
this had virtually no distortive impact on the school means.

While the high percentage of students with RBE ¼ 1.0 is a key factor, it
does not entirely account for the stability in the school means. To illustrate
this, we also examined a reduced data set in which 75% of the students with
RBE ¼ 1.0 had been randomly deleted. These deletions resulted in data with
more pronounced differential engagement: after aggregating the data by
school, the percentages of RBE scores less than 0.90 now ranged from 0% to
50%. Despite this increased differential engagement, the correlation between
standard and effort-moderated mean PBTS performance scores continued to be
nearly perfect (r > 0.999). This finding led us to consider an additional factor
that contributed to the school means stability.
The presence of a second factor can be seen through an analysis of the
relationship between standard and effort-moderated scores at the student level,
which is shown in the scatterplots in Figure 4. These scatterplots reveal two
important characteristics about the nature of the score distortion—which is
indicated by the deviations of the data points from the main trend line of
the plots (i.e., those students with RBE ¼ 1.0). First, score distortion most
frequently occurred in the lower half of the score distribution. Second, and
perhaps more importantly, deviations were commonly seen both above and
below the main trend line. This indicated that for some students, distortion
was negative (i.e., the effort-moderated score was higher than the standard
score), while for others it was positive. Negative distortion is what we gener-
ally expect to see. It is consistent with the idea that non-effortful responses
tend to negatively bias a test score, because we expect their accuracy rates to
be lower than those from solution behaviors.
Positive score distortion, in contrast, appears counterintuitive. It implies
that non-effortful responses had the effect of improving a student’s test per-
formance. To understand how this can occur, it is useful to consider what
causes distortion when non-effortful responses are present. Distortion results
when there are differences in the relative accuracy rates of non-effortful
responses and solution behaviors during a test event. If the accuracy rate for
non-effortful responses is lower, negative distortion will result. If the accuracy
rates are equal, no distortion will occur. And if the non-effortful responses
have a higher accuracy rate, the result will be positive distortion.
Instances of positive distortion have two primary influences: the achieve-
ment level of the student and how often non-effortful responses are correct.

At the school level, in schools for which non-effortful behaviors were
present, it was found that both negative and positive distortion typically
occurred. This suggests that when the data in a school were aggregated,
positive distortion canceled out much of the negative distortion. The net effect
of this, when combined with the sizable proportion of students with zero
distortion (i.e., RBE ¼ 1.0), was that the school means showed very little
change when adjusted by effort-moderated scoring. In addition, whether the
school means increased or decreased after adjustment was driven largely by
the relative numbers of students with negative or positive distortion.

Multilevel Model Results.
Table 4 presents several results from our
random intercept model that are relevant to our research question. First, as
demonstrated by the ICCs, very little of the variation in distortion is between
schools. The highest ICC is in reading at 0.007, which means that roughly
0.7% of the variance in distortion is at the school level. In practical terms,
most of the effect of rapid guessing on observed test scores is at the student
level; not much of the variation occurs between schools. Such results are com-
mensurate with a hypothesis that estimates of mean school-level achievement
are not especially sensitive to differential test engagement in part because
students do not appear to be clustered in schools based on their rapid
guessing behaviors.
Second, we found additional evidence that school-level achievement
estimates may change when based on effort-moderated scores, but those shifts
are modest. For example, using our model-based empirical Bayes estimates
of school-level mean distortion, the most severe change occurred in reading
for a school that had mean estimated achievement that was 1.2 scale score
points lower than using the original score. In most cases, changes in estimated
school achievement were well below one unit on the score scale. Thus, school
achievement estimates are likely less sensitive to rapid guessing partially
because rapid guessing rates are low and, even in the presence of rapid guess-

DISCUSSION
Despite the presence of strong correlations between mean engagement and
mean performance, as well as evidence of considerable differential mean dis-
engagement, the PBTS school means exhibited high stability after the effects
of non-effortful responses were removed via effort-moderated scoring (Wise
& DeMars, 2006). This stability was attributable to the combined effects of
dilution and—to a lesser extent—cancelation on the net distortion of aggre-
gated scores. The results of the current study suggest that the scores from
aggregated data will tend to be robust with respect to the distortive effects of
differential disengaged test taking. It should be recalled, however, that our
analyses focused on school-level aggregations of data collected in the United
States. This raises questions about the extent to which this study’s results
generalize to other, larger score aggregations, such as the international
country-level aggregations used in PISA.
The validity of a generalized conclusion that aggregated mean performance
is robust with respect to differential mean disengagement depends on three
issues. First, to what extent do the types of non-effortful responding identified
in the current study represent the collective effects of test-taking disengage-
ment? Second, to what extent does the magnitude of differential disengage-
ment found in the current study resemble what is present in data from other
assessment programs that use aggregated performance? Third, to what extent
does disengagement tend to be largely associated with lower ability students,
as was observed in the current study?
The first issue concerns the extent to which our definition of non-effortful
responding serves as a suitable proxy for test-taking disengagement. The claim

73

that rapid-guessing behavior indicates disengagement has been well validated
(Goldhammer et al., 2016; Wise, 2015, 2017; Wise & Kuhfeld, in press), and
similar validation evidence has been found for rapid omits and rapid perfunc-
tory responses (Wise & Gao, 2017). But are there other types of disengage-
ment that do not occur with rapidity? One possibility is an item response that
occurs slightly after the item’s rapid response time threshold. The time thresh-
olds are often set in a conservative manner, to avoid classifying effortful
responses as non-effortful (Wise, 2017), which suggests that some near-rapid,
non-effortful responses may not be detected. Additionally, one might imagine
a student who responds to items in a more leisurely disengaged fashion,
though it is unclear how often (if at all) this actually occurs.
The only study of which we are aware that informs this issue was an inves-
tigation of the effort-moderated model with a computerized adaptive test
(CAT). Wise and Kingsbury (2016) studied score distortion—defined, as in
the current study, as the difference between standard IRT and effort-moderated
scores—across RTE values for a sample of CAT test takers. They found that
the non-linear regression equation between RTE and distortion closely
matched that calculated from a simulated data set in which rapid guessing was
constrained to be the sole source of score distortion. Wise and Kingsbury’s
findings indicated that responses classified as solution behaviors in the
empirical data were relatively unaffected by disengaged test taking beyond
the impact of rapid guessing. Thus, there is evidence to suggest that
rapid responses account for the majority of the distortive effects due to
disengagement.
The second issue pertains to the prevalence of disengaged test taking, which
relates to the degree to which any distortive effects due to disengagement
are diluted by a high proportion of students in the aggregation group who
uniformly exhibit engaged responses. A consistent finding from studies of
rapid-guessing behavior on low stakes tests is that a sizable majority of test
takers exhibit no rapid guesses. Even in the absence of personal consequences
associated with test performance, most students appear to give good effort
when administered a test. Such motivation to remain engaged may be due to a
variety of internal factors such as conscientiousness, competitiveness, a sense
of academic citizenship, or a desire to please one’s teachers (Wise, 2017).
Hence, it may be a typical outcome that most students do not exhibit rapid
guessing, even in instances in which it might generally be viewed that student
test taking motivation was not very high. This implies that there should
typically be a strong dilution factor operating when aggregating test scores.
The third issue concerns the extent to which disengaged test taking
predominantly occurs with lower achieving students. From a metacognitive
perspective, the expectancy-value theory of achievement motivation (Penk &

Schipolowski, 2015; Wigfield & Eccles, 2000) states that the expectancy a stu-
dent has for success on a test will influence test-taking effort. Specifically,
whenever students expect to do poorly on a test, they may give poor effort—
perhaps to protect their self-esteem (Jagacinski & Nicholls, 1990; Thompson,
Davidson, & Barber, 1995). Note, too, that expectation for success may
change during a test; if students perceive that they had performed poorly on
the initial portion of a test, their expectancy for success consequently may
diminish and increase the likelihood that non-effortful responding occurs dur-
ing the remaining items. Hence, because low expectation for success is likely
correlated with true achievement, it should not be surprising that—as was
found in the current study—disengaged test taking occurred more frequently
with lower achieving students.
Returning to the central research question of the degree to which differen-
tial disengagement distorts aggregated scores, the answer depends on the
prevalence of dilution and cancelation effects. Specifically, whenever (1) only
a modest proportion of the students exhibit disengagement, (2) lower achievers
are most likely to be disengaged, and (3) the degree of differential aggregated
engagement is similar to that found in our study, aggregated scores should
show high stability. Whether or not these characteristics are present with a
particular assessment program is, of course, an empirical question that could
be evaluated by the methods used in this study if the assessment is adminis-
tered as a CBT.
To the extent that the findings of this study are found to generalize to oper-
ational large-scale international assessments, it would be reassuring to those
interpreting differences among aggregated achievement scores. The validity of
inferences about aggregated scores would thereby be supported by evidence
that the scores were not meaningfully influenced by the construct-irrelevant
effects of disengagement, even though considerable disengaged test taking
had occurred.
