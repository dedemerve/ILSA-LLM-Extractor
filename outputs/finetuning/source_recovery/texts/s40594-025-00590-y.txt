Predicting STEM competencies with machine
learning: identifying key determinants among
522,802 adolescents
Ji Liu1*, Dahman Tahri1 and Millicent Aziku1
Abstract
Background Prominence of Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) competencies has
transcended mere academic achievements, contributing cross-cuttingly to individual, societal, and global
development. Despite consensus that STEM-related outcomes are largely shaped by complex and interconnected
factors, key determinants of STEM competencies remain insufficiently explored. Guided by Dynamic Model of
Education Effectiveness (DMEE), the current study draws on Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
2022 dataset to identify key determinants of STEM competencies.
Methods This study leverages data from 522,802 fifteen-year-old adolescents across 66 economies using six machine
learning models, including Naïve Bayes, Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, AdaBoost, and XGBoost, to
identify factors contributing to STEM competencies. In addition, this study examines model effectiveness by assessing
predictive accuracy of each classifier.
Results This study identified a key set of variables that predict STEM competencies, including home possessions,
student part-time employment, gender, cognitive activation for reasoning, and mathematics anxiety. Model
evaluation indicated that XGBoost classification outperformed other classifiers, with an average test accuracy of
78.3%.
Conclusions Findings illuminate the complex interplay of student-, family-, and school-level factors in determining
STEM competencies, suggesting the need to consider multifaceted, non-linear interactions across intra- and interpersonal levels, and design targeted interventions.
Keywords Dynamic model of education effectiveness, Machine learning classification, PISA 2022 , STEM
competencies

Introduction
STEM competencies have become a central global concern, drawing heightened attention from teachers, school
leaders, policymakers, and researchers across diverse
socio-cultural and institutional contexts (Anderson & Li,
2020; Galvez et al., 2024). This growing interest reflects
an international consensus regarding the role of STEM
education in developing students’ academic and non-academic skills. A substantial body of literature suggests that
acquisition of STEM-related capabilities improves students’ disciplinary knowledge, analytical reasoning, and
problem-solving capabilities and prepares adolescents
for a high-demand STEM future (Chung et al., 2017; Kelley et al., 2020). The intensifying demand for improving
these skills has motivated research to investigate key factors influencing STEM competencies. For instance, prior
research has consistently linked students’ performance
across STEM disciplines to a broad range of individual
factors, such as background characteristics, psychological factors, and academic aptitude (Ansong et al., 2020;
Bong & Chen, 2024). Concurrently, literature on STEM
education has also examined the contribution of broader
contextual and institutional determinants to STEM outcomes, such as socio-economic status, parental support,
instructional strategies, and school learning environments (Gil‐Madrona et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024a, 2024b,
2024c). Consistent with these perspectives, improving
accurate identification of predictors influencing STEM
competencies may provide valuable insights into practices and research aimed at enhancing learning outcomes
and promoting equity in STEM-related domains.
The primary objectives and conceptual underpinnings
of STEM education have frequently been contested
among researchers, reflecting divergent theoretical perspectives drawing on an array of concepts, facets, and
outcomes that could be developed through the interdisciplinary nature of STEM education (Garibay, 2015).
While a broad set of definitions and frameworks has been
applied to address the multifaceted nature of STEM education, STEM is often conceptualized as an interdisciplinary paradigm that integrates concepts and practices
related to science, technology, engineering, and mathematics to solve real-world problems (Liu & Aziku, 2025).
In STEM research, proficiency in STEM-related subjects
is often regarded as a strong predictor of academic success and salient influencer of educational and career
aspirations (Aluko et al., 2016). Consequently, scholarly
efforts have been progressively dedicated to investigating
and identifying key determinants and mechanisms influencing learners’ STEM-related skills, academic retention,
degree completion and intentions of pursuing higher
degrees in STEM domains or employment in related sectors (Forde et al., 2023).

In light of the increasing attention STEM education,
there is a growing interest in using large-scale assessments to estimate students’ performance. According to
Elliott et al. (2019), large-scale assessments offer a valuable basis for guiding decision-making in educational
policy. To this end, data mining and machine learning
techniques are becoming instrumental in predicting
antecedents of academic achievement and determining
factors associated with STEM success (Jin, 2023). While
data mining and machine learning methods vary considerably in their unique algorithms and applications, it
is worth noting that they primarily prioritize predictive
accuracy over explanatory insights (Ley et al., 2022).
This focus on enhancing accuracy distinguishes machine
learning techniques from conventional statistical methods. On the one hand, machine learning methods incorporate iterative learning processes by selecting the most
informative features and mitigating potential issues
related to multicollinearity. On the other hand, traditional techniques often fall short due to data overfitting,
linearity limitations, and restricted scalability when handling high-dimensional datasets with complex variable
interactions (Vijayakumar & Cheung, 2018).
In this regard, to the best of our knowledge, few studies
have examined the potential influence of these factors in
relation to their interactions on students’ STEM competencies. Moreover, prior studies often apply conventional
techniques that may be limited in capturing complex
relationships among vectors of predictors. Hence, this
study sought to identify predictors at the student-, family-, and school-levels through applying machine learning
classification. This study attempts to answer the following
research questions:

Theoretical framework and related literature

Given that student performance in STEM-related subjects is influenced by a complex array of factors, including
individual characteristics, students’ motivational behaviors, school-settings indicators, and broader regional
settings, this study employs the Dynamic Model of Educational Effectiveness (DMEE) as its guiding theoretical
model to elucidate patterns of factors predicting STEM
competencies. This framework has been widely adopted
as a model of educational effectiveness (Vanlaar et al.,
2016). Conceptually, DMEE characterizes educational
effectiveness as dynamic process influenced by factors
operating at the student-, classroom-, and school-levels
(Dierendonck, 2023).

The primary advantage of using this framework lies
in its multi-level structure. Principally, this study incorporates a wide range of variables at the student level,
including background characteristics (e.g., individual
demographics), and self-perceived cognitive features
(e.g., motivation, self-efficacy, and attitudes toward
STEM subjects). Variables at the family level (such as
parental education, occupational status, and home and
financial resources) are included. At the school level, several variables on instructional environments were incorporated (e.g., learning climate, cognitive activation, and
exposure to cognitive content). Anchored on this conceptual framework, vectors of multi-dimensional variables
associated with STEM competencies is identified (see

Student-level factors

Student-level factors encompass a range of background
characteristics and self-perceived cognitive traits. First,
individuals’ demographics have attracted considerable
research attention. For instance, gender-based studies
indicated that, in most cases, male students often have
outperformed their female counterparts in STEM-related
fields (Chatri et al., 2021). Conversely, other investigations have suggested that females typically perform better than males in STEM subjects, particularly at the
primary and secondary school levels (Mullis et al., 2016).
Additionally, students’ age and grade level are salient

factors influencing STEM competencies. Existing studies
have confirmed that students STEM-related proficiencies showed cumulative progression across grade levels, with accelerated growth between the ages of 12 and
14 years old (Van Vo & Csapó, 2023). Student’s immigration background has become an increasingly important
factor in educational research, particularly among STEM
researchers. Alieva et al. (2024) investigated achievement
gaps in STEM subjects between native and immigrant
background students using combinations of international assessment studies, and showed that students from
immigrant background exhibited large achievement gaps.
Second, students’ educational pathways, including
early childhood education, grade retention, and expected
education attainment constitute critical determinants of
STEM competencies. For example, studies have found
that grade repetition is negatively associated with students’ academic performance in STEM subjects, particularly in mathematics and science (Garacia-Perez et al.,
2011; Hu & Hannum, 2020). The duration of early childhood is an important predictor of STEM proficiency
among students in adulthood. Consequently, children
who spent more years in early childhood education tend
to demonstrate higher STEM competencies(Aldemir &
Kermani, 2017). Educational aspirations also play a critical role in predicting STEM competencies . Yeung and
Igarashi (2025) investigated the predictive role of educational expectations in shaping adolescents’ academic and

Third, students’ out-of-school experiences demonstrate

Fourth, self-perceived cognitive attributes, particularly

self-concepts, including self-efficacy, intrinsic motivation,
and subject-specific interest, demonstrate a constructive
influence on STEM achievement. Schwerter et al. (2025)
emphasized that students’ mathematics self-concepts—
such as interest, motivation, and perceived efficacy—are

and female learners. Furthermore, recent empirical studies have underscored the role of motivational orientation
and self-efficacy as as key contributors toengagement and

Similarly, parental involvement in learning experiences
plays a pivotal role in shaping students’ academic interests and performance in STEM. Research underscores
that when families emphasize the value of STEM subjects
and actively support related opportunities, students tend
to develop stronger self-efficacy and favorable outcome
expectations in these fields (Nugent et al., 2015).
Extensive empirical research has established a consistent positive association between socio-economic status
(SES) and STEM subject competencies (Conger & Donnellan, 2007). For instance, studies have demonstrated
significant correlations between socioeconomic background and academic performance in science (Chen
et al., 2021) and mathematics (Wiberg, 2019). Generally, students from low-SES backgrounds tend to exhibit
reduced likelihood of achieving high proficiency in STEM
disciplines compared to their peers from higher-SES
households (Diamond et al., 2007). However, the relationship between family-level socioeconomic indices and
STEM outcomes remains contested, as methodological
discrepancies in constructing these indices yield divergent findings (Blums et al., 2017). Notably, scholars have
advocated for analyzing socioeconomic factors independently rather than aggregating them into composite
measures, as this approach may obscure nuanced relationships (Efstathiadi & Mattheoudakis, 2022). Relatedly,
parental education emerges as a key factor influencing
STEM achievement. Higher parental education levels are
often associated with increased time spent supervising
children’s academic activities and fostering a home environment conducive to learning (Paseka & Schwab, 2020).
Similarly, parental occupational characteristics—specifically, occupational prestige and sector—play a pivotal
role in shaping children’s STEM performance and career
aspirations. Research on intergenerational transmission
of educational outcomes reveals that parental attitudes,
aspirations, and occupational choices significantly predict children’s engagement and success in STEM-related
fields (Anaya et al., 2022; Mues et al., 2021).

Machine learning modelling

The term machine learning was first coined by Arthur
Samuel in 1959 to describe computational systems capable of improving performance through iterative experience in tasks such as game playing (Samuel, 1959). Over
time, its conceptual framework has evolved beyond computer science to integrate deeply with statistical modeling and data-driven methodologies. Contemporary
definitions emphasize machine learning as a multidisciplinary field leveraging computational algorithms to
enable systems to autonomously identify patterns, adapt
to new inputs, and optimize predictive accuracy (Feng et
al., 2022). These advanced analytical tools have permeated diverse domains, including educational research,
where they are increasingly employed to address complex, multivariate problems. In international large-scale

Page 5 of 18

assessments (ILSAs), machine learning models have
been systematically applied to datasets from programs
such as PISA (Gabriel et al., 2018), TIMSS (Von Davier
et al., 2023), and PIAAC ( Yun et al., 2025). Methodologically, several techniques such as support vector machines
(SVM), random forests (RF), k-nearest neighbors (KNN),
decision trees (DT), and gradient-boosted algorithms
(e.g., XGBoost) have been frequently utilized for classification, regression, and clustering tasks to analyze student performance or identify determinants of academic
success or risk factors for underperformance (Huang et
al., 2025).
Machine learning’s capacity to process high-dimensional data has enabled preciseanalyses of educational
and psychological outcomes, particularly in STEM
research. For instance, Bernardo et al. (2021) employed
binary classification algorithms—specifically SVM, multilayer perceptrons, logistic regression, gradient boosting,
random forests, AdaBoost, and k-nearest neighbors—to
identify the most influential predictors of reading proficiency.Similarly, in STEM, machine learning analyses have been instrumental in examining multilevel
influences on student achievement. For example, GilMadrona et al. (2025) applied random forest, support
vector machines, and ridge regression to analyze the
academic performance of 35,943 Spanish students, identifying salient predictors of STEM competencies. Meanwhile, Chang et al. (2023) leveraged U.S. national survey
data to investigate STEM participation, utilizing classification and regression tree (CART) models to evaluate
individual, familial, and institutional factors shaping educational trajectories.

Materials and methods
Data and participants

The current study draws on the publicly-available PISA
2022 dataset from the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD). The Programme for
International Student Assessment (PISA) is a recurring
large-scale international survey designed to evaluate
15-year-old students’ educational performance in core
subjects of mathematics, science, and reading (Liu et al.,
2025a, 2025b). PISA 2022 data collection adhered strictly
to the ethical standards established by the OECD, with
formal ethical approval obtained from the relevant institutional boards. Additionally, throughout the data collection process, informed consent were obtained from all
participants and their parents or legal guardians in accordance with the OECD Personal Data Protection Rules
and the PISA 2022 Technical Standards (OECD, 2024).
In the current study, the analysis leverages a sample comprising 522,802 students from 66 participating economies. ).

The outcome variable in this study, STEM competencies,
was derived from students’ proficiency in two key STEM
subjects (mathematics and science). Specifically, STEM
competencies variable was constructed by averaging students’ mathematics achievement (AVGMATH), and science achievement (AVGSCIE). This composite provides a
broader indicator of students’ overall STEM proficiency

Data analysis procedure

In the analytical stage, the dependent variable, STEM
competencies, was operationalized using scores at or
above the global mean, as having high-level STEM

Page 7 of 18

competencies, while students with scores below the
global mean were identified as having low-level STEM
competencies (coded as ‘1’ ‘0’ respectively). This
approach of classification has been widely adopted and
documented in previous machine learning studies due to
its effectiveness in handling high-dimensional data structure with complex variable interactions and reducing the
risk of overfitting (Maxwell et al., 2018).
The analytical framework is illustrated in Fig. 3. Specifically, data pre-processing and preparation were
conducted including cleaning, imputation, and normalization. Variables with invalid, skipped, not applicable,
or not reached responses were coded as system missing and countries with entire missing responses (0) to
any variables at the student-, family-, and school-levels
were dropped. The dataset was loaded into Python 3.12
environment to complete the data pre-processing and
imputation procedures. Following this, the dataset was
partitioned into training and testing subsets (80/20) using
‘scikit-learn’ library (e.g., Serrano-Guerrero et al., 2024).
In order to identify the optimal predictors of STEM competencies, feature elimination using XGBoost and recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV)
was performed to retain the most relevant predictors
prior to the classification (Nyamawe et al., 2025). Subsequently, to better tune the hyperparameter and reduce
the possibility of overfitting, a stratified subsample of the
training data was used to select optimal parameters using
the technique of k-fold cross-validation to ensure the reliable evaluation of model performance and locate appropriate hyperparameters (Monlezun et al., 2021). Finally,
supervised machine learning classifiers were applied,
including Logistic Regression, Naïve Bayes, Decision
Trees, Random Forest, AdaBoost, and XGBoost. The

Analytical models

The current study uses six linear and non-linear machine
learning classifiers, namely, Naïve Bayes, Decision Tree,
Logistic Regression, XGBoost, AdaBoost, and Random
Forest.
Gaussian Naïve Bayes is a probabilistic algorithm
rooted in the Bayes’ Theorem, which assumes a conditional independence among features. Naïve Bayes is computationally effective and often performs well with high
dimensionality. In addition, it provides better classification accuracy on real-time data (Mosquera et al., 2018).
Logistic regression is widely adopted for binary classification studies, that can also be extended to multi-class
type of classification. In the domain of machine learning,
logistic regression functions as supervised algorithm that
classifies the outcome variable through producing discrete class predictions (James et al., 2013).
Decision tree is a flexible machine learning algorithm
designed to perform classification, regression, and
multi-output analyses. Decision tree can perform on a
non-parametric basis, andis predominantly designed to
predict outcomes based on the decision rules taken from
the input data (Matzavela & Alepis, 2021).
Random forest is an ensemble learning method that
builds an assembly of decision trees, random forest introduces additional layer of randomness during the tree
building. Particularly, this model selects a random subset
of features and identifies the ideal split, rather than testing all available features (Luo et al., 2021).
Adaptive boosting (AdaBoost) is a classification ensemble learning approach that progressively emphasizes the
misclassified observations to produce a highly predictive
model. Adaboost is an effective model of classification
due to its ability to minimize the classification error while
maintaining the computational efficiency (Maxwell et al.,
2018).
XGBoost (extreme gradient boosting) is a scalable treeboosting technique developed to improve the efficiency,
flexibility, and overall accuracy of the model. XGBoost
has been widely adopted in large-scale data analysis for
its exceptional ability in managing data complexity (Chen
& Guestrin, 2016).

negatively to the prediction. Ranking characteristics by
their SHAP values enhances both the transparency and
effectiveness of the machine learning model employed in
this study.

Discussion
The current study aims to identify a vector of influential
predictors of students’ STEM competencies using six
machine learning classifiers to a large-scale educational

dataset of PISA 2022. The integration of machine learning models has shown effectiveness in managing data
complexity, enhancing predictive accuracy, prioritizing most influential features, and offering valuable

Liu et al. International Journal of STEM Education

(2026) 13:1

theoretical, methodological, and practical insights. A
central finding of this study is the significant influence
of student-level, family-level, and school-level factors
in predicting STEM competencies among students. The
distributed influence across these levels highlights the
multi-layered nature of factors shaping students STEM
competencies. In addition, the accurate identification of
these influential features that synergistically differentiate
varying levels of STEM competencies provided empirical
support for validating the dynamic model of educational
effectiveness and applying machine learning analysis.
Most influential factors
Student-level predictors

Findings of this study indicated that student demographics, educational pathways, and out-of-school experience
are highly relevant for students’ STEM competencies.
Additionally, self-perceived cognitive features, including
subject-specific beliefs, attitudes, feelings and behaviors
have meaningfully contributed to the predictive model.
For one, student employment, measured through paid
work hours, emerged as a significant predictor of students’ STEM competencies. This finding suggested that
students who reported more working hours tend to
exhibit lower STEM-related proficiency. This observation aligns with Post and Pong, (2009) who used TIMSS
dataset to examine the influence of paid and unpaid work
to academic achievement among secondary school students. Based on the “general framework” proposed in
their study, students’ engagement in paid and unpaid
employment consumes their time and energy that could
be devoted for learning and cognitive skills development. They added that both paid and unpaid types of
student employment have similar influence of students’
academic achievement in mathematics and science. In
addition, the analysis indicates that students’ gender was
a significant predictor in students’ STEM competencies,
with being male showing higher predicted STEM proficiency. Consistent with prior research on gender differences, STEM-related domains are commonly perceived
as male-dominated, with societal gender stereotypes and
contextual factors such as parental expectations exerting
a negative influence on the performance and future interest among female students in STEM (Chatri et al., 2021;
Wang et al., 2023). Moreover, the model indicated that
grade repetition may lead to lower levels STEM competencies due to its adverse influence on socioemotional
development and academic performance. For instance,
large-scale investigations on grade repetition revealed
similar findings regarding the negative influence of retention on students’ achievement including mathematics
and science performance (Garacia-Perez et al., 2011).
For another, findings of the study revealed that mathematics anxiety contributes negatively to students’ STEM

Page 12 of 18

performance. This negative effect of mathematics anxiety
often stems from situational, dispositional, and environmental factors such as negative feelings, traits, and attitudes towards mathematics that may affect individuals
(Rozgonjuk et al., 2020). The results are consistent with
Kargar et al. (2010), who found that students with higher
mathematics anxiety tend to possess lower STEM-related
skills. Although the interaction analysis revealed that
mathematic anxiety was negatively correlated with STEM
competencies for both genders, female students were
more sensitive and negatively influenced by mathematics anxiety, demonstrating a slightly higher adverse effect
on the model. Previous evidence revealed that the association between mathematics anxiety and achievement in
STEM subjects is more noticeable for females (Yu et al.,
2024). On the contrary, mathematics self-efficacy is identified as a salient predictor of students’ STEM competencies. This is in line with Han et al. (2021), who argued
that students’ academic self-efficacy is a strong predictor of their STEM performance. The attitudes toward
mathematics achievement have been shown to influence academic outcomes ), . Students with goal-oriented
often show higher self-efficacy, while those driven by
avoidance-oriented behaviors tend to exhibit higher level
of anxiety coupled with low achievement (Skemp, 1979).
Equally consequential, the findings showed that cognitive efforts in mathematics and tests were crucial for
students’ STEM competencies. Extensive research demonstrated that academic efforts are positively associated
with STEM achievement (Hwang et al., 2023). Additionally, Leikin et al., (2009) argued that when mathematical
learning opportunities align with learners’ personality
traits, affective characteristics, and sustained effort, their
mathematics potential can develop into improved
achievement.
Family-level predictors

Results of this study confirmed prior findings on the
effectiveness of family-related factors in predicting
STEM competencies, aligning with cultural capital theory, which posits that parental support, level of parents'
education and home learning resources are salient predictors of children’s level of STEM efficacy, performance,
and aspiration (Dotterer, 2022). Particularly, home possessions emerged as the most influential predictor of students’ STEM competencies, suggesting that household
possessions and home environment learning resources
and students’ academic achievement are positively correlated. Interestingly, although home possessions is an
essential component of students’ socioeconomic index,
ESCS was not identified as a predictor of students’
STEM competencies. These results are consistent with
prior study conducted on Mexican students using PISA
2015 to evaluate their science achievement, the study

Liu et al. International Journal of STEM Education

(2026) 13:1

reported that students with more household resources
tend to outperform students with fewer home possessions (Bazán-Ramírez et al., 2021). Similarly, research
applying educational effectiveness model on students
from 53 countries, has found that home possessions is
a salient predictor of science achievement (Hu et al.,
2022). Regarding other components of the ESCS index,
mother’s occupational status has exhibited a slight positive influence on STEM competencies. Substantial body
of research has consistently highlighted the positive associations between parents’ occupations and their children’s STEM achievement. Likewise, Erdogdu, (2022)
suggested that students’ mathematics achievement has a
positive and significant relationship with mother’s highest occupational status. Moreover, the SHAP analysis
shows that family support has positively contributed to
students’ STEM competencies. This is in line with findings from prior research highlighting the positive association between parental supportive behaviors and student
STEM achievement (Liou et al., 2019; Tang & Tran,
2023). In addition, recent studies have emphasized the
favorable influences of parental involvement on STEM
interest and STEM career choices, highlighting the role
of intergenerational transmission of STEM skills and
interest (Howard et al., 2023).
School-level predictors

The intricate design of the DMEE indicate that educational outcomes are products of interacting factors operating at the school level. Therefore, this study draws on a
broad array of school-related factors capturing students’
responses on exposure to cognitive content, teacher
cognitive support, and students’ disruptive behavior at
school. The findings showed that supportive teaching
strategies based on cognitive activation contributed to
students’ STEM performance. These instructional practices stimulate the content-related abilities and encourage
students to think critically, formulate hypotheses, conduct experiments, and draw conclusions (Liu et al., 2024a,
2024b, 2024c). Empirical findings demonstrated that
teacher support through cognitive activation strategies
was correlated with students’ performance in STEM subject (Huang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024a, 2024b, 2024c).
Similarly, empirical research suggests that cognitive activation strategies enhance problem solving skills, and it is
associated with higher achievement in mathematics and
cognitive competencies (Cantley et al., 2017). Additional
school-level factors including student disruptive behavior
such as skipping school, missing classes, and being late
to school demonstrated a negative influence on STEM
competencies. It is well established that STEM skills are
developed and improved through engagement practices
in the classroom. Accordingly, truant students experience higher academic disengagement, resulting in low

Page 13 of 18

achievement. Research on student truancy and absenteeism highlights the detrimental influence of disruptive
school climate in hindering the learning process, revealing a strong association with low academic performance
in STEM related subjects (Sälzer & Heine, 2016).
Model evaluation

Variations in machine learning models are determined
through predictive accuracy between the predicted and
observed values, while interpretability of the model
reflects the relative importance of predictors to the outcome variable (Friedman, 2001). Regarding the evaluation
and the comparison of the implemented machine learning classifiers, the findings revealed that XGBoost was
the most powerful model in predicting STEM competencies, demonstrating higher accuracy and performance
compared to the rest of models used in this research.
This aligns with the findings of studies that employed
XGBoost and other machine learning models to identify
student academic performance leveraging a macro-educational dataset, where XGBoost demonstrated higher
predictive performance compared with those of Random
Forest, Lasso, Elastic Net, Support Vector Machines, and
Decision Tree (K. Yan, 2021). Likewise, Asselman et al.
(2023) proposed a new model of prediction based on a
combination of multiple models that was evaluated on
three datasets. Their experimental findings of the analysis showed that XGBoost outperformed the model and
improved the accuracy of prediction. In another comparative machine learning analysis, using over 150 predictors from six East Asian countries to identify factors
that affect middle-school students’ STEM competencies, Huang et al. (2025) found that XGBoost exhibited
the greatest prediction accuracy compared to Multiple
Linear Regression, Support Vector Regression, Decision
Tree, and Random Forest.

Conclusion and implications
The current study attempts to identify key predictors
of STEM competencies using machine learning techniques on the PISA 2022 dataset. Findings of this study
provide important implications in the context of STEM
education. Theoretically, the study confirms the intricate
design of the Dynamic Model of Educational Effectiveness, which posits that students’ STEM competenciesare
shaped by a combination of individual, contextual, and
institutional factors. Particularly, the analysis identified
the most influential predictors across multiple levels,
reflecting the multifactorial nature of STEM education
including student employment, gender, grade repetition, mathematics anxiety, mathematics self-efficacy, and
academic effort in mathematics test at the student-level,
home possessions, mother’s occupational status, family
support at the family-level, and teacher support through

Liu et al. International Journal of STEM Education

(2026) 13:1

cognitive activation, class skipping, and school lateness
at the school-level. This analysis has offered significant
contributions to STEM literature by providing a novel
perspective in contrast to earlier studies that primarily
emphasized cognitive abilities in predicting STEM competencies (Rozgonjuk et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2024).
Methodologically, this analysis revealed that the treebased ensemble model, XGBoost, outperformed other
methods across all performance metrics. This illustrates
the strength of adopting machine learning approaches
that transcend the limitations of conventional linear methods that are widely employed in educational
research to predict STEM competencies (Ansong et al.,
2020). Applying machine learning techniques to extensive and high-dimensional dataset allowed for a resolution of issues regarding multicollinearity and enabled the
modeling intricate and non-linear interactions among
predictors. Likewise, employing the SHAP technique
has offered more detailed interpretation of the model
selected and in-depth grasp of preditor characteristics.
In more practical terms, findings have provided comprehensive insights for policymakers, educators, and
institutional leaders seeking to address disparities in
STEM competencies. First, policy initiatives should prioritize aligning labor market interventions with educational goals to mitigate barriers to student success. For
instance, implementing labor protection policies such as
the International Labour Organization (ILO) Convention
No. 138 can safeguard students from exploitative employment conditions that compromise academic engagement
(Thévenon & Edmonds, 2019). Concurrently, systemic
efforts to address gender disparities in STEM participation require targeted curricular and instructional
reforms. Prior research underscores the importance of
early childhood interventions that counteract gender
stereotypes, reduce anxiety-related barriers, and foster positive STEM self-concepts among female students
through inclusive pedagogical practices (McGuire et al.,
2020). Additionally, allocating resources toward early
academic and psychological support programs can mitigate long-term achievement gaps by addressing retention
challenges and fostering resilience in underrepresented
groups (Gómez Soler et al., 2021). For self-perceived
cognitive abilities, evidence-based strategies should prioritize interventions that cultivate academic self-efficacy
and intrinsic motivation. This includes creating inclusive,
engaging learning environments that emphasize collaborative problem-solving and curiosity-driven exploration,
both of which are essential for developing positive attitudes toward STEM (You et al., 2025).
Strengthening home learning environments through
parental encouragement, access to STEM-related materials, and active participation in educational activities can
significantly enhance children’s self-efficacy and interest

Page 14 of 18

in STEM careers (Bierman et al., 2024). Schools and
families must collaborate to implement initiatives that
integrate STEM learning into everyday contexts, such
as hands-on projects involving observation, reasoning,
and inquiry-based play. For example, parent-school partnerships that provide STEM kits or activity guides can
reinforce foundational skills while fostering a culture of
curiosity and exploration (Bierman et al., 2024).
Finally, educational institutions play a central role in
fostering equitable STEM ecosystems. Schools should
adopt dual-pronged approaches that combine supportive
student environments with professional development for
educators. Establishing mentorship programs, peer collaboration spaces, and culturally responsive pedagogies
can create inclusive climates conducive to STEM engagement (Liu et al., 2024a, 2024b, 2024c). Simultaneously,
teacher training initiatives such as the TRAILS (Teachers
and Researchers Advancing Integrated Lessons in STEM)
model demonstrate the efficacy of interdisciplinary professional development in equipping educators to address
diverse student needs and enhance classroom outcomes
(Kelley et al., 2020). By integrating these evidence-based
practices and fostering teacher-student reciprocity,
schools can cultivate environments that nurture both
academic achievement and long-term interest in STEM
fields.
Limitations and future research directions

Despite strengths of this study, several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the cross-sectional
design of the dataset limits the capacity to establish
causal relationships or analyze longitudinal developmental patterns. By capturing associations between STEM
competencies and predictive factors at a single temporal point, findings cannot fully account for the dynamic
interplay of variables over time. Future research could
utilize longitudinal datasets to trace how these factors evolve and interact across developmental stages,
thereby elucidating the temporal mechanisms underpinning STEM competencies. Second, although this analysis
draws on a dataset from OECD member countries and
partner economies, the global-aggregate approach may
obscure critical cross-national variations. Socioeconomic
conditions, cultural norms, and institutional frameworks
governing education systems are likely to differentially
moderate the influence of these factors on student outcomes. Follow-up studies could adopt comparative crossnational analyses to disentangle these contextual effects,
ensuring that conclusions remain sensitive to cultural
and institutional specificity. Third, a remaining concern
pertain to the interpretability of machine learning models, which are often characterized by algorithmic opacity.
While these models excel in identifying complex patterns,
their “black box” nature complicates the interpretation

Liu et al. International Journal of STEM Education

(2026) 13:1

of causal pathways and the communication of findings
to stakeholders. To address this, future research should
integrate interpretability techniques, such as hybrid analytical approaches that combine machine learning with
traditional statistical benchmarks (e.g., regression models). Additionally, methodologies like SHAP (SHapley
Additive exPlanations) values or feature importance analyses can enhance transparency by quantifying the relative
contributions of variables to model outputs. Such strategies not only improve the explainability of machine learning findings but also align with the methodological rigor
required for evidence-based decision-making in educational research.
