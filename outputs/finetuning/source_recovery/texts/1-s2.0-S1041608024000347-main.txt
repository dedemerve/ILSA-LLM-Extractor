Fear of failure has been an important predictor of learning outcomes. Socio-economic status and gender are
usually treated as covariates, and their interplay with fear of failure in predicting achievements is under-studied.
This study examined whether socio-economic status and gender are associated with the quantity of fear of failure
and whether they moderate the relationship between fear of failure and achievements. We applied a correlational
study design based on the data of the Program for International Student Assessment 2018 data from 536,741
adolescents (Mage = 15.79, SD = 0.29, 51 % = females) in 77 economies. Multi-level structural equation
modelling was used to analyse the data. The results indicated that (1) fear of failure positively predicted reading,
mathematics, and science achievements; (2) fear of failure was predicted by socio-economic status positively and
gender (0 = female, 1 = male) negatively; and (3) the relationships between fear of failure and achievements
were moderated by socio-economic status positively but by gender negatively. The findings indicate the
importance of accounting for socio-economic status and gender when examining fear of failure in academic
settings.
Educational relevance and implications statement: This study adopted multi-level structural equation modelling
(ML-SEM) to demonstrate that socio-economic status (SES) and gender were associated with the quantity and the
magnitude of fear of failure in predicting reading, mathematics, and science achievements. Our evidence suggests that beyond exclusive attention to psychological factors and proximal environment factors, accounting for
individuals' differences in socio-economic status and gender could help researchers and educators to have a more
comprehensive understanding of the functions of fear of failure in learning contexts.

1. Introduction
In achievement contexts, individuals are motivated by the desire to
avoid failure (Bélanger et al., 2013). This psychological mechanism is
commonly defined as fear of failure, a dispositional achievement motive
to avoid failing situations (Atkinson, 1957; Elliot & Thrash, 2004).
Previous studies have demonstrated that fear of failure is widespread
across all ethnic groups and has inimical effects on learning outcomes
such as persistence, effort, engagement, and well-being (Caraway et al.,
2003; De Castella et al., 2013; Soomro & Shah, 2022), by eliciting the
adoption of specific avoidance-based goals and strategies (e.g., selfhandicapping, slow information accumulation) (Chen et al., 2009;

Elliot & Thrash, 2004; Lerche et al., 2018). Fear of failure also positively
predicts procrastination and antisocial behaviours (Sagar et al., 2011;
Soomro & Shah, 2022). Despite the well-recognized deleterious effects
of fear of failure, mixed results have also emerged regarding the relationships between fear of failure and achievement (OECD, 2019b;
Thuany et al., 2022; Tseng, 2022; Wach et al., 2015). For instance, OECD
(2019b) and Tseng (2022) suggested that fear of failure is positively
related to reading achievement or English proficiency levels, respectively. In contrast, Wach et al. (2015) found that females' greater fear of
failure resulted in poorer mathematics achievement. The inconsistent
findings indicate the heterogeneity of fear of failure effect on learning
achievements in different conditions. We argue that the quantity of fear

To date, the majority of the existing research into fear of failure was
contextualized in sports domain (Taylor et al., 2021) and focused on
psychological factors (e.g., achievement goals, self-efficacy, shamecoping style, perfectionism) (e.g., Conroy & Elliot, 2004; Ellison &
Partridge, 2012; Tseng, 2022) and proximal environmental factors (e.g.,
teachers' interpersonal styles, coach behaviours, empowering/disempowering motivational climate) (Birr et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2023;
Huéscar Hernández et al., 2020) in predicting fear of failure. Only
scattered studies have examined the demographic differences such as
socio-economic status (SES) and gender differences in fear of failure and
the potential relationships between SES/gender and fear of failure
(OECD, 2019b; Steinmayr & Spinath, 2008; Thuany et al., 2022). In
addition, given that the relationships between SES/gender and academic
achievements have been well-recognized, it can be speculated that SES
and gender probably predict fear of failure and interfere with the
magnitude of the effect of fear of failure on academic achievement.
However, this issue remains underexplored (Wach et al., 2015). Hence,
the present study aimed to adopt the Program for International Student
Assessment (PISA) 2018 data across 77 economies and explore the
interplay between SES and gender and fear of failure in predicting
learning achievements, especially the moderation of SES and gender on
the relationship between fear of failure and learning achievements. This
study would shed light on the roles of individuals' differences in fear of
failure, advancing relevant research and providing implications for researchers and educators.

2.2. SES, fear of failure, and learning achievements
As one of the essential sociological factors, SES has significantly
positive effects on academic achievement (e.g., King et al., 2024; Liu
et al., 2020). However, the strength of associations varies across sociocultural contexts and subjects. For instance, Sirin (2005) reviewed
relevant literature and showed the strongest positive relation between
SES and mathematics achievement. By contrast, Liu et al. (2020) found
that in the context of China, SES had a stronger positive relationship
with language (i.e., Chinese and English) achievement than with science, mathematics, and general achievement.
A few other studies have indicated the relationships between SES and
fear of failure, yielding heterogeneous results (Gilbert et al., 2022). On
the one hand, the OECD (2019b) reported that in some countries and
economies, students in high-SES cities and private schools experienced
greater fear of failure than their counterparts in low SES city and rural
and public schools, respectively. On the other hand, the OECD (2019b)
suggested that students in Asian countries and economies experienced
the greatest fear of failure, whereas students in European countries had
the slightest fear of failure. Thuany et al. (2022) also verified that SES
was negatively related to fear of failure among female runners. Moreover, although high levels of heterogeneity existed, a review study by
Gilbert et al. (2022) observed a negative relation between economic
status and avoidance motivation (including fear of failure). Therefore,
we speculate that SES might also moderate the relationship between fear
of failure and learning achievement. However, to our knowledge, no
studies have substantiated this effect.

2. Literature review
2.1. Fear of failure and learning achievements
Fear of failure often refers to a dispositional achievement motive that
orients individuals to avoid failure (Atkinson, 1957; Elliot & Thrash,
2004). This achievement motive is activated when individuals perceive
failure to be a threat to their meaningful goals (Conroy & Elliot, 2004).
The level of fear mainly depends on the perceived level of risk of failing
and anticipations of aversive or threatening consequences, involving
five dimensions: (1) feeling of shame and embarrassment, (2) devaluation of one's self-estimation, (3) having an uncertain future, (4) significant others' loss of interest, and (5) upsetting important others (Conroy,
2001).
Studies examining the relationship between fear of failure and academic achievements are limited, and the results are mixed (OECD,
2019b; Thuany et al., 2022; Tseng, 2022). For instance, a few studies
have suggested a positive relation (OECD, 2019b; Steinmayr & Spinath,
2008; Tseng, 2022). Specifically, Steinmayr and Spinath (2008) found
that fear of failure positively predicted females' mathematics grades.
OECD (2019b) also reported that in most school systems, students with
higher levels of fear of failure scored higher in reading after accounting
for the SES of students and schools. This relationship is also held at the
system level. Similarly, Tseng (2022) identified that fear of failure
positively predicted students' English proficiency levels. Several studies
have also spotted negative relationships (Moyano et al., 2020; OECD,
2019b; Steinmayr & Spinath, 2008; Wach et al., 2015). Wach et al.
(2015) and Steinmayr and Spinath (2008) found that greater fear of
failure of German students resulted in poorer learning achievement (i.e.,
mid-term grades in mathematics or grade point average) in females.
Moyano et al. (2020) revealed that Spanish primary students' fear of
failure was negatively related to their global academic performance and
mathematics achievement. Similarly, the OECD (2019b) also pointed
out that many countries and economies did not follow the positive
relationship between fear of failure and reading achievements, and the
weakest and negative relationships appeared in education systems
whose reading performance was below the OECD average.

2.3. Gender, learning achievement, and fear of failure
Many studies have investigated the relationship between gender and
learning achievements, and the results are mixed. Most studies presented gender-specific advantages in various subjects across elementary
schools, secondary schools, or universities (e.g., Manu et al., 2023; Vos
et al., 2023; Voyer & Voyer, 2014). For example, a meta-analysis by
Voyer and Voyer (2014) indicated an overall female advantage in
mathematics, reading, and science, among which language courses had
the largest effects, and math courses obtained the smallest. Manu et al.
(2023) showed a trend of increasing superiority of girls' reading performance over time, while Vos et al. (2023) revealed that 18–35 year-old
women had significantly lower mathematics performance than men.
Meanwhile, Ding et al. (2023) documented heterogeneous gender gaps
in three scientific competencies. Chinese senior secondary school girls
outperformed boys when identifying scientific issues, whereas the
converse held on applying scientific knowledge in new situations. By
contrast, other studies revealed non-significant gender differences in
mathematics, reading, and science (Matthews et al., 2009; Oladejo,
Nwaboku, et al., 2023; Oladejo, Olateju, et al., 2023). For instance,
Matthews et al. (2009) found no significant gender differences in five
areas of early achievement: applied problems (math), general knowledge, letter–word identification, expressive vocabulary, and sound
awareness. The above evidence suggests that gender differences exist

Previous research has also shown that female students expressed a
higher fear of failure than male students (Borgonovi & Han, 2021;
Eriksson & Strimling, 2023; Steinmayr & Spinath, 2008). Borgonovi and
Han (2021) further found this gender gap was especially high among
high-performing student groups and in countries with higher economic
prosperity, lower societal-level gender inequality, and comprehensive
education systems. In contrast, in terms of sub-dimensions of fear of
failure, there are also inconsistent gender differences (Taylor et al.,
2021). For example, compared with female counterparts, male athletes
expressed greater fear of losing significant others' interest and lower
levels of fear of devaluing one's self-estimation and of shame and
embarrassment (Gómez-López et al., 2019; Sagar et al., 2011). However,
the predictability of gender for fear of failure is seldom examined, except
for Steinmayr and Spinath (2008), who found a negative relation.
To clarify the mechanisms leading to the gender gap in fear of failure
and achievement, detecting mean differences in fear of failure or
achievement alone is insufficient (Wach et al., 2015). These prior studies
have suggested that the relationship between fear of failure and academic achievement probably varies as a function of gender. So far, few
studies have addressed this question, with three exceptions indicating
the potential gender differences in how well the fear of failure predicts
academic achievement (OECD, 2019b; Steinmayr & Spinath, 2008;
Wach et al., 2015). Steinmayr and Spinath (2008) found that fear of
failure significantly and positively predicted only female adolescents'
mathematics grades. OECD (2019b) also found that in most school
systems, fear of failure was a much better positive predictor of reading,
mathematics, and science achievement among females than among
boys. On the contrary, Wach et al. (2015) revealed that fear of failure
negatively predicted mathematics grades only for females. Hence, more
empirical research is necessary to explore how gender moderates between fear of failure and learning achievements.

3. Methods
3.1. Study design and procedures
The current study used a cross-sectional design to explore the universality regarding the relationship between fear of failure, socioeconomic status or gender, and learning achievements. We used the
PISA 2018 data (OECD, 2019a) publicly available from https://www.
oecd.org/pisa/data/2018database/. PISA is a large-scale international
educational assessment project sponsored by OECD. PISA measures
adolescents' literacy in reading, mathematics, and science (OECD,
2019a). Together with these test scores, PISA also collected questionnaire data on demographic information (e.g., age, gender, immigration
status), student factors (e.g., emotion, cognition, personality), and
contextual factors such as their parents, teachers, schools, and the
educational systems. Given the purpose of the current study, we used
students' response data on the fear of failure questionnaire, test scores in
mathematics, reading, and science, SES, and student gender.
Students from all 77 economies were included except for those with
complete missing values on SES, fear of failure, or test scores. Enders
(2010) suggests that missing values larger than 20% of the total responses should be dropped from missing value computations as retaining them would cause more harm. Therefore, we dropped cases with
missing values on all items of the fear of failure scale, achievement in
mathematics, reading, science, and SES. This ended up with 536,741
students. Among them, missing values only occurred to 33 cases in their
SES. Given the trivial portion of missing values, we used the regression
imputation function in SPSS 26 (IBM Corporation, 2019) and calculated
the missing values before further analysis. The proportions of participants in each of these economies ranged from 1881 (0.40%) by Moscow
Region to 33,517 (6.20%) by Spain.
3.2. Instruments

2.4. The present study

3.2.1. Fear of failure
Fear of failure was measured by three statements that asked students
to endorse their extent of agreement using a 4-point scale (1 = Strongly
Disagree, 4 = Strongly Agree). These statements are ST183Q01 (“When I
am failing, I worry about what others think of me,” M = 2.6, SD = 0.9),
ST183Q02 (“When I am failing, I am afraid that I might not have enough
talent,” M = 2.6, SD = 0.9), and ST183Q02 (“When I am failing, this
makes me doubt my plans for the future”, M = 2.6, SD = 1.0). The internal consistency of the scale was α = 0.81. Note that OECD invited a
panel of experts to develop, evaluate, and revise the instrument based on
their relevance and representativeness to the content domain and
invaluable feedback from participating countries, which ensured that
the fear of failure instrument had a good content validity (Almanasreh
et al., 2019; Borsboom et al., 2004; Cai, 2015).

As the literature review above has indicated, the relationship between fear of failure and learning achievements has yet to be conclusive,
which is probably influenced by multiple factors. However, existing
studies seldom examined how SES and gender predict fear of failure and
interfere with the magnitude of the effect of fear of failure on academic
achievement in different domains. To fill in this gap, advance relevant
research, and shed light on the roles of individuals' differences in fear of
failure, this study aims to address the following three research questions:
1. Does fear of failure predict learning achievements? If the answer is
yes, to what extent?
2. Does SES predict the quantity of fear of failure and moderate its
strength in predicting learning achievements? If the answers are yes,
to what extents?
3. Does gender predict the quantity of fear of failure and moderate its
strength in predicting learning achievements? If the answers are yes,
to what extents?

3.2.2. SES
SES (socio-economic status) in PISA 2018 was represented using the
composite variable economic and social-cultural status (ESCS) that
measures students' material living conditions (Willms, 2006). The ESCS
was computed as the arithmetic mean of three component indicators
based on all participating economies: parental occupation, parental
education, and home possessions (OECD, 2019a). The ESCS score was
standardized, with a mean of M = − 0.25, SD = 1.09.

Three main hypotheses are proposed accordingly:
H1. Fear of failure positively predicts reading, mathematics, and science achievements.
H2. SES positively predicts fear of failure and moderates the predictive
effects of fear of failure on reading, mathematics, and science.

3.3. Procedures of data analysis
Correlation analysis was first performed to examine the relationships
between each pair of constructs, including achievement (reading,
mathematics, science), fear of failure, SES, and gender. Then, given that
student data in PISA were nested within schools and countries, multilevel structural equation modelling (ML-SEM) was used for primary
data analysis to decompose variance at the lower level (i.e., student level
in our case) and the higher level (i.e., school level) (Heck & Thomas,
2015). According to Rappaport et al. (2020), a researcher can focus on
the structural relationships at any level without forcefully constructing a
structural relationship simultaneously at each level. The current study
focused on the relationship at the student level: the moderation of SES
and gender on the relationship between fear of failure and achievements
in mathematics, reading, and science. Specifically, we first tested a basic
ML-SEM exploring the main effects of fear of failure, SES, and gender on
learning achievements (Model 1). Next, we constructed a latent interaction term between fear of failure and each of the moderators using the
Latent Moderated Structural Equations (LMS; Klein & Moosbrugger,
2000) (Model 2). Besides, as the maximum likelihood with robust
standard errors (MLR) estimator does not provide standardized estimates for models with nonlinear relationships (i.e., random slopes), we
also used the Bayes estimator to obtain standardized estimates (Muthén,
2010) for Model 2. All these analyses were conducted in Mplus 8.10
(Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2023).
To evaluate the model's fit without interaction (Model 1), we consulted the criteria recommended by Mueller and Hancock (2019).
Accordingly, a model with Root Mean Square Error of Approximation
(RMSEA) and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) values
no larger than 0.05 together with comparative Fit Index (CFI) and
Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) values no smaller than 0.95 was determined to
have a good fit.
To evaluate the fit of the model with interaction terms (Model 2)
with the MLR estimator, we compared the difference in the chi-square
difference (− 2 times the log-likelihood or − 2LL, symbolized using G2)
of the baseline model (Model 1) and the more complex model (Model 2).
The difference in − 2LL (ΔG2) with the difference in parameters estimated (i.e., degree of freedom) follows the distribution of chi-square
(Steiger et al., 1985). A significance in the difference would justify the
model with the interaction terms (Cai, 2020; Cai & Kunnan, 2020;
Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2023). For the estimation using the Bayes
estimator, the justification of the latent interaction terms was based on
the Bayesian 95 % credibility interval of the estimates. An exclusion of
zero suggests the significance of the estimate and the meaningfulness of
the interaction term (Muthén, 2010).

5.1. Fear of failure predicting learning achievements

(2.1)

About the first research question, this study revealed that fear of
failure positively predicted learning achievements in reading, mathematics, and science, supporting Hypothesis 1. This finding is in accordance with OECD (2019b) and Tseng (2022), which revealed a positive
relationship between fear of failure and reading or English achievement,
and with Steinmayr and Spinath (2008), who identified fear of failure as
a positive predictor of female adolescents' mathematics grades. Students
with greater fear of failure had higher reading, mathematics, and science
scores. Previous research suggests that fear of failure can be separated
into over-striving and self-protection (Martin & Marsh, 2003). In this
context, fear of failure probably drives students to achieve and persist in
facing challenges and adversity, facilitating a positive relationship with
academic achievement. This finding is also different from other studies
(Caraway et al., 2003; Moyano et al., 2020; Thuany et al., 2022; Wach
et al., 2015; Zuber & Conzelmann, 2014). The inconsistencies are
probably related to the measure methods of fear of failure and academic
achievements as well as subject differences (Schwinger et al., 2014).
Caraway et al. (2003) identified the inverse relationships by adopting
the test anxiety scale and grade point average as the indicators of fear of
failure and achievement, respectively. In contrast, when the general fear
of failure scale was employed, Caraway et al. (2003) revealed a positive
but non-significant relationship with GPA. Similarly, Wach et al. (2015)
used a general fear of failure scale and mid-term grades as the relevant
indicators. They found the associations between fear of failure and
mathematics/German grades were positive but non-significant. Moyano
et al. (2020) adopted average grades during an academic year and
presented that fear of failure was negatively associated with mathematics achievement but non-significantly with language and literature

This study investigated how SES and gender are associated with the
quantity of fear of failure and learning achievements (in reading,

Regarding the second research question, SES was a positive predictor

with Thuany et al. (2022), who used the Spearman correlation method
to recognize a negative relationship between SES and fear of failure
among female runners. The difference may be related to the demographic characteristics of the sample. Most participants in Thuany
et al. (2022) were non-professional runners (37.7 ± 9.60 years old),
whereas the participants in this study were 15-year-old students. Nonprofessional runners with low SES are more likely to aspire for success
and to acquire social ascension/social prestige, and have a higher fear of
failure than their peers (Thuany et al., 2022). In contrast, adolescents
with higher SES probably pay more attention to whether their failure
devalues their self-estimate, indicates an uncertain future, and upsets
their parents or peers compared with their peers with lower SES. In this
regard, adolescents with higher SES probably fear failure more than
their peers.
In line with most existing studies (e.g., Liu et al., 2020; Sirin, 2005;
White, 1982), SES positively predicted reading, mathematics, and science achievements. Students from high SES probably receive more
learning opportunities and resources, contributing to higher academic
achievement. The meta-analysis of Sirin (2005) revealed a decline in the
mean correlation between SES and academic achievement (0.30),
compared with the meta-analysis of White (1982) (0.34). However, even
though the social and overall policy has changed considerably over

almost two decades, this study showed the path coefficients were larger
than 0.35. The finding may suggest that the impact of SES on academic
achievements across 77 economies is still high. The educational
inequality resulting from SES still needs more attention.
More importantly and interestingly, this study demonstrated that
SES positively moderated the relationships between fear of failure and
achievement in reading, mathematics, and science, supporting Hypothesis 2. The relationships between fear of failure and academic
achievement in three subjects are stronger among students with
advantaged SES than their peers with disadvantaged SES. In other
words, SES could enlarge the magnitude of fear of failure in predicting
achievement. We argue that fear of failure probably drives students from
higher SES to make full use of family resources to improve their academic achievement. In contrast, influenced by fear of failure, students
from lower SES tend to devote limited effort and resources to learning,
which limits their learning outcomes.

positive predictor of mathematics achievement, supporting the study of

related to females' higher expectancy for success, higher self-discipline,
and lesser tendency to avoid work, which supports females to invest
much more time and effort in reading and science (Duckworth &
Seligman, 2006; Steinmayr & Spinath, 2008). However, influenced by
stereotypes and subject characteristics, females have low expectancy
and value in mathematics and perform poorly than males (Voyer &
Voyer, 2014). In this respect, males' higher self-concept and value in
mathematics probably offset females' advantages in mathematics
(Steinmayr & Spinath, 2008). These findings are, to some extent,
inconsistent with existing research on female advantages in mathematics (Voyer & Voyer, 2014) or non-significant gender differences in
mathematics, reading, and science (Matthews et al., 2009;Oladejo,
Nwaboku, et al., 2023; Oladejo, Olateju, et al., 2023). These inconsistencies also indicate that different academic domains or conditions (e.g., instructional environment) possibly impact the relationship
between gender and school achievement (Oladejo, Nwaboku, et al.,
2023; Oladejo, Olateju, et al., 2023).
More importantly, gender negatively moderated the relationships
between fear of failure and achievement in three academic domains,

following Hypothesis 3. In other words, in 77 economies, fear of failure
had a more significant effect on reading, mathematics, and science
achievement among females than males, indicating a suppression effect
of gender on the magnitude of fear of failure in predicting achievement.
The findings support and complement two existing studies (OECD,
2019b; Steinmayr & Spinath, 2008) by substantiating that the importance of fear of failure for academic achievement differs between genders. Influenced by fear of failure, girls are more likely to better selfregulate their behaviours (Matthews et al., 2009), invest much more
time and effort in learning (Steinmayr & Spinath, 2008), and then
perform better in three academic domains in comparison with boys.
6. Implications, limitations, and conclusion
6.1. Implications
This study provides theoretical and practical implications for researchers and educators in three main aspects.
First, this study identified a positive relationship between fear of
failure and reading/mathematics/science achievements, substantiating
the positive relation and generalizing it to multiple subjects and among
male and female students. Although many studies indicated the detrimental effects of fear of failure (e.g., decreasing life satisfaction), the
present study suggests its beneficial effects. Fear of failure probably
drives students to achieve and persist in facing challenges and adversity
and obtain better academic performance. The findings, to some extent,
enrich and advance extant literature on the associations between academic emotions (e.g., enjoyment, anxiety) and academic achievement,
which consistently recognized negative emotions as negative predictors
of academic performance (Pekrun et al., 2014; Pekrun et al., 2023). The
discrepancy indicated a potential mechanism underlying the relationships between fear of failure and achievement, probably different from
general negative emotions (e.g., anxiety, boredom, anger). In addition,

this study is the first to use more standard scales (i.e., standardized
achievement tests, multidimensional fear of failure scale) and
multilevel-structural equation modelling to identify the effect of fear of
failure on three subject achievements, complementing the existing
research. To our knowledge, prior research on fear of failure often
adopted teacher-assigned grades (e.g., subject grades, mid-term grades,
the end-of-the-year grades) and students' GPA as indicators of achievements and general fear of failure or test anxiety scales to explore the
relationship between fear of failure and learning achievement. In the
meantime, this study proposes a methodological issue regarding the
effects of different measure methods worthy of further investigation.
Second, this study found that SES positively predicted fear of failure
and reading/mathematics/science achievements, and gender negatively
predicted fear of failure and reading and science achievements and
positively predicted mathematics achievement, suggesting SES and
gender are both important factors influencing students' fear of failure
and achievements. In other words, like cognitive measures, motivational
measures are also prone to problems in gender-fair or SES-fair. Also,
although SES differences can be generalized across multiple subjects (i.
e., reading, mathematics, science), gender differences are probably
subject-sensitive. Hence, researchers and educators must pay attention
to SES and gender inequalities in various aspects of education, taking
measures to facilitate educational equity.
Third, as the most significant contribution of this study, it adds to the
literature by showing that the relations between fear of failure and
achievement were positively moderated by SES and negatively moderated by gender. Although SES and gender differences have received
increasing attention, the moderating roles of SES and gender in the relationships between fear of failure and achievement remain underexplored. As previous research has pointed out, detecting mean differences in fear of failure or achievements alone is insufficient for SES and
gender gaps (Wach et al., 2015). Our study is the first to address this
issue with a large sample across 77 economies, providing a possible
mechanism for explaining the relationship between fear of failure and
achievements. Based on the findings of this study, teachers and policymakers could employ appropriate strategies or policies to control the
effects of SES and gender on fear of failure and achievements, achieving
a balance between the detrimental and beneficial effects of fear of
failure.

6.3. Conclusion
In response to the three research questions, the present study firstly
demonstrated that fear of failure positively predicted learning achievements in reading, mathematics, and science. SES was not only a positive
predictor of fear of failure but also positively moderated the relationships between fear of failure and achievement in reading, mathematics,
and science. Moreover, gender negatively predicted fear of failure and
negatively moderated the relationships between fear of failure and
achievement in the three academic domains. In conclusion, the present
study demonstrated that SES and gender were associated with the
quantity and the magnitude of fear of failure in predicting achievement.
Our evidence suggests that beyond exclusive attention to psychological
factors and the proximal environment, accounting for individuals'
gender and SES could help researchers to have a more comprehensive
understanding of the functions of fear of failure in academic contexts. A
fuller understanding of the fear of failure in different conditions contributes to educational equity.

The present study has three main limitations. First, although this
study indicated the predictive effect of fear of failure on academic
achievements, a causal relationship between them should not be substantiated. Future research could conduct longitudinal or experimental
studies to verify this causal relationship. Second, this study identified
the relationship between fear of failure and academic achievements and
the moderating roles of SES and gender but did not account for other
variables that might interfere with the observed relationships. The
predictive effects of fear of failure on achievements may be interfered by
other factors such as students' cognitive and motivational resources and
other demographic variables such as their grade level, immigration
status, and grade repetition. Future research could further investigate
these mechanisms to unveil the relationships between fear of failure and
academic achievements. Third, the true association between fear of
failure and achievements consists of three components: universal features across all countries, variation across schools, and variation across
cultures/economies. The focus of the current study is only on the first
component; that is, the universal feature of the fear of failure and
achievement association at the student level, after controlling for variation at the school and culture or country level. However, the correlations between fear of failure and achievement vary with culture or
countries, such as Finland and China. Hence, future research could
examine the variation across cultures or schools and the potential
mechanisms or factors.
