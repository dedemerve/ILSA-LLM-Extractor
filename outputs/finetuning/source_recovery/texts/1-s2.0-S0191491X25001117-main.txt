A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Predictive validity

School grades

The aim of this study was to investigate and compare how two reading assessments measured in Grade 4 predict
later school grades. Specifically, the study explored how paper-based and digital reading scores were related to
school grades in Grades 6 and 9. Data from the international assessment PIRLS 2016 and its digital counterpart
ePIRLS 2016, along with Swedish register data were used. Regression results speak to the lasting effects of early
reading achievement on later achievement in various subjects. Notably, digital test scores showed stronger associations with later grades, particularly in Grade 6. Although moderate in strength, the predictive validity of
digital test scores was higher for subjects like English and mathematics, compared to paper-based test scores.
These findings lend some credibility to the ongoing transition to digital assessment, indicating that digital
reading measures may capture skills more relevant to later academic success.

1. Introduction
Assessment has long played a central role in education, serving both
summative and formative purposes. Over time, technology has transformed the ways in which students’ achievements are assessed. In the
context of testing, however, it is only in the last few decades that digital
assessments have begun to take on a more central role, as assessment
systems have started to transition their paper-and-pencil tests to digital
formats. International large-scale assessments have already shifted toward digital delivery, allowing for new types of test items, richer data
collection, and adaptive designs (OECD, 2019; Mullis & Martin, 2019).
For example, the Progress in International Reading Literacy Study
(PIRLS) introduced a computer-based online reading assessment
administered alongside the main study, in 2016. As part of a planned
transition, the 2021 cycle of PIRLS introduced digital assessment as its
primary mode, which was widely adopted, while continuing to offer a
paper-based alternative (Mullis & Martin, 2021). Meanwhile the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) began its transition
to digital testing already in 2015, with a more pronounced shift by 2018
(OECD, 2020). Similarly, many countries have partially digitized certain
high-stakes student examinations, with others currently in the process of
digitization (OECD, 2023). Despite this shift, there may be unresolved
issues, most notably whether scores obtained from digital and

traditional test formats are comparable and equally valid, and how
performance across these modes relates to later academic outcomes.
Comparability and validity have long been central concerns in research
on international reading assessments (Strietholt & Rosén, 2016; Strietholt, Rosén, & Bos, 2013). Previous studies demonstrated how differences in assessment design, linking procedures, and population
characteristics can influence how trends in reading achievement are
interpreted (Strietholt & Rosén, 2016; Strietholt, Rosén, & Bos, 2013).
These insights remain highly relevant today, as digitalisation introduces
new challenges for maintaining the integrity of reading constructs across
formats.
For instance, Fishbein et al. (2018), in their study of the transition to
digital formats in TIMSS, emphasize that even when the core content of
test items is preserved, differences in presentation, response format,
navigation, and other test design elements can introduce mode effects.
These variations can significantly impact the constructs being assessed
and, consequently, student scores. This uncertainty highlights the need
for further research to understand whether the shift to digital assessments will produce comparable outcomes in terms of student achievement and predictive validity.

reading assessments (PIRLS and ePIRLS), this study examines how scores

9.3. Model 1: overall reading achievement as a predictor
Across all subjects and grades, Model 1 confirmed that overall
reading ability (Mean achievement) was a strong and consistent predictor of academic outcomes, with standardized coefficients ranging
from moderate to high. For instance, in grade 6, mean reading
achievement strongly predicted performance in Swedish (β =.630),
Mathematics (β =.582), and English (β =.610) and GPA (β =.437).
Similarly, in grade 9, it remained a significant predictor for Swedish (β
=.549), English (β =.568), Mathematics (β =.504), and GPA (β =.510).

9. Results
9.1. Associations between early reading and later grades
Firstly, we examined the correlations between PIRLS, ePIRLS scores,
grades in core subjects and GPAs in the 6th and 9th grade (Table 2). This
allowed us to answer the first research question by focusing on the
strength of the association between PIRLS and ePIRLS and the later
measures of achievement.
The results show that both PIRLS and ePIRLS scores are moderately
to strongly correlated with grades in the core subjects across both time
points. Paper PIRLS scores correlate with core subjects in 6th grade, such
as Swedish (.600), Math (.535), and English (.549), and maintain almost
similar strength with 9th-grade subjects, including Swedish (.522), Math
(.469), and English (.536). Interestingly, ePIRLS scores display slightly
stronger correlations with both 6th- and 9th-grade subjects. For
instance, ePIRLS correlates with 6th-grade Swedish at.619, Math at.576,
and English at.604, as well as with 9th-grade Swedish (.547), Math
(.486), and English (.574). This suggests that while both paper-based
reading skills and digital reading skills are related to later academic
outcomes, the digital scores appear to be even somewhat higher, as
indicated by this initial analysis.
As expected, the correlations are strong between subject grades in
respective school years, but they are notably higher in 9th grade
compared to 6th grade. For instance, the correlation between GPAs and
Swedish and math in 9th grade are.857 and.817 respectively, while in
6th grade they are.599 and.583. This suggests a stronger relationship
between subjects and GPAs as pupils progress in their education. However, the main takeaway in relation to our research question is that early
reading skills, particularly in digital format, show substantial associations with later academic performance.

9.4. Model 2: mode effects on later achievement
Adding the Difference variable in Model 2 allowed us to assess
whether the two modes had the same predictive value, holding
achievement levels constant. If modes were related in same ways to
future performance, the effect of the difference variable would be nonsignificant- In grade 6, the Difference variable however showed significant results for Swedish (β =.050), Mathematics (β =.066), English (β
=.088), and GPA (β =.046), suggesting that students who performed
relatively better in digital reading achieved slightly higher later grades,
especially in the core subjects. This pattern also emerged in grade 9 for
English (β =.076) and Swedish (β =.053) but was non-significant for
GPA and Mathematics. These results suggest that early digital reading
skills measured in grade 4 provide a small but unique contribution to
later academic performance, beyond general reading ability, especially
in language specific subjects.
Interestingly, the predictive strength of digital reading skills appears
more pronounced in grade 6 than in grade 9. As expected, given the
shorter time span between 4th and 6th grade, the influence of early
digital reading skills remains more evident. In contrast, the longer gap
between 4th and 9th grade provides more opportunity for additional
factors to shape academic performance, thereby reducing the relative
impact of early digital reading abilities.

10. Discussion
The present study aimed to investigate how well paper-based and
digital reading results in grade 4 could predict later academic achievement in compulsory school, with a specific focus on the differences in
predictive power between the two modalities. Understanding how each
assessment mode relates to future outcomes can inform educational
policy, instructional practices, and assessment design.
10.1. Interpreting correlations between early reading and later outcomes
Our analyses confirmed that both PIRLS and ePIRLS scores were
significantly associated with students’ grades in Swedish, Mathematics,
and English, as well as GPA, in grades 6 and 9. However, ePIRLS scores
showed consistently stronger correlations. This may suggest that digital

reading skills reflect the complex reading demands increasingly present
in academic settings, such as navigating non-linear text, evaluating
multiple sources, and managing cognitive load. This finding aligns with
prior research (Baghaei et al., 2025), which conceptualizes digital
reading as a multidimensional construct involving distinct skills beyond
basic comprehension. The observed advantage of ePIRLS in grade 6 may
also be because younger students are still learning basic digital skills,
which can affect their performance in different subjects. Importantly, all
associations with teacher grades are expected to be attenuated by
measurement error in grades, which helps explain why estimates remain
moderate and why they tend to be larger in Grade 9 than Grade 6, as
Grade 9 grades are typically more consistent and higher-stakes. By
contrast, the PIRLS–ePIRLS association involves IRT-scaled latent
models and is therefore less susceptible to such attenuation, though not
entirely free of uncertainty due to conditioning and sampling.

associated with socio-educational support at home (e.g., Gustafsson
et al., 2011). Gender differences were observed but are interpreted
primarily as controls rather than substantive findings, ensuring that any
associations between reading scores and academic outcomes are not due
to systematic differences between boys and girls. Similarly, immigrant
background had minimal impact in grade 6 but became more influential
across subjects by grade 9, highlighting structural challenges that may
compound over time. Controlling for these demographic factors helps
ensure that the observed predictive effects of digital and paper-based
reading scores are not confounded by group differences. Overall,
including these controls confirms that the relative predictive power of
digital versus paper-based reading is largely independent of demographic characteristics.

10.2. Distinguishing predictive contributions: digital vs. paper-based
reading

This study examined whether digital and paper-based reading assessments in grade 4 are equally valid predictors of later academic
achievement. While both assessment modes offer valuable insights into
students’ reading proficiency and are moderately to strongly predictive
of later grades, digital reading assessments show a slightly stronger
predictive relationship with later grades across subjects, with the
longest-lasting additional predictive value observed in language-based
subjects. This does not imply that the two formats are entirely inequivalent, but rather that digital assessments may capture certain additional competencies that are increasingly relevant in modern academic
contexts. Skills such as navigating non-linear text structures, processing
information across multiple sources, and managing cognitive load may
contribute to this added predictive value.
These results have important implications for both assessment design
and educational policy. As educational systems continue transitioning to
digital testing formats, it is essential to consider not only the comparability of scores across modes but also what unique skills each mode may
be assessing. Digital reading assessments may offer a broader or more
contemporary view of student ability, one that aligns more closely with
the cognitive demands of today’s information-rich environments.

11. Conclusion and implications for future research

Regression analyses further deepened our understanding of these
relationships. Model 1 confirmed that general reading ability is a robust
predictor of academic success. However, the inclusion of the Difference
variable in Model 2 highlighted a nuanced pattern: in grade 6, students
who demonstrated relatively stronger digital reading skills tended to
perform slightly better across all core subjects and GPA, and in grade 9
this additional predictive value remained only for Swedish and English.
This pattern suggests that digital reading may capture competencies that
transfer broadly to school achievement in the short term, while over a
longer period the added predictive value is concentrated in languagebased subjects. The continued predictive value of this digital advantage in grade 9 for language subjects, but not for mathematics or GPA,
supports the interpretation that mode effects are subject-specific and
persist in literacy-based domains. Given the ubiquity of Englishlanguage content in digital media (Crystal, 2003; Shah & Lohar,
2016), it is plausible that informal exposure reinforces formal learning
outcomes in English. The predictive strength of digital reading may also
be rooted in its distinct cognitive demands. Unlike paper-based formats,
digital reading involves navigation, decision-making, and managing
information from multiple sources (DeStefano & LeFevre, 2007). These
elements increase cognitive load and require more advanced working
memory and processing speed skills that are also linked to success in
subjects like mathematics (Geary, 2011; Kliziene et al., 2022). While
digital reading was not significantly predictive of math performance in
grade 9, the modest relationship in grade 6 suggests early digital
competence may support more technical domains before academic demands become more complex and diversified.
Finally, the strong correlations among grades across subjects suggest
the influence of a general academic factor, potentially reflecting
cognitive, motivational, and self-regulatory skills that support learning
broadly. Crucially, the stronger predictive validity of the digital reading
(ePIRLS) test suggests it may capture this general factor more effectively
than its paper-based counterpart, possibly by measuring a broader set of
competencies applicable across academic domains. Furthermore,
reading skills were more predictive in grade 6 than in grade 9. This likely
reflects the temporal proximity to the initial assessment in grade 4, but
may also indicate that as students mature, a broader range of academic
and personal factors begin to influence their outcomes. These findings
suggest a diminishing influence of early reading skills over time,
underscoring the importance of continuous skill development and support throughout schooling.
While not the primary focus of our study, the role of demographic
variables deserves mention. Gender, parental education, and immigrant
background were added to account for potential subgroup differences
that could influence performance. Parental education generally show a
positive association with academic outcomes, particularly by grade 9.

12. Limitations and considerations
A limitation of the study concerns outcome measurement error, as
teacher-assigned grades reflect not only students’ achievement but also
other influences such as interpretation of criteria, classroom and school
practices, and effort or behaviour (Klapp Lekholm & Cliffordson, 2008;
Gustafsson et al., 2014; Lundahl et al., 2016; Südkamp et al., 2012). This
measurement error attenuates the estimated associations, making them
conservative. Grades in Grade 6 are primarily diagnostic and low-stakes,
whereas those in Grade 9 are decisive for upper-secondary placement
and therefore play a more critical role in influencing students’ future
academic decisions. Differences in stakes and standardisation likely
make Grade 9 grades more reliable than those in Grade 6, contributing
to stronger observed correlations at Grade 9. While PIRLS and ePIRLS
are IRT-scaled latent models based on plausible values, and their mutual
correlation is therefore less affected by such error, some uncertainty
nevertheless remains (Mullis et al., 2017). However, the correlations
show that the relationships are similar to those measured in other
studies on standardized tests and teacher judgements (Südkamp et al.,
2012). It is important to note, though, that grades in grade 6 are primarily diagnostic, and thus not considered as high-stake as in grade 9,
which play a significant role in determining high school placement and
are and play a more critical role in influencing students’ future academic
decisions.
Beyond this, several methodological considerations may affect the
interpretation of our findings. First, the test order effect represents a
potential confound: paper PIRLS was administered before ePIRLS,
meaning that mode and test order are intertwined. This makes it difficult
to disentangle mode effects from potential fatigue, practice, or

motivation effects that may arise from taking the second test. Second,
participation in ePIRLS in 2016 was optional, and only 14 out of 57
PIRLS-participating countries chose to administer the digital version.
This introduces a potential self-selection bias and limits the generalisability of findings.
Together, these limitations highlight the complexity of comparing
paper-based and digital assessments, and they suggest that observed
differences in predictive validity may reflect more than just mode,
potentially encompassing a wider array of implementation factors.
Future Research and Final Remarks
Future research should examine the mechanisms that underlie the
stronger predictive validity of digital reading, whether this reflects
broader cognitive engagement, familiarity with digital environments, or
distinct strategic behaviours. Longitudinal work could also investigate

whether targeted interventions in digital literacy yield measurable gains
in later academic achievement.
As education systems increasingly adopt digital assessments, maintaining meaningful comparability across modes is crucial for fair and
interpretable results. Understanding how digital and traditional reading
assessments differentially capture the evolving nature of literacy is not
only a methodological issue but a defining challenge for the future of
large-scale educational measurement.
