Abstract
The present study explores the sources of validity evidence of the mathematics self-concept
construct in Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022 with a focus on
concurrent, discriminant, and predictive evidence. Mathematics self-concept was provided with a
single index variable in PISA 2022. Using only the U.S. students who participated in PISA 2022, we
included 4552 students. While we grounded our analyses in the Standards (2014), we aimed to
evaluate whether the mathematics self-concept is related to mathematics motivation, distinct
from mathematics self-efﬁcacy measures and predicts mathematics achievement. Results revealed
a positive association between mathematics self-concept and motivation (χ 2 = 86008.92, p < .001,
φ = 0.153), suggesting concurrent evidence. Signiﬁcant but negligible correlations with two
separate mathematics self-efﬁcacy measures (rpb = 0.078 and 0.073, p < .001) supported
discriminant evidence. Multilevel modeling analysis demonstrated that mathematics self-concept
signiﬁcantly predicted mathematics performance (β = 20.20, p < .001), supporting predictive
validity. Validity evidence for the mathematics self-concept scale in PISA 2022 could serve as a
basis for research focused on enhancing student outcomes. Implications, limitations, and future
directions are discussed.
Keywords
mathematics self-concept, large-scale assessment, psychometrics, validity

Introduction
Academic self-concept is a key factor that reﬂects an individual’s perception of their own abilities
in an academic domain. These academic self-concepts have a critical role in shaping academic
experiences and outcomes since they inﬂuence not only a student’s engagement and motivation
but also their overall academic achievement (Cooper et al., 2018; Jansen et al., 2021; Ramazan,
Dai, et al., 2023; Steinberg et al., 2024). Domain-speciﬁc academic self-concepts, such as
mathematics self-concept, have earned considerable attention due to their direct impact on adolescents’ performance in speciﬁc subject areas (e.g., Dai et al., 2023; Gorges & Hollmann, 2019;
Perinelli et al., 2022; Ramazan, Danielson, et al., 2023). For example, mathematics self-concept,
which is typically deﬁned as the self-perceived competence and conﬁdence that an adolescent has
in their ability to understand and perform mathematical tasks, is particularly important given the
foundational role that mathematics has in education and its relevance to various career paths of
adolescents (e.g., Ramazan, Danielson, et al., 2023; Sewasew et al., 2018). Previous work has
shown that stronger mathematics self-concept is associated with mathematics-related positive
outcomes (e.g., Hammoudi, 2019; Holenstein et al., 2021; Passiatore et al., 2023).
In the context of large-scale assessments like the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), understanding the validity of mathematics self-concept measures is critical to
ensuring the accuracy and conﬁdence in results and conclusions (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014;
Hernández-Torrano & Courtney, 2021; Hopfenbeck et al., 2017). In particular, the PISA
2022 assessment offers a unique opportunity to explore the concurrent, discriminant, and predictive evidence of mathematics self-concept of adolescents on a global scale (Ding et al., 2022;
Gustafsson, 2018; OECD, 2023) since it is the most recent and mathematics-focused PISA cycle
(OECD, 2023; OECD, 2024). As it has been suggested by the Standards (AERA, APA & NCME,
2014), these aspects of validity evidence help ensure that a scale is not only theoretically sound but
also practically useful and speciﬁc in its measurement. As well, these three aspects of validity
evidence provide a solid foundation for validating a measure’s relevance and effectiveness in its
speciﬁc context (e.g., Clark & Watson, 2019; Strauss & Smith, 2009). That is, concurrent evidence
supports alignment with other relevant measures, discriminant evidence suggests the separateness
and speciﬁcity of different constructs, and predictive evidence helps establish real-world relevance
and utility (Messick, 1989, 1995). It is because “…test developers and those selecting tests and
interpreting test results need adequate knowledge of psychometric principles such as validity and
reliability” (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, p. 4). Examining concurrent, discriminant, and
predictive validity evidence of a scale is crucial in psychometrics to ensure that the respective scale
accurately and effectively measures what it is intended to measure since these sources of evidence
are classiﬁed as evidence based on relations to other variables by the Standards (AERA, APA &
NCME, 2014). By doing so, all three contribute to the overall robustness and credibility of the
scale in both academic research and practical applications (e.g., Cook et al., 2015; Song et al.,
2023; Valdivia & Li, 2021) as Standards indicated “In many cases, the intended interpretation for
a given use implies that the construct should be related to some other variables, and, as a result,
analyses of the relationship of test scores to variables external to the test provide another important source of validity evidence” (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, p. 16).
Taken together, these forms of validity evidence provide a comprehensive understanding of
how mathematics self-concept functions as a psychological construct, its relevance in educational
settings, and its potential to inform educational practices and interventions aimed at improving
student outcomes (e.g, AERA, APA & NCME, 2014; Assor & Connell, 1992; Danielson et al.,
2023; Hudson et al., 2017; Valdivia & Li, 2021). Therefore, this research aimed to examine
whether mathematics self-concept is related to mathematics motivation (i.e., concurrent evidence),
how it is distinct from mathematics self-efﬁcacy (i.e., discriminant evidence), and mathematics

self-concept is a predictor of mathematics performance (i.e., predictive evidence) in PISA 2022.
On the one hand, previous literature has suggested that mathematics self-concept and mathematics
motivation are usually found to have a strong relationship where a higher mathematics selfconcept is associated with greater mathematics motivation (e.g., Hammoudi, 2019; Niemivirta
et al., 2023; Skaalvik & Valås, 1999). This ﬁnding might seem counter-intuitive at ﬁrst, but it can
be understood in the context of self-enhancement theory (e.g., Calsyn & Kenny, 1977), which
posits that positive self-perceptions in a subject can potentially lead to greater motivation to
engage and perform well in that subject which could thereby reinforce and strengthen the selfconcept. The literature has also been interested in showcasing how different self-concept and selfefﬁcacy are from one another. Self-concept is a broad and overarching perception of one’s identity
and abilities which comprises various domains such as academic, social, and personal attributes.
Self-efﬁcacy, in contrast, speciﬁcally refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to successfully
perform tasks or achieve goals within a particular domain (Bong, 1998; Bong & Clark, 1999;
Bong & Skaalvik, 2003). An extensive body of research (e.g., Ramazan, Danielson, et al. 2023;
Sewasew et al., 2018; Skaalvik & Valås, 1999) has indicated that academic self-concept is
predictive of academic achievement where a stronger academic self-concept is associated with
higher achievement while it is also emphasized by the self-enhancement theory (e.g., Calsyn &
Kenny, 1977) since self-concept is usually seen as a prerequisite for achievement. Thus, the
following research questions have guided the present study:
RQ1: How strongly does the adolescents’ mathematics self-concept in PISA 2022 relate to
their mathematics motivation?
RQ2: To what extent does the adolescents’ mathematics self-concept in PISA 2022 differentiate from their mathematics self-efﬁcacy measures?
RQ3: How well does the adolescents’ mathematics self-concept in PISA 2022 predict their
mathematics achievement?

Methods
Participants
A total of 4552 ﬁfteen-year-old and sixteen-year-old adolescents participated in PISA 2022 across
the United States. The average age was M = 15.83 (SD = 0.29). Of the participants, 49.1% selfreported their sex as female (N = 2235) whereas 50.8% reported their sex as male (N = 2312). 0.1%
chose not to report their sex (N = 5). English was the primary language of 76.76% of the
participants (N = 3494) while 18.67% reported another language as their primary language (N =
850). 4.57% chose not to report their primary language (N = 208).

Variables
The main variable of interest was mathematics self-concept which was measured with a single
item (“Mathematics is easy for me”), was calculated and used as an index variable and was coded
with two response categories (0 = No perception of mathematics as easier than other subjects; 1 =
Perception of mathematics as easier than other subjects). This index measures whether students
perceive mathematics as easier than Test Language and Science. Examinees were assigned a “1” if
they endorsed “Mathematics is easy for me” more strongly than both “Test language is easy for
me” and “Science is easy for me”; otherwise, they received a “0.” It reﬂects the relative ease of
mathematics compared to the other subjects, rather than their absolute perception of mathematics

ease which is captured by the original item response. This implementation process has been
determined and completed by PISA (OECD, 2024).
Mathematics motivation was also measured with a single item (“I want to do well in my
mathematics class”), was calculated and used as an index variable and was coded with two
response categories (0 = Not more motivated to do well in mathematics than other subjects; 1 =
More motivated to do well in mathematics than other subjects). This index measures whether
students are more motivated to succeed in mathematics than in Test Language and Science.
Students received a “1” if they endorsed “I want to do well in my mathematics class” more strongly
than both “I want to do well in my test language class” and “I want to do well in my science class”;
otherwise, they received a “0.” This index reﬂects students’ relative motivation for mathematics,
not their absolute motivation which is captured by the original item response. This implementation
process has been determined and completed by PISA (OECD, 2024).
Mathematics self-efﬁcacy in PISA 2022 was assessed with two separate composite variables:
(1) mathematics self-efﬁcacy: formal and applied mathematics, and (2) mathematics self-efﬁcacy:
mathematical reasoning and 21st century mathematics. First, mathematics self-efﬁcacy: formal
and applied mathematics (M = 0.44; SD = 1.15) was measured with nine items (e.g., “How
conﬁdent in math tasks: Working out from a [train timetable] how long it would take to get from
one place to another”) and with four response categories (1 = not at all conﬁdent to 4 = very
conﬁdent). Second, mathematics self-efﬁcacy: mathematical reasoning and 21st century mathematics (M = 0.08; SD = 1.00) was measured with 10 items (e.g., “How conﬁdent in math tasks:
Extracting mathematical information from diagrams, graphs, or simulations”) and with four
response categories (1 = not at all conﬁdent to 4 = very conﬁdent). These two composite variables
were created by using item response theory scaling (OECD, 2024).
Mathematics performance focused on participants’ ability to deduce from what they know and
implement their knowledge in different contexts (OECD, 2023; OECD, 2024). Mathematics
performance was included in the data as 10 sets of plausible values (Means ranged from M =
461.87; SD = 95.13 to M = 463.62; SD = 95.07) due to booklet assessment designs in PISA. Since
PISA assessments cannot capture all mathematics outcomes for every student due to time
constraints and the risk of student fatigue, missing data in mathematics achievement scores
occurred by design as different students received different assessment booklets. To address this,
PISA implemented an imputation strategy to generate plausible values as proxies of achievement
scores. In other words, plausible values are estimated scores derived through imputation based on
available data. Since plausible values are only estimations, it is a standard practice to use multiple
sets of plausible values (usually 5 to 10 sets) in secondary analysis for the results to be more
accurate. This implementation process has been determined and completed by PISA (OECD,
2024). In analysis using large-scale assessment data collected from a booklet design, such as
PISA, it is standard practice to use all sets of plausible values (Rutkowski et al., 2010).
All of the variable labels, the items that were used to create the variables and indices were
provided by the PISA. More information regarding the variables of interest in the present study
and plausible values can be found in the PISA 2022 Technical Report (OECD, 2024).

Data Analysis
The data used and analyzed for the present study were retrieved from the publicly available PISA
2022 data repository (see https://www.oecd.org/en/data/datasets/pisa-2022-database.html).
The proportions of the missing data ranged from 11.9% to 13.5% across the variables of the
present study. All missing data were reported as “no response” by PISA 2022, and systematic
missing-by-design does not apply to background questionnaire data. Therefore, prior to the

analysis, the missing data were imputed by using a fully conditional speciﬁcation method with a
single imputation and 10 iterations (Enders et al., 2018).
As aforementioned, the present study examined validity evidence based on relations to other
variables according to the Standards (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014). Concurrent evidence was
examined with a chi-squared (χ 2) test between mathematics self-concept and mathematics motivation along with phi coefﬁcient (φ) and Cramer’s V as the effect size measures. Discriminant
evidence was assessed with a point biserial correlation (rpb) between (1) mathematics self-concept
and mathematics self-efﬁcacy: formal and applied mathematics and between (2) mathematics selfconcept and mathematics self-efﬁcacy: mathematical reasoning and 21st century. The correlation
coefﬁcients were also interpreted as the respective effect sizes. Finally, predictive evidence was
tested with a multilevel modeling approach where mathematics self-concept was used to predict
mathematics performance. The between-cluster variance in multilevel modeling analysis was
taken into account by employing the replication weights (OECD, 2024). In the models, the
students were nested within the schools (e.g., OECD, 2009). Explicitly, the null or unconditional
model (i.e., the model with no predictors) comprised the mathematics performance randomly
varying across the schools (i.e., random intercepts). The conditional or random intercept model
reported the parameters where mathematics self-concept was used to predict mathematics
performance.
Sampling weights were used in all analyses to obtain precise parameters (OECD, 2024). The
analyses were performed in the International Database [IDB] Analyzer v5.0.32 (IEA, 2022) and
SPSS v25 (IBM, 2017).

Results
Chi-squared test results (Table 1) revealed a statistically signiﬁcant association between the
adolescents’ mathematics self-concept and their mathematics motivation (χ 2 = 86008.920, df = 1,
p < .001, φ = 0.153, Cramer’s V = 0.153), suggesting a moderate-to-strong effect size (Akoglu,
2018). This association indicates that the observed relationship between mathematics self-concept
and mathematics motivation is unlikely to be attributable to random variation under the null
hypothesis. The results showed that higher levels of mathematics self-concept were associated
with higher levels of mathematics motivation. This supported the concurrent validity evidence of
the mathematics self-concept scale, which could indicate that it effectively aligned with and was
indicative of related constructs within the PISA 2022 framework.
Subsequently, point biserial correlation results (Table 2) showed a signiﬁcant, negative but
negligible correlation between the adolescents’ mathematics self-concept and their mathematics
self-efﬁcacy: formal and applied mathematics (rpb = 0.078, p < .001). Similarly, there was also a
signiﬁcant, negative but negligible correlation between the adolescents’ mathematics self-concept
and their mathematics self-efﬁcacy: mathematical reasoning and 21st century (rpb = 0.073, p <
.001). These negative and signiﬁcant correlations with negligible effect sizes are practically 0
(zero), suggesting that mathematics self-concept was meaningfully distinct from the constructs
measured by (1) mathematics self-efﬁcacy: formal and applied mathematics and (2) mathematics
self-efﬁcacy: mathematical reasoning and 21st century. Speciﬁcally, the negligible correlation

effectiveness in predicting mathematics performance based on students’ mathematics selfconcept.

Discussion
The ﬁndings of the present study provided support for the concurrent, discriminant, and predictive
evidence of the mathematics self-concept scale within the context of PISA 2022 as validity
evidence based on relations to other variables (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014). These results have
signiﬁcant implications for educational research, practice and policy-making in mathematics
education of adolescents. In what follows, we discussed the ﬁndings of the present study with their
relevance to the previous research.
The signiﬁcant association between adolescents’ mathematics self-concept and mathematics
motivation supported the concurrent validity evidence of the mathematics self-concept scale.
Consistent with previous research (e.g., Hammoudi, 2019; Niemivirta et al., 2023), the positive
relationship between these constructs indicated that students who perceive themselves as more
competent in mathematics were also more motivated to succeed in mathematics. This indicated the
potential importance of fostering positive self-concepts in mathematics to potentially enhance
motivation which could in turn improve student outcomes (Skaalvik & Valås, 1999).
The signiﬁcant but negligible correlations between adolescents’ mathematics self-concept and
two mathematics self-efﬁcacy measures supported the discriminant validity evidence of the
mathematics self-concept scale. The ﬁndings highlighted the distinct nature of mathematics selfconcept and mathematics self-efﬁcacy as the negligible correlations suggested that these

The results also indicated that adolescents’ mathematics self-concept is a signiﬁcant predictor
of their mathematics performance. This supports the predictive validity evidence of the mathematics self-concept scale and was consistent with the self-enhancement theory which posits that
higher self-concept could lead to better academic achievement (e.g., Calsyn & Kenny, 1977;
Sewasew et al., 2018). The results suggested that students with a stronger self-concept in
mathematics tended to perform signiﬁcantly better on mathematics assessments. As such, to put
this into perspective, we found that a positive mathematics self-concept contributed to 20.20-point
increase in mathematics performance, which potentially could shift a student’s performance from
the median to a higher percentile given the mean range of M = 461.87 (SD = 95.13) to M = 463.62
(SD = 95.07). These ﬁndings point to the importance of fostering a positive self-concept in
mathematics to enhance students’ academic performance. This predictive relationship has
practical implications for educators since interventions aimed at improving mathematics selfconcept could potentially lead to improvements in students’ academic achievement. We discuss
these implications next.

Implications
Given the fact that mathematics self-concept is related to motivation and achievement, educators
could consider strategies to enhance adolescents’ self-perceptions in mathematics. However, the
directionality of this relationship remains uncertain; it is equally plausible that higher achievement
and motivation contribute to more positive self-concept. Therefore, holistic approaches could be
taken into account to address multiple factors that are related to students’ academic experiences.
Additionally, educators should be mindful of the distinction between self-concept and selfefﬁcacy when designing interventions to ensure that both perceptions of competence and speciﬁc
task-related conﬁdence are addressed. The signiﬁcant predictive relationship between mathematics self-concept and performance may suggest that self-concept measures could be valuable in
predictive models of academic achievement. Applied researchers could consider incorporating
self-concept into machine learning or other statistical models to predict student outcomes more
accurately grounded in the Standards (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014) to adhere to principles of
validity, reliability, fairness, and transparency in educational measurement. Incorporating
mathematics self-concept into predictive models should be theoretically and empirically justiﬁed
in order to ensure consistent and unbiased measurement across diverse student populations.
Models should be interpretable and ethically designed to support equitable educational outcomes.

Limitations and Future Directions
While this study provided evidence for the validity of the mathematics self-concept scale, it is not
without limitations. First, the use of single index item measures for some variables (e.g.,
mathematics motivation) may limit the depth of the data collected. Future studies should consider
employing multi-item scales to capture a more nuanced understanding of these constructs.
Additionally, the present study focused on a speciﬁc cycle of the PISA 2022 dataset, which may
limit the generalizability of the ﬁndings to other populations or educational contexts. Future

Conclusion
In conclusion, grounded in the Standards (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014), this study provides
support for the concurrent, discriminant, and predictive validity evidence of the mathematics selfconcept scale in PISA 2022 (i.e., evidence based on relations to other variables). These ﬁndings
highlight the importance of mathematics self-concept in relation to mathematics motivation and
mathematics performance and to what extent it is distinct from mathematics self-efﬁcacy
measures.
