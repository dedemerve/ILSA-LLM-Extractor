The main purpose of this paper is to identify the key factors that impact schools' academic performance and to
explore their relationships through a two-stage analysis based on a sample of Tunisian secondary schools. In the
first stage, we use the Directional Distance Function approach (DDF) to deal with undesirable outputs. The DDF
is estimated using Data Envelopment Analysis method (DEA). In the second stage we apply machine-learning
approaches (regression trees and random forests) to identify and visualize variables that are associated with a
high school performance. The data is extracted from the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA)
2012 survey. The first stage analysis shows that almost 22% of Tunisian schools are efficient and that they could
improve their students’ educational performance by 15.6% while using the same level of resources. Regression
trees findings indicate that the most important factors associated with higher performance are school size,
competition, class size, parental pressure and proportion of girls. Only, school location appears with no impact
on school efficiency. Random forests algorithm outcomes display that proportion of girls at school and school
size have the most powerful impact on the predictive accuracy of our model and hence could more influence
school efficiency. The findings disclose also the high non-linearity of the relationships between these key factors
and school performance and reveal the importance of modeling their interactions in influencing efficiency
scores.

1. Introduction
Over the last few years the Tunisian education system has witnessed
a decrease in its quality. Based on the last PISA survey conducted in
2015, the Huffington Post published an article in December 2016 with a
shocking title “Tunisia at the bottom of the PISA 2015 ranking: The
Tunisian school system in apnea”.2 In this article, two alarming information are delivered. First, Tunisia's rank is 65th out of a total of 70
countries and second Tunisia scores in mathematics, reading and science are well below the OECD average score. Statistics based on the
2009 and [1] PISA assessment confirm this statement by revealing
Tunisia among countries' tail end. Another issue classified as highly
important is the number of students that left the school without a diploma. The dropout phenomenon in Tunisia has increased considerably
since the 1980s. Indeed, the Ministry of Education has announced that
between 1981 and 2015, school dropouts reached 4,325,127 pupils,3

and for the year 2015, 106,000 pupils left school without obtaining a
diploma. This statistic reveals a heavy problem affecting the entire
education system with all its components.
These preceding figures may indicate less confidence in public
education system. To remedy this worrying situation, one may question
about strategies that should be adopted by the Tunisian Education
Ministry. Answering this question is not straightforward. We first need
to know about the various factors that are behind these phenomena. In
other words, policy makers should identify factors on which they may
act to positively influence school performance and to prevent from
dropout. Indeed, being aware of the diverse causative and predictive
factors will help policy makers developing adequate strategies and
prevention programs.

generally seek information to justify budget for results. Specifically,
they want to ensure that schools use efficiently the resources to improve

Studies exploring these factors for Tunisian schools are very rare in

Despite these adopted guidelines regarding the sizes of schools/
classes and the proportion of enrolled girls, the Tunisian education
system continues to be judged inefficient. This may raise concerns
about the validity of such measures. More specifically, it suggests
conducting more investigations to understand whether the powerlessness of such policy in influencing positively the education performance is due to the non-adoption of the right school/class sizes or
because of the need to act on several factors simultaneously. Definitely,
to undertake a successful restructuring, more explorations in identifying possible optimal school/class sizes, apprehending the interactions
between school features and education efficiency, and providing robust
indications about ‘what works’ to improve education performance are
required.
Studies aiming to identify the true reasons behind the poor performance of Tunisian secondary schools become of great interest. This
paper contributes to closing this gap by investigating the interaction
between different components of the educational system. Modeling an
educational system is well recognized to be very complex due to the
multiplicity of its components and their interactions with each other
(schools, students, parents, educators and principals); that is why, we
apply a machine-learning (ML) approach. ML algorithms are acknowledged to have the ability of visualizing and modeling complicated
system. They aim at uncovering hidden insights through learning from
trends in the used data to produce reliable decision and results.

At least two points distinguish this current study from earlier ones.
First, dissimilar to previous two-stage non-parametric modeling studies
in education, in the first stage we use a relatively new and not often
used approach; namely, the Directional Distance Function (DDF) that
allows us to bring together desirable (academic achievement) and undesirable (school dropout) outputs. Second, we apply regression trees
(RT) and random forests (RF) to model and visualize the interaction
between the different components of the educational system, as well as
to explore factors affecting schools performance. We apply our model to
data extracted from the PISA 2012 survey including variables related to
different levels of secondary education (students, class, school and
parents).
From a methodological perspective, the innovation of this paper is
twofold. The first contribution is in applying a DDF approach aiming to
assess schools efficiency in the presence of both desirable and undesirable outputs. Undeniably, the problem of dropouts in secondary
schools has become an increasing concern in Tunisia. The second
contribution is to provide an alternative to the traditional two-stage
approach, where the nonparametric efficiency scores obtained in the
first stage are regressed in the second stage on covariates.
Unfortunately, this approach has serious drawbacks making the secondstage baseless (more details are given in Ref. [3]). Moreover, unlike
classical regression approaches, this methodology succeeds to reveal
the non-linear interactions among key components and to provide more
accurate comprehensions of the complexity of the education production
function.
The remainder of the paper is structered as follows. Section 2 provides a brief literature review. Section 3 presents and justifies the
theoretical framework of the applied methodology. Section 4 describes
the data used in the analysis. Section 5 presents and discusses the
emprical results. Finally, the article winds up with the main findings
and implications of this research.
2. Literature review
An impressive number of papers dealing with the evaluation of the
efficiency of educational institutions have been developed. Barra and
Zotti [4], Alabdulmenem [5], Essid et al. [6], Nazarko and Šaparauskas
[7], Blackburn et al. [8], and Podinovski et al. [9] are some examples
among many others. Some interresting and relevant literature reviews
include De Witte and López-Torres [10], Johnes [11], and Thanassoulis
et al. [12]. Roughly, De Witte and López-Torres [10] provide an extensive overview of the literature on education efficiency. Johnes [11]
offers more research applications of DEA in different sectors of education. Thanassoulis et al. [12] examine applications of DEA in secondary
and tertiary education.
These previous papers may be classified on two groups. The first one
exclusively focuses on efficiency assessment, however the second group
suggests more investigation by providing another stage of analysis to
identify relevant variables that are likely to explain the obtained efficiency values. Numerous approaches and different inputs/outputs
conbinations have been adopted and applied to various international
education systems to evaluate the academic efficiency. As discussed by
Ben Yahia et al. [13], it should be noted that most of these previous
studies have only considered desirable inputs/outputs in their efficiency assessement process. Particularly, they did not cope with the
dropout problem (undesirable output) when effeciency is evaluated. It
is only recently that few researches start dealing with this phenomenon
in their assessments. Also, it appears that the most popular approach
allowing to handle simultaneously undesirable with desirable inputs/
outputs is the Directional Distance Function (DDF) methodology [14].
Nevertheless, in spite of its simplicity and intuitiveness, the adoption of
the DDF approach remains limited in number.
Furthermore, the second kind of studies reveals the wide use of the
two-stage DEA in spite of the sharp debate about its statistical foundation [3]. Banker and Natarjan [15] criticize the two-stage double

[21] propose to use ML method to analyze the determinants of students'

first run multilevel regression trees to estimate school value-added.
Then, they relate the estimated school value-added to school level
variables by means of regression trees and boosting. Chung and Lee
[22] apply the random forests to predict students at risk of dropping out
using samples of high school students from the 2014 National Education Information System (NEIS) in Korea. Their predictive model
showed an excellent performance in predicting students' dropout in
terms of various metrics for binary classification. Schiltz et al. [23]
show that contrarily to classical regression approaches ML manages to
reveal pertinent non-linear interactions. By adopting a two-stage
methodology, they first construct a measure of school value-added
applying DEA model using prior achievement in mathematics, reading
and socio-economic background as inputs and students' performance as
outputs, and then they use ML approach. Sanzana et al. [24] apply RF,
classification, and regression trees to show that parents' educationalexpectation, the school type, and the index of mathematical ability
impact the students' academic performance on mathematics. Thomas
and Galambos [25] apply regression and decision tree to investigate
how students' characteristics and experiences affect their satisfaction.
They find that faculty preparedness is the principal determinant of
students' satisfaction. Cortez and Silva [26] apply four Data Mining
methods; namely, decision trees, RF, neural networks, and support
vector machines to link Portuguese students' scores in mathematics and
in reading to students' characteristics. They find that student's
achievement is highly influenced by past evaluations, in addition to
other relevant features such as school related demographic and social
variables. Ma [27] develops as well a two-stage approach. In the first
stage, the rate of growth in mathematics achievements of each student
is estimated by employing hierarchical linear model. In the second
stage, classification and regression trees are applied to relate the estimated rate to students' characteristics.
As argued by James et al. [28], ML algorithms are very usueful in
uncovering complex interactive effects among explanatory factors. In
addition they hold a great potential for researchers and policymakers to
correctly identify and model interaction effects. Recently, ML has
contributed in the development of several areas. For instance, it has
been used to predict bankruptcy and default events [29] and to forecast
cases of child abuse in public health [30]. It has been as well used in
studying the wastewater engineering [31] and applied to solve some
health community problems [32]. ML methods include a wide variety
of algorithms particularly suited for prediction, including decision trees
(classification and regression trees). According to Schiltz et al. [23],
these algorithms have been less frequently used in education studies
compared to regression methods despite the fact that the latter have
failed to identify relevant non-linear interactions between the response
and its predictors.
The above discussion raises an attractive call for more investigations
at least on two critical issues. Specifically, it advocates the importance

of considering the dropout problem in the efficiency assessment process. It supports as well the need for more explorations to discover and
understand the interactions between different education actors and
school performance in order to highlight the relative importance of
each one and hence to extract some useful education policies.
3. Methodology
We propose a two-step methodology. We first assess education efficiency using DDF approach then we apply ML techniques to explore
the key factors influencing students’ achievements.
To assess efficiency, two principal methodologies are used either the
parametric ones based on econometric techniques or the non-parametric modes based on linear programming. The two approaches presume different assumptions. Their main dissimilarities generate their
respective strengths and weaknesses. The non-parametric approach
does not require inputs-outputs relationships in algebraic or functional
forms, which is very useful when there is little a priori information
about the production process. DEA is the most used method among the
non-parametric ones. Forsund et al. [33] provide an extensive discussion of the two methodologies. Since one of the objectives of this paper
is to consider the dropout problem (undesirable output) in the school
efficiency assessment process, we adopt in the first step a DDF approach. In fact, it is acknowledged that the most commonly used approach including undesirable aspects in a DEA framework is the DDF
methodology. It is an approach that permits managing simultaneously
undesirable and desirable inputs/outputs as recognized by Chambers
et al. (1998).
In the second step, to discover the crucial factors impacting the
secondary schools achievements, we adopt ML approaches to overcome
the limitations of the regression methods. Indeed, ML permits to remedy the major weakness of regressions techniques. It lets data enlighten the relationships between the endogenous variable and the
exogenous ones without any prior assumption. Specifically, it allows
apprehending the complex relationships between the secondary schools
performance and exogenous factors. Specifically, ML algorithms have
been able to map non-linear relationships unlike linear regression
models.

3.2.2. Random forests
In order to improve the predictive performance of the regression
tree, we apply RF using R package. RF consists on taking bootstrapped
samples from the population and then building a number of RT on the
bootstrapped training sets and hence averaging the resulting predictions. The first algorithm for random decision forests was developed by
Ho [37]. Then, an extension of this algorithm was shaped by Breiman
[38]. RF method has many advantages compared to other statistical
techniques. In particular, it is characterized by its very high accuracy
and its high capacity to model complex interactions among predictors.
To ensure the non-correlation of the trees, each time a split in a tree
is considered, a random sample of m predictors is chosen as split candidates from the full set of p predictors. Generally, the number m is
approximately equal to the total number of predictors divided by three
i.e. m = p/3 (rounded down). Moreover, the number of developed trees
should not be too small to ensure that every input row gets predicted at
least a few times. Liaw and Wiener [39] provide a detailed documentation on RF package in R. To build a RF, we follow the three

Another valuable application of RF is to rank or to measure the
importance of predictors. The technique was described in Breiman [38]
as follows:
[1] Fit a RF to the data set. During the fitting process the out-of-bag
error (OOB)6 for each data point is recorded and averaged over the
forests.
[2] To measure the importance of a variable j after training, the values
of the jth variable are permuted among the training data and the
OOB is again computed on this perturbed data set.
[3] The importance score for the jth variable is computed by averaging
the difference in OOB before and after the permutation over all
trees. The score is normalized by the standard deviation of these
differences.
[4] Variables producing large values for this score are ranked to be
more important than the other variables.
4. Data
As in many other real-life problems, the major matter when applying DEA in education is the choice of relevant inputs and outputs. It
should be noted that the choice of inputs and outputs limits the interpretability of the model. The role of the schooling system is to modify
students’ capacity. It takes the students at a very young age and it
makes them different at the end of the process. Educational outputs are
the immediate effects on students in terms of knowledge acquisition,
beliefs, and skills. The measurement of these outputs is very complex.

topics in the literature. Indeed, many papers show negative relationships, others exhibit positive ones, others support the idea of an optimal
school size, and others still display that they are not connected (more
details are given in Masci [43,45]. Our empirical findings (Fig. 1) shows

however, a smaller school has an average predicted inefficiency score of

outcomes. By limiting ourselves to this outcome, one is tempted to
conclude that school size appears to have a positive effect on Tunisian
students' achievement as pointed by Essid et al. [2]. Nevertheless, further analysis of the findings shows that students in smaller schools (less
than 622 enrolled) with a percentage of girls under 49.2% outperform
those in larger schools with an average inefficiency score equal to
0.0987 against an average predicted inefficiency of 0.135. Moreover,
smaller schools (less than 622 enrolled) with a proportion of girls between 49.2% and 55% have almost the same average inefficiency level
(0.134) as that of the larger schools. These empirical results imply that
in Tunisia the relationship between secondary schools size and students'
achievement is non-linear. Furthermore, it is plausible to conclude that
there is no a single optimal size, but there are rather favorable sizes
depending on various interactions between different covariates. Our
findings are consistent with those obtained by Giambona and Porcu
[46]. Indeed, applying a multilevel random intercept model and using
2012 PISA data for Italian schools, the authors have exhibited different
possible relationship between school size and students' achievement
depending on macro-area of residence of students, the community size
where the school is located, and the availability or not of other schools
in the area. The implications of these last results confirm the need for
policy makers to take into account various interactions among different
covariates in order to decide the optimal sizes of schools to improve
students’ achievement.
Outcomes regarding the 70 largest schools (more than 622 students)
show that competition has the most important effect on students’
achievement. Indeed, regression tree reveals that schools operating in
low competition area are linked with lower inefficiency on average
(0.0817 against 0.16). In other words, a non-competitive environment
is a stimulus to perform better for large schools. This negative effect
goes against what it is widely presumed regarding the power of competition in stimulating schools to improve their outcomes. Bukowski
and Kobus [47] provide as well evidence for the negative impact on the
performance of urban public schools in Poland. For their part, Gajardo
and Grau [48] assert the absence of any consensus about the effects of
school competition on educational achievement. The negative effect
can be justified at least by two possible facts qualified by Hsieh and
Urquiola [49] as being difficult to disentangle. It may be either due to
the migration of clever students, or to the decline of school productivity. Our findings suggest that competition is only relevant in the
case of large schools. From Fig. 1, it appears that competition has no

5.2. Predictive performance
As introduced earlier, among the main objectives of this paper is to
demonstrate the ability of ML models to track down the relevant factors
linked to the success of secondary school students, to identify different
interactions between these factors as well as to visualize the non-linearity of these interactions in order to better fit the policies to be proposed by the decision-makers. In the following we develop two ML
models RT and RF. Nevertheless, in order to test the robustness of our
findings, we briefly include in the next sub-section the outputs of two
linear regression models Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) and Tobit
models.

the set of the selected factors (school location, parental pressure,
competition, proportion of girls at school, school size and class size), we
build a regression tree of which the results are displayed graphically in

on the node being true and the right one is conditional on the node
being false. The numbers at the branches bottoms indicate the mean of
the inefficiency scores for various subsets of schools. Fig. 1 provides
several valuable insights for educators and policymakers.
First of all, it displays that only “School location” variable does not
emerge in the tree. This result is somewhat unexpected. In fact, it is

From regression tree, it emerges also that the best students' outcomes come from schools operating in free competitive environments

remaining schools that have not experienced any similar pressure display double inefficiency (from 5% to 12%). This substantial difference
in inefficiency scores may indicate that parental pressure has a powerful influence on schools' performance operating in a non-competitive
environment. In accordance with many previous studies, such as Otani

involvement in adolescents' education. Nevertheless, our results reveal
that parental pressure seems to be particularly important in case of
large schools operating in free-competition environments. This outcome
may indicate that developing policies to encourage parental involvement for such schools would significantly improve their students’ performance.
Furthermore, in a competitive environment the findings indicate
that large schools may achieve better performance through combined
mechanism of some factors. More specifically, adjusting class size and
proportion of girls at school may contribute to upgrading students’
achievement. Indeed, Fig. 1 displays that the 38 large secondary schools
operating in a competitive environment and having medium class sizes
less than 35.5 students record an inefficiency average rate equal to
0.142, whereas the remaining 10 schools with medium class sizes
greater than 35.5 students show a higher average inefficiency score of
0.226. This finding confirms those obtained by some earlier studies
such as Smeyers [52], Krueger [53], Finn et al. [54], and Nye [55]. This
result may be justified by the fact that smaller classes offer better

disciplinary climate, create a more positive learning environment, and
then more effective teaching may be provided. According to Fig. 1,
among the beforehand mentioned 38 large schools, 29 reach further
improvement by combining the effect of small size classes with the
enrollment of more girls than boys (more than 51.4%). These later
schools have been able to reduce their average inefficiency score to
0.12 against an average inefficiency score of 0.214 for the 9 remaining
schools.
When it comes to smaller schools (enrolling less than 622 students),
girls' percentage variable becomes again a relevant factor. Indeed, the
right side of the regression tree (Fig. 1) shows that among these schools,
those enrolling fewer girls than boys (less than 49.2%) are more efficient than the others. In more details, 8 secondary schools achieve an
average inefficiency score equal to 0.0987; however, the remaining 27
secondary schools realize an average inefficiency score of 0.226. Note
that these last schools display a similar average performance level as
that of larger schools adopting a medium class size greater or equal to
35.5 and operating in a competition context. A deeper analysis for these
less efficient small schools exhibits as well that the girls’ percentage
variable continues to be pertinent. Specifically, schools enrolling a
percentage of girls between 49.2% and 55% have an average inefficiency score of 0.325, while those having a percentage of girls more
than 55% record a relatively better inefficiency score of 0.134. To sum
up, the regression tree reveals that in the case of small-size schools, the
only factor that interacts with schools performance is the percentage of
girls at the school. In more details, the best performance is reached
when this percentage is less than 49.2% and the lowest one is achieved
when it increases until a threshold of less than 55%. Beyond that (more
than 55%), the performance of schools is in between.

5.2.2. Random forests
In order to validate the soundness of the regression tree findings, we
suggest performing RF approach. RF algorithm provides an improved
accuracy about the importance of predictor variables in predicting the
outcome. It offers as well the possibility to plot and rank explanatory
variables according to their importance. More specifically, we construct
a forest made up of 1000 trees (ntree = 1000) using at each split a
number of predictor variables equal to m = p/3 (where p equals 6; the
total number of the selected predictors). Fig. 2 shows the part of
variability caught by each predictor in a school performance.
First, the figure confirms the high importance of school size in influencing student outcomes. Indeed, it has already been identified as
the first variable to split the root node (on the top of the tree). In addition, although it does not appear at the first level of the tree, the
proportion of girls at school has been ranked as the most important
factor that explains the variability of school performance. This result
may be justified by the fact that this variable emerges at many levels of
the tree; namely, the second, third and forth levels (Fig. 1). Moreover,
the class size is identified as being the third power affecting students’
achievement. Finally, according to RF model, the rest of variables; explicitly, competition, school location, and parental pressure, seems to
have relatively less power in informing about the variability in school
efficiency. In other words, these findings indicate that if we drop one of
the three top variables, the prediction power of our model will significantly be reduced. However, if we remove one of the bottom variables, there might not be much impact on the predictive power of the
model. Accordingly, we may deduce that Tunisian secondary school
performance is mainly associated to the school characteristics more
than its environment.

proportion of girls. In contrast, pressure from parents on schools has

5.3. Policy implications
These previous findings may provide valuable information for
educators and policymakers. They constitute a good start up to develop
intuitive interpretations and to elaborate appropriate strategies assisting the Tunisian secondary students in improving their performance.
A common first implication concerns the contrasting trends of previous
empirical studies showing negative, positive or no correlations between
some key factors and schools’ academic performance. Here, we underline a main rationalization for such inconsistencies based on correlations resulting from the joint effects of covariates. In other words,
several mechanisms acting at diverse hierarchical levels may mutually
establish associations between different covariates. Indeed, the predominant approach adopted in the aforementioned studies has been to
use linear regression models and then to judge the statistical significance of the obtained coefficients while implicitly assuming the
causal independence between multiple mechanisms. Hence, we highlight the importance of recognizing the hierarchical changes in covariates and their correlations.
Although our sample is relatively small and hence permit identifying a part of the mechanisms at play that should be interpreted with
caution, our findings still reveal some interesting and specific implications allowing researchers and policy makers to derive some possible suggestions in order to develop remedies to the stated problems at
the beginning of the paper regarding the worrying situation of the
Tunisian secondary schools.
A first specific implication is to stop assuming the linearity of the
relationships between Tunisian secondary students’ achievement and
10

Socio-Economic Planning Sciences 70 (2020) 100724

some exogenous factors. Indeed, the results bear out the non-linearity of
the relationships between the performance of secondary schools and
each of the following factors: school size, class size, proportion of girls,
competition, and parent pressure. Hence, educators and policy makers
should impede advancing strategies based on wrong hypotheses.
A second specific implication concerns the selected factors that are
related to school characteristics and to school environment. First of all,
this study reveals that almost all selected predictors may play substantial roles in stimulating academic performance of Tunisian secondary school students. Moreover, empirical findings confirm the existence of mechanisms between secondary schools performance and all
the selected factors except location. Nevertheless, since our data mostly
correspond to schools located in urban areas, we cannot confirm the
lack of influence of school location on students' performance.
Furthermore, empirical results indicate that both school size and proportion of girls at school are the factors that most influence students’
performance. Undeniably, further investigations are required to fully
detect the mechanisms at play.
A third specific implication encompasses a more in-depth look at the
current situation of Tunisian schools in terms of organization and environment as well as proposals for possible solutions. Results reveal that
larger schools tend to be more efficient than smaller ones. Nevertheless,
size by itself is not enough to ensure efficiency. Statistics corroborate
this conclusion. Indeed, in spite of adopting large sizes for Tunisian
schools (an average of 634 students per school in 2015), students’
achievement still to be low. Regarding class sizes, outcomes advocate
the existence of a range of average class sizes between 25 and 32.5
students that may help students to be more efficient. Once again, statistics reveal that decision-makers appear to have made a good decision
in this direction (an average of 25.5 pupils per class in 2015).
In contrast to class size factor, findings have let emerge the existence of a range of possible proportions of girls enrolled at school
(between 49.2% and 55%) that may contribute to the production of
nonperforming students. Nevertheless, unlike the class/school sizes, the
statistics exhibit that the adopted average percentage of girls enrolled at
school seems to more likely disfavor students' achievement (54% in
2015). In fact, schools with higher proportions of girls show better
results. This does not mean that one must eliminate boys from schools,
but this is a matter of public policy. Indeed, each situation of nonperforming school must be handled in an appropriate way. For instance,
let's consider the case of the worst performing 13 schools disclosed in
Fig. 1 (an average inefficiency level of 0.325), for which the proportions
of girls range between 49.2% and 55%. Theoretically, there are at least
two possible remedies that the supervisory authority may adopt. Carrying out either mergers or splits of some of these schools, so that the
proportions of girls become more advantageous from a performance
perspective. Note that according to our findings, it seems much better to
have small schools with a high proportion of boys (greater than 0.508)
than having schools with proportions of girls between 49.2% and 55%.
It should be noted that if the merger policy is adopted, decision-makers
should have an eye on the possible interferences with other factors in
case of a large school.
Finally, two more factors appear to be as well significant; that is,
competition and parental pressure. Both factors are categorical.
Empirical findings display an unexpected effect on students' efficiency
from competition factor. Accordingly, competition seems to be counterincentive to students' performance. However, parental pressure reveals the desired effect on students' achievement. More specifically, it
seems that only in the case of large schools that the competition factor
affects students’ performance. Nevertheless, a more in-depth analysis of
the empirical results divulges that the Tunisian Ministry of Education
has adopted a mistaken strategy of at least 48 schools by encouraging

them to operate in a competition context. In order to improve the
performance of these schools, the supervisory authority may act in the
direction of alleviating competition. Two policies may be adopted. The
first is to promote either fusion between schools or removing some of
them from the corresponding area. In parallel, developing policies to
boost parental involvement on academic standards is required. The
second is to split these schools on smaller ones with adequate proportions of enrolled girls. However, if it proves to be difficult to eliminate
competition, it would be necessary to ensure small sizes for the classes
of the schools (between 25 and 32.5 students). Regardless of the
adopted strategy, to improve the efficiencies of schools, educators and
policy makers should manage closely possible interactions between
different covariates and act often enough at the same time on many
relevant predictors.
To sum up, we conjecture that to identify suitable policies, it is
necessary to identify the main factors that can be associated with better
student outcomes, on which policy makers may act, but it is also essential to confront the problem from many points of views. In other
words, to meet the legitimate expectations of the society, the main
stakeholders, without whom any reform project would be doomed to
failure, should be involved.
6. Conclusion
Our paper fits to the increasing literature on the performance evaluation of public secondary schools and on the identification of direct
and indirect factors upon which policy makers can act in order to improve the quality of education. Specifically, in this study we assess and
analyze the efficiency of secondary schools via a two-stage process. We
choose to apply in a first stage a DDF approach using DEA method in
order to account for the undesirable output the school dropout rate in
the estimation of schools efficiency. Then, in order to raise public
awareness about the issue of inefficiencies in education, we use in the
second stage a ML approach through two powerful algorithms RT and
RF to identify and visualize variables that are associated with a high
school performance.
This paper offers some main contributions. First, it provides an interesting methodological one. To our knowledge, it is the first time that
both the DDF and the ML approaches are adopted in a two-stage
method. Second, the paper helps in clarifying the highly debated contradictory results obtained in previous two-stage studies about the interactions between efficiency scores and some exogenous variables that
traditional linear regression cannot reveal. Indeed, the paper confirms
the non-linearity of the relationships between school efficiency and
some of its characteristics and/or environment components and enlightens possible opposing connections. Third, the paper provides more
than an explanation of these interactions. It shows the hierarchical
structure of covariates correlations. It underlines the ability of ML to
capture the manner in which multiple mechanisms jointly shape associations between secondary schools performance and some relevant
factors. Forth, the paper delivers a valuable management tool for decision-makers. Indeed, the empirical outcomes provide robust indications about “what works” to improve education performance and suggest precious information on how to design adequate policies able to
provide some valuable answers to the raised problems at the introduction.
Finally, while this paper provides some investigations on the impact
of some school characteristics and environment factors on school performance, more research and explorations are needed to fully understand the mechanisms at play and then, to undertake a successful restructuring of the Tunisian education system. One avenue for future
research is to take advantage from the ability of ML to handle a huge

variables.
