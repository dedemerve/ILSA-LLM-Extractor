This study investigates the extent of inequitable teacher sorting across educational systems as well
as relevant institutional correlates. We use data from the OECD TALIS study from 2018 with a
total participating 144,316 teachers, 9063 schools across 46 education systems. We first estimate
the relationship between classroom socioeconomic composition and teacher qualifications within
each participating educational system. Next, using a three-level hierarchical generalized linear
model, we examine whether the relationship between classroom socioeconomic composition and
teacher qualifications varies as a function of institutional features via a random slope regressed on
school system-level stratification, accountability, autonomy, and competition, controlling for
national levels of economic development and teacher shortages. Results show that crossclassroom sorting by specialization is more prominent in more economically developed systems. Between-school tracking, performance-data based school accountability as well as higher
levels of school autonomy over staffing and school competition were associated with more pronounced socioeconomic teacher sorting, but the results show that institutional determinants do
not generalize easily across different measurements of teacher competence or sorting across
schools.

1. Introduction
In recent years, the inequitable distribution of teacher quality has come into focus internationally as a potentially unexplored
determinant of the socioeconomic achievement gap (OECD, 2018a, 2018b). Inequitable teacher sorting (also referred to as the ‘teacher
quality opportunity gap’) refers to the phenomenon in which pupils from more socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds receive
teachers with lower competence levels, often measured by fewer years of experience, lower education levels, or a lack of subject matter
specialization or pedagogical certification. Despite a long empirical history in the USA (Morgan & McPartland, 1981; Clotfelter, Ladd &
Vigdor, 2005; Goldhaber, Quince & Theobald, 2018), cross-national research on the topic is scarce, with less than a handful of studies
showing mixed results (Luschei & Jeong, 2019; Han, 2018). One of the core purposes of education is to promote equal educational
opportunities and to allow for social and inter-generational mobility (Hanushek & Woessmann, 2015; van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 2010).
Indeed, schools are sometimes referred to as the ‘great equalizer’ (Downey & von Hippel, 2004). Despite this, several decades of

sociological work in education have argued that educational systems tend to reinforce social inequities already present in societies
(Bourdieu, 1977; Coleman et al., 1966). The segmentation of teacher competence across socioeconomic groups is one key way in which
in which educational systems may reproduce social inequality. Evidence displays persistent or growing inequities even in the most
egalitarian societies in the world, such as Sweden and Norway (Bonesrønning, Falch, & Strøm, 2005; Hansson & Gustafsson, 2016).
The proposed mechanisms of teacher sorting are many. Teachers tend to self-select into the most ‘attractive’ schools with better
working conditions, which tend to be those with a high proportion of students with high socioeconomic status (SES). Teaching
conditions in low-SES schools (or lower-SES classrooms in more heterogeneous schools) are likely to be more challenging due to a
lower level of home educational support for the students, school climate, and compositional or peer effects (Kalogrides & Loeb, 2013;
Lazear, 2001). Teachers care more about working conditions than salary, and past research has shown that salary increases have just a
small effect on reducing teacher turnover rates in hard-to-staff settings (Bacolod, 2007; Clotfelter, Lennie, Ladd & Vigdor, 2008).
However, novice teachers may be more likely to be more ideologically motivated to make a difference in the lives of certain children,
and therefore seek out work in socioeconomically disadvantaged settings (Sims & Allen, 2018).
These sorting mechanisms based on preferences on the part of teachers are coupled with labor supply and demand (Engel, Curran &
Finch, 2014; Kraft, Marinell & Yee, 2016; Allen, Burgess, & Mayo, 2017; Sims & Allen, 2018). Teacher hiring practices tend to be based
on teacher credentials as well as governance structures such as the extent of school autonomy over hiring in a school system or region
(Kang & Hong, 2008; Engel & Finch, 2014). Within schools, the extent of teacher self-selection based on seniority or credentials may
vary, but teachers with the most seniority may leverage their positions to teach in certain classrooms (Grissom, Kalogrides & Loeb,
2015; Kalogrides et al., 2013). Last, school leaders may assign better or worse teachers to classrooms based on a host of factors having
to do with the market position of a school, such as parental preferences or accountability mechanisms (Dieterle, Guarino, Reckase, &
Wooldridge, 2014; Feng, Figlio, & Sass, 2018; Hanushek & Rivkin, 2010).
Institutional and policy-level features may therefore interact with these sorting mechanisms on the part of school leaders and
teachers in a variety of ways. First, policies which further stratify and segregate students by socioeconomic lines are likely to see such
movements mirrored by teacher competence (Jackson, 2009). Past reviews have shown that higher levels of stratification, choice and
decentralization in a school system tend to be associated with inequity in student outcomes and increased school segregation due to
increased possibilities for opportunistic decision-making on the part of school leaders and families (Strietholt et al., 2019; Strello,
Strietholt, Steinmann, & Siepmann, 2021), and this line of reasoning may extend to teachers. While increased accountability and
monitoring may go hand in hand with increases in autonomy (Brill, Grayson, Kuhn, & O’Donnell, 2018), stringent monitoring and
appraisal practices may place undue stress on teachers (Jerrim & Sims, 2021), or introduce alternative incentives for classroom
assignment (Boyd, Lankford, Loeb, Rockoff, & Wyckoff, 2008).
A small number of cross-nationally focused studies have investigated the institutional correlates of teacher sorting (Han, 2018;
Luschei & Jeong, 2019) with no clear consensus. As such, there is a need to further explore institutional and policy-relevant features
using different teacher qualifications and across different educational settings. Because of the lack of variation of many institutional
features within educational systems, international large-scale assessments such as the OECD Teaching and learning International
Survey (TALIS) study allow researchers to identify ‘systematic heterogeneity in effects that differ across countries’ (Hanushek &
Woessmann, 2014, p. 148). Additionally, lower-level analyses are prone to selection effects by parents and teachers (i.e., better
teachers seeking out schools with more autonomy). Such selection effects are bypassed when indicators are aggregated to the country
level (Hanushek & Woessmann, 2014). The TALIS study is uniquely positioned to undertake questions related to sorting, due to its
systematic sampling of schools and teachers across a wide range of educational systems. Using TALIS data from 2018 and 46 education
systems, we address the following research questions.
(1) To what extent are classrooms with a higher concentration of low-SES students more likely to receive teachers with lower
qualifications in mathematics and science?
(2) Does the relationship between teacher qualifications and classroom socioeconomic composition vary according to system-level
characteristics, such as school accountability, autonomy, competition, and stratification?
(3) Do these results vary according to different model specifications such as the subject matter of teachers or when analyses are
conducted across schools?
1.1. Measuring teacher sorting
There is mixed evidence as to whether sorting within- or across-schools is more prevalent (Hanushek et al., 2005; Kalogrides et al.,
2013; Luschei & Jeong, 2019; Sims & Allen, 2018). Luschei and Jeong (2019) note that within-school sorting is more prevalent in more
economically developed countries. Despite this, most studies examining inequitable teacher allocation do so by examining differential
exposure rates to lower qualified teachers for high- and low-SES students or students across socioeconomic school contexts. National
studies from the USA have used such measures alongside value-added measures of teacher effectiveness across many conceptualizations of social and economic disadvantage (Adamson & Darling-Hammond, 2012; Clotfelter et al, 2005; Clotfelter et al, 2008;
Darling-Hammond, 2004; Goldhaber et al, 2015; Goldhaber et al, 2018; Kalogrides & Loeb, 2013; Lankford, Loeb & Wyckoff, 2002).
Other studies model the likelihood of receiving an underqualified teacher based on student socioeconomic status, such as the work of
Sims & Allen (2018) in England. In one of the only cross-national studies on the topic, Luschei & Jeong (2019) use this measure in
reverse and examine classroom and school socioeconomic composition as a function of teacher characteristics using data from the
Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) in 2013. Luschei & Jeong (2019) also use the intra-class correlation coefficient of

1.2. Measuring teacher competence
An empirical challenge related to teacher sorting involves the measurement of teacher competence. What constitutes a ‘good’
teacher, and how can we identify them across educational settings? Thus far, there remains considerable debate as to how we define
and measure teacher and teaching quality (Hanushek & Rivkin, 2012; Jackson, Rockoff & Staiger, 2014). Despite this, teachers have
been highlighted in the literature as crucial for student outcomes, particularly for disadvantaged students (Clotfelter et al., 2007;
Darling-Hammond, 2000; Goe, 2007; Rivkin, Hanushek, & Kain, 2005; Nye, Konstantopoulos, & Hedges, 2004).
There is limited evidence as to the importance of behavioural teacher characteristics such as their self-efficacy and preparation to
teach (Goe, 2007). In the context of teacher sorting, such characteristics are difficult to interpret, as teaching behaviours and practices
are likely influenced by the classroom environment (Thrupp, 1999). The focus of the study is therefore on teacher qualifications.
Perhaps the most frequently cited qualification is years of teacher experience. By now, there is substantial evidence of a ‘learning-by-doing’ effect, where teacher effectiveness dramatically increases after the first few years on the job (Rice, 2003; Rockoff, 2004).
There is debate as to whether these returns to student achievement level off after the first 3, 5, 10, or even 19 years, however (Papay &
Kraft, 2016; Toropova et al., 2019). Still other studies call into question the primacy of the experience variable (Isenberg, Max,
Gleason, & Deutsch, 2021; Sancassani, 2021). Another important teacher characteristic is subject matter specialization, particularly in
mathematics and science (Darling-Hammond, 2000; Goe, 2007; Sancassani, 2021), and is likely associated with the degree of content
and pedagogical content knowledge a teacher has (Nilsen et al., 2018; Baumert et al., 2010). We therefore consider experience and
mathematics educational specialization as some of the most important and basic indicators of teacher competence, and are two of the
teacher qualifications without which we assume to be associated with lower teaching competence based on past research1 (Nilsen
et al., 2018; Baumert et al., 2010). Research has shown that countries display heterogeneity in sorting magnitude depending on the
qualification in focus (Luschei & Jeong, 2019; Glassow & Jerrim, 2022), and we therefore do not investigate a composite measure of
teachers.
1.3. Institutional determinants of teacher sorting
The analysis also considers how educational and institutional context (stratification, autonomy, accountability, and competition)
may play a role in the production of inequalities in access to teacher competence.
1.3.1. School stratification
The considerable research on both between- and within-school stratification and its effect on student inequity allow us to make
some relatively straightforward predictions about how it will relate to teacher sorting (Burger, 2019; Parker et al., 2016; Strello,
Strietholt, Steinmann, & Siepmann, 2021). Both between-school tracking (academic vs. vocational) and within- school tracking
(ability grouping between classes) has clearly and consistently been shown to increase performance gaps between low- and high-SES
students as well as other forms of social inequity (Strello, Strietholt, Steinmann, & Siepmann, 2021). Although students in academic vs.
vocational tracked systems tend to be more socially segregated, teacher sorting according to socioeconomic status within schools
necessarily requires more socioeconomically heterogeneous student bodies. A positive relationship between within-school ability
grouping and cross-classroom teacher sorting is therefore expected, and a negative relationship between cross-school sorting. The
mixed findings of past research (Luschei & Jeong, 2019) highlight the need for a closer look at this issue.
1.3.2. School autonomy
One of the most frequently cited determinants of teacher sorting is school autonomy (Kang and Hong, 2008; Han, 2018; Luschei &
Jeong, 2019). The primary justification for decentralization is that principals and other local decision makers may be better able to
determine how to best meet the needs of their schools and address the challenges unique to their students (Hanushek et al., 2013). The
opposing argument posits that schools may have opportunistic motivations and therefore a more central management is favorable. Past
research shows that more centralized autonomy tends to be better for student outcomes in low- and middle- income countries, while in
high-income countries higher school autonomy shows positive effects for student achievement (Hanushek et al., 2013). It is widely
claimed that a highly centralized teacher allocation policy in South Korea is responsible for the more equitable distribution of teachers
in the country (Kang & Hong, 2008; Han, 2018; Luschei & Jeong, 2019). However, some studies still find pronounced inequity in the

1.3.3. School accountability
In relation to school accountability, Boyd et al. (2008) note that if incentives based on cut-off scores are introduced, school leaders
may be less inclined to allocate the best teachers to low-achieving classes and students (who are often those in situations of socioeconomic disadvantage) because they are deemed less likely to achieve those higher grades. In addition, countries are displaying a
growing trend towards increased accountability practices which is correlated with decreases in teacher job dissatisfaction (Jerrim &
Sims, 2021). According to Hanushek and Rivkin (2012), increased accountability measures may influence teachers to exit the profession more rapidly, particularly those teaching in more challenging settings. This finding is confirmed by Feng, Figlio, & Sass (2018).
However, Luschei and Jeong (2019) find that results-based accountability has a protective effect on teacher sorting within schools, but
that central examinations are associated with increased inequity. Such conflicting findings make it difficult to predict how such
accountability mechanisms may relate to teacher sorting, and more research is needed to investigate whether past findings replicate
across different settings and with different indicators of teacher quality.
1.3.4. School competition
Previous research has suggested that school competition may exacerbate inequity in education systems (Yang Hansen & Gustafsson, 2016; OECD, 2019a), while school competition and school market proponents claim that increased school choice encourages
competition between schools, thereby promoting increased school accountability and quality by virtue of market mechanisms
(Hanushek & Rivkin, 2010). School competition and choice may also allow low-income students greater access to schools in other more
wealthy neighborhoods (OECD, 2019a; Waslander et al., 2010). In practice, however, financial or social barriers often lead to the most
well-off parents choosing the most desirable schools, thereby exacerbating student socioeconomic segregation. Low-SES schools in
systems with more school competition may therefore have a harder time attracting the best teachers to their schools.
2. Method
2.1. Data
We use data from TALIS 2018 carried out by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), which includes
46 countries, 9063 schools, and 153,682 total teachers in schools teaching ISCED 22 (lower secondary). TALIS employs a two-stage
stratified probability sampling design. According to the TALIS technical report (OECD, 2018c), strata are determined locally in
each education system. In most cases, stratification was based on a combination of geography, financing, educational program and
school size. Schools are then sampled with probability proportional to size (p. 101–102). Next, teachers are randomly selected within
these schools with equal probability based on the subjects taught at the school. TALIS has sample size requirements, at 20 teachers
within each participating school and a minimum sample of 200 schools for each country, with a minimum of 4000 teachers sampled
per country.
2.2. Variables
2.2.1. Dependent variable
Teacher qualification status. For our first dependent variable at the classroom level, we create the dichotomous category of novice
teacher by recoding all teachers with five or less years of experience to one. Next, the dichotomous category of unspecialized teacher
was created by combining two variables at the teacher level. For a visual depiction of this measure, see Appendix B. We only those
teachers who selected no to studying a subject in their formal education and training and checked yes to currently teaching these
respective subjects in the school year in focus. We compare estimates for teachers of all subject domains with those when we restrict the
sample to mathematics and science teachers in Mplus, as these are the subjects in which teacher qualifications matter the most (Goe,
2007). We combine mathematics and science to have sufficient statistical power to conduct the analyses.
2.2.2. Main independent variable
Classroom SES. To measure socioeconomic composition in the classroom, we use a variable from the TALIS teacher questionnaire
regarding the percentage of socioeconomically disadvantaged students in the target classroom, as outlined by the teacher. This is coded
as 1 = none, 2 = 1% to 10%, 3 = 11% - 30%, 4= 31% - 60%, 5 = more than 60%. This variable is therefore the teacher’s perception of
the composition of socioeconomically disadvantaged students in their target classroom.
2.2.3. Control variables at classroom- and school-levels
When estimating the effect of classroom socioeconomic composition on the likelihood of receiving a novice or unspecialized math/

a robustness check, we examine the cross-school sorting patterns and use this variable as the main independent variable.
2.2.4. System-level variables
As country-level variables were aggregated from the school to the country level, it was necessary to determine whether countries
had particularly high amounts of missing data. Almost every country had missing data under 10%, with most being under the 5% mark.
However, some education systems had higher amounts of missing data for school-level variables in particular, and therefore we impute
missing data by using multivariate imputation by chained equation in RStudio (‘mice’ package, van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn,
2011) and create an imputed data frame for the analysis. We include country dummies in the imputation model, as well as the above
school characteristics. Finally, we use the TALIS provided school weights when aggregating the information to the system-level.
School stratification. We take tracking information from PISA 2018 as this information was not available in TALIS 2018 and 2015
(for the 2 countries not available in 2018), based on the question ‘Does your school practice within- school ability grouping?’ We take
those countries which answer yes and aggregated this to the country level applying the school weights, resulting in a measure which
represents the percentage of schools practicing within-school ability grouping in a country. Following this, we include a dummy
variable for each country which practices early between school tracking (from the age of 10–14). This information also comes from the
OECD (2019b).
School autonomy. School autonomy is taken from the TALIS 2018 school questionnaire and entails the question ‘who is responsible
for the following at your school’ and includes dimensions of staffing and budgeting, among others. Since we are interested in staffing
(as this has not been investigated before using TALIS data) and since staffing and budgeting autonomy are highly correlated, we take
those schools who answer ‘just the school or principle’ for staffing and not a joint decision or solely by an external authority and
aggregate this to the country level.
School accountability and competition. We construct a school accountability measure by taking those schools who answer ‘yes’ to
using classroom, school, or external achievement data to monitor teacher performance by external individuals or bodies (i.e. school
inspectors or government authorities). Country-level school competition was calculated by aggregating those schools which claim the
existence of 2 or more competing schools (coded 1) and aggregating it to the country level.
2.2.5. System-level control variables
To account for differences in development level of countries in our sample, we take information from the Human Development
Index (HDI) from the United Nations Development Reports (UNDP, 2020). HDI is a continuous measure which combines a life expectancy dimension (comprised of life expectancy at birth), an education dimension (comprised of expected years of schooling, and
mean years of schooling), as well as a ‘standard of living’ dimension, (comprised of GNI per capita). For more information on how this
measure is constructed, see UNDP (2020). Last, national teacher shortage was calculated by reporting the proportion of schools whose
principals reported that a shortage of qualified teachers hindered student learning.
The continuous variables at the country-level were not centered as they have values between 0 and 1 and represent the proportion
of schools in an education system with full autonomy over hiring, which practice within-school ability grouping, which have 2 or more
competing schools, or which used student achievement data to monitor teacher effectiveness.
2.3. Analysis
We use RStudio and Mplus for the data analyses. When possible, weights provided by TALIS for schools and teachers were included.
Such weights are inversely proportion to the likelihood of a teacher or school being sampled. Unlike other international large-scale
assessments, TALIS does not provide senate weights which would account for the different number of teachers sampled per country3. To account for missing data for the institution-level variables, an imputed dataset was created using the ‘mice’ package in Rstudio,
including dummy variables for each country and teacher qualification variables and all school characteristics. In other cases, multiple
imputation (for the within-country models) were conducted in RStudio with dummy variables for each education system. For the
hierarchical 3-level models with categorical outcomes, Mplus accounts for missing data using the Bayesian estimator. 2-level models
for robustness checks are also conducted in Mplus, this time with a maximum likelihood estimator and the weights.

2.4. Research question 1: within-country logistic regressions
In the first part of our analysis, we model the odds of receiving a novice or unspecialized mathematics teachers as a function of the
proportion of socioeconomically disadvantaged students in a classroom using a logistic model with cluster robust standard errors (to
see how this clustering accounts for the hierarchical structure of data, see (Abadie, Athey, Imbens, & Wooldridge, 2017)) at the
school-level. This analysis is conducted for each education system. The empirical model is as follows:
(1)

Log (odds) yts = β0 + β1 ClassSES + β2 SchoolControls

Where yts is the log odds of having an unspecialized or novice designation for teacher i in school s as a function of the classroom-SES
disadvantage and the school control variables. We report the classroom SES coefficients in the forms of their exponents (the odds
ratios). In this sense, if the classroom unstandardized beta coefficient is 0.10, we raise it to its exponent (1.105), indicating that an
increase of one unit in our independent variable (a unit increase in the relative proportion of socioeconomically disadvantaged students) results in a 10.5% increase in the odds of being assigned a teacher with a lower qualification level.

3.1. Within-country regressions of teacher qualification on classroom socioeconomic composition
In Fig. 2, we plot the relationship between socioeconomic classroom composition and the odds of having a novice or unspecialized
teacher. For this research question we limit the analysis to mathematics and science teachers, as past evidence has shown that these
tend to matter most when it comes to student performance and to compare the results with previous findings linking teacher and
student data (Glassow & Jerrim, 2022). The results show that most education systems display inequity in unspecialized teacher sorting
(left panel), where the relationship between class SES and teacher qualifications reaches conventional levels of statistical significance.
Several countries show a null relationship between classroom socioeconomic composition and the odds of having an unspecialized
mathematics or science teacher, however, including South Africa, Mexico, Brazil, Lithuania, Romania, Singapore, China (Shanghai),
Italy, Turkey, Georgia, Latvia, and Chile. The median odds ratio for sorting by specialization was Israel with a value of 1.23 where the
odds of an unspecialized teacher designation increase by 23% for each category increase in the socioeconomic disadvantage scale.
Surprisingly, Finland topped the list of countries ranked by odds ratios. It should be noted, however, that the very large standard errors
and confidence intervals associated with the point estimates indicate a degree of uncertainty in the findings.
By contrast, a smaller proportion of education systems showed a statistically significant level of inequity in novice teacher sorting
when it comes to mathematics and science teachers. The majority displayed a null relationship. There were some exceptions to this,
including Latvia, Slovenia, Turkey, France, USA, South Korea, England, Norway, Malta, Kazakhstan, UAE, and Singapore. The median
odds ratio was 1.09 (South Africa). A few countries show very large effects, however. Latvia, for example, displayed an odds ratio of
1.61, indicating a 61% increase in the odds of being a novice teacher for every category increase in classroom socioeconomic
composition (in order of proportion of disadvantaged students).
To further visualize these results in relation to the countries in the TALIS 2018 sample, we plot the relationship between inequity in
teacher sorting (based on the within-country t-values from the analysis in research question 1) against country level HDI (depicted in
Fig. 3). At first glance, there appears to be a positive association between teacher sorting and HDI for unspecialized mathematics and
science teachers, but no relationship to HDI for novice mathematics and science teachers. Pearson correlation coefficients between the
Human Development Index and the degree of inequity in teacher sorting (measured by the t-values) was 0.391 in the case of unspecialized teacher sorting and 0.162 in the case of novice teacher sorting.

3.3. Institutional correlates of the classroom SES teacher qualification random slope
Table 3 summarizes results for cumulative three-level hierarchical models with two distinct categorical outcomes; first, the likelihood of having a teacher with no specialization in the subject matter they are currently teaching, and second, the likelihood of having
a teacher in the first five years of their career.
In a first step, we conduct the analysis without cross-level interactions and focus on the lower-level control variables. The point
estimates represent the median of the posterior probability distributions for each parameter. We find that female teachers are
significantly less likely to be underqualified. In terms of classroom controls, the likelihood of receiving an unspecialized teacher increases as a function of the proportion of students with special needs in the class, the proportion of students with an immigrant
background, as well as the proportion of students who do not have the language of the test as their native language. These results
generalize to the likelihood of having a novice designation, save for the relationship with the proportion of special needs students.
Looking to the school controls, the likelihood of having both a novice and unspecialized designation increased for teachers in rural and
remote schools. Unsurprisingly, teachers in privately managed schools were not associated with increased predicted probability of an
unspecialized designation. On the other hand, teachers in private schools were more likely to be in their first five years on the job.
Models 1a and 2a also show that the classroom SES – teacher qualification slope varies significantly across education systems. We
therefore proceed with the next step in the modeling and include cross-level interactions with the random slope at the system-level.
The interpretation of interaction terms in multilevel regressions with dichotomous outcomes are somewhat controversial (Norton
et al., 2004), but generally we can speak of a marginal effect, or an increase in the effect of classroom SES on lower-qualification
probability as a function of the system-level characteristics. In the absence of statistical tools to adequately handle these complexities, some emphasize simply the statistical significance and direction of the effect (Sommet & Morselli, 2017). Looking to models 1b
and 2b (Table 3), it becomes immediately clear that system-level factors have considerable heterogeneity in the significance and
directions of their effects across the teacher qualification indicators. Almost no two outcomes are alike. For instance, between-school
tracking has a positive (in other words, ‘anti-compensatory’ as it displays a stronger relationship) relationship with sorting by
specialization, but a null relationship with novice teacher sorting. A similar pattern was observed for accountability. All other institution level variables display a null relationship with sorting by specialization, save for HDI. This finding confirms the positive
relationship depicted in Fig. 3, however, and supports the notion that cross-classroom sorting by specialization is more common in
higher-HDI systems. Surprisingly, there was no statistically significant relationship between sorting by specialization and school
competition, or autonomy. Taken together, these results suggest that sorting patterns are highly country and context dependent.
In a similar fashion, only two system-level variables were associated with the novice teacher sorting slope. First, school autonomy
over staffing was positively associated with socioeconomic sorting, indicating a higher likelihood that lower SES classes had novice
teachers in school systems with less centralized hiring practices (an ‘anti-compensatory’ relationship). This was also found in school
systems with higher levels of school competition. The findings across each model make general conclusions with respect to institutionlevel variables difficult, however. We conduct a series of additional analyses to test whether the results can be replicated across other
contexts.
3.4. Specification tests and additional analyses of cross-school sorting
In a final step, the robustness of the findings are tested. First, we model whether the results from Table 3 hold if we restrict the
sample to just mathematics and science teachers. These results are depicted in Table 4. The results show that almost none of the
institutional features correlate with the sorting by specialization slope. There are a few exceptions, including HDI and tracking. For
novice teacher sorting, none of the coefficients previously found to correlate with the slope were replicated when the analysis was
restricted to mathematics and science teachers, but in general similar direction of the coefficients was found.
The final robustness check deals with whether the findings can be generalized to cross-school sorting patterns. Table 5 depicts these
results. In general, few of the findings replicate with the exception of school competition. Cross-school sorting across both

4. Discussion
International testing bodies frequently claim that low-SES students are taught by underqualified teachers (OECD 2018b). Past work
has shown that this claim may be true in several countries (Akiba et al., 2007; Glassow & Jerrim, 2022) when teacher and student data
are linked and others have confirmed this with teacher-level data (Luschei & Jeong, 2019). Although the topic is gaining traction
internationally (OECD, 2018b), studies comparing the phenomenon across many countries remain scarce. The study adds a unique
contribution by looking at the distributions of qualitatively defined teachers shown to be particularly important for student
achievement (i.e., newly qualified teachers and those with no domain-relevant specialization). In general, cross-classroom teacher
sorting appears to be common in many countries. Next, we considered different the moderating characteristics of different institutional
features, including school autonomy, accountability, competition, and stratification. We explored the data with previous national
findings related to teacher sorting (mostly from the USA) and international literature related to system-level factors and educational
inequity (Strietholt et al., 2019; Luschei & Jeong, 2019) in mind, and formed some initial hypotheses based on these findings. Against
this background, the main aim was to contribute new findings to the dearth of international literature on the topic.
4.1. Inequalities in teacher sorting across countries and cohorts
In many educational systems, lower-SES classrooms are significantly more likely to have teachers without a specialization in the
mathematics or science domain they currently teach. Somewhat surprisingly, this was more common in more economically developed
countries, as evidenced by the positive relationship between the slope and country HDI in the cumulative models as well as the
scatterplot. T-values for the regressions of specialization on classroom SES were highest in Finland, United Arab Emirates, Austria,
Croatia, Chinese Taipei, Denmark and Australia. On the other hand, T-values for experience-based sorting were highest in Turkey,
Latvia, Slovenia, Kazakhstan and South Korea. Such findings indicate that educational systems rarely struggle with sorting inequity
across similar dimensions of teacher competence, and that policymakers must work to better understand their respective unique
sorting profiles. Luschei and Jeong (2019) note that cross-classroom sorting is more common in more developed countries using a
composite measure of teacher quality comprised of self-efficacy, educational attainment and years of experience. Our findings do not
replicate this conclusion in general, as socioeconomic teacher sorting was more common in higher HDI countries in all but one case.
Reconciling this finding with other studies linking student and teacher data (Glassow & Jerrim, 2022) points to potentially important
differences in the measurement of socioeconomic status across the large-scale assessments.
Our findings show that the dimension of specialization is of particular importance in cross-classroom sorting across more
economically developed systems. There may be several explanations for why sorting by specialization is more common in these
countries. Potential mathematics and science teachers may have more lucrative job opportunities outside the teaching field in higherincome countries, and therefore may be less willing to work in more challenging classroom environments. Evidence from Sweden
shows that the average test scores of teachers have declined over the past decades (Alatalo, Hansson, and Johansson, 2021). While a
high proportion of unspecialized teachers is likely to be associated with decreased working conditions, the same may not be true for
novice teachers. For instance, a dearth of novice teachers may also reflect hiring difficulties. Novice teachers are also more likely to be
motivated to positively impact society (Sims and Allen, 2018), and may seek out jobs in harder to staff settings. This may also be an
explanation for why inequity in sorting of novice mathematics and science teachers seems much less prevalent in general across the
education systems in our sample.
The three-level models showed that classrooms with a high proportion of students with special needs, immigrant backgrounds, or
language minorities were also prone in most cases to the allocation of teachers with lower qualification levels. Similarly, teachers were
consistently found to have lower qualification levels in rural or remote schools. Policymakers should therefore not limit their focus to
socioeconomic group differences, but also consider how sorting occurs across other socio-spatial dimensions. A valid criticism of the
TALIS study may point out that classroom socioeconomic composition is likely to be somewhat unreliable as it is the teacher
perception. Future work will have to investigate whether unspecialized or novice teachers are more likely to misrepresent the number
of socioeconomically disadvantaged students in their classrooms.
4.2. Institutional determinants of teacher sorting are hard to generalize
The classroom SES-teacher qualification slope varied significantly across educational systems for both outcome measures, indicating that the degree of teacher sorting is heterogeneous across educational systems. We posit in the introduction that the degree of
inequity in teacher sorting may be moderated by institutional features via a host of mechanisms conducted by teachers and school
leaders. The findings displayed a general pattern of mixed results, depending on the teacher qualification indicator in focus, supporting
the findings of Luschei and Jeong (2019) who note that ‘there are multiple pathways among teacher performance incentives,
10

International Journal of Educational Research 119 (2023) 102170

L.N. Glassow et al.

accountability mechanisms, and teacher sorting, none of which is completely predictable’ (p. 571). However, the findings point to
several policy-relevant conclusions.
School competition was positively related to teacher sorting by specialization and experience in several of the models. More
specifically, in countries with higher levels of school competition, low-SES students were more likely to have novice and unspecialized
teachers. These results contradict past research from Hanushek and Rivkin (2003), who find that school competition positively influences student outcomes and teacher quality with US data. They write that school competition is likely to drive up quality and
working conditions due to market mechanisms. We posit several interpretations of the opposite finding from our cross-national
analysis. Prior reviews have confirmed the association between increased opportunity of choice and inequity across educational
systems (Strietholt et al., 2019). In school systems with more competition a marketization, students are more likely to be segregated
along socioeconomic lines (Jackson, 2009). Teachers with better credentials may also have more leverage and an increased ability to
move both between schools and between classrooms within schools. In turn, school leaders may be more amenable to such assignments
as boosting the performance of the students most likely to achieve may ameliorate the market position of a school.
Higher school autonomy over staffing displayed a statistically significant relationship with the cross-classroom novice teacher
sorting slope but not for specialization. Luschei and Jeong (2019) also find mixed evidence in their investigation of cross-national
teacher sorting. Han (2018) finds anti-compensatory results in a previous study (albeit with PISA data and differing teacher quality
indicators). More theoretical work (Kang & Hong, 2008) claims that more centralized teacher assignment may be responsible for the
equitable sorting of teachers in countries like South Korea. They suggest that such approaches in more decentralized educational
systems may be taken up by the ‘centralization of teacher assignment processes to country offices of education’ (p. 570). The differential effects of autonomy across the qualification indicators are also telling. Since sorting by specialization is more prominent in
countries higher on the HDI index, it is perhaps not so surprising that staffing autonomy has null compensatory effect. Prior research
suggests that autonomy functions differently depending on the level of economic development in a system and is detrimental in
developing countries but beneficial in high-income countries (Hanushek et al., 2013). Future research will have to look further into this
interpretation, however. In any case, there are evidently different mechanisms which underpin sorting depending on the qualification
in focus, and it is also possible that more centralized teacher staffing is better able to assign teachers by experience. Unspecialized
teachers represent a staffing difficulty which may not be affected by decisions about hiring, but rather reflects another problem with
the teacher workforce.
School accountability and tracking were also positively associated with the slope is a select few cases. Past research has shown that
accountability mechanisms may be related to inequitable teacher turnover and lower job satisfaction and the increased opportunistic
allocation of teachers; (Darling-Hammond, 2004). While between-school tracking was related to cross-classroom sorting, it was not
significantly related to cross-school sorting. This along with the null pattern of results regarding most of the other policy-level variables
is likely the result of within-country heterogeneity. While no cross-national pattern could be detected, it is important to stress that the
conclusion should not be that these factors never matter. One interesting case is that of Finland. Finland shows very high
cross-classroom sorting by specialization. This was surprising. However, more evidence is uncovering implicit ability grouping in
Finnish schools despite the explicit mixed attainment aim in the school system policy (Berisha & Seppänen, 2016; Kosunen, Bernelius,
Seppännen, & Porkka, 2016). Although Finland reports only a third of schools which practice ability grouping, the reality may be much
higher.
4.3. Limitations
The above interpretations should be considered in the context of the methodological boundaries and limitations of the study. The
first is the number of education systems in our sample (46). Although this is higher than many international large-scale assessment
studies, there is always the issue of unexplained heterogeneity while comparing education systems, which we have attempted to
control for by including a control for HDI. However, there may be factors not captured by HDI, which we cannot completely remove.
There are also very few countries with low HDI, just Vietnam and South Africa in our sample for example. Our next limitation relates to
the socioeconomic composition variables at the classroom (and school) levels. TALIS provides socioeconomic composition variables
estimated by teachers and principals, and not based student data. This is potentially the strongest (and most unavoidable) limitation of
our study, as there is no way to adequately determine the accuracy of the teacher estimations. Nevertheless, unless novice and unspecialized teachers systematically misrepresent the proportion of disadvantaged students in their classrooms, their perceptions are
most likely consequential. Another limitation related to this is that we do not include a measure of socioeconomic school segregation
due to inadequate available information in the TALIS dataset. Finally, we conceptualize teacher competence based on qualifications
alone. Blömeke and Olsen (2019) point out that qualification variables may be understood differently across countries, particularly
with regards to the specialization variable and what is meant by ‘formal educational training’. Nevertheless, unless this is systematically misunderstood by unspecialized teachers, for example, the inequitable teacher distributions would likely still show up. We do
not account for the interplay between specialization and experience, for instance. While we think the strengths of this approach
outweigh the limitations. Particularly in regard to policy-recommendations, the results are not generalizable to other indicators of
teacher competence. It could be, however, that some unspecialized teachers will be compensated in their teaching styles due to their
many years of experience.
5. Conclusions
The present study found that cross-classroom sorting by specialization appears to be more prevalent higher-HDI countries.

Policymakers in such education systems should therefore work harder to incentivize the best teachers to socioeconomically disadvantaged classrooms, as well as to the teaching field in general. Given that teachers with no specialization in mathematics or science
are likely to have lower levels of content and pedagogical content knowledge (Baumert et al., 2010), policymakers should also work to
ameliorate the classroom practices of such teachers currently in the field via professional development and training opportunities.
Weakening cross-classroom and cross-school novice sorting may be possible via mitigating school competition and market mechanisms
and more centralized hiring practices.
Based on prior research, we hypothesized two primary ‘mechanisms’ of inequity in teacher sorting, including more opportunistic
classroom assignment by school leaders and teacher preferences based on their credentials and seniority levels. The results show that in
most cases increased opportunity of choice in an education system (via both early tracking, more autonomous hiring and increased
competition among schools) is likely to exacerbate sorting inequities to at least some degree. Increased school autonomy over staffing
and school competition functioned differently depending on the teacher qualification indicator in focus as well as whether crossclassroom or cross-school sorting was examined. The results support the mixed findings of previous cross-national studies (Luschei
& Jeong, 2019), and indicate that sorting patterns are for the most part highly country dependent and not easily generalizable due to
institutional characteristics. Future research may examine data not available in the TALIS study, such as relative salary levels, or
