Overrepresentation of female teachers in secondary education and gender
achievement gaps in PISA 2022
Hans Luyten
University of Twente, Enschede, Netherlands

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Gender achievement gaps
Feminization of education
Cross-national comparison
PISA

This study examines the association between the gender composition of the teaching profession and gender
achievement gaps across countries. It explores to what extent girls outperform boys in countries with a higher
percentage of female teachers in secondary education. Using PISA 2022 data on 15-year-olds’ performance in
reading, mathematics, and science, combined with labor force statistics, the analysis reveals a pattern in which
gender achievement gaps covary with the degree of overrepresentation of female teachers relative to the proportion of women in the labor force. In countries where this overrepresentation is more pronounced, girls tend to
show greater academic advantages over boys. This pattern appears to reflect broader societal dynamics not
captured by studies focusing on classroom environments.

1. Introduction
Interest in the differing educational experiences, achievements, and
trajectories of males and females initially arose from concerns about
females’ perceived lack of success and engagement in subjects like
mathematics and the physical sciences. However, since the 1990s, the
focus has shifted to the challenges faced by males, particularly in
Western countries, where issues of disengagement and underachievement among boys have become increasingly prominent (OECD, 2023b).
In recent decades, many countries have made significant strides in
reducing, and in some cases reversing, female disadvantages in educational attainment. Data on gender differences in secondary education,
including key areas such as overall achievement and graduation rates,
tend to paint a more positive picture for females. In tertiary education,
women have also been closing traditional gaps with male students (Van
Bavel et al., 2018). Since 2000, PISA (Programme for International
Student Assessment) has consistently reported that 15-year-old girls
outperform boys in reading across all participating countries, while boys
generally outperform girls in mathematics, albeit to a much lesser degree (OECD, 2023b).
2. Theoretical and methodological background
2.1. Teacher gender and gender achievement gaps
Some policymakers have linked these changes to the growing

presence of women in the teaching profession, a trend often referred to
as the “feminization of education.” However, research provides little
evidence for a clear connection. Extensive analyses based on TIMSS and
PIRLS data show that “same-sex teacher effects” on test scores in secondary education science and mathematics are mostly positive, but
extremely small (De Gendre et al., 2024; Hermann & Diallo, 2017). On
average, being taught by a same-sex teacher is associated with an effect
of only 0.013 standard deviations, with hardly any variation between
countries (De Gendre et al., 2024, pp. 31–33).
Even if a link exists between teacher gender and boys’ educational
disadvantage, increasing the number of male teachers may not be the
most constructive response. Rather than focusing on teacher gender, it
seems more appropriate to prepare boys for a society in which women
frequently hold leadership roles. Learning to respect and collaborate
with female authority figures, including teachers, is an essential part of
growing up in such a society. Efforts that foster these competencies
appear more appropriate than attempting to address cultural or motivational challenges by altering the gender distribution of the teaching
profession.
Nonetheless, a deeper understanding of how gender achievement
gaps relate to the makeup of the teaching profession remains important.
Current research generally indicates that the gender of individual
teachers has only a very weak relationship with gender achievement
gaps. Still, this study provides empirical evidence showing that the
overall gender composition of a country’s teaching labor force correlates
with national patterns of gender achievement gaps, especially when

Feminization is far more pronounced in primary education. In nearly
all countries, the majority of primary school teachers are women
(Kelleher et al., 2011). Consequently, there is relatively little
cross-national variation in the gender composition of the primary
teaching workforce, which may complicate efforts to detect a correlation
between teacher gender composition and gender achievement gaps. For
this reason, the present paper focuses on secondary education. In
contrast to prior research based on TIMSS data, it also examines gender
achievement in reading among 15-year-olds, which is particularly
relevant given the large gender achievement gaps in this subject (OECD,
2023b).
2.2. Gender composition of the teaching profession versus teacher gender

2.3. Country-level analysis

Even if individual teachers’ gender does not influence gender
achievement gaps, the overall gender composition of the teaching profession may still play a role. The underlying hypothesis of this study is
that boys’ engagement with learning declines as the proportion of female teachers in a country increases. In such a context, the occasional
male teacher may have little influence, as he is likely to be perceived as
an exception. Thus, even if male and female teachers within a country
contribute similarly to gender achievement gaps, these differences may
be more pronounced in countries with a higher share of female teachers.
Schools and teachers represent a major source of authority for adolescents. They establish expectations for behavior, academic performance, and social interaction, reinforced by formal mechanisms such as
grading, discipline policies, and school regulations. Boys tend to be less
inclined to follow rules set by authorities (Moffitt et al., 2001). They may
consciously or subconsciously disengage from academics or adopt
anti-school attitudes as part of their identity (Heyder et al., 2021; Van
Houtte, 2024). Jackson (2003) has argued that “laddish” behavior,
where boys reject academic engagement to maintain a “cool” image,
contributes to gender achievement gaps. When boys perceive education
as conflicting with their gender identity, they may invest less effort and
perform worse. Boys with more traditional views of masculinity are
more likely to exhibit disruptive behavior, suggesting that adherence to
conventional masculine norms contributes to classroom disengagement
and misconduct.
This behavioral dynamic may be influenced not just by classroom
interactions, but also by broader societal perceptions of education and
teaching as gendered domains. Across the globe, most teachers are
women, especially in primary and secondary education. As a result,
education is increasingly perceived as a female domain (Kelleher et al.,
2011). This may reinforce the notion among boys that education and
learning are primarily female concerns.
Moreover, research indicates that the societal status of a profession
tends to decline as it becomes more female-dominated. Occupations
with increasing female representation often experience reductions in
wages and societal prestige (Harris, 2022; Murphy & Oesch, 2016). The
lower the perceived status of the teaching profession in a country, the
less likely boys may be to respect the authority of their teachers. This
may reduce their engagement with school and further widen the
achievement gap between boys and girls.
The tendency among male adolescents to reject academic engagement primarily reflects a broad anti-education ethos. This study posits
that high percentages of female teachers in a country may reinforce such
disengagement among boys, which in turn contributes to gender
achievement gaps. Science and mathematics may be perceived as less
“feminine” than other subjects, either due to gender stereotypes or
because they are more often taught by male teachers. This could help
explain why boys tend to perform relatively better in these domains.
However, the present study does not seek to explain why girls typically
outperform boys in reading and boys outperform girls in mathematics.

The main research questions of the present study require a countrylevel focus. Prior studies (De Gendre et al., 2024; Hermann & Diallo,
2017) demonstrate convincingly that teacher gender effects on gender
achievement gaps are negligible at the classroom level. Including
lower-level data would therefore add no new insights. By contrast, potential society-level effects have hardly been explored. This paper addresses this gap by analyzing country-level aggregates. More complex
multilevel analyses are not needed, since correlations between
higher-level aggregates can be validly assessed without lower-level data
(Snijders & Bosker, 1999). Variation between countries can only be
explained by other country-level variables, with lower-level information
collapsed into national averages. Lower-level data are, of course,
essential for explaining within-country variation, but they are not relevant for the present research questions.
Fig. 1 illustrates the core scenario addressed in this study using
simulated data from four hypothetical countries with progressively
higher shares of female secondary-school teachers. Within each country,
male- and female-led classrooms produce identical gender gaps in student achievement. This mirrors the near-zero same-sex teacher effects
reported across dozens of countries by De Gendre et al. (2024). In the
hypothetical example, however, the national gender gap widens as the
overall proportion of female teachers increases. To date, no empirical
study has examined whether teacher gender is linked to gender
achievement gaps in this way. To address this question, PISA 2022
achievement data (OECD, 2024a) were merged with UNESCO (2024)
statistics on female teacher shares and ILOSTAT (2024) data on
women’s labor force participation. Previous research has focused on
within-country comparisons of male versus female teacher effects,
typically finding only very weak associations with gender achievement
gaps. By challenging the implicit assumption that national shares of
female teachers are irrelevant, this study introduces a new perspective.

Focusing exclusively on classroom-level data may obscure important
aggregation effects. Robinson’s (1950) work on the ecological fallacy,
and the related Simpson’s paradox (Simpson,; 1951), demonstrate how
correlations between aggregated variables can differ dramatically from
the individual-level correlations. However, the reverse is also true.
Lower-level correlations do not guarantee the same patterns at higher
levels: this is known as the atomistic fallacy (Diez-Roux, 1998). A clear
illustration is the wealth–obesity relationship. At the individual level,
poverty correlates with higher obesity risk, yet, at the national level,
richer countries exhibit greater obesity prevalence (Talukdar et al.,
2020; Templin et al., 2019). Neither the individual nor the country-level
correlation alone tells the whole story; inferring one from the other leads
to ecological or atomistic fallacies.
In education, a classic Simpson’s paradox example relates to Berkeley’s 1973 graduate admissions (Bickel et al., 1975). Overall, male applicants had higher acceptance rates (44 % versus 35 %), suggesting a
gender bias in the selection process. However, when researchers broke
down the admissions data by individual departments, they found that
most departments actually admitted women at equal or even higher
rates than men. The overall pattern resulted from women disproportionately applying to popular but more selective departments, such as
those in the humanities and social sciences. Men, on the other hand,
applied more often to departments with higher admission rates, like
engineering and physical sciences. This underscores the risk of drawing
conclusions from a single level of analysis.
Building on these insights, the present study investigates whether
similar aggregation effects occur in the context of teacher gender. While
classroom-level comparisons provide little evidence for same-sex
teacher effects, country-level analyses may reveal a different pattern.

1. How strong is the correlation between the overrepresentation of female teachers in secondary education and national gender achievement gaps in reading, mathematics, and science among 15-yearolds?
2. To what extent does the correlation vary across reading, mathematics, and science?
3. How robust is the correlation when controlling for exogenous

The research questions are addressed from two complementary
perspectives: a country perspective and a global student perspective. In
the analyses adopting the country perspective, each country contributes
equally to the estimates, regardless of its population size. These analyses
reflect the association as it holds for the average country. In the analyses
adopting the student perspective, countries are weighted by the size of
their student population, thereby representing the relationship as
experienced by the average student worldwide.
4. Method
4.1. Units of analysis: PISA 2022 countries

2.4. Percentage of female teachers and percentage of female workers

This study analyses data from 65 countries that participated in PISA
2022. Fig. 2 presents a world map showing these countries. PISA,
organized by the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), evaluates whether students have acquired the knowledge
and skills needed for their future, with a focus on reading, mathematics,
and science. Its highly standardized design allows robust cross-country
comparisons.
PISA targets students aged 15 years and three months to 16 years and
two months, regardless of grade level. The assessment consists of a twohour test divided into sections on reading, mathematics, and science.
Students also complete a 35- to 45-minute questionnaire on socioeconomic status, learning habits, and home environments.
Test scores are standardized to follow a roughly normal distribution,
with an OECD average of 500 points and a standard deviation of 100. A
ten-point difference on the PISA scale corresponds to an effect size
(Cohen’s d) of 0.10 (OECD, 2023b). School-level data are also collected
via questionnaires.
Of the 81 countries and economies participating in PISA 2022, 16
were excluded from this study due to missing data on the percentage of
female teachers in secondary education and/or female workers in the
national labor force. Appendix A lists the included and excluded countries, along with the reasons for exclusion. Despite these exclusions, the
study covers a diverse sample, including more than half of the countries
outside the OECD. Table 1 shows the distribution of the countries by
continent.
The countries vary widely in population size. For example, the U.S.
and Indonesia each represent more than three million 15-year-olds,
whereas Malta and Macao-China represent fewer than 4000. Consequently, analyses in which each country is given equal weight may yield
results that differ substantially from analyses in which countries
contribute in proportion to their student populations.
For the analyses reflecting the student perspective, country weights
are applied according to the total number of students represented by
each national sample. These weights, standardized to an average of one,
produce estimates and standard errors appropriate for the population-

Across countries, women’s labor-force participation varies widely,
with the largest gender gaps in the Arab States, North Africa, and South
Asia (ILO,; 2017; OECD,; 2017). Gender segregation by sector is pronounced: women dominate education, health, and social services. Cultural norms and family expectations can discourage women from paid
work, under threat of social sanction (Badgett & Folbre,; 1999; Klasen &
Pieters,; 2012), while institutional barriers, such as tax disincentives and
scarce childcare services, further constrain participation (Jaumotte,;
2003; Rastrigina & Verashchagina,; 2015).
In PISA 2022, some countries have very low female labor-force
participation. In such countries, even a slight majority of female
teachers can signal “female domination” in schools. Thus, women’s
overall labor-force share is assumed to provide an important reference
point for how students perceive teacher gender balance. To capture this,
female overrepresentation in secondary education is defined as:
% Female Teachers – % Female Labor-Force
This straightforward index describes the gap between gender shares
in the teaching profession versus the national labor force. The larger the
value, the more teaching is skewed toward women relative to other
professions. Taking into account both the share of female teachers and
women’s labor force participation, especially the difference between the
two, provides a better understanding of whether and how the gender
imbalance in teaching relates to national patterns in gender achievement gaps.
This paper examines the statistical relationship between this indicator and gender achievement gaps. Appendix C reports regression analyses in which the two components of the indicator, are entered as
separate explanatory variables.

Variables that may mediate the relationship between overrepresentation of female teachers and gender achievement gaps are
excluded from the set of controls (e.g., student attitudes and perceptions,
school climate and policies). These factors lie on the causal pathway
between the explanatory variable and the outcome. Including them as
covariates would obscure the total effect of the independent variable
and could produce misleading conclusions about its role in the causal
system (Petersen et al., 2006).
The control variables that were included generally relate to aspects
of student life outside school (e.g., family support), school resources (e.
g., availability of computers), and school staff characteristics (e.g.,
percentage of certified teachers). Some indices were excluded because
data were not collected in a large number of countries (e.g., ICT use
outside school). In addition, the analyses controlled for four further
variables: the percentage of students in single-sex schools, PISA’s
coverage of 15-year-olds, the national average across the three PISA
subjects, and the Global Gender Gap Index.
The variable measuring the prevalence of single-sex education was
developed specifically for this study. For most countries, it is based on
school questionnaire data, while for four countries (Austria, New Zealand, Norway, and Sweden) school-level classifications were estimated
using student data. The coverage index indicates the proportion of a
country’s 15-year-old population represented in PISA (Coverage Index
3; OECD, 2024b). The Global Gender Gap Index captures gender parity
across four dimensions: economic participation, educational attainment,
health, and political empowerment. Countries are scored on a scale from
0 to 1, with 1 indicating full gender parity (World Economic Forum,
2021; 2022).

4.2. Data sources
4.2.1. PISA 2022 achievement data
Data on gender gaps in reading, mathematics, and science among 15year-olds were obtained from the PISA 2022 database (OECD, 2024a)1.
For each country, the average male score was subtracted from the
average female score, with positive values indicating a female advantage
and negative values indicating a male advantage.
4.2.2. female teachers and workers
Data on the percentage of female teachers in secondary education
were sourced from the UNESCO Institute for Statistics (UNESCO, 2024),
while data on the percentage of female workers in the labor force (ages
15–64) came from the ILOSTAT database (ILOSTAT, 2024). Both datasets primarily reflect 2022 or 2021 data, with some earlier years for
specific countries detailed in Appendix A.

4.3. Data analysis
To answer the research questions, a series of bivariate regression
analyses were conducted to examine the relationship between the
overrepresentation of female teachers and gender achievement gaps in
reading, mathematics, and science across countries. For each subject,
two regressions were run: one in which each country carried equal
weight regardless of size, and one weighted by the number of 15-yearolds represented in PISA.
The robustness of these bivariate relationships was examined using
an exploratory yet structured approach. To identify the predictors most
strongly associated with gender achievement gaps across countries, least
absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was
applied. LASSO is particularly well suited for situations with relatively

the absolute size of coefficients, LASSO constrains many to exactly zero,
retaining only predictors with explanatory value for the outcome
(Friedman et al., 2010; Tibshirani, 1996).
The initial predictor set consisted of 50 indicators. Given the relatively small number of countries (65), including all predictors in a
conventional regression model would carry a high risk of overfitting.
LASSO regression provides a principled way to address this issue by
reducing the predictor set to a more parsimonious model.
Model estimation was conducted using cross-validated LASSO. Tenfold cross-validation identified the optimal penalty parameter (λ), and
the “one-standard-error” rule was applied to avoid overly complex
models. This rule favors the simplest model within one standard error of
the cross-validation minimum, ensuring that the retained model captures the main explanatory patterns without overfitting.
To further assess robustness, a bootstrap stability analysis was
implemented. The dataset was resampled with replacement 500 times,
and the LASSO model re-estimated for each draw. For every predictor,
the selection frequency was recorded. Predictors consistently retained
across resamples were considered stable contributors to the explanation
of gender achievement gaps, whereas those with low selection probabilities were regarded as less reliable. Predictors with high selection
probabilities (≥.70) were classified as robust, while those below this
threshold were excluded from the reported regression models.
The combination of cross-validated LASSO and stability selection
(Meinshausen & Bühlmann, 2010) thus yields a focused set of predictors
that robustly account for cross-national variation in gender achievement
gaps. Appendix F provides an excerpt of the R code used for the analyses.

how the shares of women among teachers and in the labor force

5. Results
5.1. Descriptive statistics
Table 2 presents descriptive statistics on gender gaps in reading,
mathematics, and science performance among 15-year-olds. It also reports the averages and variation in the percentage of female teachers in
secondary education and of women in the labor force. Both unweighted
figures and figures weighted by country size are reported.
Student performance patterns are consistent with previous PISA
surveys since 2000. Girls outperform boys in reading in all 65 countries,
with an average gap of 26.5 points (unweighted) or 22.0 points
(weighted). The smallest difference is 2.6 points in Costa Rica, while the
largest is 54.1 points in Cyprus. In mathematics, boys generally
outperform girls, though the gap is considerably smaller. National differences range from a 21.1-point disadvantage for girls in Italy to a 19.1point advantage for girls in Albania. In science, the weighted average
gap is zero, while the unweighted average is 4.6 points in favor of girls.
At the country level, gaps range from a 14.6-point disadvantage for girls
in Costa Rica to a 32.9-point advantage for girls in Jordan.
Regarding gender composition, over 60 % of the teachers in secondary education are women, with substantial variation across countries. In Morocco, Peru, and Cambodia, women represent a minority of
secondary teachers (39.1 %, 45.5 %, and 47.1 %, respectively).
Conversely, countries such as Moldova, Lithuania, Latvia, Ukraine, and
Georgia show percentages above 80 %.
On average, the percentage of female workers in national labor
forces is markedly lower (about 43 %). Only four countries (Portugal,
Moldova, Macao, and Hong Kong) show a percentage slightly above
50 %. Arab nations (Qatar, Jordan, Palestinian Authority, Morocco, and
the United Arab Emirates) report percentages below 25 %.

between female overrepresentation and gender achievement gaps. European countries, on the other hand, are numerous but mostly small and
therefore have a much more modest impact in the weighted analyses.
Within Europe, the correlation is weak and not statistically significant,
but this does not contradict the global pattern: European countries
cluster within a relatively narrow range of both female overrepresentation and gender performance gaps, resulting in moderate internal variation, despite their position aligning with the global trends.
Appendix G discusses the distinction between European and nonEuropean countries in more detail.

cross-validated LASSO and bootstrap stability selection are reported in

out. Most importantly, overrepresentation of female teachers is the only
variable among fifty that consistently meets this criterion, across all
three subjects and for both unweighted and weighted data. Five other
potential confounders meet the criterion at least once. The percentage of
students in single-sex education does so three times: twice for reading

statistically significant with unweighted data. Gender differences in
family support meet the criterion twice, while the national mean on
family support and the national PISA mean each do so once. Appendix E
provides further details on the selection probabilities of the explanatory
variables.

The present study examined the relationship between the gender
composition of the teaching profession and gender gaps in student
achievement. The findings show that at age 15 girls outperform boys in
reading, math, and science to a greater extent in countries where female
teachers are overrepresented in secondary education relative to the
labor force.
These results contrast with earlier research, which generally found
little disadvantage for boys in classrooms with female teachers. Unlike
prior studies that focused on classroom-level dynamics, this study
highlights the role of country characteristics. The findings suggest that
broader societal factors may play an important role in shaping these
gendered academic outcomes. The observed trends are similar across
reading, mathematics, and science: in each subject, an overrepresentation of female teachers is associated with relative advantages
for female students.
The correlation is strongest for reading and weakest for mathematics.

In addition to female overrepresentation, the percentage of students

The correlation is found for reading (both unweighted and weighted

This suggests that gender gaps in favor of girls are relatively large in
countries with higher percentages of students in single-sex schools. At
first sight, this may seem surprising, since prior research within countries has not produced consistent evidence on the effects of single-sex
education on gender achievement gaps (Clavel & Flannery, 2023;
OECD, 2009). However, the present findings are not necessarily inconsistent with that literature. As the example of the relation between
wealth and obesity illustrates, correlations can differ markedly across
levels of aggregation.
Other relevant country-level factors related to gender achievement
gaps concern out-of-school influences, notably gender differences in
family support and in working for pay before or after school. By contrast,
overall gender parity, as measured by the Global Gender Gap Index, does
not show a robust relation with gender achievement gaps.
The concept of overrepresentation may help to explain the otherwise
puzzling observation of female-favoring achievement gaps in Muslim
countries. Stoet and Geary (2015) also report that national levels of
gender equality do not consistently correlate with gender achievement
gaps. They note that while Muslim countries often rank low on gender
equality, girls in these countries frequently outperform boys academically. The present study finds that female teacher overrepresentation in
several Eastern European countries (e.g., Georgia, Latvia, and Bulgaria)
is comparable to that in Muslim countries, even though female labor
force participation in the former approximates 50 %. At the other end of
the spectrum, Western European and Latin American countries fit the
general pattern as well. These countries show modest overrepresentation of female teachers accompanied by modest or even absent female advantages in achievement (depending on the subject).
However, the percentages of female teachers and female workers are
higher in Western Europe.
The findings suggest that female teacher overrepresentation may
help boost girls’ academic performance in reading, mathematics, and
science relative to boys in the same country. If so, a key question is how
this may affect long-term outcomes. Academic advantages increase the
odds of girls attaining higher qualifications, which is a strong predictor
of occupational status and income (Deutschmann et al., 2025; OECD,
2023a).
Currently, women’s labor market participation rates and incomes
still lag behind men’s in most countries. Yet, in terms of educational
attainment, women have been catching up for decades, and in many
respects surpassing men. It seems plausible that these educational gains
will continue to narrow gender gaps in labor market outcomes. Women
have already made significant progress in closing participation and income gaps (ILO, 2017; Jaumotte, 2003).
Thus, while not inevitable, continued progress toward gender parity
in employment and earnings appears likely. Girls’ academic success
positions them well for the future, although structural and cultural
barriers still hinder full economic equality. Women’s careers often slow
or stall due to pregnancy, childbirth, and caregiving responsibilities.
They remain overrepresented in lower-paid sectors (e.g., education,
healthcare). Even within the same fields, women are less likely to attain
senior or leadership roles (OECD, 2023a).

Future research might focus on longitudinal analyses, mediation

changes in female teacher overrepresentation over several decades
alongside shifts in gender attainment gaps would provide stronger evidence of a causal link. Mediation tests of boys’ disengagement could
support or challenge a causal interpretation. Prior work suggests that
boys’ disengagement and disruptive behavior contribute to male
educational disadvantage (Heyder et al., 2021; Jackson, 2003; Van
Houtte, 2024). If disengagement mediates the association between female overrepresentation and gender achievement gaps, one would
expect a clear relationship between female overrepresentation and boys’
lack of engagement.
This paper further hypothesizes that the low prestige of the teaching
profession contributes to male disengagement, and that female labor
force participation serves as a reference point for perceived overrepresentation. Empirical research into the interrelationships among
female labor force participation, perceived overrepresentation, teacher
prestige, and male disengagement would help clarify the role of teacher
prestige. Future studies should also examine whether similar patterns
occur in primary and tertiary education, to better understand how
gender dynamics evolve across educational stages.
6.3. Policy implications
Reducing female-teacher overrepresentation is by no means a
definitive solution for eradicating gender achievement gaps. The
regression intercepts in Table 3 show that even with zero overrepresentation, girls still hold an advantage in reading but lag behind
boys in math and science. Moreover, in countries with very high percentages of female teachers, only reducing the share of female teachers
will lower overrepresentation; in countries with low female labor-force
participation, boosting women’s workforce entry is the more direct
remedy. Such an approach likely yields both economic and social benefits, including higher GDP and greater women’s autonomy and wellbeing (Kabeer,; 2005).
Nonetheless, shifting the gender balance in either the teaching profession or the broader labor force would take years, if not decades, and is
unlikely to produce rapid results. That said, there are compelling reasons
to recruit more men into teaching regardless of any impact on gender
achievement gaps. Teaching shortages are a global concern and are
expected to worsen (UNESCO,; 2023). Since the teaching profession is
dominated by women in most countries, men represent an underutilized
resource in addressing these shortages. Education is a vital public good,
and it is reasonable that men share responsibility for its delivery.
Moreover, good governance dictates that key public-service providers
reflect the populations they serve, not only by gender but also by
socio-cultural background. This principle is widely accepted in sectors
such as the judiciary and law enforcement (Lawyers’ Committee,; 2005;
Shankley & Williams,; 2020).
The primary cause of male underachievement in secondary education appears to be active disengagement and low self-discipline
(Duckworth & Seligman, 2006; Jackson, 2003; Heyder et al., 2021),
which are rooted in gendered differences in personal values (Leikas
et al., 2024). These tendencies often manifest in disruptive behavior and
resistance to authority, patterns that are not limited to the classroom but
extend into broader societal contexts (Galasso et al., 2020; Moffitt et al.,
2001; Steffensmeier et al., 2006). Male identity and its link to disengagement from education is largely a cultural construct. Across societies,
prevailing gender stereotypes prescribe that males be tough, autonomous, risk-seeking, and defiant of authority (Boutyline et al.,; 2023).
