This research investigates the main determinants of educational outcomes for students aged 15–16 in Latin
America, aiming to identify differences among countries. To achieve this, data from the 2018 Programme for
International Student Assessment (PISA) were examined using the education production function and multilevel
regression, considering both the impacts created by schools and the characteristics of students and their families,
as well as in-school process variables such as instructional practices and learning environment dynamics. Not
only do the results show differences in school performance in Latin American countries, but also that failing
grades and insecurity faced by the students worsen school performance. On the other hand, studying at a school
located in a medium-to-large city increases students’ performance in the skills tested. Moreover, family background has a significant impact on students’ academic performance, suggesting profound inequalities of opportunities in Latin American countries. The results show that, in addition to socioeconomic and school-related
factors, process variables play a relevant role in explaining academic performance: a positive classroom climate
and adaptive instruction are associated with higher performance, especially in reading and mathematics, while
negative perceptions of teacher feedback correlate with lower performance, particularly among more disadvantaged students.

1. Introduction
Despite significant advances in access and coverage of the global
education system, the quality of learning is still a matter of debate in
light of international assessment results. Indeed, although Latin American countries have achieved better performances in recent tests, their
performances are still considerably distant from developed countries
(OECD, 2019; UNESCO, 2016).
During the first 15 years of the 21st century, Rivas (2015) reported
that Latin America had undergone a process of expanding educational
rights, with increased access at all levels of education, greater state
funding, and expanded rights for previously excluded and marginalized
groups. This expansion of traditional education boundaries was simultaneous. According to data from Barro and Lee (2016), the increase in
years of schooling for the population aged 25–64 in the countries3
analyzed in this research ranged from 0.5 years to 3.07 years between
2000 and 2015. The Dominican Republic recorded the largest increase
in years of study during this period – rising from around 6.7 years in

2000 to slightly over 9.7 years in 2015, making it the fourth-ranked
country in Latin America with the most years of studies. The highest
educational levels can be observed for Chile and Panama, with 10.7 and
10 years of schooling in 2015, respectively.
School performance reflects the success or failure of an education
system and has a direct impact on the socioeconomic development of a
country (Farooq et al., 2011). Poor academic performance can result in
student non-engagement and consequent school dropouts. In turn,
young people with less than secondary education face significant challenges in entering and continuing in the labor market, leading to economic and social consequences for both citizens and society. However,
despite the recognized importance of education for economic development and having expanded access to education, challenges remain in
ensuring access to and retention in school, especially for adolescents
from 14 years old onward, as illustrated by Binstock and Cerrutti (2005),
Borchers and Cunha (2021), Dios (2022), Edo et al. (2017), Plassa and
Cunha (2019), and Villarroel et al. (2021). Therefore, it can be affirmed

Thus, identifying the key factors that impact student performance, as

The main contribution of this work lies in its detailed analysis of the
determinants of school performance in Latin America and the identification of specific differences in each country in the region, aiming to
contribute to the development of more focused public policies to
improve the quality and equity of education. To achieve the proposed
objective, this paper is divided into five more sections, in addition to this

In the empirical literature, researchers have sought to understand the
determinants of student performance, including in Latin America,
because schooling is recognized as an important means by which individuals accumulate human capital. Studies conducted to verify the
determinants of student achievement show that academic performance
is defined by personal, family, socioeconomic, and school factors, and in
some cases, a limited capacity of schools to influence student learning is
observed (Aberastury, 2020; Adrogué & Orlicki, 2022; Leite et al., 2022;
Valenzuela et al., 2021).
Studies carried out in various Latin American countries have shown
the importance of variables related to preschool completion, student
failure, and gender differentiation (Curi & Menezes-Filho, 2009; Gómez,
2013; Rebatta & Aponte, 2020). Most studies have emphasized the
importance of preschool, as this stage is fundamental for the development of students’ cognitive skills, influencing their ability to learn in
later stages. Heckman and Jacobs (2009) showed that investments in
early childhood education have higher returns compared to investments
in university education, yet disparities in access persist in many Latin
American countries. In addition, other studies have highlighted the
significant impacts of the variables used to measure students’ expectations and motivation in the learning process (Alvarado et al., 2018;
Oyhantçabal, 2021).
Family environment has been addressed in research and shows the
relevance of parents’ schooling and occupation in students’ school
performance, together with the socioeconomic characteristics of the
family (Souza et al., 2018; Cambindo, 2021; Feijó et al., 2022). Some
studies highlight the relevance of the availability of cultural and
educational resources at home, such as books and computers, in children’s school performance (Contreras, 2012; Dias et al., 2017; Doneschi,
2016; Hurst, 2019). Thus, families with better socioeconomic conditions
will often be able to provide more educational resources at home or
choose the schools that will provide them with these resources. Moreover, in general, educated parents can decide to invest more of their time
and energy in their children’s education or choose to interact with their
children, contributing to their success at school.
On the school level, infrastructure quality, class size, and teacher
qualifications directly affect learning outcomes. According to Marchionni et al. (2012) and Rosero et al. (2021), the geographical location of
schools is a significant factor that positively affects school performance.
Their research observed that students in large urban cities tend to have
better grades compared to students in other areas. Another variable that
has a significant, but negative impact on student performance is violence
in schools, especially bullying. Violence not only negatively and directly
affects performance, but also increases the likelihood that the student
will not succeed in their educational training process (Prestes, 2021).
However, there are school-related variables whose effect on school
performance still lacks consensus in the literature. The impact of class
size on student performance has been addressed, whereby some studies
have indicated a negative correlation between class size and performance, although the coefficients were not statistically significant. Other
studies have found that a reduced number of students per class had a
positive and significant impact (Araújo et al., 2021; Camargo & Porto
Júnior, 2012). Camargo and Porto Júnior (2012) showed that there is a
huge controversy in the literature regarding the real impact of this
variable on school performance. When examining the relationship between proficiency and class size, the authors found a negative but not
significant relationship. Some other studies, such as Duso and Sudbrack
(2009), have argued that more students in the classroom would hinder a
more individualized teaching strategy compatible with each student’s
needs.

Coleman’s (1966) study served as a precursor to economic literature
examining the determinants of education, which found that resource
allocation has little effect on academic performance, while family
background and student characteristics have a larger impact. However,
Hanushek (1989) stated that the general production function of education expresses the relationship between the use of school resources to
produce an educational good, which can be expressed in terms of academic performance. Schools impact educational performance, arguing
that the most important explanatory variables in the education production function are family factors, which measure the development of a
region and the school, such as teacher characteristics, class size, and
facilities. In turn, Beltrán and Seinfeld (2012) classified the determinants of academic performance into demand-side factors that
relate to the characteristics of students and their households, as well as
supply-side factors associated with the school. Supply-side factors
included variables related to education management, school infrastructure quality, and teacher quality. However, their relative influence
varies depending on socioeconomic and institutional contexts, necessitating a comprehensive evaluation of these factors in Latin America.
Moreover, the discussion of equity and quality in education has been
extensively explored in the literature, with scholars emphasizing both
the conceptual foundations and the empirical assessment of educational
fairness. Equity in education is commonly understood as the extent to
which individuals have access to educational opportunities and resources irrespective of socioeconomic background, gender, ethnicity, or
other demographic factors (European Union, 2006; OECD, 2005). This
principle aligns with the broader discourse on educational effectiveness,
which considers not only student achievement but also how equitably
that achievement is distributed across different student populations
(Kyriakides et al., 2018).
Kyriakides et al. (2018) proposed a dynamic model of educational
effectiveness that integrates equity as a fundamental dimension, arguing
that school improvement efforts must be designed to address disparities
in student outcomes. Their framework suggests that effective education
systems should aim for both high achievement levels and a fair distribution of learning gains among students.
Kelly (2012) built on this by introducing a Gini-type index to measure ‘attainment equity’ in schools, allowing for a more nuanced analysis of how student performance is distributed within and across schools.
The author applied this index to UK student data, demonstrating how

The economics literature is inconclusive about the role of private
schools, for example, in the overall educational performance of their
students. The literature often suggests that private management of
schools offers superior quality and innovation, and lower cost compared
to public management. Private sector competition could also improve
public schools (Shleifer, 1998; Bishop & Woößmann, 2004). However,
this perspective is criticized in the literature. In fact, the allocation of
students between public and private schools is based on the socioeconomic characteristics of the family; therefore children from families
with higher socioeconomic levels are more likely to attend private
schools. In turn, family background contributes to determining children’s educational performance. Thus, due to this self-selection, a better
average performance of private school students is expected.
Over the past two decades, research on educational outcomes in
Latin America has increasingly emphasized the relevance of process
variables, such as instructional practices, classroom interactions, and
teacher quality, in shaping both academic performance and the equity of
educational opportunities.
Across multiple studies, the quality of teaching emerges as one of the
most consistent and impactful process variables. Escribano et al. (2020)
emphasize that, after socioeconomic status, the most important determinant of student achievement is teaching quality. Their analysis of
underperforming schools in Chile highlights significant variation in
teaching practices within schools, suggesting that effective teaching
exists even in low-performing settings, and thus can be leveraged to
improve outcomes for disadvantaged students.
This is supported by broader literature, such as the findings from
Jensen et al. (2016), who argue that “generic quality”, comprising affective support, classroom organization, and instructional quality, is
critical for student learning. These dimensions of teaching not only
support cognitive development but also contribute to motivation and
student engagement, especially when contextualized with cultural
relevance (“local quality”).
Moreover, while structural inputs (e.g., school infrastructure,
teacher–student ratios) have been traditionally emphasized in policy,
evidence increasingly supports the idea that process variables moderate
or even outweigh the effects of these structural conditions. For instance,
Gamazo and Martínez-Abad (2020) find that in high socioeconomic
status schools, academic performance is more influenced by process
variables, such as metacognitive strategies and motivation. In contrast,
in low socioeconomic status schools, performance is more dependent on
national-level economic indicators, implying weaker local instructional
conditions. Jensen et al. (2016) further reinforce this idea, explaining
that in Latin America, process variables are often under-assessed and
undervalued, despite their critical role in mediating the impact of socioeconomic disparities.
Similarly, inequality in educational opportunities in Latin America is
heavily shaped by unequal access to high-quality teaching. As noted by
Román (2010), classroom dynamics and teacher-student interactions
account for a substantial proportion of the variance in student
achievement once background factors (e.g., socioeconomic status,
parental education) are controlled. The research underscores the
importance of focusing reforms not just on access, but also on what
happens within the classroom.
Studies, such as those conducted by Escribano et al. (2020), also
show that despite systemic inequalities, there is meaningful variance in
teacher quality even within socioeconomically disadvantaged schools,
suggesting that improvement efforts should focus on intra-school
teacher development and support rather than broad structural reforms
alone.

Nevertheless, despite recognizing the importance of process variables, efforts to evaluate and improve process variables face several
challenges. Jensen et al. (2016) highlight the need for conceptually
robust assessment tools to capture instructional time, teaching quality,
and contextual relevance. These tools must be sensitive to cultural and
contextual differences, particularly in multicultural societies, for
instance Mexico. Moreover, classroom observations, such as those used
in Chile, have been instrumental in revealing the diversity in teaching
quality that standardized test scores alone cannot capture. This indicates
the need to shift from a purely outcome-based evaluation to one that
incorporates teaching processes and pedagogical practices.
Despite extensive research on school performance, there remains a
gap in understanding the combined effects of individual, family, and
school-level factors in the Latin American context and given the
persistence of educational inequalities in Latin America, further
research is needed to assess the extent to which school inputs can
compensate for disadvantages associated with socioeconomic
background.
The evidence from Latin America strongly suggests that improving
the quality of teaching and other process variables within classrooms is
essential for raising student achievement and reducing educational
inequality. Structural reforms alone are insufficient unless they are
complemented by efforts to understand and enhance what actually
happens in classrooms, particularly for marginalized populations. This
shift requires both better assessment tools and a policy focus on teaching
as a dynamic, context-sensitive process.
In this context, this study addresses the gap in understanding how
individual, family, and school-level factors jointly influence student
performance in Latin America. Using the PISA 2018 dataset, it systematically analyzes these determinants to inform targeted public policies
aimed at reducing educational inequalities and improving outcomes.
Given the persistent disparities in the region, this research contributes
by examining how these factors shape performance and impact educational quality and equity. Recognizing the varied effects across socioeconomic contexts, the study also explores the extent to which school
inputs can mitigate disadvantages, offering a comprehensive perspective
on school performance dynamics.
3. Methodology
3.1. Database
This work used the 2018 database published by the Programme for
International Student Assessment (PISA) from the Organization for
Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) as a source of information. The analysis focuses on the Latin American countries that
participated in PISA: Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica,
Dominican Republic, Mexico, Panama, Peru and Uruguay.
According to the OECD, PISA aims to assess the extent to which
students nearing the end of compulsory education have acquired some
of the knowledge and skills needed to participate fully in the knowledge
society. The tests have been held periodically every three years since
2000, assessing the performance of students aged between 15 years and
3 months and 16 years and 2 months attending a school, regardless of
the school year in which they are enrolled, in three subject areas:
mathematics, science, and reading. The sampling design is carried out in
two stages: first, schools in the participating countries are selected, with
a minimum of 150 schools in each country; then 35 students are selected
in each educational institution to be assessed (OECD, 2017).
All OECD member countries participate in each round, along with
some associated countries, such as most of the Latin American countries
on which this paper focuses. One of the most significant features of the
PISA tests, which makes them a very useful resource, is that the data can
be considered homogeneous, as both the tests and the surveys designed
for this purpose are the same in all countries. Having homogenized information allows for straightforward comparisons between countries,

Concerning the process variables, Table 3 shows that the variable
’Feedback from teachers perceived by the student’ has an average close

The ’Disciplinary climate in test language lessons’ variable, which

perception of the classroom environment than the OECD average,

index had an average of 0.3700. This reflects a slight predominance of

Overall, these descriptive statistics reveal important aspects of the
educational environment in Latin America. The analysis highlights significant disparities among schools in terms of the availability of resources, classroom discipline, pedagogical practices, and teacher
behavior. These findings underscore the challenge of ensuring equitable
and high-quality education across the region.

feedback, although often highlighted as fundamental to learning,

6. Discussion
This paper aims to identify the main factors affecting school performance in Latin America, and some relevant results are worth
mentioning. Firstly, differences in the level of performance between the
countries studied and in the skills assessed were found. While the best
performances in reading, mathematics, and science are found in Chile
(417, 452, and 444) and Uruguay (418, 427, and 426), respectively, the
worst results in these skills are found in the Dominican Republic (325,
342, and 336) and Panama (353, 377, and 465), respectively.
Approximately 26 % of Latin American academics feel insecure, and
this fact has an impact on their school performance. Prestes (2021)
found a similar result in their study on the impact of violence on the
academic performance of Brazilian students. The study showed that
violence has a direct and negative effect on academic performance, and
also increases the likelihood of students failing to complete their
educational training.
The results indicate that children with a higher background perform
better than those with a lower socioeconomic and cultural level, which
is in line with the literature (Souza et al., 2018). Thus, families with
better socioeconomic conditions will often be able to provide more
educational resources at home or choose the schools that will provide
them with these resources. Moreover, in general, qualified parents can
decide to invest more of their time and energy in their children’s education, or they can choose to interact with their children in ways that
help them succeed in school. However, only 57 % of mothers and 54 %
of fathers have a high school education or more in Latin America.
Regarding individual aspects, the results indicate the significant
impact of failing on performance, which reaches 27.5 %, 40.8 %, and
34 % of Latin American, Colombian and Brazilian students, respectively.
This result corroborates those found by Aberastury (2020), Gómez
(2013), and Marchionni et al. (2012). In other words, repeating at least
one school grade has negative and significant impacts on educational
performance.
The importance of preschool is a matter of discussion in the literature. Most of the studies emphasize its importance because this phase is
essential for developing students’ cognitive skills and can influence their
ability to learn in subsequent phases. It is noteworthy that more than
80 % of Argentinian, Mexican, and Uruguayan students have completed
preschool, while only 55 % of Costa Rican students have done so. The
evaluation found positive school performance in only one of the assessed
skills, Mathematics. This contrasts with other tests carried out in Brazil,
for example, using the SAEB database, such as the one developed by Curi
and Menezes-Filho (2009), in which preschool had a significant and
favorable impact on performance in both Portuguese and Mathematics.

Instructional adaptation was one of the most consistently positive
factors, particularly for students from disadvantaged backgrounds.
Adaptive teaching practices that consider students’ pace, interests, and
learning difficulties are effective in reducing educational inequalities.
This upshot aligns closely with that of Gamazo and Martínez-Abad
(2020), who emphasize the pivotal role of pedagogical adaptation in
socially vulnerable contexts. Jensen et al. (2016) emphasize instructional adaptation as a core element of ’local quality’ in teaching and a
critical factor in diverse and unequal contexts, such as those found in
Latin America.
Furthermore, teacher behaviors that hinder learning, such as disorganization, lack of preparation, or demotivation, showed a positive
correlation with science performance. When recurrent, such behaviors
compromise not only content learning but also the overall classroom
climate. Román (2010) emphasizes that the teacher’s attitude has a
direct impact on students’ motivation and engagement, making it a
critical variable for educational success. Escribano et al. (2020) warn
that harmful teacher practices can undermine student and school efforts,
even when other resources are present.
Thus, public policies that expand school infrastructure, the educational level of teachers, and the quality of education can contribute to
reducing inequalities of opportunity. In addition, these differentials in
educational attainment in favor of the highest-income bracket are
maintained in relation to school characteristics, which have greater effects for the highest-income bracket. On the other hand, the characteristics of schools contribute more to the educational performance of
students from families with a background below the average for OECD
countries. Importantly, process variables, such as instructional practices,
classroom climate, teacher feedback, and adaptation of instruction, also
play an important role, especially for disadvantaged students, as they
directly influence learning experiences and can mitigate the effects of
socioeconomic disparities. Therefore, by focusing on equity-driven
policies, governments can help ensure that all students, regardless of
their background, have access to quality education and equal opportunities for academic success.

8. Final conclusion
This paper aimed to verify the impacts of the main determinants of
school performance of students from ages 15–16 for Latin American
countries, considering the educational production function and the 2018
PISA database conducted by the OECD. The estimates show the importance of individual and family characteristics on students’ educational
performance. Among the students’ characteristics, preschool completion
can be mentioned, as well as their repetition status and gender, leading
to significant impacts on all the skills assessed by PISA. There are also
significant differences between Latin American countries.
Regarding the socioeconomic and cultural characteristics of the
family, it is important to highlight that the main variable influencing the
school performance of Latin American students is the availability of
cultural and educational resources, books, and computers at home. In
addition, parental schooling also has a positive and significant effect on
the academic achievement of Latin American students. Examining the
determinant factors at the school level, it can be seen that the municipality size had significant impacts on the school performance of Latin
American students in 2018.
Thus, the results indicate that, on average, students from economically advantaged backgrounds perform better than those from less
advantaged ones. This implies that existing public policies are insufficient to address the economic disparities faced by students in Latin
America. Therefore, higher-income students are more likely to perform
better, and lower-income students are more likely to perform worse. In
addition, the study reinforces that process-related factors, such as
teaching strategies, classroom climate, and feedback mechanisms, are
critical for understanding and enhancing student performance, particularly in contexts of socioeconomic vulnerability.
From this perspective, the State in Latin America has not yet been
able to provide education in a way that compensates for existing social
inequalities. Thus, the results of this work suggest that the education
system in Latin America has some important challenges. The main one
would be to expand public policies aimed at reducing social inequalities
that perpetuate inequalities in educational opportunities. Another major
challenge would be to ensure the safety of students in schools, as unfortunately, insecurity is still a reality in Latin American schools. Largesmall city achievement gaps can be better addressed through initiatives
or reforms at the school level to take into account differences in student
composition. Additionally, there is a need for teacher training and
improvement programs, investments in infrastructure and modernization, and projects that value education and increased student retention
in schools.
Despite its contributions, this study has limitations, including the
reliance on cross-sectional data, which restricts causal inferences, and its
focus on ten countries, limiting generalizability to other contexts.
Additionally, the 2018 PISA sample for Latin American countries is only
representative at the national level, which restricts the possibility of
making inferences by subnational regions.
Therefore, incorporating the regional dimension into the educational
inequalities study, particularly in countries with large regional disparities, would represent a major advance. Future research should also use a
mixed-methods approach to further strengthen the understanding of
how structural and instructional factors shape educational outcomes in
the region.

7. Implications and contributions
This study makes a significant contribution by incorporating not only
structural determinants of educational performance, such as student,
family, and school-level characteristics but also process variables related
to instructional practices and classroom environment, which have been
underexplored in the Latin American context. By applying a multilevel
education production function using the PISA 2018 dataset, the research
provides robust evidence of how classroom-level processes can either
mitigate or exacerbate the effects of socioeconomic disadvantage.
From a theoretical perspective, the study advances the literature on
educational effectiveness by integrating process-based dimensions into
traditional models that often focus only on inputs and outcomes. The
findings underscore the value of multidimensional frameworks that
consider how pedagogical practices mediate structural inequalities.
In terms of policy, the results suggest that improving educational
equity in Latin America requires more than increasing access to resources. Investments must also prioritize teacher training, classroom
management, and adaptive teaching strategies—especially in disadvantaged schools where students are more vulnerable to contextual and
socioeconomic challenges.
Regarding educational practice, the evidence supports a shift toward
pedagogical approaches that are responsive to students’ needs. Interventions that promote a positive disciplinary climate encourage
teacher adaptability, and changing the way feedback is delivered can
have a significant impact on student learning. Schools must therefore
focus on enhancing day-to-day instructional quality as a complement to
systemic reforms.
