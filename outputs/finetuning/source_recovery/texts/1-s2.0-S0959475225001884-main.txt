Background: For enhancing student learning in classrooms, high-quality teaching is essential. Research highlighted core dimensions of effective teaching, including classroom management, student support, and cognitive
activation. However, traditional methods of assessing teaching effectiveness dimensions (e.g., student surveys)
have limitations, including rating biases and resource intensiveness.
Aims: To overcome these challenges, we explored machine learning (ML) algorithms for the automated assessment of teaching effectiveness.
Sample: The study analyzed multimodal data—such as video, audio, and transcripts—from the Global Teaching
Insights study, which included video recordings and transcripts from 46 teachers and 1,132 students in Germany.
Method: Scores for 18 teaching effectiveness subdimensions from three core dimensions were automatically
generated by training attention-based ML models on multimodal features extracted from pretrained encoders.
These ML-generated scores were compared with scores provided by human experts. A content validity study was
conducted, where human experts evaluated the plausibility of ML-generated scores against human-generated
scores. Structural equation models were used to assess the relationship between teaching effectiveness subdimensions and students’ tested achievement.
Results: ML-generated scores were more reliable for some subdimensions (e.g., nature of discourse), and they
were also plausible and content valid. ML-generated scores achieved higher absolute accuracy than human scores
in 11 of 18 subdimensions. Limitations include reliance on human ratings as ground truth and inconsistent
predictive validity, underscoring the need for refined models to generate actionable insights, such as real-time
feedback systems.
Conclusions: The findings provide valuable insights for the development of automated feedback, enhancing the
practical application of teaching effectiveness assessments.

Traditionally, dimensions of teaching effectiveness are assessed with
student ratings (Jaekel et al., 2020), teacher ratings (Wagner et al.,
2016), or lesson observations (Praetorius & Charalambous, 2018). Still,
these approaches bring challenges, including reliability, validity, and
cost-effectiveness issues. Thus, it seems promising to investigate the
potential to employ machine learning (ML) algorithms for an automated
and alternative assessment of teaching effectiveness. Unlike traditional
methods, ML can analyze teaching and learning interactions from a
third-party perspective, integrating diverse classroom data sources such
as lesson plans, board notes, and student work to enhance reliability and
validity. Additionally, ML is cost-effective, potentially overcoming the
limitations of manual classroom observations while bringing unique
affordances and constraints. A decisive advantage of ML-based automated assessments of teaching effectiveness is that multimodal data can
be analyzed efficiently, providing comprehensive and immediate (or
even real-time) insights into complex teaching and learning interactions,
extending prior work on single subdimensions (Hou et al., 2024) to a
holistic evaluation of 18 subdimensions across three core dimensions.
A recent study showed that encouragement and warmth (a subdimension of student support) can be reliably assessed with ML-based
methods applied to multimodal data, including video, audio, and transcript data (Hou et al., 2024). Moreover, in a follow-up study, the authors presented a multimodal ML model for automated classroom
discourse assessment, integrating text, audio, and video to evaluate key
discourse dimensions (Hou et al., 2025). The results showed that text
played a dominant role, whereas acoustic features improved consistency
with human ratings, achieving reliability comparable to human raters.
In this study, we extended the latter approach to additional subdimensions of teaching effectiveness to get a cost- and time-efficient but
holistic (e.g., capturing the complexity of classroom dynamics comprehensively) understanding of complex teaching and learning interactions.
This approach allowed us to compare automated assessments of key
teaching effectiveness subdimensions by leveraging multimodal teaching data, including video recordings, audio data, and transcripts. We
explored the extent to which dimensions of teaching effectiveness can be
accurately predicted by automated methods compared to human expert
raters and analyzed the reliability and validity of the automated scores.

outcomes unfold via student engagement processes, which act as
mediators—specifically at the student level—such as time on task, depth
of processing, motivation, emotions, and affect during learning (Alp
Christ et al., 2024; Klieme et al., 2009).
2.2. Conceptualizations of teaching effectiveness
Conceptualizations of teaching effectiveness vary widely and include
approaches that focus on teachers’ effects on students’ achievement (e.
g., Rivkin et al., 2005), emphasize domain-specific teacher competencies (e.g., Kleickmann et al., 2013; Kunter et al., 2013), or define
overarching dimensions of teaching effectiveness (Klieme et al., 2009;
Pianta & Hamre, 2009). These dimensions are conceptualized as
domain-general practices that transcend specific subjects, focusing on
pedagogical interactions rather than content-specific methods. The
present study followed the tradition of the latter approach, which focuses on overarching dimensions of teaching effectiveness that are
crucial for successful learning and students’ academic achievement.
More specifically, this approach focuses on underlying quality subdimensions, such as the quality of the teacher’s pedagogical interactions, meaningful cognitive engagement, support of students’
academic and social development, and the clarity of instructional goals
(Klieme et al., 2009; Lipowsky et al., 2009; Pianta & Hamre, 2009;
Seidel & Shavelson, 2007) rather than on specific learning arrangements
(e.g., teacher-led vs. student-led approaches) or specific methods (e.g.,
direct instruction, inquiry-based learning).
German-speaking countries commonly distinguish between three
basic dimensions (TBD; i.e., classroom management, student support, and
cognitive activation) of teaching effectiveness (Klieme et al., 2009;
Praetorius et al., 2018, 2020), a differentiation that is also found in international work (e.g., Pianta & Hamre, 2009). According to the TBD
framework, effective classroom management includes clear rules, routines, and proactive handling of disruptions, ensuring that learning time
is maximized and students remain attentive and engaged. Student support emphasizes the importance of a learning environment that caters to
individual student needs, including teacher sensitivity, fostering positive relationships between teachers and students, and providing
emotional and social support. Finally, cognitive activation involves
engaging students in intellectually stimulating tasks that promote deep
understanding and critical thinking.

2. Theoretical background
2.1. Teaching effectiveness as a crucial factor for successful student
learning
Teaching effectiveness, encompassing classroom management,
cognitive activation, and student support, has been demonstrated to be
crucial in student learning processes and outcomes (Klieme & Nilsen,
2022; Seidel & Shavelson, 2007). For instance, classroom management
strategies, such as establishing rules and routines, helped reduce
disruptive student behavior (Oliver et al., 2011) and predicted student
achievement (Korpershoek et al., 2016; Patall et al., 2023). The effects of
teaching on student learning follow a relatively systematic pattern, with
the strongest effects observed for domain-specific aspects of teaching
that are most closely linked to students’ learning processes (Seidel &
Shavelson, 2007).
However, previous research has also shown that the effects are not
always consistent, and the patterns of findings are somewhat more
complex (Seidel & Shavelson, 2007). In fact, the effects of teaching
effectiveness dimensions on student outcomes were confirmed in about
half of the studies in a literature review on multilevel longitudinal designs by Praetorius et al. (2018). For instance, analyzing data from the
2019 Trends in Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) and its national
extensions in Norway, Senden, Teig, and Nilsen (2023) found statistically significant positive relationships between classroom management
and student achievement. By contrast, the effects of cognitive activation
and student support varied, particularly across grade levels. Blömeke

2.3. Assessing teaching effectiveness
The assessment of teaching effectiveness also differs between studies
and countries (Klieme & Nilsen, 2022; Senden et al., 2022; Teig & Luoto,
2024), and any measurement of teaching effectiveness will inevitably
have limitations (White et al., 2025). Teaching effectiveness is measured
using a variety of approaches, ranging from artifacts, teacher and student
surveys, observations, and simulations to the collection of physiological

data (Bell et al., in preparation). However, we focused on student surveys,
teacher surveys, and observations, as they can capture the entirety of
teaching in real classrooms.
Surveys are used in large-scale studies such as TIMSS and the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA). Teachers and
students hold different perceptions of lessons, resulting in divergence
between their assessments of teaching effectiveness (Wisniewski et al.,
2021).
Teacher surveys are valuable because teachers are essential stakeholders in a classroom. Surveying teachers offers insights that are
challenging to obtain through other methods (e.g., teachers’ internal
state; Holzberger & Prestele, 2021). However, teacher ratings have low
predictive validity and may be influenced by individual biases and
classroom contexts, leading to variability in reliability and validity
(Wagner et al., 2016).
Student surveys are valuable because students are also important
stakeholders in a classroom and can provide valid insights into teaching
effectiveness (De Jong & Westerhof, 2001; Fauth et al., 2014; Senden,
Nilsen, & Teig, 2023). Moreover, they are time-efficient and
cost-efficient, and predict students’ achievement (cf. Jaekel et al., 2020,
2022). However, student ratings are idiosyncratic perceptions (Göllner
et al., 2018) affected by rating tendencies (Göllner et al., 2016). Additionally, slight variations in item wording can influence the measurement of teaching effectiveness (Praetorius & Charalambous, 2018), and
student ratings of teaching effectiveness show limited comparability
across countries (Senden, Teig, & Nilsen, 2023).
Structured classroom observations by trained raters, often guided by
standardized protocols, provide external and systematic evaluations of
teaching practices (Praetorius & Charalambous, 2018). Classroom observations often have high content validity, and when conducted using
recorded classroom videos, raters can take the time to review and reflect
on classroom situations by rewatching and pausing the videos, allowing
them to review artifacts and, for instance, zoom into the board, and also
apply multiple teaching effectiveness frameworks. Well-known observation protocols, all of which share the assumption of the importance of
underlying teaching effectiveness dimensions, include the Classroom
Assessment Scoring System (CLASS; Pianta et al., 2008), the Unterrichtsfeedbackbogen (UFB, an observation system for school practice in
German-speaking countries; Fauth et al., 2024), the protocol from the
Global Teaching Insights (GTI) study (OECD, 2020a), and
domain-specific protocols in, for example, mathematics (e.g., Mathematical Quality of Instruction [MQI]; Hill et al., 2008; Learning Mathematics for Teaching Project, 2011) or English (e.g., Protocol for
Language Arts Teaching Observation [PLATO]; Grossman et al., 2014).
For an overview of classroom observation manuals, see Bell et al. (2019),
Klette and Blikstad-Balas (2018), and Praetorius and Charalambous
(2018). However, classroom observations are resource-intensive in
terms of time and financial cost, and are prone to unreliability (i.e., low
interrater agreement; Bell et al., 2019; Göllner et al., 2016, 2018).
Due to the limitations of traditional approaches for assessing teaching effectiveness, it has been challenging to capture the complexity of
classroom dynamics comprehensively. Thus, important research questions remain underexplored, such as the variability in teaching effectiveness dimensions within teachers over time (Polikoff, 2015), the
dynamics of teaching effectiveness across multiple lessons by the same
teacher (for a rare exception using traditional assessment methods, see
Fauth, Wagner, et al., 2020), or the interplay between classroom compositions and teaching effectiveness.

(Blikstein, 2013, pp. 102–106), can be used. Automatic AI-based assessments of teaching effectiveness dimensions can provide teachers
with feedback on their teaching during or after lessons, enabling them to
optimize their teaching practices (Grossman, 2018; Grossman et al.,
2009; Kincade et al., 2020), such as using classroom management scripts
(Wolff et al., 2021). At least two strands can be identified when focusing
on AI-based applications in real classroom teaching.
As a first strand, there are numerous AI-based applications that
support teachers’ daily teaching activities (e.g., tracking students’
progress and real-time performance, scoring written responses and essays). For instance, in a randomized controlled study with upper secondary English learners, Meyer et al. (2024) showed that
GPT-3.5-turbo-generated feedback for essay revisions improved students’ revision performance, motivation, and positive emotions
compared with no feedback. The potential of Large Language Models
(LLMs) to score student essays and thus relieve teachers was also shown
in a recent study by Seßler et al. (2024), who evaluated the performance
and reliability of different LLMs, including GPT-3.5, GPT-4, o1, LLaMA
3–70B, and Mixtral 8 × 7B for student essay scoring. Shi and Choi (2024)
provided a systematic review of such AI-based applications in teaching.
As a second strand, several studies have focused on automatically
capturing student behaviors (e.g., engagement and attention) and
teacher behaviors (e.g., hand gestures, facial expressions, and body
language) using behavioral data, often multimodal data, such as video
and audio data (see Fütterer et al., 2025). For instance, Goldberg et al.
(2021) explored the development and validation of automated methods
for evaluating students’ visible engagement during classroom instruction. Their research focused on creating a machine vision-based system
that analyzes students’ gaze, head pose, and facial expressions to
determine their attention levels. Sümer et al. (2023) investigated automated methods for measuring student engagement during authentic
classroom instruction by using audiovisual recordings, training deep
embeddings —that is, compressed high-dimensional feature representations learned through deep neural networks—for attention and affective features, and developing engagement classifiers that achieve
notable AUC scores. More examples include studies on the automated
detection of students’ hand raising (e.g., Bühler et al., 2023), engagement (e.g., Sharma et al., 2022), or mind wandering (e.g., Bosch &
D’Mello, 2021; Bühler et al., 2024).
Besides these two strands, only a few previous research works have
made initial attempts to automate the assessment of teaching subdimensions to gain insights into the quality of whole lessons. Some
previous research has focused only on one modality (e.g., texts) to assess
teaching practices. For instance, Liu and Cohen (2021) showed that
automatically generated scores of interactive instruction, based on
lesson transcripts, positively predicted value-added scores. A rare study
using multimodal data by Pan et al. (2022) applied a multimodal ML
framework to predict the quality of online teaching (i.e., clarity, classroom interaction, technical management of online teaching, empathy,
and time management) and presented promising results regarding the
capability of ML. Foster, Korban, et al. (2024) and Foster, Youngs, et al.
(2024) evaluated the effectiveness of neural network models (i.e.,
BaS-Net, MLAD, and LSTR) for detecting teacher and student instructional activities in audio and video data from elementary classroom
videos (i.e., a multimodal approach), focusing on low-inference
instructional activities (e.g., students worked alone at a separate desk
or in small groups, teacher moved around the classroom or not, teacher
sat on a chair or not, student sat on floor or not, teacher nodded). Lazarides et al. (2024) employed a computer vision-based ML model to
assess teachers’ nonverbal immediacy (i.e., behavioral aspects such as
gesture intensity, perceived physical distance, and facial expression) in
classroom video data. Nonverbal immediacy mediated the relationship
between teacher self-efficacy for student engagement and students’
perception of enthusiastic teaching, ultimately influencing student
interest.

Such approaches, which focus on teachers’ visible behavior and have
been dubbed low-inference approaches, are also used in commercial applications, such as Speaker Coach in PowerPoint from Microsoft. Speaker
Coach is a tool that presenters can use to practice their verbal presentations by receiving personalized insights into their speech during a

Another research group is exploring the automated detection of
relevant teaching situations, working on the Video Interaction Analysis

can use as a basis for reflection. The system is a work in progress. In the

However, most existing automated approaches that assess teaching
aspects tend to focus predominantly on specific, low-inference instructional features that are relevant but have limited ability to comprehensively cover students’ learning activities and outcomes,2 rather than
on theoretically grounded and evidence-based dimensions of teaching
effectiveness. A few exceptions focus, for instance, on automatically
capturing classroom discourse (for asking questions, see Donnelly et al.,
2017, pp. 218–227; Huang et al., 2020; for teaching discourse such as
instructional speaking, see Jensen et al., 2020, pp. 1–13). For instance,
Tran et al. (2023) presented promising results for natural language
processing (NLP) techniques that automatically assess classroom discussion quality using rubric scores (e.g., the teacher presses students for
information, and students support claims with evidence).
One of the few examples focusing on teaching effectiveness dimensions is the study by Wang and Demszky (2023), who utilized a
ChatGPT model (gpt-3.5-turbo; zero-shot prompt) to predict observation
scores based on the CLASS and MQI rubrics. They found that ChatGPT
performed poorly at scoring teaching transcripts. For instance, the
models consistently predicted the same scores for the same MQI or
CLASS items, indicating that the predictions were more influenced by
the dimension descriptions rather than the transcript evidence or
prompting methodology. Additionally, the model tended to assign
higher values to all CLASS dimensions than human ratings.
As another example, Bradford (2024) explored the usefulness of ML
(e.g., logistic regression, random forests) to score teacher observations,
focusing on aspects of project-based learning or teachers’ integration of
Next Generation Science Standards in teaching. She demonstrated that
ML can improve scoring efficiency and accuracy relative to baseline
random predictions. For instance, the baseline accuracy was 32 %,
whereas ML methods achieved an accuracy range of 49 %–57 %.
However, the results highlight ongoing challenges, such as overfitting
and lower accuracy compared with human raters. Hou et al. (2024)
demonstrated the potential of a multimodal approach for evaluating
Encouragement and Warmth, a subdimension of student support, by
combining facial and speech emotion recognition with large language
models. This study leveraged the GTI data set, analyzing classroom
videos with multimodal ML techniques and ChatGPT-based zero-shot
annotations. The findings showed that these methods achieved

predictive accuracy comparable to human interrater reliability (r = .51),
highlighting the importance of verbal and emotional cues in assessing
this subdimension of teaching effectiveness.
In summary, with few exceptions, existing approaches to automating
the assessment of teaching and learning interactions often focus on individual student activities rather than the entire classroom situation or
on low-inference aspects, whose ability to comprehensively cover students’ learning activities and outcomes may be limited.
2.5. Research questions
Despite progress in recent studies, a comprehensive study addressing
the automated assessment of a broader range of teaching effectiveness
subdimensions—spanning core dimensions such as classroom management, student support, and cognitive activation—remains lacking. Such
research is crucial for advancing the automated, holistic evaluation of
teaching effectiveness using established evidence-based criteria. AI
systems that can process multimodal data sets (e.g., text, audio, and
video) can enhance the assessment of teaching by gaining holistic insights (Lee et al., 2023).
Accordingly, we explored the possibilities of automated predictions
of teaching effectiveness subdimensions using multimodal teaching data
such as video recordings, audio data, and transcripts. We aimed to
investigate whether it is feasible to imitate human ratings of teaching
effectiveness subdimensions and thereby develop an AI-based rater,
which would allow for the efficient generation of observational ratings
of teaching effectiveness subdimensions without compromising reliability or validity. Regarding this objective, we investigated three
research questions (RQs). Based on prior multimodal studies (e.g., Hou
et al., 2025), we hypothesize that ML-generated scores will match
human reliability in discourse-related subdimensions but may underperform in classroom management due to contextual nuances.
RQ1 addresses the reliability of ML-generated scores of teaching
effectiveness subdimensions:
RQ1) How does the reliability of ML-generated scores for teaching
effectiveness subdimensions compare to that of human-generated
scores?
RQ2 and RQ3 address the validity of ML-generated scores of teaching
effectiveness subdimensions. It remains crucial to investigate whether
ML-generated scores of teaching effectiveness subdimensions can achieve content validity comparable to human-generated ratings, ensuring
their reliability and applicability in educational research and practice.
RQ2) How does the content validity of ML-generated scores for
teaching effectiveness subdimensions compare to that of humangenerated scores?
As outlined in the theoretical background, teaching effectiveness is
assumed to be important for successful student learning processes and
outcomes. However, the strength of this relationship has varied (e.g., in
some data, no effect or even negative effects were observed). For the
specific GTI data we used, statistically significant relationships between
the provision and use of core teaching effectiveness dimensions (e.g.,
effective classroom management) were not observed in the participating
German classrooms (Grünkorn et al., 2020). Nevertheless, we aimed to
explore the relationship between ML-generated scores and students’
achievement to check whether ML-generated scores show the same
patterns as human-generated scores, to gain insights into whether
different variants of the ML-generated scores may help to improve the
understanding of teaching effectiveness, and to generate additional
hypotheses for future directions when elaborating more in depth on
teaching and learning interactions.
RQ3) How does the predictive validity of ML-generated scores of
teaching effectiveness compare to that of human-generated scores?
Importantly, we did not compare the teaching effectiveness scores of
specific subdimensions derived from different sources (i.e., MLgenerated, students, teachers, and observers), as these relationships

3.4. Achievement test

3. Method

We used students’ mathematics achievement at posttest as the second
dependent variable. The achievement test pertained to quadratic equations, a standard topic across educational systems worldwide (Grünkorn
et al., 2020). The students were administered 30 (pretest) and 25
(posttest) items (e.g., multiple-choice, modeling problems) covering
quadratic equations to assess procedural skills and basic understanding,
transformations (e.g., completing squares) to assess conceptual knowledge, and deriving equations for real-world scenarios to assess
problem-solving and application skills. All items were scored for the
accuracy and completeness of the solution, and item response theory
(IRT) scores (i.e., weighted likelihood estimates; Warm, 1989) were
used to ensure a more precise and reliable measurement of student
performance. For an example item from the mathematics test used in
Germany, see Grünkorn et al. (2020). The pretest and posttest in our
study demonstrated perfect internal consistency (KR 20).

3.1. Database, sample, and instruments
We used data from the GTI study (Klieme et al., 2023), which was
part of the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) study
conducted by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS)
study conducted in Germany, aiming to better understand the quality of
teaching practices (OECD, 2020a). The GTI study is alternatively
referred to in the literature as the Talis Video Study (Ingram et al.,
2020).
The data set we used comprised 92 video records of mathematics
lessons focused on quadratic equations (40–90 min), data from 46
teachers (M = 14.94 years of teaching experience [SD = 11.38], 45 %
female [binary variable—male/female]), and data from 1132 students
(M = 15.00 years old [SD = .81], 53 % female [binary variable—male/
female]) on the lower secondary level.
The same teaching unit was assessed in all participating classrooms.
Two lessons (each lasting about 45–60 min) on the teaching unit
(typically consisting of 8–12 lessons) were assessed for each teacher,
depending on the school’s schedule and curriculum. Data were gathered
in the form of video recordings, questionnaires, and an achievement test that
was administered before (t1) and after (t2) the teaching units (i.e.,
repeated-measures design).

3.5. Data analysis
3.5.1. Automated assessment of teaching effectiveness subdimensions
In Step 1, we predicted human-generated scores in the teaching
effectiveness subdimensions (see Table 1) following the approach
introduced by Hou et al. (2025). A detailed description of the preprocessing routines, model pipeline, and training setup can be found in
the paper by Hou et al. (2025). Fig. 1 depicts our proposed multimodal
architecture, which integrates rich information from diverse data
streams (i.e., video, transcript, and audio) and leverages multitask
learning to assess multiple dimensions of teaching practices
simultaneously.

3.2. Video recordings
Two cameras videotaped the lessons simultaneously at 25 frames per
s. Camera 1 was positioned to focus on the teacher (i.e., recorded the
teacher’s movements, gestures, and interactions with the class), and
Camera 2 was positioned to capture the entire classroom (i.e., recorded
the layout of the classroom, student movements, and interactions among
students). Multimodal data streams (i.e., audio, video, and transcripts)
based on these recordings were available for analysis. However, it is
important to note that the quality of the video data was constrained. For
example, not all students were continuously visible throughout the recordings, which may limit the completeness of interaction analyses.
Each video-recorded lesson was divided into 16-min segments (i.e., 367
16-min segments in total for the 92 video recordings), and two randomly
assigned human raters scored each segment on 18 teaching effectiveness
subdimensions of six components reflecting three core dimensions (see
Table 1). The 18 teaching effectiveness subdimensions, the six components, and the three core dimensions were based on diverse perspectives
from the participating countries and the existing research literature, but
largely overlapped with the TBD (OECD, 2020a). For detailed documentation of the operationalization, see the Technical Report (OECD,
2020b). Two certified human raters scored each segment using the
study’s observation protocol (see the GTI’s Observation Tools).

3.5.1.1. Unimodal feature extraction. First, we employed state-of-the-art
pretrained models as unimodal encoders to process each modality
independently and generate respective embeddings (i.e., feature vectors
consisting of numerical values), serving as input for the subsequent
fusion and prediction modules. These models are typically trained on
vast amounts of data, equipping them with the capability to capture
sophisticated semantic features from raw signals. For the text modality,
we utilized JinaBERT-Base-de,3 an advanced text-embedding model
capable of handling long German sequences up to 8,192 tokens,
particularly suitable for our extensive transcripts. The model converts
each word into a 768-dimensional embedding vector, which we subsequently aggregate at the utterance level to optimize computational efficiency. Similarly, we employed XLSR-German4 and clip-vit-largepatch14-3365 to extract acoustic and visual representations from the
audio and video data for every 10-s chunk.
3.5.1.2. Multimodal fusion. Different modalities likely provide complementary information featuring diverse perspectives on classroom
dynamics. Drawing inspiration from recent advances in multimodal affective computing (Shi & Huang, 2023, pp. 14752–14766; Wu et al.,
2024, pp. 3588–3602), we adopted a text-driven multimodal fusion
approach based on attention mechanisms (Vaswani et al., 2017). The
mechanisms mirror human cognitive processes by selectively focusing
on salient information while filtering peripheral details. As illustrated in
Fig. 1, our architecture incorporated two sequential cross-attention encoders to facilitate intermodal interactions through text-guided retrieval
of relevant features from the audio and video modalities. A subsequent
self-attention encoder processed the temporal dynamics of the fused
multimodal representations.

3.5.2. Implementation
We applied a teacher-independent five-fold cross-validation
approach to model construction. The data set (i.e., 367 segments) was
divided into five equal parts, each serving as a test set once, while the
remaining four parts were used for training. By rotating through all
folds, we gathered predictions for each segment in the data set. Moreover, to evaluate a model’s generalizability to new teachers, all segments from the same teacher were kept within the same fold. The choice
of five folds reflects a balance between computational efficiency and
evaluation stability, particularly given the limited number of teacher
identities in the data set. The average human rating for each segment
served as the ground truth for model training.

3.5.5. Predictive validity of ML-generated scores (RQ3)
To gain insights into the predictive validity of the ML-generated
scores, we examined whether they predicted student mathematics
achievement as well as the human-generated scores did. We performed
structural equation modeling (SEM) using the R package lavaan version
.6.17 (Rosseel, 2012) to investigate the relationships between teaching
effectiveness scores and student outcomes. Separate models were estimated for different types of teaching effectiveness scores: (a)
human-generated mean scores, (b) ML-generated scores based on text,
(c) ML-generated scores based on text and audio, and (d) ML-generated
scores based on text, audio, and video. In the first model (M1), the
dependent variables were predicted solely by the teaching effectiveness
scores. In the second model (M2), we extended M1 by including the
teacher-level and student-level covariates (see Table S1 in our OSF
project) to account for additional sources of variance. We applied the
Benjamini-Hochberg correction (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) to control the false discovery rate (i.e., we adjusted the p values for multiple
comparisons within each model).
To appropriately model the hierarchical structure of the data—students nested within teachers—we employed cluster-robust standard errors. This approach accounts for the dependency among
observations and provides more accurate standard errors without
requiring the explicit modeling of random effects (McNeish et al., 2017).
We used Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) estimation to
address missing data. FIML is particularly advantageous, as it uses all
available data while producing unbiased parameter estimates, assuming
data are missing at random. Because FIML outperforms traditional approaches (e.g., listwise deletion), it has been recommended in the
literature as a best practice for handling missing data in SEM (Graham,
2012; van Buuren, 2018).

To gain insight into the content validity of the ML-generated scores,
we examined the plausibility ratings of ML-generated scores compared
with those of human-generated scores by human expert raters, as displayed in Table 3. To allow for a realistic interpretation, it is important
to note that the content validity evaluation was based solely on transcripts, without access to the corresponding video data.
We found that, except for the subdimensions clarity and understanding of subject matter procedures and processes, for which the interrater reliability was just acceptable, the two human expert raters could
not agree on whether the human-generated or the ML-generated scores
were more plausible (across all 18 subdimensions, the mean kappa was
kmean = .125). Interestingly, both raters rated the ML-generated scores as
slightly more plausible on average across all 18 subdimensions (Rater 1:
54.16 %, Rater 2: 53.85 %). The human expert raters found the MLgenerated scores for the subdimension engagement in a cognitively
demanding subject matter particularly plausible (Rater 1: 63.64 %, Rater
2: 78.18 %). By contrast, the two subdimensions of classroom management routines and disruption were the only two in which human expert
raters rated the human-generated scores as more plausible.

The importance of the core dimensions and corresponding subdimensions of teaching effectiveness as characteristics of high-quality
teaching for successful student learning has been recognized for decades. However, traditional approaches to assessing teaching effectiveness (e.g., student and teacher ratings or classroom observations) face
reliability, validity, and scalability challenges in both research and
practice. Against this backdrop, in this study, we addressed the limitations of traditional teaching effectiveness assessments by exploring ML
algorithms to automate evaluations using multimodal classroom data
(video, audio, transcripts) as a scalable and efficient alternative. We
compared ML-generated teaching effectiveness scores from real teaching situations with human-generated scores and examined their reliability, content validity, and predictive validity.

became evident that the reliability of different ML models regarding
different combinations of modalities (i.e., text, audio, video) depended
on (a) the reliability measure and (b) the teaching effectiveness
subdimension.

5.2. Content validity of ML-generated teaching effectiveness scores
In this study, humans trained to assess teaching effectiveness subdimensions based on the GTI manual did not consistently rate the
human-generated or ML-generated scores as more plausible. Descriptive
analyses even suggested that the ML-generated scores were perceived as
slightly more plausible. These results are aligned with findings from
studies where participants were asked to distinguish between AIgenerated and human-generated content, such as poetry (Porter &
Machery, 2024) and student essays (Fleckenstein et al., 2024). These
studies have revealed that humans often struggle to differentiate between AI-generated content and content created by humans. We interpret this finding as indicating that ML-generated scores are
content-valid, suggesting that ML-generated scores of teaching effectiveness subdimensions may capture essential patterns in teaching
effectiveness that are aligned with human intuition.
Interestingly, on the one hand, the human expert raters found the
ML-generated scores for the subdimension engagement in cognitively
demanding subject matter particularly plausible. By contrast, for the two
classroom management subdimensions, routines and disruptions, the
human-generated scores were perceived as more plausible. Regarding
the subdimension engagement in cognitively demanding subject matter, if
the ML model integrates text and audio data, it might capture subtle but
meaningful engagement patterns that human raters might overlook or
assess inconsistently (e.g., speech patterns, turn-taking, and depth of
explanations), as human raters may struggle to process this information
comprehensively. Moreover, the subdimension is often explicitly reflected in student discourse, teacher questioning, and extended
reasoning, which ML models can analyze effectively through transcripts
(Demszky et al., 2023). By contrast, human raters may rely on more
implicit cues (e.g., classroom atmosphere or teacher behavior) that
might introduce less plausibility. Regarding the routines and disruptions
subdimensions, at least two explanations are possible. First, routines and
disruptions may encompass rather observable teacher behaviors
(Brophy, 1983; Kounin, 1970), making them easier for human raters to
assess consistently. These dimensions involve clear, structured actions
(e.g., transitions, classroom order), which likely lead to higher agreement among raters, potentially reinforcing the plausibility of
human-generated scores. Second, ML models may struggle with
contextual nuances (e.g., determining whether disruptions are managed
effectively), which could make their assessments seem less intuitive.

Moreover, we found that subdimensions related to discourse, such as
the nature of discourse, questioning, and explanations, could be determined more reliably in our study using text-based or text-and-audiobased ML models than by humans. These subdimensions are defined
by characteristics such as the type of questions or teacher- and studentprovided written or verbal explanations that are particularly explicit and
quantifiable in text-based features, such as sentence structure (e.g., interrogatives for questioning), keyword frequency (e.g., because or
therefore for explanations), and turn-taking patterns (e.g., teacher vs.
student discourse balance). Thus, ML models trained on text and audio
data may identify linguistic markers better than humans. Humans may
overlook subtle patterns or apply inconsistent interpretations, whereas
ML models detect patterns systematically.
Finally, we found that subdimensions related to the assessment of
and responses to student understanding, such as eliciting student
thinking, teacher feedback, and student-aligned instruction, could also
be determined more reliably in our study using text-based or text-andaudio-based ML models than by humans. It might be the case that
text-based and text-and-audio-based ML models systematically and
consistently analyze structured linguistic patterns. By contrast, human
raters may differ in how they interpret, weigh, or focus on various aspects of the interaction, resulting in lower interrater reliability. For
instance, ML models could detect the frequency and depth of student
responses when assessing student thinking by identifying explicit lexical
and syntactic features (e.g., sentence complexity and open-ended
question structures). Likewise, in evaluating teacher feedback, ML
models could reliably monitor specific feedback markers, including
clarifications (e.g., “What do you mean by … ?”), affirmations (e.g.,
“Yes, that’s correct because …”), and error corrections (e.g., “That’s
incorrect because …”). Moreover, when analyzing the student-aligned
instruction, ML models could evaluate whether teachers effectively
incorporate student responses into their instruction by identifying
referential connections in dialogue.
Additionally, ML models could track structural and prosodic cues in
teacher feedback, making them particularly effective for capturing

5.3. Predictive validity of ML-generated teaching effectiveness scores
Regarding predictive validity, the findings were inconsistent for both
the human-generated and ML-generated scores. All predictions are
estimated while statistically controlling for all other subdimensions of
teaching effectiveness, which is critical to keep in mind.
On the one hand, we found positive associations for certain teaching
effectiveness subdimensions. The human-generated scores showed positive relationships between questioning and understanding of subject
matter procedures and processes and students’ mathematics achievement. Similarly, the ML-generated scores (text- and audio-based models)
showed positive associations between routines and engagement in
cognitively demanding subject matter and student achievement. These
results are in line with prior research in suggesting that effective questioning, clear instructional routines, and cognitively engaging tasks are
critical for student learning (Klieme et al., 2009; Seidel & Shavelson,
2007).
On the other hand, we found that several subdimensions showed
negative associations with student outcomes. Explicit patterns and
generalizations and student-aligned instruction (human-generated)
negatively predicted student achievement, encouragement, and
warmth; and multiple approaches to/perspectives on reasoning (MLgenerated) were negative predictors across different models. Reasons for
these findings can be discussed from two perspectives: (a) the ML models
utilizing multimodal data and (b) research on teaching and learning.

Against this backdrop, differential effects may have caused the
negative associations found in this study. For instance, teaching strategies that prioritize deep conceptual understanding (e.g., multiple perspectives, explicit generalizations) may initially challenge students,
leading to short-term decreases in confidence (self-efficacy) before longterm gains emerge, potentially accompanied by lower student achievement. The subdimension of encouragement and warmth, whereas
generally beneficial, could be perceived differently by students in
mathematics classrooms—perhaps signaling a compensatory strategy
when students struggle rather than an indicator of high achievement.
Similarly, frequent monitoring (which was negatively associated with
achievement) may signal increased teacher control accompanied by low
intrinsic learning motivation or interventions in low-performing classrooms rather than fostering independent learning (e.g., Prenzel, 1997).
In addition, knowing that relationships between the provision and use of
core teaching effectiveness dimensions have not been found in previous
research for these data (Grünkorn et al., 2020) and the fact that
ML-generated scores, like human-generated scores, show no clear picture might be interpreted as a positive sign of their validity. These results
underscore the need to further intensify the exploration of students’
learning processes when researching teaching effectiveness (Seidel &
Shavelson, 2007).
Future studies could test mediation models (e.g., via student
engagement as per Klieme et al., 2009) to explain these inconsistencies,
potentially revealing indirect effects.

(a) It is possible that multimodal data integration is crucial for the
predictive validity of ML-generated teaching effectiveness scores.
Interestingly, after correcting for multiple comparisons, the
model incorporating all three modalities (i.e., text, audio, video)
did not show any statistically significant relationships with student achievement. This finding suggests that adding more data
modalities does not necessarily improve predictive validity and
may even introduce noise or leading to redundancy across modalities, making it harder to detect meaningful associations. On
the one hand, this finding is surprising as one would expect a
better performance when more modalities are included. On the
other hand, it is in line with previous research (e.g., Hou et al.,
2024). One possibility is that certain instructional subdimensions
are best captured through specific modalities (e.g., questioning in
text and warmth in audio), whereas multimodal models dilute
these effects. These findings highlight the need for more nuanced
fusion strategies and careful modality selection in future work.
(b) Although teacher practices were directly associated with student
outcomes in some previous research (e.g., Nilsen & Gustafsson,
2024), it is possible that the assumptions about the relationships
between teaching effectiveness dimensions and student outcomes
are indeed too simplistic. When conceptualizing teaching as a
complex interaction system between students and teachers, these
findings are not surprising and are aligned with previous research

5.4. From evidence to impact: where do we stand regarding actionable
insights
Our study demonstrates that automated, multimodal assessment of
teaching effectiveness is feasible and, in some cases, even matches or
exceeds the reliability of human-generated scores. However, to derive
actionable insights, it might be valuable to examine the results at the
level of individual teaching effectiveness subdimensions. For instance,
for the subdimension engagement in cognitively demanding subject matter,
the text- and audio-based scores show higher absolute and relative accuracy than the human-generated scores, but also exhibit a substantially
higher bias, indicating that the automated scores rate this subdimension
more positively. Whether this upward bias is problematic cannot be
determined solely from the bias metric. Instead, it requires consideration
of content and predictive validity. In our case, the automated scores
were judged as more plausible (content validity) and statistically
significantly predicted students’ mathematics achievement when controlling for covariates, whereas the human-generated scores did not.

Such nuanced patterns underscore the importance of careful,
subdimension-specific interpretation when discussing actionable
insights.
Moreover, the extent to which these ML-generated scores can be
translated into actionable insights depends on their application.
Whereas the use of ML-generated teaching effectiveness scores in
research settings already seems viable, their integration into teacher
education and professional development is within reach but still requires refinement. However, the use of such automated assessments in
teachers’ daily practice remains a distant goal, requiring further advancements in AI technology and research on teaching and learning.
In research contexts, ML-generated scores offer clear advantages,
enabling large-scale investigations on teaching effectiveness that were
previously limited by the cost and inefficiency of human rating processes. The scalability of ML-generated assessments enables systematic
and objective comparisons across classrooms, facilitating studies that
would otherwise require extensive observational resources. However,
moving beyond research applications and making these assessments
meaningful for teacher education and professional development requires additional steps. One promising approach is to provide teachers
with detailed, subdimension-level feedback that is enriched with examples of high-quality teaching. If teachers receive not only a numerical
score but also contextualized explanations and illustrative examples,
ML-generated assessments could serve as a powerful tool for selfreflection and targeted professional growth. The transition from mere
scoring to meaningful feedback would make these assessments truly
actionable for practitioners.
Despite these opportunities, important challenges remain. One key
concern is the reliability of human-generated ratings, which currently
served as the ground truth for ML models in our study. Our plausibility
ratings revealed substantial disagreement between human raters,
potentially underscoring the subjectivity inherent in traditional assessment methods. Thus, the question arises as to whether AI should simply
imitate human judgments or move beyond them. If human ratings are
used as the benchmark, their quality must be improved—perhaps by
aggregating the assessments of multiple highly trained raters to establish
a more stable ground truth. Alternatively, future research could explore
AI models that learn directly from classroom interactions without
relying on human labels. Recent advances in multimodal foundation
models, which can process large amounts of text, audio, and video data
without extensive labeled training sets, suggest a promising direction for
developing more robust and unbiased assessments of teaching
effectiveness.
Another critical factor for making ML-generated assessments
actionable is explainability. A numerical score alone is of limited use to
teachers and researchers unless it is accompanied by an explanation of
how it was derived. Future research should explore methods for
enhancing the interpretability of AI-generated ratings, for example, by
integrating large language models (LLMs) that not only assign scores but
also highlight specific moments in a lesson that influenced the assessment. Such an approach would allow teachers and researchers to engage
with AI-generated feedback in a more meaningful way, using it as a basis
for reflection and improvement.
Moreover, automating the assessment of teaching effectiveness
should not be seen as a simple replacement for human raters but as an
opportunity to expand the scope and applicability of such evaluations.
Whereas ML-generated scores may never be perfect, their scalability
allows for entirely new applications, such as providing accessible,
individualized feedback for teachers or enabling large-scale research in
classrooms without the logistical barriers of human observations. As AI
models continue to improve, they have the potential to make teaching
effectiveness assessments not only more efficient but also more
insightful and impactful. Future research should therefore advance both
ML models and teaching and learning research in tandem to ensure that
automated assessments are not only technically feasible but also practically meaningful.

Ultimately, as AI-based systems for evaluating teaching effectiveness
become increasingly scalable and integrated into educational contexts,
it is critical to consider the associated ethical risks. Although the GTI
dataset used in this study was de-identified and collected under strict
ethical protocols, future applications may involve real-time, longitudinal monitoring of student behavior. If such data are leaked or used
beyond their intended educational purposes—e.g., by universities or
employers—this could have severe implications for student autonomy
and equity. In a recent systematic review on AI in classrooms (Fütterer
et al., 2025), we found that ethical considerations are often given limited
attention in empirical studies. This highlights an urgent need for clear,
normative guidelines, transparent data governance, and robust privacy
protections to ensure that AI-based tools support, rather than undermine, educational fairness. Importantly, such technologies could always
be used to assist and empower them in meaningful, developmentally
appropriate ways, but also to control students’ learning or teachers’
instructional practices.
5.5. Strengths, weaknesses, and future research
Our study represents an important step forward in the automated
assessment of teaching effectiveness dimensions by leveraging multimodal data sources. This study is the first to comprehensively predict
teaching effectiveness dimensions using multimodal data, including
text, audio, and video. Previous studies have often relied on singlemodal approaches, limiting the scope of automated assessment. Our
findings provide empirical evidence for the feasibility of multimodal ML
models in capturing complex classroom dynamics. Additionally, the
comparative evaluation of different ML pipelines against humangenerated scores highlights the potential of AI-driven approaches to
improve traditional methods of assessing teaching effectiveness.
However, whereas the findings are promising, there are several
limitations that should be addressed in future research to further
enhance the reliability, validity, and scalability of ML-based teaching
assessment models.
First, the content validity evaluation was based solely on transcripts,
without access to video data. Thus, raters’ abilities to fully assess the
plausibility of ML-generated scores may have been restricted, as
nonverbal cues (e.g., gestures, facial expressions) were not considered.
Future research should incorporate video-based plausibility assessments
to provide a more holistic validation of automated teaching assessments.
Second, we employed a specific ML pipeline that has yielded strong
results in prior studies. Current ML models replicate human judgments,
but AI could be leveraged to go beyond human ratings and generate
novel assessments that identify complex classroom interactions (Vieluf
& Klieme, 2023). However, alternative ML approaches, such as LLMs,
could also be explored. Future studies should experiment with different
ML architectures, particularly unsupervised and foundation models,
which could improve performance by learning complex interactions
without relying on human-labeled training data. This approach could
improve validity by enabling ML models to capture latent teaching
effectiveness patterns, even if the exact decision-making process remains opaque (black-box issue).
Third, the ML models were trained on human-generated scores,
which are known for not always demonstrating high reliability as also
shown in this study. This low reliability may limit model performance,
as the models are learning from ratings with inherent variability. A
critical next step is to develop multimodal foundation models that can
learn unsupervised representations of teaching effectiveness, reducing
dependence on human labels. Moreover, the AI rater was trained on the
mean of only two human raters, which might not be the most robust
ground truth. Future studies should aggregate scores from a larger pool
of raters (e.g., 10 human raters) to reduce variability. Moreover, alternative ground truths should be explored, such as aggregating classroomlevel student ratings or teacher self-assessments to capture multiple
perspectives on teaching effectiveness.

Fourth, we relied on pre-existing secondary data, which constrained
data quality (e.g., not all students were visible in the videos, blackboard
artifacts were partially captured, background noise was present in audio
recordings). Future research should work with higher resolution classroom recordings and better data collection methods to mitigate these
issues. Additionally, as more powerful AI models become available, they
can better process higher quality multimodal data to enhance predictive
power.
Further, whereas this study employed teacher-independent crossvalidation to evaluate model generalizability to individual teachers, it
did not assess potential biases across teacher subgroups, such as their
demographic characteristics (e.g., gender or years of experience). This is
a critical consideration, particularly when the nature of teaching effectiveness evaluations is of high stakes. Future research should systematically examine model performance across teacher demographics and
explore multiple bias mitigation approaches, including post-processing
calibration methods and ensemble techniques that combine predictions from models trained on demographically balanced subsets.
Additionally, a human-in-the-loop approach, which involves diverse
stakeholders such as teachers and administrators in the bias assessment
process, could help ensure that mitigation strategies are aligned with
real-world concerns.
Finally, the ML-generated scores did not demonstrate predictive
validity, but this finding was not entirely unexpected given the data set
(Grünkorn et al., 2020). Future research should further explore predictive validity using different data sets where stronger associations between teaching effectiveness and student outcomes have previously
been established. Additionally, the generalizability of the findings
should be tested across new data sets and diverse educational contexts.
In conclusion, this study provides a strong foundation for the automated assessment of teaching effectiveness dimensions using multimodal AI models. Whereas the findings highlight significant progress,

they also underscore the need for continued refinement, validation, and
methodological advancements to fully unlock the potential of AI in
classroom teaching analysis, something we aim to address in the newly
established Emerging Field Group of the European Association for
Research on Learning and Instruction (https://www.earli.org/efg-13).
