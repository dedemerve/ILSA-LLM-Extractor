ABSTRACT

Latin America’s education systems – with a high prevalence of students not achieving the basic competencies,
entrenched inequalities and weak connectivity, and longer schools closures – were highly affected by the
COVID-19 pandemic, experiencing sizeable learning losses. The role of education policies to boost students’
engagement for remote learning were critical during schools shutdowns. Yet, there is a lack of evidence
on whether pandemic educational policies were effective. Using explainable machine learning methods and
a sample of ten countries from PISA 2022, this paper fills this gap by analysing the association of three
education policies for remote learning (students’ resources, teachers’ resources and teachers’ communication)
implemented during the pandemic with the probability of students reaching competency (level 2) in math and
reading. I find that policies’ effects on learning are non-linear with specific thresholds where policy become
more efficient to keep students’ chances of level 2 achievement, with these thresholds linked to schools’ length
of closure and the intensity of policies. I also find that policy intersectionality is important with family wealth
and educational inputs being pathways to counteract associations under longer schools’ closures, and policies
not being capable of narrowing learning loss by gender (against girls). Results by educational sub-systems for
the policy on students’ resources indicate that by school type there is similarity on associations for reading,
but for math this policy is more productive in private than public schools, as for students from urban schools
under longer period of closure than students from rural schools.

1. Introduction

Before the onset of the pandemic, however, LAC’s education systems
were already experiencing low learning levels and deep structural
inequalities (Arias Ortiz et al., 2024b; Brunori et al., 2024; Delprato
et al., 2015; Fernández et al., 2024; Krüger, 2019; Murillo et al., 2023;
Neidhöfer et al., 2018).
Arias Ortiz et al. (2023)’s study for the LAC region estimates that
75% of the students are low performers in math (not reaching the basic
competencies given by level 2), and 55% of students lacks basic reading
skills (failing to understand a simple text). The COVID-19 pandemic,
in turn, worsened the already prevailing and detrimental educational
inequality in the LAC region. Prolonged school closures in the region
led to loss of learning (Betthäuser et al., 2023; Gómez and Andrés Uzín,
2022), shifts on schools’ efficiency (Delprato and Antequera, 2025),
increased inequalities (Bracco et al., 2025), and negative impacts on
psychosocial health and well-being of students and teachers (CamachoZuñiga et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022), with vulnerable students
experienced the greatest losses (Asadullah et al., 2023; UNESCO, 2024).

Around 1.7 billion learners worldwide have been affected by the
pandemic, including 99% of students in low (LIC) and lower-middle
(LMIC) income countries (Reimers, 2022). In those countries with
resources-constrained educational systems, the negative effects of the
pandemic have been more profound because the wider barriers they
had to tackle given by the triple burden of schooling deprivation,
learning inequality and learning poverty (Asadullah et al., 2023). The
Latin America and the Caribbean (henceforth: LAC) region experienced
the longest period of school closures with poor connectivity (Gómez
and Andrés Uzín, 2022; Munguia, 2023). Schools in region were closed
for around 270 days on average between 2020–2012, representing a
disruption of 1.42 years of schooling (Bracco et al., 2025). Consequently, in LAC region, the pandemic pushed a large cohort of students
further down in the learning distribution – mostly affecting the more
disadvantaged students (Jakubowski et al., 2023) – with estimates
pointing towards a lost of years of schooling between 0.9–1.1, (from
7.8 years to 6.9–6.7 years in gloomy scenarios) (Azevedo et al., 2021).

Yet, little is know about whether different educational policies implemented in the LAC region for remote learning during school shutdowns
helped to at least either maintain pre-pandemic learning levels or
prevent substantial drops on educational performance in the region
and, additionally, for which group of students these policies were more
or less beneficial for waning learning inequalities.
A key hurdle for LAC students’ engagement with online education
and governments’ education interventions throughout the pandemic
was connectivity. Regional estimates indicate that nearly half of the
population does not have access to an internet connection (CEPAL,
2022). There are also entrenched wealth inequalities at the regional
level in ICT access (Vera et al., 2023), with 70% for those falling
into the lower income quintile not having access of internet at home
(CEPAL, 2022), and students in the top wealth quartile being four times
as likely (in comparison to the students from the bottom quartile) to
have a laptop at home (Berlanga et al., 2020). This digital divide is
likely to have been enlarged by the pandemic’s households income
shocks leading to higher poverty (which in the region are estimated
between 23%–39%; Bracco et al., 2025).
On the back of the high prevalence of students not reaching competency levels in key subjects, as well as the escalation of educational
inequalities in the LAC region brought by the pandemic (Arias Ortiz
et al., 2024b) due to income shocks affecting relatively more students
stemming from the most disadvantaged households and the already
unequal distribution of schools resources – among which ICT were key
for connectivity and remote learning – in the region by school type and
location, the paper’s objective is to evaluate whether COVID-19 educational policies were in fact counteracting mechanisms against students’
learning losses in an unprecedented scenario of schools shutdown and
remote learning delivery, allowing students to sustain pre-pandemic
education proficiency levels. This objective is therefore further assessed
by students∕households drivers and schools configurations because
these channels are likely to play a role on the degree of effectivity and
heterogeneity of education pandemic policies.
I also rely on interpretability machine learning (IML) models applied to the LAC region educational landscape to achieve this aim by
dividing performance levels into two students binary groups: low (nonachieving) level and high competency levels. For this, I first evaluate
different classification machine learning (ML) models and then, after
obtaining the most efficient model, I use Shapley values as an analytical
tool to boost interpretably of COVID-19 educational policies associations with students’ probabilities of reaching competency in math and
reading. Another issue the paper attempts to address is whether these
associations vary by the duration of closure of schools as, most likely,
diminishing returns of educational policies across time may be at play
when the educational sector resources investments are rather limited,
a reality of the LAC’s education systems.
Specifically, in this paper, based on PISA (Programme for International Student Assessment) data for the year 2022 for 10 LAC countries,
I analyse the association of three education policies implemented during the pandemic with the probability of students reaching at least
level 2 (competency levels) in math and reading. The specific policies analysed are: resources to support students learning, resources to
support teachers for remote learning, and teacher communication with
students and parents. As far I am aware, this is the first LAC study which
weighs up associations of education policies with learning competency
during the pandemic. This paper fills this gap and, methodologically,
it innovates in the application of recently developed interpretable
machine learning (ML) methods based on learning surveys for policy
analysis, adding to the scarce literature (Bu and Chen, 2023; GómezTalal et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024) which exclusively focuses on
richer education systems.
In particular, further to establishing which are the leading determinants of learning proficiency (level 2 or above) in the LAC region
through a novel explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) method, that
is, the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanation: Lundberg and Lee, 2017)

method, the aim of this paper is to answer the following research
questions (RQ) regarding the leverage effects of educational policies
implemented during the pandemic:
• RQ1. What are relationships of educational COVID-19 policies
(e.g., resources to support students learning or teachers for remote
learning) with the probability of students reaching level 2 in math
and reading in the LAC region, and how these effects change by
the extent of school closures?
• RQ2. How do these relationships vary by households’ educational
inputs (family SES and degree of family educational support) and
by students’ gender?
• RQ3. How do the leverage effects of educational COVID-19 policies vary across four education sub-systems (namely: private
against public schools, and urban against rural schools) of the
LAC region, and are these policies associations with learning
heterogeneous?
The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 contains
a brief discussion about educational policies implemented during the
pandemic in the LAC region. In Section 3 I present the data employed
for the analysis. Section 4 outlines the methodological approach –
i.e., the ML model used for estimations, gradient boosted trees – as
well as the explainable ML method followed (i.e., the SHAP method).
Section 5 contains the results, and concluding remarks are shown in
Section 6.
2. Background
After the outbreak of COVID-19 pandemic, school closures forced
countries in the LAC region to suddenly implement various distance
learning modalities throughout 2020 as an urgent response to the crisis
to sustain students’ pedagogic continuity (Parker and Alfaro, 2021;
Pedró and Ramos Torres, 2022; Vincent-Lancrin et al., 2022). The LAC
region’s evidence on the impact of COVID-19 educational policies on
remote learning and students’ engagement with schoolwork has been
mixed (Huepe et al., 2023), both due to specific countries’ challenges
and their different degree of preparation based on previous ongoing
strategies on remote learning. The implementation of distance learning varied based on each country’s capacity related to connectivity,
access to digital devices and teachers’ preparation for remote learning
(Sanchez et al., 2022), with an array of formats (printed material to be
used at home, media∕TV∕radio and digital applications) and an overall
expansion of learning and teachers online platforms (Ainscow et al.,
2025; Miralrio et al., 2024; Paya Rico, 2022).
Countries such as Mexico, for instance, had a long history on
distance learning through ‘‘Telesecundaria’’, having as a result an advantageous situation when it suddenly had to switch to the new format
of education delivery. The Learning at Home (‘‘Aprender en Casa’’) initiative was launched within this existing programme reaching 25 millions students (Ripani and Zucchetti, 2020) and it was complemented
by radio, broadcasting content on television and internet (YouTube),
and online evaluations aiming to ensure pedagogic continuity during
lockdown (Vincent-Lancrin et al., 2022).
The existence of different educational approaches of LAC countries
shows that the most common employed strategy was synchronous
online classes and online delivery of learning materials and assignments to students, followed by the delivery of learning materials to
students and the delivery of audio lessons with pre-recorded videos
(Parker and Alfaro, 2021). Which country’s approach was prioritised
hinged on students’ ICT stocks at home and connectivity. For schools
whose students had ICT disadvantages and barriers, physical delivery
of learning materials was the leading strategy, as well as synchronous
distance learning methods (through phone calls, radio and TV broadcasts) (Parker and Alfaro, 2021; OREALC/UNESCO, 2022). The use
of mobile phones and WhatsApp, for instance, was a popular choice

do not have the same utility for complex educational tasks which may
lead to frustration and, ultimately, decreasing students’ engagement

on learning, the majority of LAC educational authorities supported
distance learning by distributing digital devices (Huepe et al., 2023).
Nevertheless, connectivity was not addressed, with estimates showing
the rate of internet access in LAC region was below 50% (Barron Rodriguez et al., 2020; CEPAL, 2022), adding to quality issues of remote

As in the case of Mexico discussed above, in other LAC countries
(including countries comprising the paper’s working sample) there
were different educational initiatives which were complemented by
social and economic support for the most marginalised. For example,
in Argentina, the government tried to boost the chances of remote
learning during the pandemic by approving an Emergency Program
for ICT access in marginalised neighbourhoods, increasing toppings on
food cards and universal child allowance by 19% and 28%, respectively
(Delprato, 2025b). In Peru, the programme ‘‘Aprendo en Casa’’ (I Learn
at Home) was re-launched including new materials and resources, using
radio stations and the country’s national channels to broadcast secondary level educational content alongside a teachers’ training portal
‘‘PeruEduca’’ which included, among other elements, applications for
communication with students and classroom management (Martín and
Ramos, 2024). In Uruguay, the ‘‘Plan Ceibal’’, a successful education
policy for ICT access combined with distance learning, which had
been implemented in 2007 and where primary and secondary students
and teachers were given a PC (plus boosting connectivity in public
schools). This was modified and extended due to the pandemic (Ceibal
at Home) and it included both technical and pedagogical aspects, such
as creating synchronous and asynchronous learning modalities, teacher
training in the use of the new functionalities, and a huge virtual library
(Quezada-Morales, 2022; Vincent-Lancrin et al., 2022).
However, aggregated LAC evidence with regards to the efficiency of
governments’ deployment of resources to support online learning and
students’ engagement or for teachers to boost remote learning, geared
towards minimising learning losses, is non-existent. The mixed use of
technology and standard methods of teaching integrated in learning
management systems had significant barriers, restricting its effectiveness due to deep equity and school infrastructure issues. For instance,
according to a recent UNICEF report on the education response to the
COVID-19 pandemic in the LAC region and its challenges and achievement, out of the 33 countries∕territories, only 19 facilitated teachers’
access to technology, and with just one in five countries putting in place
mechanisms for teacher accompaniment (UNICEF, 2024). Tailoring
programs pitched at given students’ learning levels where implemented
in most LAC countries, though formative assessments and accelerated
programs where less common in the region (UNICEF, 2024).
Ultimately, the main barrier the LAC region had to face was the
pre-existing socioeconomic inequalities which further hampered the
take-up of remote learning by students (Munoz-Najar et al., 2021). Students coming from poorer families suffered the most because students’
engagement with remote learning is generally low where parents or
caregivers have lower educational levels. For remote learning to be effective it requires three complementary, critical components: effective
teachers, suitable technology and engaged learners (Munoz-Najar et al.,
2021). Students’ engagement needs interesting and engaging content
as well as frequent interactions with teachers and motivation which is
boosted by a suitable home learning environment as factors that can
influence the engagement of students (Kennedy et al., 2022). Family
involvement was a vital element in the educational process, with 75%
of LAC countries adopting measures to assist parents and caregivers

during the pandemic (UNICEF, 2024). Yet, economic conditions deteriorated throughout the pandemic (Bracco et al., 2025) and that is the
rationale why the paper’s analytical approach is to interact teachers’
and students’ policy support variables with the duration of schools’
closure (a proxy for the pandemic duration), as well as to look into
heterogeneity effects by established drivers of educational inequity in
the region (e.g., gender, family SES, parental input in education, and
schools’ configurations).
3. Data
The paper’s analysis is based on the post-pandemic PISA dataset
for the year 2022, using data from the LAC region for 10 countries
(that is: Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Dominican Rep., Mexico, Panama, Peru, Paraguay and Uruguay). PISA measures secondary
students’ learning achievement (at 15 years-old), i.e., their ability to
use their reading, mathematics and science knowledge and skills to
meet real-life challenges and, vitally, it is the only source for LAC
measuring learning levels after the pandemic and their associations
with COVID-19 policies and background variables (e.g., school closure
days, connectivity barriers for remote learning, etc.). PISA data was
originally planned to take place in 2021 but the disruption caused
by COVID-19 global pandemic forced the assessment to be postponed
to 2022, and the content was updated with questions linking the
‘‘resilience of education systems’’ from the point of view of fairness and
inclusion within the pandemic’s remote learning (OECD, 2023a). The
resilience of education systems in PISA 2022 was assessed through a
new framework focusing on students’ capacity for continuous learning
and adaptability, in addition to academic performance.
PISA data collection entails significant efforts and resources to
obtain cultural and linguistic breadth and balance in the assessment
materials, with quality assurance mechanisms (e.g. including several
rounds of review of the assessment materials by PISA participating
countries) applied in translation, sampling, and data collection, thus
giving PISA’s results a high degree of validity and reliability (OECD,
2023a). The main stages of PISA data collection are: instrument development, field trial, sampling, translation and verification, main survey
data collection, quality monitoring, data submission, and data analysis and reporting. Data submission in the LAC region happened in
December 2023. Some of the quality controls included, for instance,
linguistic quality assurance (minimising translation or cultural context
differences), expert item review (with preliminary testing and ensuring
to be culturally appropriate), and standardised sampling procedure (led
by an independent contractor who obtained a representative sample
of 4500 to 10,000 students per country to ensure valid and reliable
national-level estimates) (for more details, see OECD, 2023a, Chapter
1). The two outcomes the paper relies on are math and reading achievement. In the math domain, the test attempts to assess the capacity to
reason mathematically and to formulate, employ and interpret mathematics to solve real, practical and concrete problems, whereas within
the reading domain, it assess a student’s capability to understand, use,
evaluate, reflect on and engage with texts to achieve given goals developing a student’s potential to participate in society (OECD, 2023a). In
addition to the test, there is a comprehensive questionnaire that collects
relevant information about the students’ family and socioeconomic
backgrounds, their attitudes toward learning and study habits and a
school’s background features.

with here each school appears exactly once in the test set across
all folds. This prevents information leakage across different schools,
leading to a more reliable estimate of model’s performance. These tasks
are carried out for the whole sample and sub-sample dataset and for the
two outcomes.

coefficients) and two inverse of regularisation strengths (C = 0.1, 1).

and 0.01). For the GBT model, I assess various specifications (in total,

6.3. Limitations and future research
The analysis of the paper has some limitations. The usual caveat
is that the paper’s estimates represent associations rather than causal
impacts due to the cross-section nature of the PISA data on which the
analysis is based and, in consequence, estimates reported should be
interpreted with caution and not in a causal sense. Additionally, the
paper’s results only capture immediate post-pandemic policy impacts,
but not long-term effects of policies given the cumulative deterioration
of learning achievement in the region after schools’ reopening. With
regards to the analytical analysis, a limitation is the compounded
nature of CEP measures I relied upon to derive associations with
the probability of reaching competency levels in math and reading.
Albeit some disaggregated results were briefly presented for students
resources, this would additionally need some comparisons item by
item and the extension of this disaggregation approach also for teaching support items. This is an interesting avenue of future research
since sub-components of COVID-19 educational policies can be more
easily identified and matched with existing policies frameworks implemented during the pandemic in the LAC region, broadening the
existing evidence within the policy domain of recovering learning
programs to actual effects of policies on learning brackets of achievement, increasing the resilience of LAC education systems. Likewise, a
heterogeneity analysis of CEP using additional markers of disadvantage
identifying vulnerable populations of students (such as Indigenous
communities, migrants∕refugees, individuals without internet access,
people living remote areas, and those with special needs; UNICEF,
2024) can be beneficial for narrowing inequalities in the region and for
a better preparation against future unprecedented shocks and a growing
tendency for an online learning delivery of education.

6.2. Policy implications
The paper yielded different implications for future designs of educational policies for remote learning in the LAC region. Given the
non-linear associations of COVID-19 educational policies with learning
found driven by the extent of schools’ period of closure, Latin American
governments should accelerate investments on ICT access and internet
connectivity so that make educational engagement of students have
a better chance of being more continuous during prolonged schools
shutdowns. And, crucially, it is important that these investments are
aligned with the length of remote learning (Miralrio et al., 2024),
particularly for students facing wider socio-economic barriers. Having
a reliable connection (as well as quality ICT such as laptops∕PC rather
than mobile phones; Lezama et al., 2023; Santos et al., 2021) for
school activities for extended periods of remote learning is therefore
crucial. Taking into account the whole sample estimates, this kind of
investments on ICT and connectivity infrastructure should be priority
for public school’s students to boost math’s proficiency levels as well as
in rural schools where connectivity is very low (CEPAL, 2020). Alongside these interventions, it is also vital to boost students’ social and
emotional wellbeing as they were linked to educational engagement
during the pandemic (Salas-Pilco et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022) as
students’ life satisfaction is found to be among the top ten predictors
for both math and reading proficiency.
Policies should also place a special attention to the inequality
effect that has been observed over the pandemic (Huepe et al., 2023),
focusing on students who faced greater obstacles for whom policies
were less effective; that is, poorer and female students as shown by
the paper’s intersectionality analysis, confirming related studies for the
region (e.g., Meneses et al., 2025). Besides, the lack of a consistent
positive estimates for the policy variable of teachers’ resources support
