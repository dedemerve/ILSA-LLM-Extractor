A B S T R A C T
Persistent gaps in Philippine science performance and competency demands under the Department of Education 
(DepEd) K-12 curriculum have signaled the need for interdisciplinary, problem-centered instruction. This study 
designed and evaluated an Integrated Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics with Problem-Based 
Learning (I-STEM-PBL) module for General Biology 1 (Biomolecules) in Grade 11 Senior High School (SHS). The 
module was informed by the 3C3R problem design model (content, context, and connection; researching, 
reasoning, and reflecting; affect, difficulty, and teamwork) and adapted PBL scaffolds appropriate for SHS. Using 
a quasi-experimental one-group pretest–posttest design, Grade 11 STEM students (n = 38) completed the six- 
week intervention. Conceptual understanding served as the primary outcome. A paired-samples analysis 
showed statistically significant gains from pretest to posttest, with a medium effect size (Cohen's d = 0.539). 
These results indicate that the I-STEM-PBL module improved learners' understanding of biomolecules while 
remaining aligned with DepEd K–12 expectations. The findings contribute evidence that structured PBL, when 
integrated with explicit STEM connections and scaffolded inquiry, can enhance concept learning in senior high 
school biology.
1. Introduction
Large-scale assessments point to specific performance gaps. In the 
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022, only 16% 
of Filipino 15-year-olds reached at least Level 2 in mathematics (OECD 
average 69%), 24% did so in reading (OECD 74%), and 23% in science 
(OECD 76%) (OECD, 2023). Level 2 represents the baseline proficiency 
expected for everyday tasks. In the Trends in International Mathematics 
and Science Study (TIMSS) 2019, Grade 4 students averaged 297 in 
mathematics and 249 in science, the lowest among participating sys­
tems; the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational 
Achievement (IEA) also flagged these estimates with reservations 
because more than 15% of students had achievement levels too low for 
estimation (Mullis et al., 2020). These indicators motivate the present 
SHS intervention focused on improving science conceptual under­
standing. Here, conceptual understanding means a well-connected, 
transferable grasp of scientific ideas that supports explanation, predic­
tion, and application beyond recall, distinct from procedural knowledge 
of steps or routines (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001). This focus is war­
ranted because PISA and TIMSS highlight shortfalls on application and 
reasoning items, General Biology 1 competencies emphasize explanation 
and transfer, and the six-week, classroom-based module offered limited 
scope for laboratory procedures; therefore, conceptual outcomes are the 
most appropriate primary target.
Beyond low averages, PISA emphasizes applying knowledge to real- 
world contexts; Philippine results show that many students are below 
the baseline needed to interpret data and reason scientifically (OECD, 
2023). TIMSS likewise reports weak performance on items requiring 
application and reasoning among Grade 4 students (Mullis et al., 2020). 
These findings suggest that there is a pressing need to reform science 
education to better prepare students for the demands of the modern 
world. The current research aims to address these deficiencies through 
an Integrated STEM with Problem-Based Learning (I-STEM-PBL) 
module.

While STEM education is hailed as the cornerstone for equipping 
individual students with the necessary skills to navigate the complexities 
of the world and for fostering innovative thinkers to support the future 
workforce and sustainable development (Funa et al., 2024; Tan et al., 
2019; Zhou et al., 2025), there exists a lack of consensus among STEM

effective methods for integrating its components to promote quality 
education (Funa & Gabay, 2025; Roehrig et al., 2021). The concept of 
STEM was initially developed in collaboration with the National Science 
Foundation in the United States to bring together four distinct disci­
plines. Since its inception, STEM education has found widespread 
application in educational policies and curricula, encompassing out­
comes relevant to economic development, technological advancement, 
21st-century skills, and national security (Bybee, 2013). In practice, 
STEM education enriches students' understanding of how the world 
operates and enhances their utilization of technology by introducing 
engineering concepts at an early stage, involving problem-solving and 
innovation, and integrating group activities, laboratory investigations, 
and projects (Funa, 2023). Additionally, it offers opportunities to 
develop crucial 21st-century skills. Moreover, STEM education equips 
students to make informed decisions related to health, energy efficiency, 
environmental sustainability, resource management, and national se­
curity (Bybee, 2010).
However, despite the considerable impact and outcomes associated 
with STEM, educators and students often encounter challenges in fully 
leveraging the new opportunities it presents (Koh, 2018). The broad 
scope, purpose, and utilization of STEM pose intricate challenges in 
terms of understanding, establishing frameworks, implementing pro­
grams, and evaluating the effectiveness of STEM initiatives. Conse­
quently, STEM education needs to prioritize the improvement of 
students' abilities to comprehend and engage with complex real-world 
issues, adopting an integrated STEM approach as a daily practice.
McComas and Burgin (2020) propose three strategies for integrating 
the four elements of STEM: transdisciplinary, integrative, and inte­
grated. Transdisciplinary STEM education involves applying knowledge 
and skills from two or more STEM domains to disciplines beyond 
traditional STEM fields. Integrative STEM education uses engineering 
design methodologies to teach science and mathematics alongside 
technology/engineering education. Integrated STEM education empha­
sizes the intentional and meaningful integration of STEM subjects into 
the curriculum, emphasizing their connections and application to 
real-world problem-solving (McComas & Burgin, 2020). To avoid 
confusion, the term I-STEM (Integrated STEM, teaching with two or 
more elements together) was chosen in the current study to distinguish it 
from traditional STEM. The application of I-STEM in education offers 
students a comprehensive perspective and approach to designing solu­
tions to real-world problems related to sustainable development, 
encouraging alternative thinking and the application of discipline-based 
knowledge to address complex real-world challenges (Funa, 2025; Funa 
et al., 2024).
1.2. PBL conceptualization and effectiveness
PBL is an educational approach that positions students at the heart of 
their learning journey, fostering active participation, problem-solving, 
and the development of crucial 21st-century skills, self-regulation, in­
quiry, and metacognition within the context of real-world challenges 
(Barrows, 1996; Grant & Tamim, 2019). As a pedagogy, PBL is defined 
by authentic, ill-structured problems that drive learning; small-group 
collaboration; a facilitator who guides inquiry rather than transmits 
content; iterative cycles of problem analysis, self-directed study, and 
reporting; and assessments aligned with both conceptual understanding 
and inquiry processes (Savery, 2019). Over the years, scholars have 
conceptualized PBL in diverse ways, leading to a range of perspectives, 
such as viewing it as an investigative process, a tool for enhancing 
learning ability, or a mechanism for constructing mental frameworks of 
the world (Schmidt et al., 2009, 2019). The various viewpoints sur­
rounding PBL have culminated in the identification of six fundamental 
characteristics, which include the integration of problem scenarios to 
stimulate learning, the formation of small collaborative groups, 
mentorship from teachers or tutors, reduced dependence on 
conventional lectures, an emphasis on self-directed learning, and suffi­
cient time allocation for exploring and resolving these challenges 
(Evensen & Hmelo-Silver, 2000; Hmelo-Silver, 2004; Schmidt, 1983, 
1993). These features distinguish PBL from general project work by 
centering the problem before content coverage and by requiring struc­
tured facilitation and reflection. PBL has also been institutionalized in 
university education since the late 1960s—first in medicine and subse­
quently in engineering and science (Kolmos, 2009).
A meta-analysis from 2016 to 2020 was conducted to assess the in­
fluence of PBL on students' academic performance in the field of science, 
revealing a substantial positive impact, particularly among secondary- 
level students (Funa & Prudente, 2021). Notably, the analysis indi­
cated that factors such as grade level and scientific discipline did not 
significantly affect student achievement in PBL. Furthermore, it un­
derscores the importance of incorporating reflective activities into PBL. 
Drawing from the findings of Funa and Prudente (2021), it is evident 
that while PBL stands as an effective educational tool, amalgamating it 
with other pedagogical strategies holds potential for addressing the 
dynamic requirements of contemporary education. Considering this, the 
current study combines the I-STEM approach with PBL, thereby inves­
tigating the combined impact of these methodologies.
1.3. I-STEM-PBL as a more potent approach
PBL has been shown to impact students’ interest directly and posi­
tively in pursuing future STEM careers (LaForce et al., 2017). When PBL 
is integrated with STEM, it forms an effective instructional approach for 
fostering higher-order thinking skills, encompassing cognitive aspects 
such as critical thinking and creativity, content elements including 
interdisciplinary perspectives, technology utilization, and establishing 
connections, as well as collaborative learning elements, including 
collaboration, communication, and self-directed learning (El Sayary 
et al., 2015; Funa, 2025; Funa & Gabay, 2025). The potential of 
combining PBL and the I-STEM approach to enhance students' under­
standing of science concepts has captured the interest of the present 
researcher. This integration has the potential to complement and rein­
force the two approaches, resulting in a more potent pedagogical 
approach for teaching and learning in the realm of science.
Recent PISA/TIMSS results and DepEd K–12 competency demands 
motivate the adoption of interdisciplinary, student-centered strategies. 
PBL is a well-established pedagogy used across disciplines, including 
STEM, and has been associated with gains in performance (e.g., Funa & 
Prudente, 2021). In this study, the researcher implemented an I-STEM 
module taught through PBL (I-STEM-PBL) for Grade 11 General Biology 
1, organizing tasks with the second-generation 3C3R model and aligning 
activities to DepEd K–12 competencies (Funa, 2023; Funa et al., 2024; 
Funa & Gabay, 2025). Using a quasi-experimental research design, the 
researcher designed and delivered the module to examine its potential to 
improve students’ conceptual understanding. The term “I-STEM-PBL” 
(Funa, 2023; Funa et al., 2024) is a descriptive label for this Grade 11 
implementation and is not presented as a new pedagogy.
Although PBL is established across disciplines, SHS science in the 
Philippines lacks classroom-tested, DepEd-aligned modules that inte­
grate STEM with PBL using the second-generation 3C3R problem-design 
model and SHS-adapted K–16 scaffolds. Clear documentation of design 
rationales and implementation procedures that practitioners can 
reproduce is also limited. Hence, the researcher addressed these gaps by 
developing and implementing an I-STEM-PBL module intended to 
enhance students’ conceptual understanding in biology. Specifically, 
this study aimed to address the following key questions: (1) What are the 
activities included in the I-STEM-PBL module? (2) What are the re­
viewers' evaluations of the I-STEM-PBL module in terms of its adherence 
to standards and learning competencies? And (3) To what extent does 
the I-STEM-PBL module improve students' conceptual understanding of 
biomolecules? This study contributes one of the first classroom-tested, 
DepEd-aligned I-STEM-PBL modules for Grade 11 General Biology 1,

2. Methodology
2.1. Research design
The researcher employed a quasi-experimental one-group pretest­
–posttest design to determine whether the I-STEM-PBL module could 
improve students' conceptual understanding. This design was chosen 
because the aim was to evaluate the module's effect in an intact SHS 
General Biology 1 class and to generate actionable knowledge for the 
local context rather than estimate population-level effects. Following 
Creswell and Creswell (2023), quasi-experimental designs involve an 
intervention without random assignment; therefore, the study used a 
one-group pretest–posttest design with an intact class to examine change 
under real school constraints such as the timetable, curriculum, and 
fixed class composition.
At the same time, the study incorporated a developmental compo­
nent in which the I-STEM-PBL module and its assessment tools were 
systematically designed, refined, and evaluated. The researcher drew 
inspiration from the research process and the stages of instrument and 
module development outlined in Funa (2023) and Funa et al. (2024), 
and from the Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and 
Evaluation (ADDIE) model (Dick et al., 2009) for module development. 
Because random assignment was not feasible, the researcher imple­
mented procedures to support internal validity (for example, consistent 
instruction, standardized testing intervals, and identical test forms) 
while prioritizing ecological validity and practitioner-led refinement, 
consistent with a quasi-experimental classroom evaluation.
The researcher selected one intact Grade 11 STEM class enrolled in 
General Biology 1, developed a 20-item two-tier Meaningful Conceptual 
Understanding (MCU) test aligned to DepEd course competencies, and 
implemented a six-week PBL module while collecting pre–post scores, 
reviewer ratings, and implementation records. These instruments were 
designed and developed to address the study's objectives, purpose, and 
the specific needs of the target respondents. To ensure the instruments' 
robustness, a panel of reviewers (n = 6), consisting of experienced 
teachers, initially scrutinized the draft versions. Subsequently, the in­
struments underwent revisions based on their recommendations. After 
these revisions, the instruments were resubmitted for re-evaluation. 
Once the reviewers endorsed the instruments, they were put into ac­
tion. The process of instrument validation is crucial before widespread 
implementation, as it aims to verify the accuracy and comprehensive­
ness of the data while also confirming that the instrument measures 
what it is intended to measure (Fitzner, 2007; Funa & Ricafort, 2019a, 
2019b).
Taken together, the systematic ADDIE-guided development of the 
module, the expert-validated MCU test, and the quasi-experimental one- 
group pretest–posttest evaluation in an intact SHS class form a coherent 
methodological approach. This combination is a strength of the study 
because it balances ecological validity under real school conditions with 
sufficient rigor to draw reasonable inferences about changes in con­
ceptual understanding in this specific context.
2.2. Participants
The researcher employed a total-enumeration sampling method, 
encompassing one intact class (n = 38). The class was purposively 
selected in the public SHS where the researcher teaches. It met three 
criteria: General Biology 1 was offered that term; the school approved a 
six-week in-class implementation; and the intact Grade 11 STEM 
composition aligned with the module's scope, schedule, and target 
competencies. Total enumeration was appropriate given the small, 
relatively homogeneous intact class. The students were Filipino, 
enrolled in Grade 11-STEM, and were concurrently studying General 
Biology 1 during the study. The instructional focus was Biomolecules, a 
topic well suited for examining the macromolecules of the Pili plant 
(Canarium ovatum), a tropical tree known for its edible nuts and tradi­
tional medicinal uses (Funa et al., 2022). This choice aligned with the 
context of the contextualized problem presented to the students within 
the developed PBL instruction. The PBL problem challenged students to 
explore ways to enhance the sustainable development of C. ovatum, an 
indigenous product in their locality. Selection prioritized curricular 
alignment, feasibility, and ecological validity over population repre­
sentativeness; therefore, findings are context-bound to this class and 
school. This approach aimed not only to deepen students' understanding 
of biomolecules but also to encourage them to apply their knowledge to 
real-world, locally relevant issues.
2.3. Research instruments
The researcher utilized three instruments: the module evaluation 
checklist (MEC), the I-STEM-PBL module, and the meaningful concep­
tual understanding (MCU) test. The MEC served as the tool for evalu­
ating the I-STEM-PBL module, focusing on both face and content aspects 
prior to implementation, thus addressing the first problem in the current 
study. It consisted of two main segments, including an evaluation of 
adherence to the DepEd Standards for Module Development (DepEd, 
2021) and an assessment of the integration of STEM activities (Funa, 
2023; Okulu & Oguz-Unver, 2021). The segment concerning adherence 
to the DepEd Standards was modified from the Department of Education 
(DepEd) guidelines in the Philippines. This segment comprised six fac­
tors: (a) intellectual property rights, (b) learning competencies, (c) 
instructional design and organization, (d) instructional quality, (e) 
assessment, and (f) readability. The final component of the MEC 
encompassed a 29-item questionnaire on the integration of STEM ac­
tivities, adopted from the research of Okulu and Oguz-Unver (2021). 
This questionnaire was structured around four overarching themes, 
including the STEM Learning Environment, Activation of Students, 
STEM content and practices, and Connecting STEM. Each of these 
themes was further broken down into specific subthemes, allowing for a 
comprehensive evaluation of the module's integration of STEM activ­
ities. Okulu and Oguz-Unver (2021) conducted a pilot study with two 
researchers and two STEM activities, determining the reliability of the 
instrument through an inter-rater agreement of 89% and 93% for the 
STEM activities.
The primary tool utilized in this study, known as the I-STEM-PBL 
Module (see Fig. 1), underwent evaluation by a panel of six teachers. 
This evaluation panel consisted of three master teachers from DepEd and 
three educators from Higher Education Institutions (HEI). The assess­
ment process involved the use of the MEC before the module's imple­
mentation. The development of the I-STEM-PBL Module was grounded 
in the conceptual framework of the present study, which is centered on 
the I-STEM-PBL-ESD instructional framework (Funa, 2023; Funa et al., 
2024; Funa & Gabay, 2025). For the detailed specification of the inte­
grated STEM approach (roles of Science, Technology, Engineering, and 
Mathematics within task design and facilitation), see Funa et al. (2024); 
the present study applies that framework in the current context. This 
module's creation drew from the second-generation 3C3R (Core com­
ponents [3C]: Content, Context, Connection; Processing components 
[3R]: Researching, Reasoning, Reflecting; Enhancing components 
[second-generation]: Affect, Difficulty, Teamwork) problem design 
model (Hung, 2019) to generate problems, the K-16 PBL model by the 
Center for Problem-Based Learning (Torp & Sage, 2002) to scaffold the 
lessons, and the I-STEM approach to solve problems, see Fig. 2.
In this study, the second-generation 3C3R problem-design model 
guided task construction. The core components ensured alignment with 
General Biology 1 competencies, local authenticity through the Pili (C. 
ovatum) case, and explicit cross-links among biomolecule concepts. The

The third instrument used was the MCU test, a 20-item two-tiered 
multiple-choice test to assess and evaluate student learning related to

enzymes. The first tier evaluates the students' content knowledge, while 
the second tier delves into the reasoning behind their answers in the first 
tier. For example, one item asks which carbohydrate form is most readily 
absorbed by the body (Tier 1: monosaccharide), with the reason that 
single sugars require no further hydrolysis (Tier 2); another asks how 
many water molecules are released when forming a 500-monosaccha­

both tiers. This scoring system minimized the likelihood of obtaining the 
correct answer through mere guessing (Tsui & Treagust, 2010). For

Prior to the intact-class implementation, the I-STEM-PBL module was 
piloted in a laboratory high school with one Grade 11 section (n = 25) at 
the same grade level and curriculum coverage. The pilot examined 
practicality, clarity of task prompts and materials, classroom timing, and 
feasibility of facilitation moves. Data sources included expert appraisal 
using the MEC, classroom observations with teacher field notes, and a 
brief Students’ Evaluation Checklist at the sequence end. Feedback 
indicated feasibility within scheduled periods and adequate clarity of 
requirements. Revisions made after the pilot included tightening in­
structions, adjusting time allotments across steps in the Torp and Sage 
cycle, adding a graphic organizer to scaffold Tier-2 reasoning, and 
refining rubric language. The revised materials were then used in the 
quasi-experimental implementation.
2.4. Data gathering procedures
The researcher requested permission to conduct the study from the 
Superintendent and the Principal of the secondary public school where 
the research took place. The intact STEM section was then identified as 
the target group for the study. To ensure ethical standards, the 
researcher, with the assistance of the classroom adviser, explained the 
study's purpose, procedures, and ethical considerations to the students, 
subsequently obtaining informed consent from their parents or 
guardians.
Following the consent process, an interactive orientation session was 
conducted to facilitate a two-way exchange between the researcher and 
the students. During this session, the researcher introduced themselves, 
outlined the study's objectives, and requested the students' cooperation 
throughout the research process. This session also provided an oppor­
tunity for students to express their expectations and ask questions, 
fostering an environment of transparency and engagement.
To assess the students' prior knowledge, a pretest was administered. 
The results of this pretest served as a baseline for comparison with the 
posttest results to be gathered later. The I-STEM-PBL module, designed 
for the General Biology 1 course, was delivered to students in electronic 
format. This six-week module focused on the topic of biological mole­
cules and began with preparatory discussions on PBL, the specific type of 
PBL to be used, and the concept of I-STEM. Students were introduced to 
their new roles as experts in various STEM fields, and the researcher 
clarified the teacher's facilitative role and the central role of students in 
the learning process.
The study employed Pure PBL, placing the responsibility for 
mastering the content on biomolecules on the students, with the un­
derstanding that the teacher would be available for guidance and to 
address any misconceptions. To accommodate the students' needs, the 
deadline for finalizing and submitting their outputs was extended to the 
end of the term. Upon completion of the module, a posttest was 
administered to evaluate the students' learning outcomes, with the 
pretest and posttest results undergoing thorough analysis.
Throughout the implementation of the I-STEM-PBL module, the 
researcher conducted brief semi-structured exit interviews with two to 
three students after each lesson, time permitting. The researcher served 
as the interviewer. A semi-structured interview guide (reviewed by the 
same expert-teacher panel that examined the instruments) covered: (a) 
how students analyzed the problem, (b) inquiry steps taken, (c) team­
work and roles, (d) difficulties and supports needed, and (e) perceived 
learning and misconceptions addressed. Interviews were five–ten mi­
nutes, held in a quiet corner of the classroom or an adjacent hallway, and 
conducted in Filipino or English, depending on student preference. 
Audio recording was used with consent; when recording was not 
feasible, the researcher kept contemporaneous field notes. Students 
were selected using rotating purposive sampling to hear from different 
groups and roles across the six weeks. After implementation, the 
researcher conducted additional semi-structured follow-up interviews to 
capture overall reflections on the I-STEM-PBL experience. Interview 
notes and recordings were anonymized and later summarized to trian­
gulate the pretest–posttest results.
2.5. Data analysis
Conceptual understanding was operationalized in line with Ander­
son and Krathwohl (2001) and DepEd K–12 competencies as four facets: 
(a) concept connections; (b) explanation/justification (Tier-2 reasons); 
(c) application/transfer to real-world contexts; and (d) misconception 
diagnosis via the two-tier structure (Treagust, 1988). Items on the 
Meaningful Conceptual Understanding (MCU) test were written to elicit 
these facets. Consistent with the study's scope and class-size constraints, 
outcomes are reported as total MCU scores (0–20); facet-level analyses 
are planned for future work.
Pretest–posttest differences in MCU total scores were examined using 
a paired-samples t-test. Prior to analysis, assumption checks were con­
ducted: the same instrument was administered at both time points; 
difference scores were screened for outliers via a boxplot; and the dis­
tribution of difference scores was assessed for approximate normality 
using histogram inspection and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapir­
o–Wilk tests. While the lack of randomization may raise concerns about 
internal validity, that is, whether observed effects are attributable to the 
intervention or other factors—this does not preclude the use of a t-test, 
provided that other assumptions are satisfied and the analysis aims to 
understand differences within the sample (Campbell & Stanley, 2015). 
Additionally, Cohen's d was calculated to evaluate the magnitude of the 
difference between the pretest and posttest scores. Interview results 
were also analyzed to provide a richer, qualitative perspective that 
complements the quantitative findings, offering a more comprehensive 
understanding of the students' experiences and learning outcomes.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Design and activities of the I-STEM-PBL module
The I-STEM-PBL Module (Funa, 2023; Funa et al., 2024) is a 
six-week, digital sequence for Grade 11 General Biology 1 (Bio­
molecules) built around a single authentic problem: how to enhance the 
sustainable development of C. ovatum (Pili) using biomolecular knowl­
edge (see Fig. 3). The design integrates Science, Technology, Engineer­
ing, and Mathematics in an explicit way and is organized using the 
second-generation 3C3R problem-design model (Content, Context, 
Connection; Researching, Reasoning, Reflecting; Affect, Difficulty, 
Teamwork) together with the K–16 PBL scaffolding cycle of Torp and 
Sage. Small collaborative groups with defined roles work on the problem 
across nine interrelated activities that move from orientation, to prob­
lem analysis and inquiry, to solution design, communication, and

3.1.1. Phase 1: orientation to I-STEM-PBL (activities 1–2)
The first activity, “Prepare the Learners,” orients students to the I- 
STEM-PBL approach. The module introduces the definition and type of 
PBL used, the underlying models for problem design (3C3R) and scaf­
folding (K–16 PBL), and clarifies the roles of the teacher (facilitator), 
students (self-directed problem solvers), and the learning environment 
(classroom, laboratory, or other collaborative spaces). This ensures that 
students understand why they will work in small groups, how they are 
expected to inquire, and how different STEM domains will be integrated 
when solving the problem.
In the second activity, the PBL problem is formally introduced 
through a contextualized letter from a fictional company. The letter 
specifies that students are treated as STEM “experts,” outlines their 
missions and roles, and poses the core challenge: to use their under­
standing of biomolecules to propose ways to enhance the sustainable 
development of C. ovatum. The problem is written using 3C3R criteria to 
ensure clear content alignment, authentic local context, and explicit 
conceptual connections.
3.1.2. Phase 2: problem analysis and inquiry (activities 3–6)
Activities 3–5 guide students through an iterative cycle of analyzing 
what is known about the problem, identifying what still needs to be 
known, and planning how to gather and share information. In the third 
activity, learners list prior knowledge, data, and experiences related to 
the Pili case and biomolecules, and they generate “need-to-know” 
questions and initial ideas. The fourth activity requires students to 
construct a concept map (see Fig. 4) that integrates the four STEM do­
mains, making explicit how biological, technological, engineering, and 
mathematical ideas relate to the problem and potential solutions.
In the fifth activity, students engage in comprehensive planning for 
information gathering and sharing. They take on the dual responsibility 
of not only procuring data that substantiates their responses in the 
"need-to-know" section but also developing a structured approach for 
collecting pertinent information. This process includes evaluating the 
relevance of the acquired data to the central problem and crafting 
effective strategies for presenting this information within the classroom 
setting. This phase's culmination involves students consolidating and 
summarizing the information they've gathered, setting the stage for the 
subsequent activity. The third, fourth, and fifth phases are iterative (see 
Fig. 3), allowing students to move back and forth between them until 
they feel prepared to proceed to the sixth activity.
During the sixth activity, students are tasked with generating po­
tential solutions. They are encouraged to articulate their strategies while 
assessing the advantages, disadvantages, and repercussions associated 
with each proposed approach. In this context, a "strategy" is a crafted 
plan of action designed to accomplish specific objectives. Here, "pros" 
denote the affirmative aspects and benefits linked to a given decision, 
action, or scenario, while "cons" encompass the adverse facets or 
possible drawbacks. Additionally, the term "consequences" pertains to 
the resulting outcomes of a particular decision, action, or situation, 
encapsulating both immediate and long-term effects.
3.1.3. Phase 3: Solution design, communication, and reflection (activities 
7–9)
The seventh activity revolves around the critical task of discerning 
the most suitable solution. The PBL problem in the I-STEM-PBL module 
provides clear criteria for evaluating the optimal solution, covering key 
aspects (Hung, 2019) like safety, feasibility, cost-effectiveness, envi­
ronmental sustainability, profitability, and social impact. Furthermore, 
the PBL problem in the I-STEM-PBL module incorporates essential ele­
ments of the solution proposal, which encompass Basic Information, 
Technical Description, Timeline, Budgetary Requirements, and Refer­
ences (Torp & Sage, 2002). Students are not only tasked with identifying 
the best-fit solution but also with conveying it in an engaging and 
illustrative manner. They have the creative freedom to present their 
solutions using a diverse range of media, such as diagrams, cartoons, 
posters, or any other medium of their choice. This allows them to 
effectively communicate their proposals in a visually compelling and 
informative manner.
In the eighth activity, students embark on the process of developing 
and presenting their proposed solutions. This stage requires students to 
adeptly convey their solutions to a specific target audience, which could 
range from consumers and businesses to fellow researchers. Importantly, 
students enjoy the creative liberty to choose the medium that best suits 
their presentation, be it videos, posters, mind maps, or any other format 
that effectively conveys their ideas. The assessment of their work is 
guided by a set of well-defined criteria, which includes an evaluation of 
their adherence to STEM, the substantive content of their presentations, 
the levels of originality and creativity demonstrated, and the overall 
quality of their output. This multifaceted evaluation ensures that stu­
dents are not only generating solutions but also effectively communi­
cating them while demonstrating a comprehensive understanding of 
STEM principles.
The ninth activity involves a debriefing session that marks the 
culmination of the I-STEM-PBL module. This post-evaluation phase is 
designed to prompt students to engage in reflective thinking. They are 
presented with a set of thought-provoking questions that encourage 
them to contemplate their learning journey, including their experiences, 
thoughts, feelings, and values related to the subject matter. By guiding

3.3. Enhancing conceptual understanding through I-STEM-PBL
To determine if the I-STEM-PBL module improves students' concep­
tual understanding of biomolecules, the researcher administered both a 
pretest and a posttest. Conceptual understanding, in this context, refers 
to students' capacity to amalgamate their existing knowledge with 
domain-specific information and apply this knowledge to gain a deeper 
comprehension of the world (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001; Mediana 
et al., 2025; Roth, 1990). The results of the meaningful conceptual un­
derstanding test are presented in Table 4.
As depicted in Table 4, the pretest scores of individual students, 
when aggregated, showed an initial combined correct-response rate of 
24.2%, which increased to 30.5% in the posttest, an absolute gain of 6.3 
percentage points corresponding to an overall normalized gain of gp =
0.08. By indicator, mean gains were Carbohydrates & Lipids gp = 0.06 
and Proteins & Enzymes gp = 0.10 (both low), indicating improvement 
in conceptual understanding with room for deeper learning. It is worth 
highlighting that individual student performance exhibited variance, as 
indicated by the range of scores and the divergent directions of change. 
Notably, some items, such as Item 12 (essential amino acids; gp = −0.06) 
and Item 20 (oil extraction in plants; gp = −0.03), showed a decline from 
pretest to posttest. This decline may be due to the complexity of these 
items, insufficient focus during instruction, or individual differences in 
learning. For example, Student 35 commented, “I found item 12 chal­
lenging because the second-tier choices seemed almost correct, making 
it hard to determine the right answer,” while Student 10 said, “Item 20 
contained information we only briefly covered, such as the off-taste of 
aldehydes.” Additionally, factors like test fatigue could have impacted 
performance, as noted by Student 8 who mentioned, “I felt overwhelmed 
by the end of the test because, with the two-tiered format, getting one 
part wrong meant losing all points for that item.”
In their written reflections, several students also described how 
manageable the module felt in everyday classroom conditions. One 
student commented, “I was able to cope somehow because I can manage 
my time and schedule. I have already read some studies on Pili and 
biomolecules, and I am really interested in continuing our study next 
term. I feel that I have learned what I need to know about the concept of 
biomolecules, and sometimes even more than what the curriculum re­
quires” (Student 35). Another student emphasized the learner-centered 
design of the module, noting, “The teaching method is okay because 
we understand that it is student-centered. We were given the freedom to 
study biomolecules based on what we want and need to solve the 
problem” (Student 25). Together, these responses suggest that students 
experienced the I-STEM-PBL module as useable in practice and capable 
of encouraging effortful, self-directed engagement with biomolecules.
Upon analyzing the specific items with the highest scores, interesting 
trends emerge. During the pretest, the top three highest-scoring items 
were 11 (n = 25), 9 (n = 20), and 2 and 13 (n = 17 each). These items 
encompass diverse facets of biomolecules. Item 11 pertains to nucleic 
acids, Item 9 delves into the cellular composition concerning bio­
molecules, Item 2 focuses on energy generation derived from bio­
molecules, and Item 13 relates to the presence of amino acids. These 
results suggest that students demonstrated a relatively strong under­
standing of these specific topics during the pretest, showcasing a foun­
dational grasp of these concepts within the realm of biomolecules. In the 
posttest, the three items with the highest scores were 16 (n = 25), 10 (n 
= 24), and 11 (n = 22). Item 16 investigated the role of nucleic acid in 
various organisms, Item 10 examined lipids, while Item 11 focused on 
the concept of nucleic acids and mutations. Consistent with these shifts, 
several items showed substantive positive item-level N-gains, including 
Item 16 (gp = 0.54) and Item 10 (gp = 0.39), indicating marked 
improvement in these concepts.
Students’ comments also reveal how this problem-driven, integrated 
approach was experienced in relation to high-stakes assessments. One 
learner explained, “The knowledge that we have is different from my 
other classmates. I am afraid that what I might learn is less than what we 
need in the final exam or in the college entrance exams” (Student 25). 
Another student, who reported reading additional materials on Pili and 
biomolecules, shared that they were “really interested in continuing our 
study next term” and felt that they had gone “beyond what the curric­
ulum requires” in terms of understanding biomolecules (Student 35). 
These accounts indicate that while the module supported deeper con­
ceptual work on key biomolecular ideas, some learners still weighed this 
against perceived demands of traditional examinations, highlighting a 
tension between authentic, useable practice and exam-oriented 
coverage.
Interestingly, the consistent high performance on Item 11 in both the 
pretest and posttest suggests a strong and sustained understanding, 
possibly due to well-focused teaching. The shift in high-scoring items 
from basic topics like cellular composition and energy generation in the 
pretest to more complex topics like the role of nucleic acid and muta­
tions in the posttest shows that students' knowledge deepened. This 
progression indicates that the educational interventions successfully 
enhanced students’ understanding of biomolecular concepts, moving 
from foundational to more detailed knowledge.

To determine the appropriateness of conducting a t-test on the pre­
test and posttest data, a series of analyses were performed to assess the 
normality of the data distribution and the presence of outliers. As 
illustrated in Fig. 5, the histogram of the differences between pretest and 
posttest scores appears to follow a normal distribution, and the 
accompanying boxplot reveals no outliers. These observations suggest 
that the data meet the preliminary requirements for performing a t-test 
(Campbell & Stanley, 2015).
Further analysis of the normality of the data was conducted using 
statistical measures. The skewness of the data was found to be 0.238, 
and the kurtosis was −0.386. These values indicate a relatively normal 
distribution of the data, as skewness values between −1 and 1 and 
kurtosis values between −2 and 2 are generally considered acceptable 
for normality (Field, 2024). Additionally, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test 
yielded a statistic of p = .200, and the Shapiro-Wilk test produced a 
statistic of p = .504. Both p-values are greater than the conventional 
alpha level of 0.05, suggesting that there is no significant deviation from 
normality in the dataset (Field, 2024). Overall, the combination of visual 
inspections and statistical tests supports the assumption of normality for 
the pretest and posttest scores. Thus, the use of a t-test for analyzing the 
differences between the pretest and posttest results is deemed appro­
priate. A paired-samples t-test was conducted to assess whether there 
was a significant increase in the students’ conceptual understanding 
using the I-STEM-PBL module (see Table 5).
The intervention yielded a statistically significant improvement in 
conceptual understanding at the whole-test level. Mean MCU scores 
increased from 4.81 (SD = 2.00) to 6.14 (SD = 2.43), t(37) = 2.89, p =
.007, d = 0.48, indicating a small–medium educationally meaningful 
effect. The corresponding student-level normalized gain was g = 0.08. 
Using the item-percentage aggregation, correct responses rose from 
24.2% to 30.5% (g = 0.08), corroborating a low but positive gain. 
Together, these metrics suggest that the I-STEM-PBL sequence improved 
outcomes while leaving headroom for deeper conceptual consolidation.
Disaggregation by indicator shows a differentiated pattern. Proteins 
& Enzymes improved significantly from 2.22 (1.35) to 3.11 (1.83), t(37) 
= 2.65, p = .012, d = 0.44, g = 0.10, whereas Carbohydrates & Lipids 
increased from 2.58 (1.46) to 3.03 (1.38) without reaching significance, 
t(37) = 1.37, p = .180, d = 0.23, g = 0.06. Item-level diagnostics are 
consistent with this profile: the largest gains occurred on Item 16 (gp =
0.54; roles of nucleic acids) and Item 10 (gp = 0.39; lipids), whereas 
declines on Items 12 (essential amino acids; gp = −0.05) and 20 (oil 
extraction; gp = −0.03) indicate persistent difficulty in specific applied 
contexts.
Two factors may account for these contrasts. First, several I-STEM- 
PBL tasks explicitly targeted nucleic-acid mechanisms and lipid 
reasoning, which likely concentrated practice on those constructs, as 
reflected by the stable/high performance on Item 11 and the strong 
gains on Items 10 and 16. Second, items with highly plausible Tier-2 
reasons (e.g., Item 12) or industrial/process applications (e.g., Item 
20) appear to have exceeded the breadth of students’ self-directed in­
quiry, and some students reported cognitive load near the end of the 
two-tier test. These observations suggest that Tier-2 reasoning scaffolds 
and brief applied mini-tasks should be strengthened, particularly for 
biochemical applications not central to the problem product.
The I-STEM-PBL implementation produced a statistically reliable, 
small–medium overall improvement, with stronger effects for Proteins & 
Enzymes than for Carbohydrates & Lipids. The item diagnostics pinpoint 
specific misconceptions and under-addressed applications that can be 
targeted in the next iteration (e.g., structured evaluation of near-miss 
explanations, short application vignettes, and spaced retrieval checks). 
This refinement agenda is expected to convert low normalized gains into 
moderate gains while preserving authenticity and student agency in the 
PBL cycle.
The overall effect is consistent with evidence that PBL produces 
positive learning outcomes in science, particularly at the secondary 
level, with effects robust across topics (Funa & Prudente, 2021). Gains 
concentrated where the module's activities repeatedly engaged target 
constructs—mirroring reports that carefully aligned problem scenarios, 
opportunities for explanation, and reflective prompts strengthen out­
comes (Savery, 2019; Schmidt et al., 2009, 2019). When PBL is inte­
grated with STEM, studies note improvements in higher-order thinking, 
integration across domains, and collaboration (El Sayary et al., 2015;

match findings that short, single-cycle implementations yield modest 
growth unless reinforced by distributed practice and explicit meta­
cognitive support (Funa & Prudente, 2021; Hmelo-Silver, 2004).

3.4. Implications of I-STEM-PBL module for Science education
The evaluation of the I-STEM-PBL module against DepEd standards 
underscores the importance of aligning educational materials with 
established standards. Instrument evaluation is crucial to check if the 
instruments fit with the goals of the researcher (Fitzner, 2007; Funa & 
Ricafort, 2019a, 2019b). This process ensures that the learning module 
not only meets the necessary competencies, instructional quality, and 
readability standards but may also enhance the overall curriculum 
quality. Moreover, the involvement of reviewers with fields spanning 
diverse scientific domains, along with an English language reviewer, 
emphasizes the significance of an interdisciplinary approach in educa­

The positive results obtained from the evaluation of the I-STEM-PBL 
module indicate the sound use of the PBL approach in teaching STEM 
subjects. The emphasis on PBL within the I-STEM-PBL module signals a 
departure from passive learning towards active involvement. PBL en­
courages students to explore real-world challenges, collaborate with 
peers, and develop critical thinking and problem-solving skills (Barrows, 
1996; Grant & Tamim, 2019; Ramallosa et al., 2022). This approach 
nurtures a more profound comprehension of concepts and encourages 
the practical application of knowledge (Funa et al., 2023). By inte­
grating PBL into the curriculum, students are empowered to become 
dynamic participants in their own educational journey, leading to 
increased motivation, engagement, and the cultivation of essential 
21st-century competencies.
However, it is important to recognize that PBL and MCU tests are not 
universally suitable for all students. The effectiveness of these methods 
can vary significantly based on the unique needs, learning styles, and 
educational contexts of the students (Funa, 2023, 2025). Teachers must 
carefully assess whether these instructional strategies align with their 
students' specific characteristics and capabilities. This thoughtful eval­
uation is crucial, as a one-size-fits-all approach may lead to suboptimal 
learning outcomes. Some students may thrive in a PBL environment, 
where active involvement and real-world problem-solving are empha­
sized, while others might find these methods challenging or over­
whelming (Funa, 2023, 2025; Funa et al., 2023). Similarly, the MCU 
tests, designed to probe deeper understanding, may not be the best 
assessment tool for every learner. By tailoring instructional strategies to 
fit the diverse needs of their students, teachers may foster more effective 
learning experiences. This personalized approach helps prevent poten­
tial disengagement or frustration, ensuring that all students have the 
opportunity to succeed and benefit from the curriculum.
In addition, the integration of STEM with real-life scenarios high­
lights the importance of practical application in education. By bridging 
academic disciplines with real-world challenges, students are better 
prepared to apply their knowledge and skills in practical settings, 
nurturing their critical thinking and problem-solving abilities (McComas 
& Burgin, 2020). The I-STEM-PBL module carries profound implications 
for the progress of educational curricula. Its incorporation of I-STEM 
within the educational framework underscores the critical significance 
of interdisciplinary education (El Sayary et al., 2015).
Traditional curricula often compartmentalize subjects, limiting stu­
dents' ability to recognize the interrelationships between various fields 
of study. Through the integration of science, technology, engineering, 
and mathematics, the I-STEM-PBL module fosters a comprehensive un­
derstanding of knowledge and encourages students to apply principles 
and competencies across diverse domains. This interdisciplinary 
approach equips students to address the intricate challenges of the 
contemporary world, where solutions frequently require expertise from 
a multitude of disciplines and knowledge domains (Nurius et al., 2017).
4. Conclusion and recommendations
The evaluation and implementation of the I-STEM-PBL module have 
revealed significant insights into STEM education and curriculum 
development. The multifaceted approach employed in this module, 
combining interdisciplinary learning, PBL, and real-world application, 
has demonstrated its potential to transform teaching and learning in the 
STEM fields. The positive outcomes observed in the evaluation of the I- 
STEM-PBL module reinforced the efficacy of PBL and the Integrated 
STEM approach. This student-centered method promotes conceptual 
understanding. The integration of STEM may enrich the learning expe­
rience, ensuring students are well-prepared for the challenges they will 
face in their future careers. The I-STEM-PBL module represents a 
transformative approach to education. It emphasizes the importance of 
interdisciplinary learning, active participation, and real-world rele­
vance. These elements are not only vital for comprehensive STEM ed­
ucation but also align with the skills and knowledge needed in the 
contemporary workforce.
Teachers can leverage the findings to enhance their instructional 
methods by incorporating more interdisciplinary and problem-based 
learning activities. The success of the I-STEM-PBL module suggests 
that educators should consider integrating similar approaches to foster a

5. Limitations
When considering the present study, it is important to acknowledge 
its limitations. The study's use of a total enumeration sampling method 
may restrict the generalizability of findings to wider student pop­
ulations. While the study focused on promoting student responsibility 
for content mastery and problem-solving, with the teacher adopting a 
facilitative role, this approach might not be universally applicable to all 
educational settings, which may demand varying levels of teacher 
guidance and student autonomy. Furthermore, the study concentrated 
on a six-week I-STEM-PBL module, providing valuable insights into 
short-term learning outcomes. Additionally, it is crucial to recognize 
that the study was conducted within a specific educational context, 
where external factors like available resources, teacher expertise, and 
student motivation could have influenced the results. Replicating the 
study in diverse settings becomes imperative to assess the broader 
applicability of the I-STEM-PBL module. Notwithstanding these limita­
tions, the present study underscores the significant potential of the I- 
STEM-PBL approach in enhancing education and calls for continued 
research to unlock its full benefits.
