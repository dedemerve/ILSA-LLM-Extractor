Predicting students’ problem-solving success in computer-based simulations at an early stage
allows adaptive educational systems to provide learners with personalized support. In this paper,
we predict students’ problem-solving success by applying a machine-learning model, the random
forest, to produce a binary classification (more vs. less successful students). During a businessrelated problem scenario that lasted 55 min, early behavioral data (during the first 5, 10, and
20 min) such as mouse clicks and keyboard strokes (approx. 29,800 early-window clickstreams
and keystrokes during the first 20 min) of 234 trainees were recorded, mirroring the students’
problem-solving behavior. We used the n-gram sequence mining technique, which was originally
introduced within the emerging disciplines of natural language processing, text mining, and
machine learning and has proven to be effective, particularly in the examination of online
behavior. We trained the random forest model with training datasets that included all features
(bigrams), as well as selected features (the most predictable bigrams explaining inter-group differences). Our results show that early predictions based on the first 10 and 20 min contained
sufficient information to accurately predict problem-solving success, while predictions that are
too early (based on the first 5 min) do not. As the size of the initial time window expanded, the
classification performance improved. Moreover, the selection of the most predictable features
improved the models’ performance for all three time intervals. The model that was trained with
only selected robust features that occurred in the first 20 min achieved the highest ROC AUC
score of almost 0.70. This result falls within the range of accuracy scores observed in similar
studies. From the instructor’s perspective, predictions help in the early identification of weak
students and can provide them with personalized learning prompts. For more successful students,
tasks can be enriched adaptively.

higher-order competences such as problem solving (Binkley et al., 2012; World Economic Forum, 2023). Particularly in the face of
growing information diversity at the workplace, it is crucial to classify and critically evaluate various sources of information in order to
cope with current conditions (“Multiple Document Comprehension” (MDC), Hahnel et al., 2021; Schoor et al., 2020, 2021). In the
‘Controlling’ domain, for example, employees must be able to perform variance analyses based on both relevant and irrelevant information to identify financial discrepancies. Another workplace example involves the selection of suppliers by evaluating factors such
as cost-effectiveness, quality, and delivery times. Open-ended learning environments (OELEs) such as computer-based simulations are
used to develop these so-called 21st century abilities. According to a meta-analysis by Chernikova et al. (2020), simulations have
strong effects on learning in different domains. Computer-based simulations are characterized mainly by two aspects. First, they enable
the deployment of real-life situations, allowing authentic learning and a valid learning environment (Braunstein et al., 2022). Second,
the integration of computer-based assessment opens up new possibilities for analyzing and supporting problem-solving behavior.
When learners navigate in computer-based environments, their interactions with the software (log data) are usually time-stamped and
recorded in individual log files. Although problem solving is processual in nature, learners have predominantly been evaluated based
on their solution (“product”), while the actual processes that lead to these products often remain a “black box” (Baker et al., 2020, p.
19; Mayr et al., 2011, p. 8). In recent years, the analysis of these log data has gained increasing attention as they offer insights into
latent and interwoven cognitive, metacognitive, and non-cognitive processes. Log files are also referred to as “trace data” (Hadwin
et al., 2007), although this latter term can also refer to think-alouds, eye-tracking, or facial recognition (Azevedo et al., 2013). Log files
allow the complete reconstruction of a user’s individual problem-solving process in terms of mouse clicks and keystrokes (some
implementations even record mouse movements).
Through educational data mining (EDM) techniques, natural language processing (NLP), and machine learning (ML), problemsolving strategies can be analyzed by inferring latent cognitive processes from the recorded trace data (Wang et al., 2023). Specifically in the field of business education, analyzing students’ cognitive processes (e.g. use of tools and document retrieval) during
problem solving enables the identification of heuristics and algorithms as different types of problem-solving strategies. Furthermore,
the analysis of students’ problem-solving processes enables predictions of their performance and learning success (Brandl et al., 2021;
Li et al., 2017; Tomasevic et al., 2020). Early predictions can then be used to promote learning and problem solving using prompting
(Bannert, 2009; Sabourin et al., 2013; Ulitzsch et al., 2022). Adaptive environments, enriched with personalized support, for example,
in the form of metacognitive and cognitive prompts, play a crucial role in fostering effective problem-solving skills (Bannert, 2009;
Bannert & Mengelkamp, 2013). Embedding prompts within the learning context enhances the overall educational experience by
providing tailored assistance to individuals with diverse proficiency levels. Especially for weaker students adaptive simulations can be
helpful but even for stronger students, difficulty can be adapted to their knowledge status. Previous research has shown that while
students’ engagement level (i.e., the frequency of actions or duration spent on a specific task) provides some information about their
problem-solving behavior, the analysis of adjoining interactions yields more fine-grained insights into their behavior (Kinnebrew et al.,
2013). Hence, sequence pattern mining that considers the occurrence and frequency of not only single actions (“unigrams”) but also
combinations of actions (“bigrams”, “trigrams”, etc.) can be more informative (He & Von Davier, 2015; Gitinabard et al., 2019; Park
et al., 2023) because it considers the transition from one activity to another (e.g., making notes after reading a relevant document). By
employing selective, robust n-grams with strong predictive capabilities that play a significant role in explaining variances among
different performance groups and classification models, it becomes possible to predict students’ success. By using machine learning,
the openness and dynamic nature of problem-based tasks, the huge amount of data and the categorization of presumed theories can be
considered. Moreover, embedding machine learning algorithms into the software enables problem-based learning with personalized
support.
Several studies have already investigated the use of robust n-grams and predictions in computer-based environments. However,
problem-solving are specific (Achtenhagen & Weber, 2018; Dörner & Funke, 2017; Herl et al., 1999), and to the best of our knowledge,
only a few studies had yet explored behavior actions but no behavior sequences for tasks with a long duration that are performed in a
very open learning environment (such as our applied office simulation) by vocational education and training (VET) students (see also
the systematic review by Wang et al., 2023). Hence, our study aims to examine the relationship between activity sequences and
students’ problem-solving success to identify predictors for successful and unsuccessful problem solving in this setting, using a random
forest model to construct a binary classification of students’ problem-solving success based on their log files at an early stage of
processing. Specifically, we address three research questions.
(1) How do groups with different levels of problem-solving success differ in their problem-solving behavior in a computer-based
office simulation?
(2) Which features (bigrams) are robust and powerful predictors for problem-solving success?
(3) At what time can overall problem-solving success be predicted based on problem-solving behavior?
2. Theoretical background
2.1. Problem solving in computer-based simulations
Computer-based simulations are particularly suitable for problem-based learning because they mirror authentic situations and
allow new insights into the learners’ problem-solving behavior. In the field of psychology, “problem solving” refers to the process of
discovering a solution to a presented problem without sufficient prior knowledge of the solution. This requires self-regulated learning

processes such as planning, goal setting to achieve the desired solution, and progress monitoring and reflecting (Taub et al., 2022).
Thus, complex problem solving requires a multifaceted interplay of mental processes (Dörner & Funke, 2017; Frensch & Funke, 2014;
Herl et al., 1999) involving cognitive (e.g., domain knowledge), meta-cognitive (e.g., planning and monitoring), and non-cognitive
competences (e.g., emotions and motivation). In other words, both active knowledge acquisition (exploring the problem space) and
knowledge application (working on a concrete task) are necessary to construct an internal, mental problem model (Funke, 2001).
In this context, problems are distinguished as being either well-defined or ill-defined (Funke, 2003). Well-defined problems are
characterized by clear processes and a limited set of operators to solve the problem, while ill-defined problems involve multiple
possible operators and have multiple potential solutions (Funke, 2003). Typically, ill-defined problems are considered to be problems
with a higher degree of complexity. Funke (2019) also notes that simple problems require less background knowledge and involve a
smaller number of processes to achieve successful solutions, while complex tasks demand more time and effort. Furthermore, two
specific types of problems have been identified: “algorithmic problems” (Jonassen, 2000b) and “information problems” (Brand-Gruwel et al., 2009). Algorithm problems require individuals to apply domain-specific algorithms and rules to arrive at a solution,
while information problems require deductive reasoning about the relevance of given or retrieved information. Behrens et al. (2012)
proposed that the action scope in simulations can be classified into four spaces: the problem space, tool space, solution space, and
response space. Depending on the width of these spaces, the level of task complexity can be adjusted. On a continuum, for instance, the
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), a well-known large-scale computer-based assessment, is an example of a
simulation with narrow problem spaces such as the well-structured “MicroDYN.” In contrast, real-world problems are typically
characterized by greater complexity and dynamics, for example, in “microworlds” (Brehmer & Dörner, 1993; Plass & Schwartz, 2014).
Sophisticated instructional design software is necessary for designing more comprehensive problems with realistic characteristics—such as Intelligent Tutoring Systems (Bouchet et al., 2012), which offers a broader problem space and enables simulating
authentic scenarios.
In the pursuit of achieving desired goals, learners employ various strategies (e.g., taking notes; Trafton & Trickett, 2001; Trevors
et al., 2014) to apply the acquired knowledge. The term ‘strategy’ is extensively discussed in the field of problem solving (Winne,
2001). One well-known strategy employed in problem-solving processes is the use of heuristics, such as trial and error (Middleton,
2002; Winne & Nesbit, 2010). Heuristics are domain-general strategies that rely on past experiences and lead to rapid conclusions
across various domains. However, in the case of complex problems and unfamiliar environments where knowledge might be
incomplete, heuristics can be more prone to error, resulting in systematic errors or biases. To address this issue, a more reliable
strategy, although domain-specific, is the use of algorithms (Middleton, 2002; Newell & Simon, 1972; Simon & Newell, 1965). While
algorithms require more time and effort due to their iterative nature, they offer a higher likelihood of finding a solution (Ludwig &
Rausch, 2023). Van Merriënboer (2013) categorizes problem-solving methods into four types along a continuum: (1) “weak
problem-solving methods” (e.g., note-taking; Ludwig & Rausch, 2023), (2) “knowledge-based problem-solving methods” (e.g., information reasoning, ebd.), (3) “strong problem-solving methods” (e.g., using domain-specific algorithms in a spreadsheet; ebd.), and
(4) a combination of “strong” and “knowledge-based methods.” According to Van Merriënboer (2013), algorithms are strong
problem-solving methods, whereas heuristics are weak problem-solving methods that have a domain-generic applicability to problem
situations. For example, using a notepad to retrieve or insert notes can be categorized as a weak method.
In problem solving, the acquisition of domain-specific knowledge is closely related to information retrieval and the use of cognitive
tools. The information retrieval process involves analysis and MDC (Hahnel et al., 2021), which enables constructing a coherent
representation of a given topic for successful problem solving, even when redundant, inconsistent, or incomplete information is present
(Schoor et al., 2020). Efficiently acquiring knowledge through document-based means should lead to effective goal-oriented application of knowledge to accomplish desired objectives (Fischer et al., 2012; Hahnel et al., 2021). To cope with the complex challenges
within a simulation, learners are typically provided with authentic tools to support their cognitive processes. As mentioned in Ludwig
and Rausch (2023), Jonassen (2000a) emphasizes the importance of providing comprehensive cognitive tools that assist in developing
necessary skills and guide problem solvers throughout their tasks. Lajoie (1993; secondary cited in Ludwig & Rausch, 2023) further
categorizes four cognitive tools that facilitate the acquisition and application of knowledge, leading to the achievement of sub-goals
and enhancing the external representation of internal cognitive processes (Liu & Bera, 2005). According to Lajoie (1993), these tools
can be classified as follows: (1) cognitive process support tools, such as notepads or calculators, which help facilitate cognitive processes; (2) cognitive load-sharing tools, such as databases or files containing multiple documents, which help distribute the cognitive
load by providing access to relevant information sources; (3) tools that enable engagement in cognitive activities typically beyond
one’s reach, including domain-specific tools such as spreadsheets or voltmeters, which may not be readily available in a classroom
setting; and (4) tools that enhance hypothesis generation and testing, such as soliciting feedback on partial solutions. Embedded
cognitive tools are often inspired by real-world design and functionality, and thus the distinction between domain-general and
domain-specific tools becomes apparent in their implementation. Understanding the cognitive processes of participants, such as the
use of tools in digital learning environments, to predict success relies on an analysis of log data. In the following section, we elaborate
on the methods and approaches employed in this analysis.

2.2. Analysis of log files in computer-based simulations
Computer-based psychological and educational assessments as well as learning environments offer a convenient and non-intrusive
way to capture and save learners’ interactions as log files (Stadler et al., 2019; Taub et al., 2022). The log files provide a detailed record
of each interaction, time-stamped and separated into discrete actions such as mouse clicks, cursor movements, dragging, keystrokes,
and idle time (He & Von Davier, 2016; Stadler et al., 2019). Log files also offer a distinct advantage in terms of objectivity, as they do
not rely on students’ self-reports that might be biased or incomplete (Taub et al., 2022). For instance, students often overestimate their
use of metacognitive strategies and may provide invalid information about their cognitive and metacognitive activities (Artelt, 2000;
Veenman & van Cleef, 2019).
Behavior process indicators or metrics that reflect specific actions or behaviors within the simulation are then derived from the log
files based on two EDM techniques: (1) theory-driven analysis (a “top-down” approach) and (2) data-driven analysis (a “bottom-up”
approach; for the two approaches see Drachsler & Goldhammer, 2020; Goldhammer et al., 2021; Hahnel et al., 2023; Mislevy, 2019).
For theory-driven analyses, researchers define behavioral indicators based on existing theoretical models, while for data-driven analyses, behavioral indicators are derived directly from observable process data. Both approaches allow inferences about the latent
problem-solving processes (e.g. Greiff et al., 2015; He & Von Davier, 2015; Stadler et al., 2019). Wang and Mousavi (2023) differ
between generic (such as total session length/time-on-task or number of interactions) and course-related log data (such as total
number of tasks and videos watched, number of attempts to solve an exercise, or total number of asked hints). For example, the
frequency and duration of document retrievals can serve as course-specific indicators of cognitive processes, such as a lack of
knowledge differentiation between relevant and irrelevant sources (Funke et al., 2018; Wilson et al., 2018, pp. 181–210).
In our study, we focus on data-driven, exploratory approaches to log file analysis. Data-driven methods aim to identify meaningful
patterns within long and repetitive problem-solving sequences (Stadler et al., 2019). A prominent approach uses n-grams, which
involves decomposing complete behavior sequences into smaller units or mini-sequences (Han et al., 2019; He & Von Davier, 2016). In
text mining, each word (unigram) is treated as a distinct feature, and the collection of all words in the text constitutes a “bag of words”
(Damashek, 1995). N-grams, which were initially used in NLP for tasks like text mining and classification, represent sequences of n
contiguous words (Damashek, 1995). The n-gram approach offers several benefits. It not only encodes keywords but also considers the
ordering of words, making it a valuable tool for generating features that preserve significant information about the sequence order
(Han et al., 2019). This method has been adopted in various domains, including e-commerce, where its benefits in differentiating
behavioral sequences—such as analyzing customers’ clickstream data from websites—were quickly recognized (Mobasher, 2007;
Ulitzsch et al., 2022). In process mining, n-grams are used to represent adjoining actions, and each action is treated as a single feature
(in a “bag of actions”; Han et al., 2019, p.7). Similarly, in problem-solving analysis, holistic problem-solving sequences, such as
learning journeys, can be translated into subsequent individual activities (e.g. He & Von Davier, 2015). The occurrence and frequency
of multiple activities (bi- or trigrams) provide more informative sequence pattern mining (Gitinabard et al., 2019; Han et al., 2019; He
& Von Davier, 2015). Researchers can gain valuable insights by analyzing the transition from one activity to another, such as making
notes after reading a relevant document (see Table 1 for a fictive example of n-grams). Furthermore, the time-stamped nature of log-file
data conjoined with consideration of other educational data such as outcomes allows for predicting performance (Han et al., 2019; Liao
et al., 2019; Stadler et al., 2019). Several researchers have leveraged the exploratory n-gram approach to predict success based on
behavioral analysis. In the following section, we discuss recent studies that have utilized this approach to gain insights into learners’
behavior and performance.

problem-solving success. For instance, He and Von Davier (2015) conducted a study using process data generated in the Programme for
International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) 2012 b y two performance groups (correct and incorrect answers) across
three countries (the United States, the Netherlands, and Japan). They utilized n-grams and two feature selection methods (chi-square,
Oakes et al., 2001, and the weighted log likelihood ratio test; WLLR) to reveal significant differences between the behavior sequences
of the two groups, which remained consistent across countries. Feature selection focuses on identifying favored features occurring
more frequently in selected classes (Dong & Pei, 2007, secondary cited in He & Von Davier, 2015), which supports a more accurate
classification. For example, participants who correctly solved the well-defined problem “job search task” showed more frequent use of
actions such as sorting and searching, a better understanding of subgoals, and improved recovery from mistakes. In contrast, those who
answered incorrectly displayed a higher frequency of clicking the “Cancel” button, indicating hesitancy, lower task comprehension,
and greater reliance on help functions. Liao et al. (2019) conducted another study that used process data to predict problem-solving
success in computer-based environments, analyzing data from the United States sample of PIAAC 2012. Using n-grams and a chi-square
feature selection model, the authors extracted robust behavior sequences deployed by different subgroups of participants. They found
that income and employment type were robust factors for predicting correctness in the Meeting Room Assignment item. The authors
also noted that participants who filtered out irrelevant emails demonstrated an understanding of the goal of the task. Another study
conducted by Han et al. (2019) analyzed process data from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2012. Using an
n-gram approach to generate predictive features based on time-related log data and problem-solving behavior indicators, the authors
used the random forest machine-learning approach to predict problem-solving success on a Climate Control item. Participants needed
to complete the Climate Control problem-solving item by manipulating several variables, which included drawing a diagram (“D”),
applying the changes indicated by a slider (“A”), and resetting (“R”). The authors showed that drawing arrows “D" and applying the
reset function “R” indicated students’ decisiveness and that restrained use of the “D” and “R” sliders demonstrated solution confidence.
Stadler et al. (2019) also used PISA log data and an n-gram approach to investigate the problem-solving process of participants who
“applied the correct strategy to a problem but failed to solve it and those applying the strategy and solving the problem successfully” (p.
1). They found that the “vary one thing at a time” strategy to solve the task, following the MicroDYN approach, resulted in a higher
probability of successful problem-solving. Moreover, the chi-square approach to feature selection revealed the most discriminate
patterns of behavior: The authors observed that students who did not solve the task worked for an extended period on the scenario (S =
working on the scenario) (SS, SSS, and SSSS), while sequences indicating model (M = working on the model) changes (for example,
MM) did not have high chi-square scores, and this behavior pattern was consistently more prevalent among those who did not solve the
task. Brandl et al. (2021) used log data for medical students working on five complex cases and somewhat ill-defined problems on a
simulation-based learning platform and, to avoid overfitting of the proposed random forest model, exclusively used bigrams (two
subsequent collaborative diagnostic activities). With a score of 0.95, the model reached a very high level of accuracy in predicting
performance (diagnostic skills). Most recently, Ulitzsch et al. (2022) also used an ensemble machine-learning approach, the extreme
gradient boosting algorithm (XGBoost), to predict success or failure at an early processing phase based on log data generated by
students in two PIAAC 2012 Problem Solving in Technology-rich Environment tasks. The authors derived indicators such as the
frequencies of opening an email, writing an email, copying and pasting, and so forth, and they defined “earliness” based on the time
and the number of actions.
Despite the findings, further research is needed to examine the generalizability of these studies, as they consider different degrees of
task complexity and different knowledge domains, simulations, and samples. The scope of the actions and the requirements for selfregulation were rather restricted in the tasks of these studies. Since research on self-regulated problem-solving behavior in more
complex and open-ended environments is scarce, this study builds on the methods described above and applies them to log data gained
from an office simulation to predict success at an early stage of task processing.
3. Method
3.1. Sample
This empirical study used a sample of 234 VET students who worked on a problem-based scenario within the web-based office
simulation Luca. Dual training in VET programs is characterized by a combination of teaching theoretical knowledge at school and
providing practical experience in the workplace. The VET students in this study had an average age of 22.1 years, and roughly 68%
were female. Participation was voluntary, and all participants provided written informed consent.
3.2. Procedure
First, after a short introduction to the aims of the study, participants were familiarized with the fictive company and the functionalities of the office simulation during a 15-min onboarding scenario. Next, participants engaged in a complex work scenario that
requested a supplier selection (see Fig. 1a for a potential student procedure to solve the task and Fig. 1b for more details on the subtasks). The participants took on the role of an employee at a bicycle manufacturer and received an e-mail with the task assignment from

argument based on correct calculations in the spreadsheet. The spreadsheet included three criteria (price, quality, and delivery time)

with researchers and student assistants actively present. Additionally, participants had the flexibility to conclude the study before the
designated 55-min timeframe.
The scenario was implemented in Luca, a browser-based office simulation that is both a learning and an assessment environment.
The simulation provides typical office tools such as an email client, a file system, a PDF viewer, a spreadsheet program, a calculator, a
notepad, and a light enterprise resource program (see Fig. 2). It was designed to meet the requirements of instructional designers,
allowing them to develop and embed complex scenario-based tasks using a graphical user interface. The mirrored interfaces of office
tools within the simulation evoke real-life problem-solving behavior.
3.3. Measures
3.3.1. Problem-solving success (“product perspective”)
Problem-solving success was assessed based on fine-grained scoring rubrics that were developed according to a competence model
consisting of four logically sequenced dimensions of planning, applying, deciding, and communicating (Rausch et al., 2021). The
assessment procedure involved trained raters who assigned partial credit scores. The kappa value for inter-rater reliability was 0.87.
We calculated sum scores of the partial credit items for our analysis (M = 52.06; Md = 53; Min = 7; Max = 80). For the classification
task, the group was divided into two classes having the same sample size (class 1: more successful; class 0: less successful) since
classification algorithms work better with a balanced data set. To this end, the threshold for the cut was chosen to be 53 points, and
borderline cases (n = 10) were not considered (Rausch et al., 2021).
3.3.2. Behavioral process data (“process perspective”) – feature selection (n-grams) and analysis
The applied log data analysis can be divided into four phases (see Fig. 3). First, the raw data were exported in JSON format. A
custom-built R package lucar (Brandt, 2023) was then utilized to preprocess the data. During this process, participant details such as ID,
school, and behavioral data were structured within nested lists and then transformed into a more easily readable data frame (CSV
format) using the same software package. The data frame (see Fig. 4) includes columns for the timestamp (“time”), event code
(“code”), event duration (“duration”), activity type and document details (“label” and “data”), and the cumulative scenario time. In
addition, this phase involved removing the participants who worked outside the web-based simulation from the analysis and aggregating subsequent events of the same kind into one activity (e.g., aggregating the input of letters in the notepad). Furthermore, events
that do not provide additional information due to the design of the simulation were removed (e.g., double clicks or the closing of a tool
to open another tool). In Fig. 4, an exemplary aggregation of less informative information “downloading email attachment 1” shows
that unnecessary double clicks (such as T01DLA2039 – T01DLA 2039) were aggregated to only one log entry (T01DLA 2039) and
relabelled as “T01DLA” (downloading email attachment) (see Phase 1. Data Preprocessing Part I in Fig. 3).
Second, we used early clickstream data and keystrokes (the first 5, 10, and 20 min of the total duration) since we focus on early
prediction. Based on these clickstream data, course-specific behavioral indicators (e.g., reading relevant document 1, calculating in the
relevant spreadsheet, etc.) were derived (see Phase 2. Data Preprocessing II in Fig. 3).
Third, we followed the explorative n-gram approach (He & Von Davier, 2015; Li et al., 2017) to extract behavior sequences with the

machine-learning models, we did not exceed the length of bigrams. We calculated the frequencies of features to identify the most

using the Python package caret. Each of the three training sets used 70% of each data set, and the testing sets used the remaining 30%.
Splitting the data helps in evaluating the performance of the three models (M1, M2, and M3). We used the random forest classifier from
the scikit-learn Python library (Pedregosa et al., 2011) as the classification model, which belongs to the ensemble learning method. The
random forest method entails growing numerous decision trees, with each tree built on a distinct random subset of the training set. By
combining the predictions of multiple trees, the model typically produces better results than a model with a single tree (Breiman &
Cutler, 2004). Three key factors influenced our decision to use the random forest model: (1) Prior studies have successfully employed
ensemble learning techniques, including XGBoost and a random forest, to explore problem solving using n-grams (e.g., Brandl et al.,
2021; Ulitzsch et al., 2022). (2) The random forest method can quickly train individual decision trees in parallel, which is beneficial
when analyzing student log files to predict problem-solving success. The model can handle a large number of features, which are
present in the (comprehensive) student log files that contain a wealth of valuable information about the students’ behavior (such as the
number of attempts made, types of errors encountered, and time spent on different tasks). (3) Another benefit of the random forest
model is that it is robust and resistant to overfitting (Tomasevic et al., 2020), which can occur when a model is excessively complex and
closely fits the training data, resulting in poor generalization to new data.
Moreover, in machine-learning-based classification tasks, feature selection is a common step to examine the most robust and
important features with high prediction power (high variance) and discard redundant or irrelevant predictors to improve the accuracy
of the model (Prasetiyowati et al., 2022; Speiser et al., 2019). Feature importance is calculated as “the mean and standard deviation of
accumulation of the impurity decrease within each tree” (Pedregosa et al., 2011; scikit-learn, n.d., p. 1). We investigated the feature
importance with the Python library scikit-learn. The selection of important features (in this case bigrams) can be beneficial for the
random forest performance if the sample consists of many predictor variables (see Phase 4. Classification Modeling in Fig. 3).

3.3.3. Evaluation metrics
Several measures are computed to evaluate the performance of the applied random forest classification algorithm: the confusion
matrix, the accuracy, the F1 score, and the ROC AUC (Compute Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve; Powers,
2020). The computed confusion matrix shows the performance of the random forest classification algorithm that was used and the
types of errors it produced for each class (in this study, less successful = 0 and more successful = 1). The accuracy metric is defined as
the fraction (number) of correct predictions. The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity, with a value that ranges
between 0 (poor performance) and 1 (high performance). The ROC AUC and F1 scores are often used as evaluation metrics for
classification tasks. The ROC AUC curve shows the classifier’s trade-off between the number of correct positives on the y-axis (i.e.,
sensitivity; True Positive Rate) and incorrect positive predictions on the x-axis (1− specificity; False Positive Rate; Niyogisubizo et al.,
2022; Yang & Berdine. 2017). The ROC score ranges between 0.5 and 1.0 (ebd.): The higher the ROC score, the better the quality of the
classifier. Moreover, we decided on cross-validation for the AUC ROC score since the estimation of the model’s performance becomes
more robust. Cross-validation involves partitioning the available data into multiple subsets, or “folds.” Given that the model is
evaluated on multiple subsets of the data, the estimation of the underlying model’s performance is also more robust. The most common
form of cross-validation is the k-fold cross-validation. The data are divided into k equal-sized folds, and the evaluation results are then
averaged to obtain a single performance metric representing the model’s performance. We decided on k = 10 for the AUC ROC score in
this underlying contribution. Accuracy is a commonly used measure in the fields of sociology and psychology, while the F1 and ROC
AUC scores are evaluation metrics that are used in the discipline of machine learning (Ludwig et al., 2021).
4. Results
4.1. Problem-solving behavior (unigrams) across the more successful and less successful groups (descriptive statistics, RQ1)
This study analyzed problem-solving behavior (unigrams) in a problem-based scenario to predict more successful and less successful students. The descriptives show similarities but also differences between the groups of more and less successful participants.
The analysis revealed that participants with higher problem-solving success exhibited a greater overall frequency of activities
(unigrams) compared to the less successful participants during the first 5 min (Table 2), during the first 10 min (Table 3), and during
the first 20 min (Table 4). The first 5 min were characterized by actions that represent an exploration phase; most actions were instances of opening the relevant email with the task assignment and downloading its attachments (which can involve four offers, one relevant
spreadsheet, and one potentially relevant document; see Table 2). The groups showed only a few significant but not remarkable
differences during the first 5 min (see Table 2). However, the groups differed slightly in the frequency of downloading attachments,
opening relevant document 1, calculating in the relevant spreadsheet, and taking notes. During the first 10 min, the more successful performers began writing notes with a higher frequency (see Table 3). They wrote significantly more notes and opened more relevant
documents 2 than the less successful performers. The actions performed most by the less successful performers (along with the
downloads of attachments) were instead opening relevant document 1 and opening relevant document 2. During the first 20 min, the
actions performed most by the more successful group were writing notes and working in the relevant spreadsheet with a higher
frequency compared to the first 10 min and significantly more than the less successful group within the first 20 min. The actions most
performed by the less successful group were taking notes and still opening relevant document 2. It is notable that the more successful
students took notes more frequently than the less successful performers in all three time periods.

4.2. Robust features (bigrams) as powerful predictors for problem-solving success
Fig. 5 demonstrates how the importance of features (bigrams) changes across the three early time windows. For the sake of
conciseness, only exemplary robust features are shown. The most robust features of the first 5 min (showing the highest variance across
the groups) present the initial exploration phase. Downloading a document after another document (0.041), reading the relevant email
with the task assignment before receiving the irrelevant email (0.022), and writing notes and reading them are the most robust features
within the first 5 min (0.020). The most robust features of the first 10 min present the first information preprocessing sequences. The
student groups differ in opening the notepad before taking notes (0.020), opening the relevant document 2 and reopening it (0.018),
and taking notes before reading them (0.017). The most robust features of the first 20 min show that the groups differ in terms of
calculations on the relevant spreadsheet and opening the less relevant documents (irrelevant and potentially relevant document. The
most robust features are selecting a specific cell of a relevant spreadsheet and calculating on the relevant spreadsheet (0.017), opening
and reopening the irrelevant document 2 (0.015), and opening and reopening the potentially relevant document (0.013).

5. Discussion
The results show differences in the problem-solving behavior of the two groups of more and less successful problem solvers. The
evidence shows that there is a positive relationship between the frequency of participation in activities and the achievement of successful outcomes in problem-solving efforts. This positive relationship is consistent with the conclusions of several other scientific
studies (e.g. Rausch et al., 2017). In particular, taking notes as a ‘weak problem-solving method’ according to Van Merriënboer (2013)
proves to be an effective domain-general strategy as confirmed not only by the present study but also by a previous study conducted in
a similar context of office simulations (as indicated in the review presented in (Ludwig & Rausch, 2023). This could be due to the fact
that note-taking can help to reduce cognitive load as information is externalised from memory as shown by Moos (2009) in a learning
hypermedia. Moreover, the structured procedure of downloading the attachments consecutively (bi-gram ‘download attachment →
download attachment’), representing an example of a ‘heuristic approach’ (Winne & Nesbit, 2010), emphasizing a general strategy
without any domain-specificity. In addition, the careful selection of the cell and the calculation in the ‘spreadsheet’ (bi-gram ‘select cell
of spreadsheet → calculate in spreadsheet’) has been shown to be an effective problem-solving strategy, as demonstrated in a previous
study conducted with a different office simulation and a different sample (Ludwig & Rausch, 2023). Moreover, it is also obvious is that
the better performers demonstrated a tendency to access more documents and spreadsheets, even if they were only potentially relevant
or irrelevant This behavior could serve as an interesting indicator of conscientiousness in students. However, it is important to note
that exceeding a critical threshold of irrelevant documents could potentially act as distractors. Within the first 5, 10 and 20 min, a
pattern of engagement can be recognized in the weaker group. In particular, this group opened and read the first document, as shown
by the bigram sequence ‘Open rel. document 1’ ◊ ‘read rel. document 1’. However, they did not engage with other documents
compared to relevant document 1. This pattern suggests that participants did not go beyond the first document, probably due to the
perceived information overload caused by the large number of documents, which led them to focus mainly on document 1. Given these
empirical findings, it is feasible to consider learning strategies such as careful note taking (e.g., Trafton & Trickett, 2001; Trevors et al.,
2014) and more thorough exploration of the environment as potentially beneficial approaches. Adaptive learning environments offer
significant benefits in education by tailoring experiences to individual student behavior. The integration of carefully designed prompts
provides support for each student’s overall learning process. In particular, both cognitive and metacognitive supports can be activated
in response to specific situations. For example, if ‘at-risk’ students have not downloaded all the attachments (supplier’s offers) within
the first 5 min, a meta-cognitive prompt can be triggered within the first 10 min. This prompt would inform them that relevant

The classification performance demonstrates that predictions that are made too early (based on the first 5 min) do not contain
sufficient information for predicting success. However, with the expansion of the time window (to the first 10 and 20 min of a 55-min
period), the model’s performance increases. This is in line with Ulitzsch et al. (2022), who found that, when early-window datasets
were generated based on the count of executed actions, the classification performance showed enhancement across all evaluation
metrics as the early-window size increased. According to Yang and Berdine (2017, p. 35), AUC scores ranging between 0.6 and 0.7
ensure “acceptable discrimination”. Moreover, the final AUC score of 0.69 in the present study seems reasonable and solid when the
result is compared with several other studies in the field of education. For example, Salles et al. (2020) achieved an AUC ROC value of
0.78 with a random forest and theory-based and course-specific features. However, it may be difficult to reach such a good score with
only early behavior data (Ulitzsch et al., 2022). Moreover, He (2023) achieved an AUC score of 0.67 with adjoined activities (bi-grams)
and random forest using the unit “Traffic” in PISA 2012 Problem Solving Section. Regarding the evaluation metrics accuracy, Pathan
et al. (2022), for example, achieved a high accuracy of 0.84. However, they enriched their prediction with additional students’
think-aloud protocols. A comparison of their work with the present study also implies that traditional machine-learning models, such
as the random forest, can show a performance similar to that of neural networks. It is important to acknowledge that the majority of the
previous research used the entire problem-solving process and not simply early behavior to classify behavior, as we did, and that our
study has consequently (intentionally) omitted potentially important information that might increase the accuracy score.
Feature selection is also a common practice in machine-learning tasks and is crucial in reducing uninformative features and
enhancing models’ performance. This has been shown in several other studies, such as Li et al. (2018) and Qiu et al. (2018).
Furthermore, as discussed in our prior research (Ludwig et al., 2021) it is hard to discern where to draw the line between borderline
cases for the two classes of students. To ensure the robustness of our algorithms, we employed multiple evaluation methods to assess
performance on unseen data. Specifically, in addition to a standard train-test split, we implemented a 10-fold cross-validation. This
rigorous approach ensures that our model’s performance is not overly dependent on the initial data split and that it generalizes well
across various subsets of the training data (Kohavi, 1995). We reported key performance metrics, including accuracy, F1-score, and
AUC-ROC score, to provide a comprehensive understanding of the model’s effectiveness on previously unseen data. These metrics
highlight the model’s potential for generalizability. Furthermore, we compared our algorithm’s final performance with baseline
models (M1 and M1 robust) to contextualize our results. To fully validate our findings, it is essential to cross-verify them using other
problem-based tasks in similar domains, ensuring broader applicability and generalizability (see also future research and limitation).
We hope our approach can serve as a valuable guide for the community dealing with similar datasets or problems. For further
reference, we have made the Python code available on GitHub. Given our moderate-sized sample and in order to enable a balanced
data set, we set the threshold/cut based on the performance score. Additionally, human labeling is time consuming and can continue to
be error prone despite comprehensive rater training and elaborate scoring guidelines. To ensure reliable scores, it can be helpful to
have more than three raters in order to reduce misclassifications (Ludwig et al., 2021). In addition, ethical considerations should be
taken into account when handling data with machine-learning models. A question arises regarding whether educators might intervene
early in the process to “protect” students from making mistakes, which could prevent them from learning from their mistakes. Some
students benefit from exploring the material and making mistakes when learning a new subject (Ludwig et al., in preparation).
This study has limitations that should also be considered when interpreting the findings and formulating future research directions.

First, given the study’s exploratory nature, it is challenging to make theoretical inferences and prove theory-derived indicators (Han

domain. Third, while our study provides valuable insights, a limitation lies in the omission of individual characteristics data for

interesting for future research. Moreover, future research could triangulate the findings with cognitive theory or theory-driven process
indicators to provide a better interpretation. Future research should also cross-validate our findings using other tasks in similar domains to determine their generalizability (Liao et al., 2019). In the future, we see the potential in applying similar machine learning

the theoretical foundations of problem solving in educational contexts. Moreover, embedding machine-learning algorithms into the
software enables future problem-based learning with personalized support.
6. Conclusion
In conclusion, the random forest is a robust machine-learning algorithm that is well suited for analyzing student log files to predict
problem-solving success during an early stage of processing. The algorithm’s ability to handle large data sets, noisy data, and overfitting makes it a valuable tool for educational researchers and practitioners. Using a random forest to analyze student log files enables
valuable insights into the student behaviors that lead to more successful or to less successful problem solving. Given the insights drawn
from this study, adaptive tools and educators can provide adaptive and personalized scaffolding at the appropriate time. This can foster
successful students’ problem solving and also prevent weak students from poor performance or frustration, although the specific
reasons for less successful problem solving (e.g., lack of media competences or no interest in the topic) should be understood to develop
effective interventions (Ulitzsch et al., 2022). Because machine-learning models and early-window log data enable an early understanding of students’ problem-solving processes, they are helpful tools for improving problem-solving capabilities.
