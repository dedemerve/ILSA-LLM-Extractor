Irt‑latent regression with many predictors: 
limits and solutions
Paul A. Jewsbury1*   , J. R. Lockwood2 and Matthew S. Johnson1 
Abstract 
Many large-scale assessments model proficiency with a latent regression on contextual 
variables. Item-response data are used to estimate the parameters of the latent variable 
model and are used in conjunction with the contextual data to generate plausible val-
ues of individuals’ proficiency attributes. These models typically incorporate numerous 
contextual variables to facilitate a broad scope of inferences, but ambitious increases 
in this scope introduces methodological challenges. Using analytical and simulation 
results, we isolate a source of estimation instability that imposes limits on this method, 
and explore this instability with respect to assessment designs, predictor selection, 
and other types of regression models. Based on the results, we provide suggestions 
to improve estimation in large-scale assessments.
Introduction
Many large-scale assessments follow similar design principles and analysis methods 
(Jewsbury, 2023), including NAEP (von Davier et al., 2007), NAAL (Kutner et al., 2007), 
PISA (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2017a), PIAAC 
(Kirsch & Lennon, 2017), TIMSS (Martin et al., 2016), PIRLS (Martin et al., 2017), and 
NEPS (Pohl & Carstensen, 2012). While these analysis methods align well with the 
objectives of large-scale assessments, their limits have been challenged by ambitious 
expansions in assessment scope. In this paper, we explore the behavior of the methods in 
ambitious applications, such as to support a very large number of inferences and when 
latent variables are measured with few items.
Distinguishing large-scale assessments from other testing paradigms, the focus shifts 
from individual proficiency scores to conditional distributions of proficiency vari-
ables for policy-relevant populations. This focus enables short testing administrations, 
achieved by assessing each individual on only a subset of the item pool (Johnson, 1992). 
The modeling of proficiencies as latent variables using item response theory (IRT) allows 
individuals to receive item subsets that differ in psychometric properties and difficulty.
Using IRT, maximum-likelihood estimates (MLEs) of proficiency for each test taker 
may be calculated. In the context of large-scale assessment, proficiencies of individu-
als are nuisance or incidental parameters. The conditional distributions of the test-taker 
MLEs could be used to infer the conditional proficiency distributions of interest using

the invariance property of maximum-likelihood estimates (Casella & Berger, 2002). 
However, estimators of this nature may not be consistent due to the incidental param-
eter problem (Neyman & Scott, 1948), where the number of incidental parameters grows 
with the sample size. Mislevy et  al. (1992a) provide an example of unacceptable bias 
when the distribution of test-taker MLEs is used to infer the distribution of a latent vari-
able under IRT.
To address biases and overcome the incidental parameter problem in estimating distri-
butions of IRT-latent variables, marginal-maximum likelihood IRT-latent regression was 
developed (Mislevy, 1984, 1985). Marginal-maximum likelihood avoids the incidental 
parameter problem by marginalizing out the latent proficiency variables.
The difficulty for secondary users to replicate analysis with marginal-maximum likeli-
hood IRT-latent regression prompted the development of plausible values (PVs; Mislevy, 
1991; Mislevy et al., 1992a), offering imputed values for latent variables using method-
ology adapted from multiple imputation theory (Rubin, 1987). PVs are convenient as 
secondary users can conduct simple analyses using complete-data estimators in com-
mon statistical models (e.g., t-tests) that can produce approximately equivalent results 
to inferences based on IRT-latent regression models (Mislevy et al., 1992a; von Davier 
et al., 2009).
Plausible values in large-scale assessments are imputed values drawn from distribu-
tions estimated with an IRT-latent regression. Accordingly, the IRT-latent regression 
model is the imputation model. The congeniality of analyses with the imputation model 
is crucial to avoid a source of bias (Meng, 1994; Mislevy, 1991). In the context of large-
scale assessments, biases on means may occur if indicator variables for the focal groups 
are unaccounted for by the predictors in the IRT-latent regression. The direction of the 
bias is opposite to the direction of the correlation between the indicator variable and the 
latent proficiency variable, conditional on the predictors used in the IRT-latent regres-
sion to impute the plausible values. The magnitude of the bias is dependent on the mag-
nitude of this conditional relationship as well as the individual-level reliability of the 
assessment (Marsman et al., 2016; Mislevy, 1991). In practice, this usually suggests that 
the estimated group mean will be shrunken towards the overall population mean, with 
greater shrinkage for latent proficiencies measured with fewer items per test taker and 
for groups less well explained by the predictors in the IRT-latent regression.
The inclusive strategy, popular in the multiple imputation literature, aims to include as 
many predictors in the imputation model as possible (Collins et al., 2001; Little & Rubin, 
2019; Murray, 2018; Rubin, 1996; Schafer, 2003). Because of the risk of non-congenality, 
the inclusive strategy has also been applied to large-scale assessments as well (Schofield 
et al., 2015; von Davier, 2013; von Davier et al., 2019). However, supporting studies for 
the inclusive strategy were generally conducted in the context of imputation of values 
for observed variables with a linear model (e.g., Collins et al., 2001), not imputation of 
values of latent variables non-linearly related to item responses, which as we will show 
behaves differently.
While our focus is on large-scale assessments, the principles discussed extend to 
imputation of latent variables in diverse fields (e.g., Boeschoten et al., 2018; Carlin, 1992; 
Dicke et al., 2015; Gorter et al., 2015; Joinson et al., 2012; Rhee et al., 2013). In these 
contexts, applying the inclusive strategy with latent variable imputation has also been

Present study
The scope and complexity of inferences drawn from large-scale assessments have 
expanded significantly since the introduction of the large-scale methodology in the 1980s 
when ETS was first awarded the NAEP contract (Beaton et al., 2011; Thomas, 2000). 
Compared to early large-scale assessments, modern large-scale assessments report 
on more latent proficiencies (e.g., subscales or domains) within subject areas, involve 
populations more diverse in proficiency (e.g., internationally), report on a wider array of 
statistics (e.g., achievement levels, percentiles), involve sample sizes that may differ sub-
stantially across years, and collect far greater quantities of contextual information.
This increase in the scope of inferences has tested the limits of the large-scale assess-
ment methodology. Estimation instability has been observed under conditions such as a 
high number of predictors relative to the sample size (e.g., Oranje & Ye, 2014; von Davier 
et al., 2019), a low number of items measuring a given latent proficiency for each test 
taker (e.g., Xi et al., 2017) and a significant mismatch between assessment difficulty and 
population proficiency (e.g., Dion et al., 2008). In this paper, we show that these estima-
tion instabilities share common underlying statistical mechanisms and propose meth-
odological enhancements to address these challenges.
To examine the issue of estimation biases in large-scale assessments, we explored the 
impact of increasing the number of predictors in different assessment design conditions 
(e.g., different number of item responses observed per test taker) in a simulation study. 
The simulation was designed to controlled for non-focal sources of estimation bias and 
variance in IRT-latent regression models. Specifically, a) every IRT-latent regression 
model fit was consistent with the distributional assumptions of the generating model 
for latent proficiency, to avoid confounds associated with distributional assumptions 
when comparing IRT-latent regression models with differing numbers of predictors (see 
Mazzeo et al., 2006 and Moran et al., 2007). b) Every analysis was congenial with every 
IRT-latent regression model fit, to avoid confounds associated with violations of conge-
niality (Meng, 1994). c) The homogeneity of residual variance assumption was satisfied 
in every IRT-latent regression model, to avoid confounds with model misfit. d) The item 
parameters were fixed to the generating parameters, consistent with operational prac-
tice where the item parameters are estimated in a previous step and with a much larger 
dataset than used for the latent regression (Jewsbury, 2023; National Center for Edu-
cation Statistics, 2023a), to avoid confounding associated with item parameter estima-
tion. Finally, e) the results were evaluated with both PV-based and plug-in estimators, to 
evaluate whether the estimation instability is associated with the use of PVs or could be 
attributed to the instability of the regression parameter estimation.

will show, for latent regression models in which observed indicator are not linearly 
related to the latent variable of interest, this type of invariance does not hold.

Method
Data generation
General. Contextual information xi , latent proficiency θi , and item responses yi were 
generated for N = 1000 test takers for each of 1000 simulated assessments within each 
simulation condition. Item responses were generated with the simdata function in the 
mirt 1.30 R package (Chalmers, 2012), while other data were generated with base and 
stats functions in R 3.5.3 (sample, runif, rnorm, rbeta; R Core Team, 2018).
Contextual information. Each of the 1000 simulees in each simulated assessment 
were assigned to one of five focal groups with probabilities 10%, 15%, 50%, 15%, and 10%. 
The generating θ focal group means were −1.2, −.3, 0, .3, 1.2 , selected to examine the 
impact of increasing the number of predictors on group means across the proficiency 
range. The choice of −.3 , 0, and .3 represents the range of means for large, heterogene-
ous groups such as U.S. states. The choice of −1.2 and 1.2 represents the range of groups 
with major policy importance in U.S. samples.1 Five dummy variables were created for 
use in the IRT-latent regression.
As contextual information, 300 binary variables were independently sampled from 
Bernoulli distributions. Within each replication, the generating probabilities for the 
binary variables were independently sampled from a uniform distribution from 1% to 
99%. To avoid redundancy, the binary variables were regressed on the five-category 
focal-group variable.2 A principal component analysis was fit to the correlation matrix 
of the residuals from the regression. Finally, the principal component scores were stand-
ardized to have variances of one. These principal component scores were used in both 
the data generation and model estimation. Except for standardizing the principal com-
ponent scores, this follows standard procedures in large-scale group-score assessments 
such as NAEP and PISA (Oranje & Ye, 2014).

Item parameters. Responses for a pool of 40 dichotomous items were generated with 
the 3PL model. Slope-intercept parameterization without the scaling parameter was 
used (as in Eq. 1). For each simulated assessment, the generating item parameters were 
sampled from distributions that depended on the item type condition. Specifically,
•	 For the difficult 3PL condition: a ∼U(1, 2.7) , t ∼N(−.3, .7) , c ∼Beta(5.5, 24).
•	 For the moderate 3PL condition: a ∼U(1, 2.7) , t ∼N(0, .7) , c ∼Beta(5.5, 24).
•	 For the moderate 2PL condition: a ∼U(1, 2.7) , t ∼N(0, .7) , c = 0 .

correspond to real mathematics and reading assessments (see National Center for Edu-

as conditions with more symmetrical test information function, to contrast to the dif-
ficult 3PL condition which may be more realistic. Note b = −a

and no scaling parameter was used.
Item responses. The number of observed item responses for each simulee from the 
pool of 40 items again depended on the simulation condition. Specifically,
•	 For the No missing condition: each simulee had observed responses to a random 
selection of J items and missing responses to the other items, where J depended on 
the simulation condition.
•	 For the Missing condition: each simulee had a 10% chance to have only one observed 
item response, 10% chance to have two observed item responses, 10% chance to have 
three observed item responses, and 70% chance to have J observed item responses. 
These missing data were introduced to mimic what may be observed in real data, 
where individual test takers may be presented with only a few items linked to a par-
ticular latent variable dimension, or may provide scorable responses to only a few 
items. The missing data was generated under the assumption of Missing Completely 
at Random (MCAR; Jewsbury et al., 2024b), meaning that the probability of an item 
response being missing was independent of all other variables.
The item parameter condition, the number of item responses, and the Missing vs 
No missing condition are referred to collectively as the assessment design conditions.

Model and plausible value estimation
IRT-latent regression. For every simulated assessment, seven IRT-latent regression 
models were estimated with increasingly more predictors. In every latent regression 
model, the five focal-group dummy variables were used as predictors, along with PC 
principal components, where PC ∈{0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300} . The predictors were 
ordered based on the principal component eigenvalues. For example, PC = 50 indicates 
that the largest 50 principal components were used as predictors, in addition to the five 
focal-group dummy variables, with a total number of predictors P = PG + PC = 55 , and 
no intercept parameter.
For each IRT-latent regression model, the item parameters were fixed to the generating 
values rather than estimated. This was done for two reasons: (1) to reduce confounds due 
to poorly estimated item parameters; and (2) to roughly correspond to IRT-latent regres-
sion analysis of state and country samples in national and international assessments 
respectively, where item parameters are first estimated in a large national or interna-
tional sample or even multiple such samples collected at different times, then IRT-latent 
regression models are fit to much smaller subsets of that sample (i.e., state and country 
samples), with item parameters fixed to the estimates of the first stage (National Center 
for Education Statistics, 2023a; Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Develop-
ment, 2017a).
The IRT-latent regression models were estimated with the mirt function in the mirt 
R package (Chalmers, 2012). The estimation was marginal-maximum likelihood via an 
EM algorithm (Bock & Aitkin, 1981; Mislevy, 1984, 1985), using 51 equally spaced quad-
rature points between -5 and 5. The maximum number of EM cycles was increased to 
5000.
Plausible values. PVs were drawn from approximated posterior distributions of each 
test taker in one of two conditions: conditional on the fixed maximum-likelihood esti-
mates of the regression parameters, and conditional on values of the regression coef-
ficient matrix sampled from an approximated posterior distribution. In both conditions, 
PVs were generated with the fscores function in the mirt R package assuming the 
test taker conditional posterior distributions are normal (as typically assumed; see 
Thomas & Gan, 1997 and Mazzeo et al., 2006 for discussions of this approximation). 
The quadrature settings from the IRT-latent regression model estimation were used, and 
20 PVs were generated for each simulee. The results in the main text use the PVs drawn 
conditionally on the fixed maximum-likelihood estimates. The PVs drawn conditionally 
on sampled regression coefficients are considered in Appendix D.
When generating PVs conditional on the fixed maximum-likelihood estimates of the 
regression parameters, fscores was run with the intended functionality of generating 
PVs conditional on the fixed maximum-likelihood estimates ˆŴ and ˆσ 2 . When generat-
ing PVs conditional on values of the regression coefficient matrix sampled from an esti-
mated posterior distribution, the variance-covariance matrix of the Ŵ estimator was first 
approximated based on the Hessian matrix used in the EM algorithm. Estimates for the 
regression coefficients (i.e., ˆŴ (m) ) were sampled from a multivariate normal distribution 
with mean ˆŴ , and the stated variance-covariance matrix, using the rmvnorm function in 
the mvtnorm R package with eigenvalue decomposition (Genz & Bretz, 2009). M = 20

matrix values were sampled, and fscores was run once for each of the 20 values, out-
putting one PV for each simulee each time, resulting in M = 20 PVs for each simulee.
Results and discussion
Table  1 shows the effects of increasing the number of predictors P on estimators of 
population parameters in the difficult 3PL condition. In the difficult 3PL condition, as 
P increases, a) the estimator bias for the population mean and skewness of θ becomes 
increasingly negative, b) the estimator bias for the population variance and kurtosis of 
θ increases, and c) the estimated percentiles become more outwardly biased (estimates 
for lower percentiles decrease, estimates for higher percentiles increase). The rates of 
change is greater for fewer items per test taker J, and greater when 30% of test takers 
only have 1–3 item responses (Missing condition).
Table 2 shows the effects of increasing the number of predictors P on estimators of 
group parameters in the difficult 3PL condition. Across all conditions, as P increases, 
a) group mean estimates become more outwardly biased (e.g., the bias for the lowest 
ability group G1 becomes more negative while the bias for the highest ability group G5 
becomes more positive), b) the biases on the group variances increases, especially for the 
lowest and highest ability groups G1 and G5, and c) the changes are asymmetrical (e.g., 
estimates for G1 are biased downwards more than estimates for G5 are biased upwards). 
Again, the rate of changes is greater for fewer items per test taker J, and greater when 
30% of test takers only have 1–3 item responses (Missing condition).
The results in Tables 3, 4, 5, 6 for the moderate 3PL and moderate 2PL conditions 
show a reduced rate of increase in estimator bias with increasing P, especially for the 
moderate 2PL conditions. The rate of change in the mean estimates for the population 
mean and skewness of θ is slower for the moderate 3PL versus the difficult 3PL condi-
tion, and there is no change for the moderate 2PL condition which has a symmetrical 
test information function.
Comparing different types of statistics, Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 indicate that estimators of 
variances and extreme percentiles (10th and 90th) are much more sensitive to the num-
ber of predictors than estimators of means and moderate percentiles (e.g., 50th). How-
ever, the mean estimator for the low-ability group G1 also showed substantial estimation 
biases in the 3PL conditions for low J and/or in the Missing condition, reflecting lower 
test information for low ability in these assessment design conditions.
Notably, a clear pattern emerges that some assessment design conditions are much 
more robust to the number of predictors P than others. In the moderate 2PL condition 
with No missing, where few simulees will have very poor measurement precision, the

Discussion
These results have significant implications for research in large-scale assessment. Sys-
tematic and random errors in the regression parameter estimators introduces corre-
sponding biases and variances into the PV-based estimators of parameters of interest. 
The bias results from an interaction of the sample size, the predictors, and the assess-
ment design. The bias is greatest for regression parameters estimated from few item 
responses and the bias occurs due to the non-linearity of the IRT model.
The results suggest that an ideal large-scale assessment design necessitates at least 
moderate measurement precision for all test takers. This level of precision may be 
achieved with as few as 4 to 6 two-parameter logistic (2PL) items. With difficult or 
three-parameter logistic (3PL model) items, more items are required because of reduced 
measurement precision for low proficiency test takers (see also Appendix B). Obtaining 
moderate measurement precision for all test takers is more important than enhancing 
precision beyond moderate measurement precision for some test takers. Under condi-
tions of moderate measurement precision for all test takers, estimators exhibit substan-
tial robustness to the number of predictors in the IRT-latent regression.
Our findings reveal a shared underlying cause for ostensibly unrelated estimation 
instabilities observed in previous studies, offering insights into mitigating these instabili-
ties. For instance, while our findings align with previous research observing biases when 
the number of predictors is high relative to the sample size (e.g., Oranje & Ye, 2014), 
we demonstrate how careful assessment design can ensure robustness to the number 
of predictors. Furthermore, we show that judicious selection of predictors can mitigate 
estimation biases. Similarly, our results align with previous studies observing biases 
when latent proficiencies are measured using few items (e.g., Xi et al., 2017) or when 
assessment difficulty is not well matched to sample proficiency (e.g., Dion et al., 2008). 
However, we show that even under these challenging conditions, meaningful inferences 
remain feasible through careful predictor selection.
The results and discussions in this paper are intended to elucidate the limits of the 
IRT-latent regression methodology generally used in large-scale assessments. As such, 
we focus the discussion on where the number of items and the ratio of the sample size 
over the number of predictors is relatively low, as these conditions define the limits of the

Appendices
To improve readability of this paper, many important observations were relegated to the 
Appendices or excluded altogether. This section will summarize the primary conclusions 
for the studies in the Appendices.
Appendix A describes the case when the number of predictors in the IRT-latent 
regression approaches the sample size, showing that the distribution of the PVs becomes 
equal to the distribution of the MLEs. For typical large-scale assessment designs, this 
means that the PVs will typically become more dispersed and more negatively skewed as 
the number of predictors increase.
Appendix B describes theoretical results for a simple IRT-latent regression model and 
relates these results to logistic regression. This shows that the bias on the regression 
coefficients can be understood in reference to the non-linearity of the IRT-latent regres-
sion model and, in an extreme case, related to complete separation in logistic regression. 
Note that as the number of items per test taker increases, IRT-latent regression approxi-
mates an observed and linear regression. This helps explain why estimation biases are 
smaller in assessment designs with more items per test taker, even with a large number 
of predictors, despite that regression parameters would still be noisy.
Appendix C compares PV-based and plug-in estimators. We confirm that plug-in 
estimators produce approximately equivalent results to PV-based estimators, when the 
PVs are generated conditional on fixed maximum-likelihood estimates of the regres-
sion parameters. This result shows that the estimation biases observed are not directed 
associated with the use of PVs, but rather the regression parameter estimation, which is 
reflected in both PV-based and plug-in estimators.

latent regression approximates a linear model under these conditions.
Potential or alternative improvements
As noted previously, the estimation biases were a function of the sample size, the predic-
tors, and the assessment design. We explored the estimation biases in the context of a 
single IRT-latent regression intended to accommodate a large array of potential analyses 
and with maximum-likelihood estimation of the regression coefficients, following stand-
ard practice in large-scale assessments (Jewsbury, 2023., Jewsbury et al., 2024a).
As the estimation instability results from an interplay of multiple factors, estimation 
robustness may be improved by making changes in a number of different areas. As the 
factors interact, improvements in one area (e.g., model estimation) may allow for greater 
flexibility in another area (e.g., predictor selection).
Assessment design. A repeated observation in this paper was the importance of moder-
ate measurement precision for all test takers to ensure estimators of parameters of inter-
est are robust to the number of predictors. More information for some test takers can 
not fully compensate for very little information for other test takers, at least when the 
predictors are selected incautiously.

PCA-covariance is highly suited to the challenge of large-scale assessments such as 
NAEP. However, other methods to select the predictors for the latent regression may 
also show benefits and costs. For example, exclusion of a contextual variable from the 
predictors only introduces a source of bias if the contextual variable contributes to the 
explanation of the latent proficiencies over what has already been included as predictors 
(Mislevy, 1991). Consequently, methods that select predictors with respect to explain-
ing the latent proficiencies such as the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator 
(LASSO; Tibshirani, 1996) may have advantages (Johnson, 2011), although there is also 
the risk that these methods may increase overfitting.
Model estimation. This paper, including the simulation designs and theoretical results, 
are premised on the use of maximum-likelihood estimation of the regression param-
eters. The estimation biases on means reflect that maximum-likelihood estimates of 
IRT-latent regression parameters are biased when estimated on the basis of few item 
responses. Future research could consider whether estimators are more robust with 
alternates to maximum-likelihood regression parameter estimates, such as the use 
of a prior on the regression parameters (Culpepper & Park, 2017), or penalization of 
the regression parameters with ridge regression (Hoerl & Kennar, 1970) or elastic net 
regression (Zou & Hastie, 2005).

of a single latent regression intended to accommodate a very large number of potential

Summary
In this paper, we identified a source of estimation instability that imposes limits on 
the conventional large-scale assessment methodology, explaining and unifying previ-
ous instances of estimation instability in specific scenarios (Dion et al., 2008; Moran & 
Dresher, 2007; Oranje & Ye, 2014; von Davier et al., 2019). In contrast to the behavior of 
linear regression on an observed outcome variable, we showed that inclusion of a predic-
tor in an IRT-latent regression does not guarantee that mean proficiency estimation for 
that predictor is unbiased nor invariant to what other predictors are included. Instead, 
we showed that bias may be substantial, especially when the predictors are selected 
indiscriminately and when some test takers have few observed item responses.
We show that the estimation challenges result from an interaction of the assessment 
design, predictor selection, and the sample size. Improvements in one area may allow 
for greater flexibility in the other two. By showing that previously observed estimation 
instability (e.g., Dion et al., 2008; Oranje & Ye, 2014; von Davier et al., 2019; Xi et al., 
2017) reflect common statistical causes and by thoroughly exploring these statistical 
causes, this paper provides a framework to improve and mitigate estimation instability 
in large-scale assessments through better assessment and analysis designs.
Appendix A: saturated latent regression
When the number of predictors in the latent regression is equal to the sample size, the 
latent regression is saturated. As shown below theoretically and with a simulation, when 
the latent regression is saturated the PVs for each test taker equal the maximum likeli-
hood estimate (MLE) of the test taker’s latent ability
Consideration of this special case is helpful for several reasons. First, the distribu-
tion of the MLEs for a given assessment design shows how the distribution of the PVs 
may change as the number of predictors becomes large with respect to the sample size. 
MLEs are more dispersed for abilities where test information is low and are biased out-
ward from the middle of the proficiency distribution for typical assessment designs 
(Lord, 1983; Warm, 1989). Furthermore, when the assessment comprises 3PL items or 
relatively difficult items, the bias for low values of ability is greater than the bias for high 
values, resulting in the distribution of PVs becoming more skewed as the latent regres-
sion approaches saturation. As the distribution of the PVs is used as an estimate for the 
distribution of ability, the biased distribution of PVs may lead to invalid inferences about 
ability.
Second, the theoretical results for the saturated latent regression show that ensuring the 
predictors in the latent regression explain a grouping variable does not guarantee unbi-
ased estimation of mean ability conditional on levels of the grouping variable. In the satu-
rated model, all grouping variables are explained by the predictors. However, mean ability

estimation with the PVs from a saturated latent regression with be biased because MLEs are 
biased, and are non-negligibly biased for the short test lengths in large-scale assessments 
(Lord, 1983). This contradicts the somewhat ubiquitous claim in large-scale assessment 
research that ensuring a grouping variable is explained by the predictors guarantees unbi-
ased mean estimation (e.g., Braun & von Davier, 2017).
Third, the below results also suggests that the mean of the PVs for a test taker will equal 
the MLE for that test taker, conditional on their responses, if an indicator variable for that 
test taker is explained by the predictors in the latent regression. In other words, a test taker 
may be uniquely saturated in a latent regression even if P < N . In the IRT context, this is 
particular relevant as the error of the MLE depends on ability, and may be very large for low 
and high ability test takers (Lord, 1983). For test takers with all incorrect, all correct, or all 
incorrect on non-3PL items and guessing level performance on 3PL items, the MLE for that 
test taker converges to infinity. Saturating a test taker or group of test takers with such a 
response pattern may introduce large errors into the PVs. As described in Appendix B, this 
is related to separation in logistic regression. This also helps provide context for the simula-
tion study, where estimators only showed large biases for large P in the assessment designs 
that simulated test takers with large errors on their MLEs, such as the difficult 3PL and 
Missing conditions. In the moderate 2PL and No missing condition with adequate J, large 
errors on test taker MLEs were rare, and the estimators were robust to P.

Simulation
To illustrate the poor quality of the estimators when the number of predictors 
(almost) equals the number of test takers, unidimensional IRT-latent regression mod-
els with up to 99 predictors was fit to a simulated dataset ( N = 100 ). Every simu-
lee had 40 2PL item responses. The simulation was conducted following the same 
procedures described in detail in the main text, except that only one replication 
was conducted, the focal group predictors were excluded and an intercept term was 
included, the quadrature points were increased to 100,000, the convergence criteria 
was decreased to a more stringent value, and the data was re-generated until data was 
obtained with no all correct or all incorrect responses for a given simulee to ensure 
no MLEs were undefined.

variance of the test-taker MLEs.

Separation
Separation is defined as when the regression coefficient estimate is undefined, and is 
related to the issue of complete separation in logistic regression. Although the estimate 
is technically undefined, the likelihood increases as the estimate goes to ±∞ . For the 
2PL and GPCM, we may define positive complete separation as yij = 1 for all i ∈Sp and 
negative complete separation as yij = 0 for all i ∈Sp . Based on observation with the EM 
algorithm, the estimate will tend to increase in magnitude over the iterations until the 
convergence criteria is met. Therefore, the regression coefficient and any plug-in estima-
tor of a mean that depends on the regression coefficient will approach ∞ as the conver-
gence criteria becomes more tight.
In the simple IRT-latent regression, the probability of complete separation for group p

responses across j.) The probability of separation is very small except in extreme situ-
ations (small Jnp and large |a(θ −b)| ), although the bias introduced from separation 
occurring may be large.

where Sxg is the (P × P) variance-covariance matrix of the contextual variables for group g.
Analysis involving regressing PVs on independent variables is often described as 
exactly replicating analysis based on IRT-latent regression model parameters when the 
independent variables are explained by the predictors used in the latent regression, 
except for some Monte Carlo error associated with the use of a finite number of PVs 
(Mazzeo et al., 2006; Mislevy et al., 1992a). For analyses where the independent variables 
are not fully explained by the predictors, some degree of shrinkage on mean estimates 
is expected due to lack of congeniality between the analysis and the latent regression. 
However, there is expected to be less shrinkage on PV-based estimators than the plug-in 
estimators, depending on the reliability of the test (Marsman et al., 2016; Mislevy, 1991).
However, we found that PV-based estimators only approximate the plug-in estimators 
when the PVs are generated conditional on the fixed maximum-likelihood estimates of 
the regression parameters (i.e., PVs are drawn from the test-taker posterior distribution 
conditional on ˆŴ and ˆ ). In many large-scale assessments including NAEP, the PVs are 
generated conditional on sampled regression coefficients (von Davier et al., 2007), using 
a procedure detailed in Appendix D.
We compared plug-in estimators to PV-based estimators in the simulation, using PVs 
generated conditional on the fixed maximum-likelihood estimates of the regression 
parameters. As Table A1 shows, the PV-based estimators were approximately equivalent

Below, we show that these PV-based estimators that are used in practice by many 
large-scale assessments are not consistent with plug-in estimators for variances (con-
tradicting Mazzeo et al., 2006; Mislevy et al., 1992a). Instead, the PV-based estima-
tors produce more positive estimates of variances than the plug-in estimators, and the 
magnitude of the difference is positively related to the approximated posterior vari-
ances of the regression parameters used in the PV-generation. If the posterior vari-
ances are calculated to be larger, the PV-based estimators of variances also becomes 
larger.

To isolate sampling and measurement sources of variance, we define an infeasible 
plausible value-based estimator as an estimator based on PVs generated conditional on 
regression parameters estimated with only sampling error. Specifically, an infeasible PV-
based estimator is defined as an estimator based on PVs generated from an IRT-latent 
regression model, but with regression parameters fixed to observed regression estimates 
(had θ been observed directly). First, an observed regression model is fit using x as pre-
dictors, but with θ as the observed outcome variable. This is possible in a simulation, but 
infeasible with real data where θ is not observed. Second, PVs are generated but instead 
of using latent regression model parameter estimates, the PVs are drawn from the pos-
terior distribution of each test taker conditional on regression parameter estimates from 
the observed regression. Third, the usual PV-based estimators (e.g., Eq. 6) are applied to 
the resulting PVs.
In the below two tables, we show the results for the simulation study but using the 
infeasible PV-based estimators calculated from PVs generated conditional on observed 
regression parameters. Notably, no bias on the estimators is observed as P increases. As 
the regression parameters have been estimated from an observed regression, the PV-
based estimators share the ideal properties of invariance to what other variables have 
been included in the model (as described in Appendix C).
Statistically, the estimation biases found in this paper are best understood as related 
to non-linearity in the IRT model (see Appendix B), not due to measurement variance 
or the latency of ability. For example, a latent regression where ability is linear with the 
item responses will also show the ideal properties of variance on means (Appendix B). 
However, the behavior of an IRT-latent regression model will approximate an observed, 
linear regression model as measurement variance decreases (e.g., as J →∞ ), therefore
