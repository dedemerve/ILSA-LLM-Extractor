We examine how teacher motivation types are associated with job satisfaction directly and indirectly through
stress-related factors. Using cross-sectional data from the 2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey in
Taiwan and drawing on self-determination theory, we differentiate between personal utility, social utility, and
value-based intrinsic motivation. Structural equation modeling of a sample of 3598 junior high school teachers
reveals that job satisfaction is positively associated with all three motivation types and negatively associated with
high perceived workload and frequent student behavior stress. Additionally, teacher motivations are indirectly
associated with job satisfaction: personal utility motivation is linked to lower satisfaction through heightened
student behavior stress, whereas social utility and value-based intrinsic motivations are associated with higher
satisfaction through lower stressors. Overall, job stress partially mediates between motivation and satisfaction.
Intrinsic and altruistic motives may relate to lower stress levels and higher job satisfaction, providing insights for
supporting teacher well-being and retention.

Lower secondary school teachers worldwide are increasingly
affected by work-related stress, mainly associated with excessive
workloads and increasing student misbehavior (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2023). Teachers are
responsible for lesson planning, instruction, grading, parent communication, and administrative duties. When these responsibilities pile up or
time is limited, stress levels rise (Kyriacou, 2001). Additionally, managing student behavior is a significant stressor. It encompasses challenges related to maintaining discipline, handling confrontations, and
managing classroom atmosphere in the face of disruptions (e.g., talking
out of turn, disobeying rules, showing disrespect) that interrupt instruction and raise stress levels when not supported by effective management strategies or school-level assistance (Hastings & Bham, 2003).
For instance, 58% of teachers identify student discipline issues as major
stressors, and over 40% of US K–12 educators report feeling frequently
overwhelmed, highlighting teaching as a high-risk profession for stress
and burnout (Riiska, 2025). Such stress is known to erode teachers’
well-being and job satisfaction, often leading to burnout or intentions to
leave the profession (Liu & Onwuegbuzie, 2012; Skaalvik & Skaalvik,
2016).

Taiwan's educational landscape has undergone substantial change in
recent years, and the occupational pressure on teachers increasingly
extends beyond their instructional responsibilities. In particular,
following relevant legal revisions, the Campus Incident Resolution
Council (CIRC) has become the core mechanism for addressing disputes
involving teachers in schools. Research indicates that controversies
arising during CIRC proceedings not only disrupt principals' leadership
arrangements and intensify the workload and stress of administrative
staff but may also leave participating teachers feeling aggrieved and
unfairly treated (Chen & Lin, 2023). Furthermore, shaped by public
opinion and media effects, the CIRC mechanism means that virtually any
complaint against a teacher (e.g., alleged mishandling of student issues)
can rapidly escalate into a formal investigation, placing teachers under a
constant shadow of suspicion and sharply increasing psychological
pressure (Chen & Ho, 2025). Chen (2023) further notes that in some
schools, the CIRC has been treated as a tool to warn teachers and deter
them from exercising professional discretion when handling student
incidents (e.g., discipline or conflict mediation), as complaints may
quickly escalate into formal investigations and foster a climate of fear on
campus. These dual pressures may undermine teachers' work motivation

Beyond pressures, teacher motivation significantly impacts educational outcomes, as highly motivated teachers tend to demonstrate
better instructional quality, stronger professional commitment, and
greater job satisfaction (Deci & Ryan, 1985). A multilevel analysis of the
2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) across 48
countries found that self-efficacy and personal and social utility motivations significantly predict instructional practices (Bas, 2025). These
results highlight the importance of motivation, indicating that motivational profiles shape how teachers approach their instructional work,
perceive job demands, and experience satisfaction.
Teacher job satisfaction reflects teachers' overall assessment of their
work setting, as well as the emotional reactions that come from that
assessment (Locke, 1976). For instance, enabling meaningful professional development and maintaining a positive work environment can
enhance teachers’ satisfaction; teachers who feel a strong sense of
belonging and experience lower emotional exhaustion likewise report
higher satisfaction levels (Day et al., 2007; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2011).
International evidence confirms that teachers who benefit from effective
school leadership, positive student behavior climates, and manageable
workloads report significantly higher job satisfaction (Eryilmaz et al.,
2025; Zakariya et al., 2020).
According to self-determination theory (SDT; Deci & Ryan, 1985),
the quality of motivation, whether intrinsic or extrinsic, significantly
influences engagement and well-being. Intrinsic motivation (e.g., a
genuine passion for teaching) tends to enhance engagement and resilience, whereas more extrinsic motives can leave teachers vulnerable to
stress and burnout (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000). In parallel,
Watt and Richardson's (2007) Factors Influencing Teaching (FIT)-Choice
model outlines three primary types of motivation for choosing teaching:
personal utility (extrinsic benefits such as job security and salary), social
utility (altruistic goals such as contributing to society), and intrinsic
career values (the inherent enjoyment of teaching itself). However, few
studies have simultaneously examined multiple distinct motivation
types and their stress-related pathways to satisfaction. The present study
fills this gap by investigating how different teacher motivation profiles
(personal utility, social utility, intrinsic values) relate to job satisfaction
both directly and indirectly via work stressors.

Expectancy–value theory posits that individuals’ achievementrelated choices are influenced by their beliefs about how well they
will do on a task (expectancy) and the value they place on the task

personal utility, and social utility values. These constructs represent
reasons for entering the teaching profession, including interest, personal
benefit, and social contribution.
SDT, in contrast, centers on the psychological needs of autonomy,
competence, and relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000).
Motivation quality is conceptualized along a continuum from autonomous (intrinsic) to controlled (extrinsic). Although SDT is not the
foundation of the FIT-Choice model, it provides a useful framework for
interpreting how different types of motivation relate to well-being.
Rather than conflating these frameworks, we employ the FIT-Choice
model to categorize teacher motivations, while drawing on SDT to
interpret the quality and consequences of these motivations in relation
to job satisfaction and stress.
2.2. Types of teacher motivation and their relationships with job
satisfaction, workload, and student behavior stress
Teachers enter the profession for a variety of reasons, and their
motivations are multidimensional. According to the FIT-Choice framework (Watt & Richardson, 2007), three primary categories of motivation
influence individuals' decision to become teachers: personal utility, social utility, and intrinsic career values. A growing body of literature has
examined teacher motivation in relation to job satisfaction. For
example, Shoshani and Eldor (2016) report that teachers with higher
autonomous motivation also exhibit higher job satisfaction at work.
Neves de Jesus and Lens (2005) similarly emphasize that teachers'
motivational orientations can significantly influence their emotional
experiences on the job (including satisfaction and stress). More recently,
Chang and Sung (2024) demonstrated that the effect of teachers' motivation on job satisfaction is partly mediated by teachers’ sense of efficacy, highlighting one mechanism by which motivation translates into
satisfaction. Overall, prior research suggests that intrinsically or altruistically motivated teachers tend to be more satisfied with their jobs,
whereas those motivated by personal extrinsic benefits may be less
satisfied.
Personal utility values refer to practical and extrinsic considerations
such as job security, salary, work–life balance, and career transferability. These motivations are typically self-oriented and emphasize
instrumental benefits (Wang & Hall, 2019; Wang & Klassen, 2023).
Teachers who are mainly driven by personal utility values may be more
prone to frustration and stress, particularly when facing high workloads
or classroom difficulties (Fernet et al., 2012). Research indicates that
such extrinsic, personal utility motives are not strongly linked to sustained commitment or long-term teaching effectiveness (Wang & Klassen, 2023); indeed, teachers driven primarily by external rewards often
report lower engagement and higher burnout over time (Fernet et al.,
2012; Wang & Hall, 2019). Based on these findings, we formulate the

teachers’ personal resources over time, thereby reducing their capacity
to remain satisfied in their jobs.
Student behavior stress is closely associated with symptoms of

2021). Teachers often need to spend considerable time and energy

levels. Frequent student discipline issues not only exhaust teachers but
also erode their job satisfaction, as confirmed by Skaalvik and Skaalvik
(2011), who found that student misbehavior substantially lowers
teachers’ satisfaction in their work.

Intrinsic career value refers to the personal enjoyment and inherent
interest in the act of teaching. Teachers who are motivated by intrinsic
factors typically find genuine satisfaction in day-to-day instructional
activities, interactions with students, and the overall learning process.
This form of motivation is associated with a strong professional identity;
intrinsically driven teachers often see teaching as a core part of who they
are, and it relates to better coping with occupational stress (Han & Yin,
2016). In other words, teachers with high intrinsic motivation experience more positive emotions and a sense of accomplishment from their
work, helping buffer against stress. Educators driven by intrinsic motives report higher job satisfaction and lower emotional exhaustion,
indicating a protective effect against burnout (Hultell & Gustavsson,
2011; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2016). Such teachers tend to display greater
resilience in coping with classroom challenges, as their inner drive
provides a stable source of energy and commitment even under stress
(Hultell & Gustavsson, 2011). Moreover, when teachers’ core values and
passions are engaged, they meet their psychological needs on the job,
which is associated with improved well-being and reduced stress levels

While these three types of motivation are grounded in expectancy–value theory, they can also be understood through the lens of SDT,
which differentiates motivations by degree of autonomy. We have
clarified that personal utility motivations correspond to a controlled
extrinsic regulation (i.e., doing the work for external rewards or pressures), social utility motivations represent a more internalized extrinsic
motive (similar to identified regulation, where contributing to society is
personally endorsed and valued), and intrinsic career value aligns with
intrinsic motivation (engaging in teaching for its inherent enjoyment
and interest).
Internalized or autonomously regulated motivations (such as social
utility and intrinsic career value) are more likely to fulfill basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness. This
fulfillment may help reduce stress and promote well-being. In addition,
recent studies based on social cognitive career theory, such as Chang and
Sung (2024), have shown that motivation can indirectly affect job
satisfaction through mediating variables such as self-efficacy and
perceived competence.

3. Methodology
3.1. Research design
We used data from TALIS, an international survey initiated by the
OECD to collect information from school principals and teachers about
school learning environments and teachers’ working conditions. Specifically, we conducted a secondary analysis of data from lower secondary teachers in Taiwan who participated in TALIS 2018. Therefore,
no additional informed consent or institutional ethics approval was
required.
Our choice to conduct a secondary data analysis was intentional:
leveraging an existing high-quality, large-scale dataset allowed us to
investigate our research objectives with greater statistical power and
representativeness than a new small-scale sample could provide. By
using the internationally validated TALIS 2018 data, we ensured
comparability with other studies and capitalized on their broad scope to
derive robust insights. Recent studies continue to draw on TALIS 2018 to
examine teacher job satisfaction and working conditions, underscoring
that despite being dated, the data remain a relevant and valuable source
for current educational research (Admiraal & Røberg, 2023; Moreover,
TALIS 2018 remains the most recent large-scale survey of teachers of its
kind; its findings were still being used in 2023 to address contemporary
issues such as teacher stress (OECD, 2023).

Beyond emotions, teacher motivation influences observable behaviors. A multilevel analysis of the 2018 TALIS across 48 countries found
that self-efficacy and personal and social utility motivations significantly predict instructional practices (Bas, 2025). These results highlight
the importance of motivation, indicating that motivational profiles
shape how teachers engage, perceive job demands, and experience

3.2. Data source and sample
In accordance with the TALIS 2018 Analysis Plan (Price & Carstens,
2020), we employed a complex survey design framework to ensure valid
population-level inferences. Rather than using listwise deletion of
missing data, we employed a multiple imputation procedure based on
the Markov chain Monte Carlo method to handle missing responses
across key constructs. Specifically, we generated 20 imputed datasets
(using Markov chain Monte Carlo multiple imputation). The imputation
model included all major study variables (the three motivation subscales, two stress subscales, and the job satisfaction scale) as well as
relevant demographic variables (teacher gender, age, and years of
experience). The imputation was performed at the teacher level prior to
applying any survey weights. We then conducted our analyses on each
imputed dataset with the final weights in place and pooled the results
using Rubin's (1987) combination rules.

3.3. Measures
3.3.1. Teacher work motivation
We used three subscales from the TALIS 2018 teacher questionnaire
(OECD, 2019a; 2019b) to assess teacher motivation, each measured with
a 4-point Likert scale (1 = Not at all important to 4 = Of high importance). Personal Utility Motivation (T3PERUT) was assessed using four
items (TT3G07A, TT3G07B, TT3G07C, TT3G07D), which evaluated the
degree to which personal benefits such as job stability and salary influence an individual's decision to become a teacher. Social Utility
Motivation (T3SOCUT), measured with three items (TT3G07E,
TT3G07F, TT3G07G), captured the altruistic motivation to contribute to
society and help others through teaching. Value and Policy-Oriented
Motivation (T3VALP) was measured using three items (TT3G54C,
TT3G54D, TT3G54E) that reflect the extent to which teachers are driven
by a desire to influence educational values and policies, treating
teaching as a mission or personal calling (Table 1). Although labeled
“Value and Policy-Oriented' in the TALIS instrument, this subscale
captures the inherent enjoyment and sense of calling associated with
teaching itself, and is therefore operationalized in the present study as
the indicator of intrinsic career value as conceptualized in the
FIT-Choice framework (Watt & Richardson, 2007).
We performed a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess the
validity of the measurement model for the different types of teacher
motivation. The model fit for Personal Utility Motivation was excellent,
as indicated by the following indices: comparative fit index (CFI) =
0.994, Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) = 0.983, root mean square error of
approximation (RMSEA) = 0.077, and standardized root mean square
residual (SRMR) = 0.013. The internal consistency of this construct was
also high, with a Cronbach's α of 0.87. Social Utility Motivation and
intrinsic career value were each measured using three items, resulting in
just-identified models for which model fit indices are typically not reported. Nonetheless, both constructs demonstrated acceptable internal
consistency, with Cronbach's α values of 0.85 and 0.84, respectively.
Higher scores on all subscales indicate stronger perceived motivation to
pursue and stay in the teaching profession (OECD, 2019a; 2019b).

3.4. Data analysis

4.2. Structural model results

We employed SEM to test the research model. Following a two-step
modeling approach, we first tested a measurement model via CFA
including all latent constructs, then proceeded to a full SEM with the
hypothesized structural paths. We specified a single-step multiplemediator model (simultaneous multiple mediators with no assumed
causal ordering). Model estimation was conducted using maximum
likelihood.
To establish mediation, we examined the significance of indirect
paths from each motivation type to job satisfaction through the mediators, alongside direct paths. As our analysis incorporated complex
survey weights, we did not perform a conventional bootstrap of the
sample for mediation testing (the balanced repeated replication
approach already provides a resampling-based variance estimation).
Instead, we assessed indirect associations using the delta method (Sobel
tests) with replicate weight-adjusted standard errors. All hypothesized
indirect associations were found to be significant at the 0.05 level. In
addition, as a robustness check, we conducted a supplementary biascorrected bootstrap analysis with 500 resamples on an unweighted
version of the model; this confirmed that the 95% confidence intervals
for all indirect associations did not include zero.
The model fit was assessed using standard indices and criteria: a nonsignificant chi-square (χ2, p > .05) indicates good fit (though χ2 is sensitive to large sample sizes), RMSEA <0.08 (preferably <0.05 for close
fit), SRMR <0.05, and CFI and TLI >0.90. We also report the chi-square
to degrees of freedom ratio as an additional fit indicator. Scale scores
were computed as item means on the original TALIS metric and were not
z-standardized before SEM; standardized path coefficients are reported
for comparability.

4.2.1. Direct associations
The SEM analysis showed that teacher motivation variables were
significantly directly associated with both job satisfaction and the stressrelated mediators, while the mediators, in turn, were associated with
satisfaction. Regarding job satisfaction, all three types of motivation
were positively associated: personal utility motivation was positively
directly correlated with job satisfaction (β = 0.07, p < .001), social
utility motivation had a stronger positive direct correlation (β = 0.16, p
< .001), and intrinsic career value also had a significant positive correlation (β = 0.13, p < .001). Regarding the stressors, perceived workload had a significant negative direct association with job satisfaction (β
= − 0.18, p < .001), and student behavior stress had a similarly negative
association with satisfaction (β = − 0.20, p < .001). Together, these results support H1–4: teacher motivation types are associated with higher
job satisfaction, whereas high workload and problematic student
behavior stress are correlated with lower satisfaction.
In addition, the motivation types were significantly related to the
stressor variables. Personal utility motivation was associated with higher
levels of student behavior stress (β = 0.09, p < .001), indicating that
teachers driven by personal benefits tended to report more frequent
disruptive student behavior stress in their classes. However, social utility
motivation was associated with significantly reduced perceived workload and student behavior stress (β = − 0.04, p < .05 and β = − 0.12, p <
.001, respectively). Similarly, value-based intrinsic motivation had significant negative relationships with the stressors: it was associated with
lower workload (β = − 0.14, p < .001) and student behavior stress (β =
− 0.12, p < .001). These findings suggest that intrinsically or altruistically motivated teachers feel less stress from workload and misbehavior,
while those motivated by personal utility may face more classroom
management difficulties. This pattern provides evidence for how motivation types can shape the work stress context (supporting H1–3
regarding their associations with stressors, and H4 regarding the association of stressors with satisfaction).

4.1. Model fit
All aspects of the measurement model met the criteria. No negative
error variances were observed, and all standardized factor loadings
ranged from 0.52 to 0.89, reaching statistical significance (p < .001).

The indirect (mediated) pathways were examined to test H5–7. We
found that personal utility motivation was significantly negatively
indirectly correlated with job satisfaction through the pathway of student behavior stress (β = − 0.02, p < .001). Thus, although personal

associated with slightly lower satisfaction. The indirect association via

behavior stress (β = 0.02, p < .001). This indicates that social utilityoriented teachers reported lower perceived workload and lower
perceived student behavior stress, each of which was associated with

workload and behavior stress, further boosting satisfaction. Job stress
(workload and student behavior) partly mediates these associations,
supporting the hypothesized stress pathway model.

5. Discussion
The findings partly support the proposed hypotheses and highlight
important links between teacher motivation, work stressors, and job
satisfaction. H1 was partly supported: personal utility motivation was
associated with slightly enhanced job satisfaction but not with workload
and was correlated with greater stress related to student misbehavior.
This suggests that extrinsically oriented motives can yield temporary
satisfaction when external goals are achieved but may undermine
teachers’ ability to cope with everyday challenges. According to SDT,
controlled forms of motivation, such as those driven by external rewards
or pressures, provide limited satisfaction and fail to meet basic needs for
autonomy and competence, thereby increasing stress (Deci & Ryan,
1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000).
Moreover, our results mirror the patterns reported by Vermote et al.
(2020), who showed that teachers' motivational orientations predict
their teaching style, with intrinsic motivation and growth mindsets
leading to more engaging, need-supportive instruction and controlled
motivation or fixed mindsets leading to more controlling, demotivating
approaches. This suggests that enhancing teachers’ intrinsic and prosocial motivations may not only reduce stress but also foster more
motivating instructional practices, thereby indirectly boosting job
satisfaction and teacher well-being. Consistently, recent research shows
that teachers high in autonomous motivation tend to both plan and
implement more need-supportive (promotive) instructional strategies,
whereas those driven by controlled motives or amotivation are more
prone to adopt controlling or chaotic approaches that suppress student
motivation (Wang, Meng, et al., 2024). Daily diary studies by in Jansen
in de Wal et al., (2024) demonstrate that stress and buoyancy fluctuate
substantially within teachers and are negatively related both within and
between persons, despite nonsignificant cross-lagged day-to-day effects.
These findings suggest that stress and buoyancy are malleable, state-like
constructs whose dynamics unfold on timescales shorter than daily intervals, underscoring buoyancy as a promising target for interventions
to sustain well-being and career longevity.
In the context of our results, this implies that teachers with stronger
intrinsic or altruistic motivation (who reported lower stress) may also
demonstrate greater buoyancy in the face of daily challenges. In other
words, high-quality motivation could bolster teachers’ capacity to
recover from classroom setbacks, which aligns with the observed link
between internal motives and reduced stress.
H2 and H3 were both supported, pointing to the protective role of
altruistic and value-driven motives. Social utility motivation was positively associated with job satisfaction while being correlated with lower
perceived workload as well as student behavior stress. Teachers driven
by altruistic motives appeared more resilient, faced fewer stressors, and
found greater meaning in their professional roles. Such motives support
psychological needs for autonomy and relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
and are consistent with findings that socially motivated teachers are

better able to reframe stress and remain engaged (Skaalvik & Skaalvik,

with greater job satisfaction and lower stress related to workload and
student misbehavior. Prior research has shown that teachers who view
their profession as a calling, or who are guided by strong educational
ideals, tend to exhibit higher levels of engagement and resilience
(Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2018). By aligning their work with their values,
they manage demands more effectively, respond to challenges with
patience, and experience higher satisfaction despite external stressors.
H4 was also supported. Both perceived workload and student
behavior stress were significantly associated with lower job satisfaction,
confirming that excessive demands and disciplinary issues erode wellbeing and heighten attrition risk. These results corroborate earlier
findings that work overload and behavioral challenges are central predictors of teacher burnout and turnover (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2011).
More recent studies further highlight chronic misbehavior as a key
predictor of teacher attrition (Buckman & Pittman, 2021). Collectively,
these findings underscore the importance of mitigating excessive
workloads and enhancing classroom behavior support to promote
teacher retention and job satisfaction.
The current findings regarding perceived workload can also be
interpreted in light of the broader digital learning ecosystem. As Seufert
et al. (2024) emphasize, school-level support spanning infrastructure,
leadership, and pedagogical assistance can function as a contextual
facilitator that reduces teachers’ workload-related barriers to effective
teaching (e.g., time spent troubleshooting, preparing digital materials,
and managing digital platforms). International survey data further
indicate that only about 43% of teachers feel well-prepared to use information and communication technology in teaching, and roughly one
in four principals reports insufficient digital resources as a hindrance to
instruction (OECD, 2019b). These patterns suggest that inadequate
technological support and training often translate into additional time
pressure and work intensification, thereby elevating perceived workload. Consistent with our results linking stressors to outcomes,
strengthening school-level information and communication technology
infrastructure, targeted professional development, and leadership
guidance may alleviate perceived workload, which in turn can indirectly
reduce stress and support more sustainable teacher motivation (Seufert
et al., 2024).
In digitally enriched classrooms, teachers with strong intrinsic or
altruistic motivation can thrive when given adequate technology support; they are more inclined to adopt innovative digital tools to enhance
learning, seeing new technologies as opportunities rather than burdens.
Conversely, a lack of guidance or rigid mandates around educational
technology can lead to frustration and stress, as evidenced by the challenges of managing students' off-task technology use during instruction
without clear policies (McGarr, 2024). Research has underscored that
improving teachers’ digital competencies and confidence (Lohr et al.,
2024) alongside providing strong institutional support (Seufert et al.,
2024) may help the digital learning ecosystem bolster, rather than
hinder, teacher motivation and effectiveness.
The mediation hypotheses (H5–7) were largely supported. H5 indicated that personal utility motivation was indirectly associated with
reduced job satisfaction by heightening stress related to student
misbehavior, but not workload. Teachers with extrinsic motives may
lack emotional resources to cope with behavioral issues, thereby
diminishing the satisfaction gained from personal rewards (Skaalvik &
Skaalvik, 2011). In contrast, H6 and H7 showed that social utility and
value-based intrinsic motivations were indirectly associated with
enhanced job satisfaction by reducing both workload and
behavior-related stress.
These findings align with conservation of resources theory (Hobfoll,
2011) and the challenge–hindrance stressor framework, suggesting that
altruistic and value-driven teachers are better equipped to reframe demands as meaningful challenges, mobilize resources, and sustain
well-being (O'Brien & Schillaci, 2002; Richardson & Watt, 2014).

Overall, stress emerged as a partial mediator between motivation and
satisfaction: intrinsic and altruistic motives protected against stress,
while extrinsic motives amplified it, thereby diminishing overall satisfaction (Zagni et al., 2025). These findings also resonate with Jiang and
Yin's (2025) work showing that teachers with a strong sense of mission
frequently adopt adaptive regulation strategies such as reappraisal,
acceptance, and interpersonal connection. Taken together, the results
suggest that high-quality motivation may be associated with reduced
stress and greater resilience at both behavioral and emotional levels.
In Taiwan, teaching is often regarded as a “calling” or mission, and
many enter the profession with a strong sense of altruism or passion
(Chang & Sung, 2024). Our findings reinforce that these higher-order
motives indeed correspond to better outcomes (less stress, more fulfillment) under the demanding conditions of Taiwanese schools. Meanwhile, the finding that personal utility motivation can undermine
satisfaction via stress is telling: although teaching in Taiwan is stable
and fairly paid, those who choose the career chiefly for those external
benefits may still become stressed by the day-to-day challenges (e.g.,
heavy administrative loads, pressure related to student results) and thus
feel less satisfied.
The above is consistent with the idea of person–organization fit
(Wang & Klassen, 2023). If a teacher's primary motives are extrinsic, the
intrinsic stressors of the job (long hours, emotional labor) might create
dissonance, even in a system that outwardly rewards teachers well.
Furthermore, Taiwan's exam-oriented culture and frequent policy
changes (e.g., curriculum reforms, new administrative requirements)
likely intensify teachers' sense of workload and accountability stress
(Chao et al., 2024). In such a context, our results suggest that having
strong intrinsic or social motivations can serve as a protective resource,
helping teachers find meaning and cope with pressures. Conversely,
without that intrinsic drive, the high expectations placed on teachers
(for student performance, etc.) may become a source of frustration.
In sum, this study extends SDT by empirically demonstrating how
different forms of teacher motivation shape job satisfaction both directly
and indirectly through stress pathways, thereby integrating motivation
theory with stress and coping perspectives. At the practical level, the
findings highlight the importance of fostering intrinsic and altruistic
motives while alleviating excessive workload and disciplinary pressures
to sustain teachers’ well-being and commitment. By bridging theoretical
and applied perspectives, this study underscores that enhancing the
quality of teacher motivation is not only central to advancing psychological theory but also vital for improving educational practice and
teacher retention.
6. Conclusions
This study offers new insights into how different types of motivation
and stress interact to shape job satisfaction. First, teachers’ work motivation is positively associated with job satisfaction across all three dimensions: personal utility, social utility, and value-based intrinsic
motivation, leading to greater fulfillment and achievement. These results align with SDT (Deci & Ryan, 1985), which emphasizes that when
intrinsic motivations are activated, engagement and satisfaction
increase.
Second, job stress is negatively correlated with job satisfaction.
Stress from heavy workloads and student behavior reduces teachers’
satisfaction, enjoyment, and sense of accomplishment. This aligns with
research that links stress to burnout, highlighting the importance of
stress reduction for teacher well-being. For example, international
studies indicate that teachers working in more supportive environments,
characterized by effective school leadership, positive student behavior
climates, and manageable workloads, report substantially higher job
satisfaction than those in more challenging contexts (OECD, 2019a;
Zakariya et al., 2020). Likewise, teachers who benefit from such favorable working conditions are significantly more satisfied with their jobs
(Eryilmaz et al., 2025). These cross-cultural patterns underscore the

importance of alleviating key stressors in order to improve teacher
satisfaction and well-being, reinforcing the need to reduce excessive
workload and student misbehavior pressures as highlighted above.
Third, job stress partially mediates the relationship between motivation and satisfaction, with different types of motivation following
distinct paths. Personal utility motivation is associated with increased
stress from student misbehavior, lowering satisfaction. Conversely, social utility motivation is associated with lower workload and fewer
stressors, boosting satisfaction. Similarly, intrinsic career value is associated with decreased stress perceptions, enhancing satisfaction. These
results indicate that teachers’ motivational mindsets influence their
stress experiences and responses, affecting their overall satisfaction.
Fourth, different types of motivation affect stress perception in
varying ways. Teachers with social utility or value-based intrinsic motivations report less stress from workload and student misbehavior,
suggesting that altruistic views help reframe stress as an opportunity for
growth. Conversely, teachers motivated by personal utility are more
likely to report higher stress, especially in relation to student discipline,
suggesting that a focus on personal gain increases vulnerability to
classroom challenges.
Overall, the theoretical model demonstrated strong explanatory
power and practical relevance. The excellent model fit confirms the
hypothesized relationships between motivation, stress, and satisfaction,
offering empirical validation for a multiple mediation framework that
integrates motivational theory with stress and coping processes. This
contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of teacher wellbeing and provides a theoretical foundation for future research.
Several practical implications emerge from these findings. At the
policy level, educational authorities should design initiatives that foster
teachers' intrinsic and altruistic motivations, for example, enabling
meaningful professional development, recognizing the societal value of
teaching, and involving teachers in decision-making processes. Policies
can also address workload by hiring additional support staff or teaching
assistants so that teachers can delegate administrative tasks, thereby
streamlining paperwork and non-teaching duties and reducing stress. At
the school level, administrators can take concrete steps to reduce
teachers’ burden and improve morale. This includes simplifying or
outsourcing routine administrative work (e.g., data entry, paperwork) to
clerical staff, improving classroom management support systems, and
offering well-being programs such as counseling services and familyfriendly policies. Fostering a collaborative and respectful school culture is also key; for instance, ensuring teachers have collegial support
and a voice in school decisions can enhance their sense of autonomy and
purpose.
At the individual level, teachers may benefit from targeted stress
management interventions (e.g., mindfulness-based programs) and
participation in professional learning communities that promote peer
support and reflective practice. Interventions that help teachers reconnect with the moral and personal purpose of their work, such as structured mentorship programs, value-based reflection exercises, and
mission-driven workshops, may enhance resilience and long-term
commitment. Additionally, cultivating a supportive school culture
(with opportunities for informal teacher learning and collaboration) can
amplify these efforts, as evidence shows that teachers in positive
learning climates report higher motivation and job satisfaction.
Finally, given the increasing integration of digital tools in education,
schools and policymakers should strive to ensure that the digital
learning ecosystem is supportive of teachers. Introducing new technologies alongside adequate training and technical support may help
intrinsically or altruistically motivated teachers to experiment and
innovate, while reducing the risk that technology-related demands
simply add to workload stress (Lohr et al., 2024; Seufert et al., 2024). At
the same time, research on digital distraction highlights that educators'
responses and interventions can vary in the absence of shared guidance
and norms, which may contribute to frustration when managing students' technology use in learning settings (McGarr, 2024). Overall,

attending to teachers’ digital competencies and providing autonomy in
how educational technology is used may help the benefits of
high-quality motivation be realized in digitally enriched classrooms.
Despite these contributions, this study has several limitations. First,
its cross-sectional design prevents causal inference; the observed relationships should be interpreted as correlational. Second, the use of
self-reported measures may introduce biases such as those related to
social desirability and recall. Third, while the sample is large and
representative of junior high school teachers in Taiwan, generalizability
to other educational contexts or countries may be limited. Additionally,
unmeasured contextual variables (such as school leadership style or
organizational climate) could play a significant role in shaping teacher
motivation and stress responses. Notably, TALIS 2018 includes
principal-reported indicators of school leadership (e.g., instructional
leadership and distributed or participative leadership), which have been
documented and psychometrically supported in the TALIS technical
documentation and prior secondary analyses. Future studies could
incorporate these established leadership measures as contextual controls
or moderators to further clarify the mechanism linking motivation,
stress, and job satisfaction (Hsieh et al., 2024; Hsieh et al., 2025; OECD,
2019a, 2019b).
Future research should consider employing longitudinal or mixedmethods designs. For instance, daily diary studies could be used to
capture day-to-day fluctuations in teacher stress and coping, thereby
clarifying how motivation and well-being evolve (in de Wal et al., 2024).
Investigating subgroup differences by teaching experience, subject area,
or school level may offer further insights into individual variability.
Additionally, future models should account for key background characteristics that influence teacher outcomes. Recent evidence highlights
that contextual factors such as school setting (rural vs. urban) can shape
teachers’ stress, emotion regulation, and motivating style usage.
Considering such background variables, along with teacher demographics (e.g., age, gender, and experience), in motivation–stress–satisfaction models will help ensure that findings are robust and
applicable across diverse teaching contexts (Wang, Xing, & Moè, 2024).
Moreover, given emerging digital teaching challenges, researchers
should examine how factors such as online or hybrid instruction, virtual
classroom management, and technology-induced workload influence
the motivation–stress–satisfaction dynamic for teachers. Ultimately,
experimental or quasi-experimental studies that assess the effectiveness
of interventions designed to promote intrinsic and altruistic motivations
could inform policies aimed at enhancing teacher well-being and
retention.
