A B S T R A C T
Japan is ahead of most other countries in the global population ageing process. Its working-age population (aged 
20–64) peaked in 1998, compared to 2011 in the European Union, while continued growth is expected in the 
United States until 2070, albeit at a declining pace. In this paper, we analyse employment and productivity 
developments in Japan since 1998 and examine how Japan differs from other OECD countries in various di­
mensions potentially related to ageing. Rising employment among women and older workers has lifted Japan’s 
employment rate to one of the highest levels in the OECD. However, older workers tend to be employed in low- 
productivity jobs. Despite rising labour shortages, immigration is much lower in Japan than in most other OECD 
countries. Ageing has led to the adoption of some labour-saving technologies, notably robots. Nevertheless, 
business dynamism is weak in international comparison and population ageing creates challenges for business 
succession. Changes in the consumption structure have been modest. Age-related spending contributed signifi­
cantly to the deterioration of fiscal positions. Overall, even though Japan has been able to cope with ageing 
relatively well, its economic developments over the past decades illustrate the challenges of enhancing job 
quality at the same time as raising employment rates, sustaining innovation and preserving public finance 
sustainability in an ageing society.
Introduction
Adapting to population ageing is a major challenge for both 
advanced and emerging economies. While longevity continues to prog­
ress at a steady pace globally, young cohorts are becoming smaller. Two- 
thirds of the world’s population live in countries with fertility below the 
replacement rate (MGI, 2025). Japan is well ahead of other countries in 
the ageing process. Its working-age population (aged 20–64) peaked in 
1998, compared to 2011 in the European Union, according to the United 
Nations’ population statistics. The growth rate of the working-age 
population in the United States is also declining but is predicted to 
remain positive throughout 2070, even if only barely so (Fig. 1). Hence, 
other countries may learn from Japan’s experience, even though idio­
syncratic economic and societal factors need to be recognised.1 In this 
paper, we examine economic developments in Japan over the past thirty 
years and analyse differences with other OECD countries in various di­
mensions potentially related to ageing.
A decline in the share of working-age individuals in the total 
population reduces GDP per capita, unless it is offset by higher 
employment rates or a rise in labour productivity. In many OECD 
countries, the working-age population is projected to fall in absolute 
terms over the coming decades, generating concerns about potential 
labour shortages. Spending on pensions, health and long-term care 
weighs on public finances. Nevertheless, healthy ageing offers oppor­
tunities for longer working lives and stronger involvement of older 
people in society. Many OECD countries have scope to raise employment 
rates in all age groups and to improve job quality, thereby mitigating 
tensions on labour supply. Productivity gains, notably thanks to adopt­
ing advanced technologies such as artificial intelligence, could also 
support economic growth.
However, ageing itself may affect productivity through multiple 
channels playing in opposite directions, making the overall economic 
impact of ageing on GDP per capita very uncertain (Andr´e et al., 2024). 
On the negative side, older workers may be less productive than their 
younger counterparts due to health impairments or skills obsolescence, 
ageing societies may become less dynamic and innovative, demand may

Japan’s working-age population peaked shortly after the burst of its 
asset price bubble at the beginning of the 1990s, which triggered a 
financial crisis followed by a long period of economic stagnation (Hoshi 
and Kashyap, 2004; Jones, 2022). Several factors likely contributed to 
the weak post-crisis recovery, including a slow monetary policy 
response (Bernanke, 2000), ill-timed tax increases (Posen, 2003) and 
delayed financial restructuring, which led banks to continue lending to 
“zombie” firms, hampering economic renewal and the emergence of 
more productive businesses (Posen, 2003, Hoshi and Kashyap, 2004). 
However, macroeconomic and financial difficulties may have been 
compounded by the decline in potential output partially induced by 
population ageing.2 Furthermore, economic difficulties may have pre­
vented some economic adjustments, which would have mitigated the 
impact of ageing on the Japanese economy and public finances (Heller, 
2016). In particular, deflation skewed the distribution of income in 
favour of older people on fixed nominal pensions, increasing the burden 
on the smaller working-age cohorts (Aoki, 2013) and weakening the 
sustainability of the pension system (Hosen, 2010).
Ageing affects economic growth through various channels. In 
particular, GDP per capita can be decomposed into three components. 
First, the ratio of working-age to total population, which is a purely 
demographic component. A reduction in this ratio mechanically reduces 
GDP per capita, all else equal. Second, the total number of hours worked 
divided by the size of the working-age population, which reflects both 
the employment rate and the average number of hours worked by em­
ployees.3 While ageing may reduce this ratio, as older workers tend to 
have lower employment rates and shorter working hours than prime-age 
workers, this may be at least partly offset by rising employment rates 
over time among older workers, as well as in other groups, notably 
women. Third, the labour input effect may be either compounded or 
counteracted by labour productivity (GDP per hour worked) de­
velopments, which may themselves be affected by ageing.
In Japan, the decline in the share of working-age population has 
reduced GDP per capita growth by more than half a percentage point per 
year since 1998 (Fig. 2). The contribution of hours worked is minor, 
reflecting two opposite trends. The number of hours worked per 
employee declined by nearly 13% from 1998 to 2022,4 while the 
employment to population ratio in the 20–64 age group rose by over 
11%. Productivity growth contributed 1.1% to GDP per capita growth, 
which compares to 1.6% in the United States, 1.2% in the European 
Union and 0.9% in the euro area over the same period. These de­
compositions show that Japan’s productivity growth (measured as GDP/ 
hours) has been on par with Europe’s and that Japan’s particularly low 
GDP per capita growth is explained predominantly by a declining share 
of the working-age population.
This paper is organised as follows. The next section describes de­
mographic developments in Japan and other main OECD regions, dis­
cusses the role of migration and reviews recent employment trends. The 
following section examines the potential links between ageing and 
productivity developments. The last section concludes.

Demographic trends
Population and dependency ratios
As shown above, the Japanese working-age population started 
declining in 1998. The total population peaked in 2010 at 128.2 million, 
falling to 124.4 million in 2023. In the medium variant of the 2024 UN 
World Population Prospects it is projected to fall to 91 million in 2070. 
The population aged 20–64 peaked at 79.4 million in 1998, fell to 67.4 
million in 2023, and is projected to shrink further to 44 million in 2070.5
In 2023, Japan had by far the highest old-age dependency ratio, defined 
as the ratio of the population aged 65 or over to the population aged 
20–64, in the OECD, at 0.55, with Finland coming second at 0.42. It is 
projected to rise to 0.80 in 2054, before declining to 0.76 in 2070.
In the early stages of a demographic transition driven by falling 
fertility rates, the increase in the old-age dependency ratio is generally 
offset by a decline in the youth dependency ratio, which allows for 
continued increase in the working-age population share. At later stages, 
the youth dependency ratio stabilises and no longer offsets the impact of 
the old-age dependency ratio, pushing up the overall dependency ratio. 
Hence, economic growth, which is a linear function of the working-age 
share of the population, is a non-linear function of the old age de­
pendency ratio (Lee and Shin, 2019). This was also the case in Japan, 
where the working-age share of the population increased despite a 
gradually rising old-age dependency ratio during much of the second 
half of the 20th century, driven by a strong decline in the youth de­
pendency ratio. However, since the late-1990s, Japan’s overall de­
pendency ratio has soared and is projected to peak around 2055. In 
contrast, the overall dependency ratio in the European Union and the 
United States only started rising around 2010, and at a slower pace, 
especially in the United States (Fig. 3).
Healthy ageing
Healthy ageing can enhance individual well-being, raise productivity 
and support participation in work and society. The ability of older 
people to live independently, engage in society, be it through formal 
employment or other activities, and enjoy well-being, depends heavily 
on their health. In turn, healthy ageing has major implications for eco­
nomic productivity, public finances, and broader societal costs. On 
average between 2000 and 2019, about two-thirds of the increase in life 
expectancy at 60 across OECD countries was spent in good health. In 
Japan, this proportion reaches three-quarters. The country has both the 
highest life expectancy and the highest health-adjusted life expectancy 
in the OECD (Fig. 4). Factors contributing to Japan’s outstanding health 
outcomes include universal access to high quality health care and rela­
tively healthy lifestyles, including a low prevalence of overweight and 
obesity, low illicit drug consumption, below-OECD average alcohol 
consumption and a relatively high level of physical activity. Neverthe­
less, air pollution in Japanese cities remains a concern, contributing to 
premature deaths (OECD, 2023a).
Immigration
Immigration is often viewed as an important policy tool to slow 
population ageing. However, since its working-age population started 
declining, Japan’s net migration rate has been among the lowest in the 
OECD (Fig. 5). Net migration not only contributes to population growth 
but also changes the age structure of the population, as migrants tend to 
be concentrated among the young working-age population. Neverthe­
less, although migration has a sizable impact on the growth of the 
population, its effects on its age structure are more limited. As migrants 
themselves are ageing, those who initially lower the old-age dependency 
ratio eventually contribute to its rise when they reach retirement age. To 
permanently make the population younger, it would be necessary that 
either migrants would arrive at a young age and leave as they get old, or 
that migration rates would continually rise.
Some simulations based on UN World Population Prospects and 
plausible assumptions about fertility rates and the age distribution of 
migrants illustrate the order of magnitude of the migration flows that 
would be necessary to keep the age structure of the population un­
changed. For the OECD as a whole, a continued net migration rate of 
around 0.3% per year, as was observed over the 2000–2020 period in the 
OECD, would be sufficient to prevent a fall in the working-age popula­
tion until the mid-2030s. However, stabilising the old age dependency 
ratio would require a much higher net migration rate of about 2% per

Canada and New Zealand have translated into favourable labour market

skilled workers and promote growth (Pan and Theseira, 2023). In 
addition, Japan faces more obstacles to attracting migrants than many 
OECD countries which benefit from closer economic integration and 
language and cultural ties with other countries.
Even so, more immigration could have eased some labour shortages. 
In 2022, foreign-born individuals accounted for just 2.3% of Japan’s 
population and 2.6% of its labour force, among the lowest shares in the 
OECD. Despite government measures to facilitate work immigration, 
especially for high-skilled workers, residency rules remain much stricter 
than in most other OECD countries (Jones, 2024).

Employment trends
In countries facing rapidly ageing populations, it is essential to 
mobilise labour resources in all age groups. In 2024, Japan recorded the 
second highest employment rate for men aged 20–64 in the OECD at 
89.7% (after Iceland at 90.2%). The corresponding rate for women was 
78.2%, the 8th highest in the OECD. The employment rate among prime- 
age women has been rising steadily over the past 50 years. Since the turn 
of the century, the employment rates of both women and men over 60 
have risen very rapidly (Fig. 7). Social security reforms, notably in­
creases in the eligibility age for public pension benefits, have pushed up 
labour force participation among older people, which has also been 
facilitated by improvements in health and longevity, rising education 
levels and a shift towards less physically demanding jobs (Oshio, Usui 
and Shimizutani, 2019). While similar developments have been 
observed across OECD countries, employment rates for men and women 
over 60 remain significantly higher in Japan than in the European Union 
or the United States. Given Japan’s already elevated participation rates, 
the scope for offsetting population ageing through further increases in 
participation is more limited than in other OECD countries (Andr´e et al., 
2024).

Ageing and productivity
Ageing may affect labour productivity through a variety of channels. 
At the microeconomic level, workers may become less productive as 
they age, due to physical impairments or skills obsolescence – although 
they also accumulate experience. Innovation and entrepreneurship may 
weaken if younger people are the most likely to innovate and take risks. 
Conversely, ageing may induce labour scarcity, which in turn may 
incentivise productivity-enhancing automation. Macroeconomic mech­
anisms, such as developments in aggregate savings and interest rates 
may also bring about capital deepening, raising labour productivity. 
However, ageing can also weaken aggregate productivity by shifting 
demand towards sectors, such as labour-intensive personal services (e.g. 
care or leisure), where productivity gains are more difficult to achieve, 
and by crowding-out productive investments through increased age- 
related spending. As different channels have opposite effects on pro­
ductivity growth, the overall effect of ageing on productivity is theo­
retically uncertain. In the remainder of this section, we try to shed light 
on the main channels through which ageing affects productivity, 
drawing on international literature.
The role of age in individual and firm-level productivity
A common concern is that older workers may be less productive than 
younger ones. In that case, an ageing workforce could result in lower 
firm-level and aggregate labour productivity. However, considerable 
uncertainty remains regarding the relationship between age and 
individual-level productivity, as well as its macroeconomic significance. 
The age-productivity link at the individual level is commonly viewed as 
hump-shaped. Productivity rises with age as workers gain experience 
until a point where deteriorating health or obsolescence of skills reverses 
this relation. International research drawing on psychometric tests of 
cognitive performance or output-based measures like piece rates often 
finds that productivity peaks when workers are in their early 40s and 
diminishes when they get older, but with large heterogeneity across 
measures of productivity (Skirbekk, 2004; Prskawetz and Lindh, 2006). 
However, individual measures of productivity should be taken with 
caution as workers contribute to firms’ performances through their 
participation in teams, which are generally diverse and to which older 
workers often contribute in different roles (e.g., managerial, advisory) 
than younger ones (US National Research Council, 2012). Companies 
that combine prime-age workers with older and younger workers tend to 
be more productive than others (OECD, 2020a).
Labour market institutions and practices may constitute significant 
obstacles to lifting older workers’ employment. Seniority-based wage 
setting is still widespread in Japan, potentially resulting in a decoupling 
of wage and productivity at older ages and discouraging labour mobility 
and employment in regular jobs. Most Japanese firms set a mandatory 
retirement age at 60, the minimum age imposed by government since 
1998. Since 2013, firms are required to offer employment to employees 
wishing to continue working until age 65 and a 2020 law encourages 
firms to keep employees until age 70. However, a large majority of 
employees over 60 become non-regular (part-time, fixed-term contract 
or dispatched) workers, usually earning lower wages (Jones, 2024). 
While these arrangements have raised older workers’ employment rate, 
they often come at a cost in terms of productivity, as many workers over 
60 probably do not contribute as much as they would in regular jobs. 
Meanwhile, several OECD countries have either abolished mandatory 
retirement ages as a valid reason for terminating labour contracts 
(Australia, Canada, Denmark, Poland, the United Kingdom and the 
United States) or have raised the applicable age (OECD, 2019).
Lifelong learning is essential to maintain the employability and skills 
of an ageing workforce. In line with the lifetime employment system, 
training in Japan has traditionally focussed on firm-specific training. 
However, the decline of this model has not been accompanied by a 
significant expansion in more general forms of training. Participation in 
adult learning in Japan remains relatively low in all age groups, espe­
cially when compared to the United States, New Zealand and Northern 
Europe (Fig. 8). The participation gap between full-time and part-time 
workers is also the highest in the OECD, at 18 percentage points, 
compared to an average of seven percentage points, reflecting labour 
market dualism. The rising share of non-regular workers since the early

training find it very useful, compared to an OECD average of 46%

Japan has potential to further lift female employment at all ages, and 
to improve the quality of jobs for women. This would require improving 
work-life balance, expanding access to high-quality and affordable 
childcare and long-term elderly care and fighting discrimination and 
gender stereotypes. Women are more likely than men to be employed in 
non-regular jobs, especially after a career break to raise children. 
Addressing labour market dualism would create better employment 
opportunities not only for women but also for older workers, with a 
positive effect on both their employment rate and productivity (Jones 
and Seitani, 2019b; Jones, 2024). Overall, the Japanese case shows that 
rising employment can mitigate the impact of ageing on labour supply, a 
trend that is already noticeable in many OECD countries that are less 
advanced in the ageing process. It also highlights the importance of 
adapting labour market practices and investing in lifelong learning to 
allow sustaining productivity growth as the workforce ages.
Innovation, entrepreneurship, and business dynamism
Productivity growth ultimately results from innovation and entre­
preneurship. Even modest differences in growth rates can, over extended 
periods, lead to large level differences in GDP, far exceeding whatever 
the static impact of population ageing on aggregate productivity might 
be. Thus, the long-run consequences of population ageing critically 
depend on how ageing impacts innovation, entrepreneurship, and 
business dynamism. Population ageing may depress innovation through 
a compositional effect if innovation and entrepreneurial activity is 
mostly driven by young individuals, whose population share is 
declining. However, this concern may be overstated, as empirical evi­
dence suggests that scientific productivity and successful entrepre­
neurial activity tend to be highest at mid-career ages and that young age 
cohorts are not always more innovative than those closer to retirement 
(Simonton, 1988, 1991; Azoulay et al., 2020). Moreover, evidence 
suggests that, in scientific fields, significant achievements are made at 
older ages today than in the past (Jones, 2010).

entrepreneurship and business dynamism through several channels. 
Hoshi et al. (2025) document hump-shaped relationships between age 
and entrepreneurship propensity and between the entrepreneur’s age 
and the firm’s total or per worker sales in Japan and show that this can 
lower total factor productivity over the next three decades, albeit only 
by a modest amount. Engbom (2019) argues that population ageing has 
a negative effect on the aggregate job switching rate, as older workers 
tend to have lower job mobility, thereby reducing dynamism by driving 
up hiring costs and discouraging potential entrepreneurs. Liang et al. 
(2018) argue that workforce ageing reduces entrepreneurial activity by 
slowing the career progression of young workers, which limits their 
ability to accumulate the experience required for successful entrepre­
neurship. Derrien et al. (2023) argue that access to younger labour 
markets increases corporate innovation. Taking a demand-side view, 
Bornstein (2019) argues that a larger share of older households increases 
aggregate consumer inertia, which in turn raises incumbent companies’ 
market power and lowers incentives for innovation and firm entry.
Alongside population ageing, slowing population growth may also 
have contributed to declining business dynamism. Shrinking market 
sizes can reduce investment opportunities and may contribute to secular 
stagnation (Hansen, 1939). Moreover, slowing workforce growth 
directly impacts firm entry rates, since incumbent firms employ a larger 
share of the available workforce (Hopenhayn et al., 2022; Karahan et al., 
2024). Finally, limited or negative workforce growth may also depress 
productivity by lowering aggregate worker mobility and preventing the 
efficient reallocation of labour across sectors (Cociuba and MacGee, 
2018).
Innovation performance in Japan has weakened over the past three 
decades. For example, the citation performance of Japanese patents 
registered with the United States Patent and Trademark Office declined 
markedly relative to that of other countries since the early 1990s 
(Yamashita, 2021). This decline does not reflect a lack of research and 
development (R&D), but rather low returns on R&D investments. In fact, 
while Japan consistently spends more on R&D and files more patent 
applications than the United States, the quality of its innovation appears 
to be lagging (Jones and Kim, 2015; Bahar and Strauss, 2020). The 
timing of Japan’s relative decline in innovation, coinciding with both 
the 1990s economic downturn and an acceleration in population ageing, 
makes it difficult to determine whether demographics played a role. 
Arora et al. (2013) document that innovation in the Information Tech­
nology (IT) sector became increasingly software-intensive during the 
1990s, a shift that favoured US firms given their access to a larger supply 
of software talent. While differences in immigration are the primary 
reason for the faster growth of software talent in the United States, it is 
possible that Japan’s relative shortage was exacerbated by an ageing 
population, where cohorts entering the labour market each year are 
small relative to the overall workforce, making it more difficult for the 
economy to adapt to rapidly evolving skill demands.
Business dynamism in Japan is also relatively weak in international 
comparison. For example, Japan’s firm entry and exit rates are much 
lower than those of the United States, United Kingdom, Germany or 
France (Takizawa, 2025). These differences are not due to dissimilarities 
in the sectoral composition of the economy but may be linked to higher 
costs of entry (more regulation) and exit (a less developed market for the 
sale of used capital) in Japan (Mukoyama, 2009). Other explanations 
point to Japan’s bank-based financial system and a culture of long-term 
lending relationships, which allow unprofitable firms to survive for 
longer and reduce the allocative efficiency of capital (Imai, 2016; OECD, 
2024).
However, subdued business dynamism in Japan could also be partly 
due to demographics. Without changes in average firm size or aggregate 
exit rates, declining labour force growth translates one-to-one into a 
reduction of the firm entry rate, as less firm creation is needed to absorb 
a smaller labour force. Zhu (2025) documents a persistent decline in 
Japan’s firm entry rate during the second half of the 20th century and 
argues that much of it can be accounted for by slower labour supply 
growth. Moreover, the total effect of reduced labour force growth on 
entry rates can be exacerbated by an implied shift in the age distribution 
of firms. Lower firm entry leads to a growing relative share of older 
firms, which are on average larger and have lower exit probabilities, 
thereby reducing aggregate exit rates and further depressing equilib­
rium entry rates (Hopenhayn et al., 2022; Karahan et al., 2024). Indeed, 
the age distribution of firms in Japan has shifted markedly to the right, 
even if the implied effect on entry rates seems to have been limited (Zhu, 
2025).
Finally, population ageing may also qualitatively affect the firm 
reallocation process in Japan. According to a white paper by Japan’s 
Small and Medium Enterprise Agency, a significant share of firm exit in 
Japan is not driven by economic necessity, such as declining produc­
tivity, but by the ageing of business owners and managers (Fig. 10) 
coupled with the difficulty of finding suitable successors (Ministry of 
Economy, Trade and Industry, 2019; Harada, 2007; Tsuruta, 2020, 
2021). This has implications for the productivity enhancing effect of 
firm dynamism. In particular, if firm exit is not driven by economic 
fundamentals, higher churn rates may fail to produce the aggregate 
productivity gains typically associated with firm dynamism.
Adoption of labour-saving technologies
Ageing raises incentives to innovate and adopt new technologies 
when labour becomes increasingly scarce (Habakkuk, 1962; Romer, 
1990). Acemoglu and Restrepo (2017) find no evidence of negative 
correlation between ageing and GDP per capita growth in a sample of 
169 countries over the period 1990–2015 and relate this result to greater 
adoption of robots and other automation technologies in ageing soci­
eties. Deng et al. (2023) report a positive association between shortages 
of unskilled workers and robot adoption in the early stages of robot­
isation in Japan. Kushida (2024) shows that Japan’s demographics are 
shaping technological trajectories of automation and worker skills 
augmentation. Among OECD countries, Japan and Korea stand out as 
rapidly ageing societies with significant investments in robotics. Japan 
ranked fifth in the world for robot density in the manufacturing industry 
in 2023, behind Korea, China, Singapore and Germany (International 
Federation of Robotics, 2024). Chen and Liu (2025) employ industry-

Robotisation is generally found to raise both employment and pro­
ductivity in Japan. Dekle (2020) finds that over the period 1979–2012, 
robotisation had a negligible labour displacement effect, a positive 
impact on the productivity of older workers, part-time employees and 
women within industries, and a positive general equilibrium effect on 
employment and output, as productivity gains lifted aggregate demand. 
Lee et al. (2020) argue that Information and communication technology 
(ICT) investments can improve productivity and extend working lives. 
They show that ageing has a positive impact on labour productivity in 
Japan and Korea when older workers are working in ICT-intensive 
industries.
In many OECD countries, the share of expenditure on services tends 
to increase with consumer age. However, most services are more diffi­
cult to automate than the production of goods, resulting in weaker 
productivity improvements in services (Sorbe et al., 2018). Studies on 
the impact of automation on employment and growth in services are 
scarce. Lee et al. (2025) find that robot adoption in Japanese nursing 
homes is associated with higher employment (especially of non-regular 
workers), better staff retention, improved quality of care and higher 
productivity. The positive relation between robot adoption and pro­
ductivity can result from the ability of workers to devote more time to 
caregiving tasks, improving the quality of services. Although nursing 
homes represent a specific case, their experience suggests that robot 
adoption can help alleviate labour shortages, while improving working 
conditions and quality in the service sector. Similar opportunities may 
exist in other sectors, as labour shortages seem to have eroded service 
quality in parcel delivery, hospitals, restaurants, elementary and junior 
high school education, convenience stores, government services and 
taxis (Morikawa, 2018). Other OECD countries, many of which are 
facing severe labour shortages in sectors characterised by low pay and 
strenuous working conditions, such as accommodation, health and so­
cial care, transportation and storage (Causa et al., 2025), could also 
benefit from robotisation, even though cultural attitudes towards robots 
may make adoption more difficult than in Japan.
New developments in technology, including Artificial Intelligence 
(AI), may offer further opportunities for automation. A 2019 survey 
found that more than a quarter of Japanese non-manufacturing firms 
with more than 50 employees intended to use robots in future business 
and that more than a third intended to use AI and big data (Morikawa, 
2020). The survey also shows a positive relation between the use of big 
data and AI and the share of highly educated workers, a relation which is 
absent for industrial robots in the manufacturing industry. This 
complementarity highlights the importance of investment in skills for 
the adoption of technology in the service sector. However, despite 
widespread intention to integrate AI in production processes, actual AI 
adoption rates remain relatively low. According to a survey by the OECD 
Global Forum on Productivity, Japan ranks below the OECD average in 
terms of firm-level AI adoption rates (Filippucci et al., 2025a). Similarly, 
a survey conducted by Japan’s Ministry of Internal Affairs and Com­
munications reports that regular AI use by businesses in Japan is only 
about a third of that in US companies (MIC, 2024). Japan’s compara­
tively slow uptake may reflect its economic structure, which is skewed 
toward manufacturing sectors where the latest generation of AI tech­
nology, building on large language models (LLMs), is less readily 
applicable than in knowledge-intensive services. Fig. 11 shows the 
value-added shares of the five most AI-exposed industries across the G7 
economies, highlighting Japan’s relatively lower exposure to generative 
AI. Even though Japan will benefit from its strong capability in robotics 
as physical AI (which allows machines to autonomously perform com­
plex tasks in the real world) progresses, its ability to fully capture pro­
ductivity gains may be lower than in countries like Germany or Korea, as 
lower wages reduce adoption incentives (Kajita, 2025).
Sectoral composition of consumption
Consumption generally exhibits a hump-shaped profile over the life 
cycle, largely reflecting developments in income. However, demand for 
different types of goods and services show specific patterns, which af­
fects aggregate productivity, as productivity varies widely across sec­
tors. Older households may spend a larger fraction of their income on 
services with lower potential for productivity growth, such as health 
care. Population ageing may therefore drag down productivity growth 
by shifting economic activity towards less productive sectors. In the 
United States, older households have higher health-related expenditure 
than younger ones. They also spend more on household furnishing and 
operations and utilities. Conversely, they have lower vehicle-related 
expenditure and spend less on education, clothing, food consumed 
away from home and recreation (Nie and Gautam, 2019). Cravino et al. 
(2022) estimate that population ageing in the United States accounts for 
about one fifth of the observed increase in the service share in con­
sumption between 1982 and 2016. The age differences in consumption 
patterns are relatively similar in the euro area. Older households spend 
more on health care, even though household health care spending is 
much lower than in the United States, which is largely due to wider 
social protection. They spend more on housing and utilities (bundled 
together), but less on transport and clothing (Nerlich and Schroth, 
2018).
In Japan, the main categories of goods and services whose con­
sumption increases with age are utility charges, medical care and 
furniture, while spending on housing, clothing and footwear decreases 
with age. Consumption of food, transport and communication, educa­
tion, culture and recreation exhibit a hump-shaped pattern over the life 
cycle (Kitao and Yamada, 2025).6 Hence, although data are not fully 
comparable as they come from different sources, the consumption pat­
terns at different ages look quite similar in the United States, the euro 
area and Japan, at least if we exclude health-related expenses because of 
differences in financing sources across countries. The consumption of in- 
kind services provided by the government sector, such as health and 
education, is recorded as government individual consumption in the

Increased government spending on pensions and health
Public social spending in Japan rose from less than 14% of GDP in 
1998 to nearly 23% in 2019, when pensions, health and long-term care 
accounted for 84% of the total (Fig. 12).7 Over the same period, current 
government spending and revenue increased by respectively about 
seven and six percent of GDP. Hence, age-related costs account for a 
large part of the deterioration in the Japanese fiscal position. The in­
crease in public social spending was much larger in Japan than in the 
United States and the euro area, which is consistent with a faster ageing 
population, although other societal, economic and policy factors also 
affect these outcomes. Total public social spending is now higher in 
Japan than in the United States, although still lower than in the euro 
area. Beyond different ageing profiles, this is partly due to the role of 
private pension and health insurance schemes in the United States. Weak 
economic conditions and deflation have hampered reforms that would 
have improved the sustainability of the Japanese pension system, as the 
price indexation of benefits and a system to adjust benefits in line with 
changes in the number of contributors and life expectancy introduced in 
2004 were not fully implemented (Hosen, 2010; Aoki, 2013; Jones and 
Seitani, 2019a). Nevertheless, Japanese pension replacement rates 
remain relatively low by OECD standards (OECD, 2023b).
Health care expenditures are relatively well contained in regard of 
the country’s older population and outstanding health outcomes. Total 
health care spending as a percentage of GDP, including private sources 
of financing, was much lower in Japan (11%) than in the United States 
(16.6%) and close to the euro area level (10.2%) in 2019, even though 
some inefficiencies in the health system persist, such as heavy reliance 
on hospital care, large spending on pharmaceuticals and a high number 
of consultations, which may be in part supply induced (Jones and Sei­
tani, 2019a). At the same time, life expectancy is the longest in the OECD 
and avoidable mortality rates are among the lowest (OECD, 2023a). 
Although ageing typically accounts for only about a third of health 
spending increases in OECD countries (Lorenzoni et al., 2019), many 
countries may struggle to contain health care costs while maintaining 
quality as their populations age.
In Japan, the introduction in 2000 of long-term care insurance to 
which all persons aged 40 and over contribute helps finance a steep rise 
in spending mainly driven by the number of long-term care users, as the 
number of individuals aged over 75 increases. As of 2020, only eight 
other OECD countries collected income-based contributions for long- 
term care, the others mainly relying on taxation and state budget allo­
cations (OECD, 2020b). However, containing the rise in long-term care 
spending in Japan would also require enhancing efficiency, which could 
be achieved through better targeting towards care needs, as opposed to 
auxiliary services, increasing co-payments and moving the responsibility 
for long-term care insurance from municipalities to prefectures to 
improve service delivery and risk pooling (Jones and Seitani, 2019a).
In addition to pushing up public spending, ageing may weaken tax 
revenue through the erosion of tax bases as income, consumption and 
employment stagnate or decline. However, in Japan this effect has been 
offset by increases in social contribution and sales tax rates. Moreover, 
despite the lower income of older people, personal income tax revenue 
has remained broadly constant as a share of GDP since the mid-1990s, 
due to tax reforms in 2005–2006 (Yashio and Hachisuka, 2014). Alto­
gether, the increase in social spending was the main contributor to the 
government deficits run by Japan over the past three decades, which 
brought gross government debt close to 240% of GDP at end-2023. Low 
interest rates, reflecting the relatively conservative preferences of Jap­
anese investors, who hold a sizeable share of outstanding government 
bonds, as well as accommodative monetary policy, have allowed rela­
tively low-cost debt financing. As a result of quantitative easing, the 
Bank of Japan is the central bank holding the highest nominal amount 
and the largest share of national government debt (over 50%) in the 
world. Unconventional monetary policy, implemented to fight deflation 
and support economic activity, also considerably alleviated the gov­
ernment debt burden, contributing to public debt sustainability 
(Alberola et al., 2023). OECD countries would likely face much greater 
financing challenges with similar government debt levels and higher 
interest rates may crowd-out productive investment and weaken pro­
ductivity growth.
Concluding remarks
Japan’s working-age population has declined since 1998, well ahead 
of other OECD countries. Overall, Japan has coped relatively well with 
these demographic headwinds. The impact of ageing on labour supply 
has been mitigated by rising employment rates among women and older 
workers. Similar trends, even though on average weaker, are observed in 
other OECD countries, suggesting that higher employment rates could 
partly offset the impact of ageing on labour supply in these countries 
over the coming years.8 One driver of prolonged working lives in Japan 
is healthy ageing, which requires attention in other countries. Immi­
gration to Japan has remained limited, despite some government ini­
tiatives to loosen restrictions, particularly for skilled workers. While 
employment among older workers has risen in Japan, it is often in non- 
regular jobs, reflecting labour market practices, such as mandatory 
retirement and seniority wages, and insufficient investment in lifelong 
learning, which has prevented older workers from using their full po­
tential. Similarly, many women work in non-regular jobs.
Beyond raising employment rates, other countries will need to pay 
attention to the quality and productivity of jobs. Japan’s labour pro­
ductivity growth has been lacklustre since 1998, though not excep­
tionally low in international perspective, especially considering the 
major financial crisis that occurred in the early 1990s. Ageing has led to 
the adoption of some labour-saving technologies, notably robots. 
Nevertheless, while Japan’s overall productivity growth since 1998 has 
been comparable to that of the European Union, it has lagged that of the 
United States. The strong US productivity performance is largely related 
to advantages in digital technologies. According to Draghi (2024), the 
productivity gap between the European Union and the United States is 
largely explained by the tech sector. Recent estimates suggest that an 
increase of Japanese software and intangible assets to US levels would 
close approximately half of the productivity gap between Japan and the 
United States (Oiwa, 2025).
Several interrelated factors are likely to have hampered digital­
isation in Japan. Shibata and Lechevalier (2025) point to significant 
institutional shifts in Japan’s financial system and labour practices since 
the early 1990s, which have contributed to a lack of investment in res­
killing and upskilling and to a growing digital divide between large firms 
and SMEs. Ageing has compounded these obstacles by exacerbating la­
bour and skills shortages and reducing business dynamism and
