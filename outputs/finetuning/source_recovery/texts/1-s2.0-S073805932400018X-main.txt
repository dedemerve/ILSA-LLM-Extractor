Global competence has gained considerable attention in education within a diverse and globalized world.
Drawing from the OECD PISA 2018 framework for students’ global citizenship, we employ hierarchical linear
modelling to examine the effects of individual attributes, family background, and school characteristics on
students’ global competence in six countries/regions. The results reveal that gender, student self-efficacy in
global issues, familial socioeconomic status, and teachers’ intercultural attitudes significantly influence students’
global competence in all six areas. Moreover, region-specific differences were found. The study provides valuable
insights for advancing research, furthering the conceptualization and implementation of education for global
competence.

1. Introduction
Since the beginning of the 21st century, global competence has
gained significant attention in the educational endeavors of international organizations and countries seeking to determine the direction of
sustainable development education within the context of globalization
(e.g., Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD,
2016, 2018; United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO), 2013, 2015). Considerable efforts have been made
to imagine the younger generations’ global competence in an interconnected and diverse world, in response to the pressing need for
transforming education in the face of global changes (e.g., Mansilla and
Jackson, 2022; Wintersteiner et al., 2015). While these studies have
offered valuable insights on global competence education, they have
primarily focused on framing the conceptual aspects of global competence and debating potential issues rather than delving into how adolescents develop their global competence in real-life contexts. A gap
remains in our comprehension of the interconnections within this
developmental process.
Students are involved in a complex web of interactions between
themselves, the school, and the family, wherein any compromise has an
explicit or implicit impact on their development as global citizens

(Woods and Kong, 2020; Yemini and Maxwell, 2020). Although studies
have demonstrated the importance of specific factors on students’ global
competence within particular regions (Baker et al., 2022; Jang et al.,
2023; Solhaug and Kristensen, 2020), a comprehensive understanding of
the effects of individual, family, and school-related factors in predicting
students’ global competence is lacking. Specifically, most studies in this
field have primarily concentrated on factors associated with schools and
pedagogy concerning global competence while neglecting individual
differences and family backgrounds. As a result, we can only see a partial
understanding of factors related to students’ global competence.
To fill this gap, this study explores the influence of individual attributes, family background, and school context on students’ global
competence using data from six countries/regions in the OECD Program
for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2018. Additionally,
considering the potential for global competence to exhibit variations
across diverse national settings, influenced by local governance and
historical culture (Goren and Yemini, 2017a), this study aimed to provide insights into and elucidate potential commonalities and variations
in these factors’ impacts on student global competence across the six
countries/regions under investigation.

2. Literature review
2.1. Global competence
Against the backdrop of globalization and educational transformation, the past two decades have witnessed an increasing interest in
introducing “the global” into schooling in response to current global
challenges. This has resulted in a vast body of empirical and theoretical
scholarship (Goren and Yemini, 2017a; Pashby et al., 2021), including
related conceptual frameworks proposed by international organizations,
non-governmental organizations, and governments (e.g., Oxfam, 2006;
UNESCO, 2015). In 2014, the OECD introduced “global competence” to
the 2018 cycle of PISA data collection and, subsequently, released two
reports to outline the underpinning model and assessment strategy (cf.
OECD, 2016, 2018). In the 2018 report, global competence was defined

2.2.2. Family background and student global competence
Previous research has indicated that immigrant experience, family
socioeconomic status (SES) and parents’ global attitudes—the primary
purveyors of global competence education—hold an indispensable role
in shaping students’ global competence (e.g., Baker et al., 2022; Cao and
Meng, 2020; Žegunienė, 2020).
First, per the existing literature, immigrant experience positively
correlates with higher levels of global competence (Baker et al., 2022;
Chui and Leung, 2014). Immigrant students often possess greater
intercultural experience and involvement in racial issues (Dyrness,
2023; Parejo-Llano et al., 2020). This might contribute to their enhanced
intercultural and global awareness.
Second, family SES is a comprehensive notion that attempts to capture the financial, social, cultural, and human capital resources accessible to students. Within the PISA framework, a student’s socioeconomic
status is assessed through the Economic, Cultural, and Social Status
(ESCS) index—a composite of various factors pertaining to students’
family backgrounds, which are divided into the following three categories: parents’ education, parents’ occupation, and the index of home
possessions (Avvisati, 2020; OECD, 2019). Myers-Walls and Somlai
(2019) note the positive impact of SES on young people’s attitudes and
engagement with global issues and indicate that parents with higher
levels of education exhibit more positive childrearing practices for
global citizenship. Moreover, students hailing from advantaged families
are more prone to engaging in activities such as study abroad, international service learning, and study tours (Allan, Charles, 2015; Cheng and
Yang, 2019; Kohel, 2016). These experiences are widely regarded as
valuable means to reduce stereotypical thinking and racial attitudes and
enhance multicultural awareness (Huang et al., 2023; Schenker, 2019).
Third, recent evidence suggests a significant correlation between
parents’ attitudes toward global issues and different cultures and students’ global competence (Jang et al., 2023; Žegunienė, 2020). The
manner whereby families impart information and viewpoints regarding
the world to their children profoundly influences students’ perception of
others (Myers-Walls and Somlai, 2019).

in open, appropriate and effective interactions with people from different

Further, OECD (2018) outlines the assessment approaches, which
include a cognitive test to assess 15-year-olds’ knowledge and skills as
well as a student questionnaire to evaluate relevant knowledge, cognitive skills, social skills, and attitudes.
In the scholarly discourse on global competence and related but
distinct concepts, it is vital to highlight the highly debated nature of the
global competence concept within this field (Sälzer and Roczen, 2018).
Pashby et al. (2021)’s mapping of global citizenship discourse—from
which global competence emerged—indicates three competing conceptualizations, as follows: The neoliberal type refers to a technicist
economic instrumentalist agenda and narrative; the liberal type encapsulates traditional notions of cosmopolitanism and highlights common
humanity; and the critical type draws on the critique of global power
structures, modernization, and epistemological violence.
While the notion of global competence presented by the OECD PISA
2018 has faced criticism for its perceived neoliberal orientation and its
foundation in a US-based social imagery (Auld and Morris, 2020; Robertson, 2021; Martini and Robertson, 2022), we recognize the OECD’s
efforts in introducing a global competence framework and the value of
the assessments based on it. The PISA 2018 provides a comprehensive
perspective on young people’s development of global competence. The
cognitive test and the questionnaire items offer valuable insights into the
preparation of families, teachers, and schools for cultivating adolescents’ global competence, thereby enabling cross-country comparisons
and in-depth national analyses (e.g., The Scottish Government, 2020).
The PISA 2018 dataset serves as an accessible resource for empirical
investigations and further discussions regarding the implementation of
global competence education.

2.2.3. School context and student global competence
School context as an immediate environment is emphasized as a
catalyst for enhancing students’ global competence (J. Hu and J. Hu,
2021; X. Hu and X. Hu, 2021; Tormey and Gleeson, 2012). Hameed
(2020) highlights the variability in the practical implementation of education for global competence, wherein factors such as curriculum,
available resources, school culture, the values of school leaders and
teachers, as well as the allocation of human and financial resources, all
might influence a school’s engagement with global competence.
A developmentally appropriate curriculum—along with relevant
activities, practices, and a conducive atmosphere within the school—are
pivotal for nurturing students’ global competence (Torres, 2019).

2.2. The effects of individual attributes, family background, and school
context on students’ global competence

global competence has identified gender and self-efficacy as pivotal
factors in determining their global competence (e.g., Jang et al., 2023;
Karvonen et al., 2023; Tormey and Gleeson, 2012).

regional variations, as noted by existing studies. Research conducted in
Ireland (Tormey and Gleeson, 2012), Denmark, Norway (Solhaug and

Studies by Yemini and Furstenburg (2018) and Lasten et al. (2022)
demonstrate that international schools and private schools exhibit
higher ability to provide the necessary conditions for fostering global
citizenship than local public schools. Moreover, various studies have
demonstrated the positive effects of specific global courses and pedagogical approaches—such as international school partnerships (Edge
and Khamsi, 2012), intercultural online courses (Jackson, 2016), and
game-like learning environments (Lim, 2008)—on students’ global
competence.
Additionally, teachers’ intercultural understanding and perceptions
of global issues are regarded as contributing to cultivating students’
global competence across diverse cultures and institutional contexts
(Carter, 2020; Kerkhoff and Cloud, 2020; Khoo, 2022; Steele and Leming, 2022; Van Werven et al., 2023). Increased emphasis on the value of
global citizenship by teachers in the classroom results in greater student
interest in other cultures and global issues (Aydin et al., 2019). Drawing
on data from students who interacted with international teachers,
Ganley et al. (2019) note that students’ geographical knowledge
increased after exposure to international teachers, indicating that
interaction with international teachers is associated with heightened
receptivity and interest in non-U.S. nations or cultures among elementary and middle school students.
As Woods and Kong (2020) suggest, the intricate interplay among
schools, parents, and students can present challenges that complicate
the development of both students and citizens. Most prior studies have
tended to concentrate on one dimension or single factors without concurrent consideration of variables at multiple levels. Moreover,
acknowledging that global competence, as a phenomenon, can vary
across national and cultural contexts owing to the influence of political
and historical issues, is essential (Goren and Yemini, 2017a). Thus, this
study delves into the factors impacting students’ global competence,
encompassing individual, family, and school-related dimensions—striving for an all-encompassing overview as well as providing distinct
key findings tailored to each data source location.

The study included samples from Chile, Korea, Croatia, Hong Kong,
Malta, and Panama, as part of the PISA 2018 assessments, for three
primary reasons, as follows: First, the global competence cognitive test
was administered in 27 countries/economies during the PISA 2018.
However, only these six completed the full set of student, parent, and
school questionnaires, providing comprehensive data on personal, familial, and school-related factors. Second, prior research on global
competence has predominantly focused on the USA and UK, with limited
attention to regions such as other European countries, South and Central
American countries, and Asian countries (Goren and Yemini, 2017a).
These seemingly overlooked cultures and societies equally form an
indispensable part of the diversity in the global competence realm. A
focus on these societies can be a major step forward in our comprehension of the development of student global competence. Third, by
including samples from six countries/economies rather than limiting the
study to one region, we offer a broader perspective for understanding
students’ global competence. This approach allows for cross-country
comparisons, enhancing our awareness of global competence differences and similarities across diverse cultures and societies.

3. Data analysis
3.1. Data and samples
The data used herein were obtained from the PISA 2018, an international assessment that measured 15-year-old students’ reading,
mathematics, and science literacy in 79 countries and economies. About
600,000 students participated in the PISA 2018, representing approximately 32 million 15-year-olds from the schools of 79 participating
countries/economies. The PISA 2018 assessed an innovation domain,
global competence, defined as the multidimensional capacity needed to
live in our interconnected and changing world (OECD, 2020).
In the PISA 2018, global competence was defined as a multidimensional capacity that encompasses the ability to “examine issues
of local, global and cultural significance; understand and appreciate the
perspectives and worldviews of others; engage in open, appropriate and
effective interactions across cultures; and take action for collective wellbeing and sustainable development” (OECD, 2020, p.17). The PISA 2018

The dependent variable was the global competence of 15-year-old

were computed for each student. The PISA 2018 global competence

Individual attribute and family background variables were derived from

Some measures were derived using the item response theory scaling
methods. The weighted likelihood estimates of these derived variables
were used as scores for individual participants and transformed into a
scale with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one in OECD
countries, with equal weights for each country (OECD, 2017; Zhang,

Individual attributes variables included student gender and selfefficacy regarding global issues. Gender was a dichotomous variable,
comprising female and male students. Student self-efficacy regarding
global issues measured their ability to explain or discuss global issues
using six items, such as “How easy do you think it would be for you to
perform the following tasks on your own? Explain how economic crises
in single countries affect the global economy.” This scale was rated on a
4-point Likert-type ranging from 1 (I could not do this) to 4 (I could do
this easily).
The family background variables involve immigration status,

parents’ global awareness, parents’ attitudes toward immigrants, parents’ interest in learning about other cultures, and family ESCS.
Immigration status was a dichotomous variable comprising native and
immigrant. Parents’ global awareness measured their awareness of
global issues using six items, such as “How informed are you about the
following topics? Climate change and global warming.” This scale was
rated on a 4-point Likert-type ranging from 1 (I have never heard of this)
to 4 (I am familiar with this and I would be able to explain this well).
Parents’ attitudes toward immigrants were measured using four items,
such as “People are increasingly moving from one country to another.
How much do you agree with the following statements about immigrants? Immigrant children should have the same opportunities for education that other children in the country have.” This scale was rated on
a 4-point Likert-type ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly
agree).
Parents’ interest in learning about other cultures was measured by four
items, such as “How well does each of the following statements below
describe you? I want to learn how people live in different countries.”
This scale was rated on a 4-point Likert-type ranging from 1 (not at all
like me) to 4 (very much like me).
Family ESCS in PISA comprised responses provided by students,
collecting data on parental occupation and education, learning resources available at home, and cultural possessions.
The school context variables encompassed school type, attention to
global competence in the curriculum, teachers’ intercultural attitudes,
the content of global competence activities at school, and approaches to
global competence activities at school.
School type was a dichotomous variable, comprising public and private schools.
Attention to global competence in the curriculum measured whether
global competence was included in the formal curriculum to assess its
presence using six items, such as “Is there any formal curriculum for
communicating with people from different cultures or countries in
<national modal grade for 15-year-olds> .”
Teachers’ intercultural attitudes were measured using four items, such
as “Thinking about teachers in your school: To how many of them do the
following statements apply? They have misconceptions about the history of some cultural groups.” This scale was rated on a 4-point Likerttype ranging from 1 (to none or almost none of them) to 4 (to all or
almost all of them).
The content of global competence activities at school focused on the
content that global competence students learned at school and was
measured by five items, such as “I learn about the interconnectedness of
countries’ economies.” Additionally, approaches to global competence
activities at school emphasized students’ methods of learning about
global competence at school among students and was measured using
five items, such as “We read newspapers, look for news on the internet or
watch the news together during classes”.

a complex sampling design. In PISA 2018, student-level data were
clustered within the country-level data. HLM has statistical advantages
over traditional regression methods, such as removing aggregate bias
and underestimating standard errors (Raudenbush and Bryk, 2002).
Thus, the HLM was employed to analyze the relationship between
different student- and school-level variables and student global
competence. Several different approaches for using and scaling sampling weights in hierarchical models have been proposed. Furthermore,

4.1. Effects of individual attributes on student global competence
4.1.1. Gender
The results in Table 3 show that student gender and self-efficacy
toward global issues have significant effects on students’ global
competence. Male students had significantly lower global competence
scores than female students in all the six countries/regions (Chile, β =
− 9.81; Korea, β = − 15.25; Hong Kong, β = − 10.02; Croatia, β = − 9.91;
Panama, β = − 7.26; Malta, β = − 21.97). This finding is consistent with
Jang et al. (2023)’s analysis based on the data from Korea. Kim et al.
(2022)’s investigation of global leadership-related competences of
youths from the Association of Southeast Asian Nations and Korea also
shows that female participants are more likely to exhibit an increase in
global competence in the context of online international exchange
activities.
4.1.2. Self-efficacy regarding global issues
Additionally, students’ self-efficacy regarding global issues had a
significant positive effect on their global competence in all six countries/
regions (Chile, β = 11.86; Korea, β = 19.14; Hong Kong, β = 17.86;
Croatia, β = 14.23; Panama, β = 10.62; Malta, β = 22.90). Self-efficacy
refers to an individual’s perceived capacity in certain domains (Bandura,
1997; OECD, 2019; Van Dinther et al., 2011). Numerous studies have
shown that students’ self-efficacy influences their academic performance and learning behaviors in educational settings (Schunk, 2003;
Schunk and Usher, 2012). In the PISA 2018, self-efficacy in global issues
involved students’ perceived capacity in global cognition and knowledge, such as whether they could “explain how carbon-dioxide emissions affect global climate change” and “discuss the different reasons
why people become refugees” (OECD, 2018; Schunk and DiBenedetto,
2016). Such positive effects of self-efficacy on global issues indicate that
the greater the belief about one’s knowledge of global issues, the higher
the score gained in actual global cognitive tests.

weights were used to adjust for the oversampling of schools with underrepresented minority students (Bhutoria and Aljabri, 2022). Missing
data in the PISA assessments can be observed at random, as students
were randomly assigned testlets based on the PISA assessments designs
(Hu and Yu, 2021). Multiple imputation was used to impute missing data
in our datasets for high precision, rather than simple listwise deletion.

identities (Wu et al., 2020). By contrast, in South Korea, multicultural
families—characterized by one Korean spouse and one immigrant
spouse, predominantly wives—are considerably prevalent. Multiculturalism is of great importance for social harmony and stability,
potentially resulting in greater discussions about other cultures within
Korean families (Olneck, 2011).

4.2.2. Parents’ attitudes, interest, and awareness
Parents’ attitudes toward immigrants had significant positive effects
on children’s global competence scores in both Chile (β = 2.35) and
Hong Kong (β = 8.32). Parents’ interests in other cultures had positive
effects on students’ global competence in Korea (β = 6.58), Croatia (β =
2.33), and Malta (β = 4.24). Parents’ global awareness had significant
positive effects on students’ global competence in both Panama (β =
4.90) and Malta (β = 2.80). Considering these findings in conjunction
with the strong correlation between global competence and ESCS
mentioned above, it seems that tangible financial, material, and
resource-related aspects of households on students’ global knowledge
and understanding is more direct and stable. By contrast, imparting
parents’ global attitudes and awareness in their children is a more
complex endeavor without definite outcomes. In fact, instead of
engaging in parents–children conversations about global and multicultural issues, there are evidence showing families are inclined to nurture
their children’s global awareness by sending them abroad for education
and travel experiences (Myers-Walls and Somlai, 2019; Žegunienė,

inadequate teacher feedback and explanations during global competence activities. In the questionnaire, students were surveyed about their
participation in school-based global competence activities (OECD,

viewpoints, participating in cultural events, and engaging in discussions
with classmates. These activities are typically student-driven. However,
comprehending intricate global issues without sufficient teacher guidance and explanations can be challenging for students (Brown, 2018),
especially given the high-quality and complex curricula and activities
focused on global education within school systems (McNaughton, 2012).
Hence, the development of students’ global competence does not solely
rely on their engagement in self-directed learning activities; the crucial
factor is the guiding and instructive role of teachers in these activities.

4.2.3. Family ESCS
Family ESCS had significant positive effects on students’ global
competence in all six countries/regions (Chile, β = 8.95; Korea, β =
11.83; Hong Kong, β = 4.11; Croatia, β = 9.34; Panama, β = 4.98; Malta,
β = 17.37)—consistent with previous studies (e.g., Allan, Charles, 2015;
Myers-Walls and Somlai, 2019). Cao and Meng (2020) revealed that
students’ international direct contact was positively related to both
bonding and bridging social capital, through the mediators of global
skills and attitudes. In affluent families, social capitals and additional
resources could be utilized to assist students in gaining exposure to and
understanding of diverse cultures, as well as interacting with individuals
from varied backgrounds (Kormos et al., 2014; Wilson and Dalton,
1997).
4.3. Effects of school context on student global competence

5. Conclusion and implications

4.3.1. School type
A notable disparity was observed in global competence between
students in private and public schools in Chile, Panama, Malta, and
Croatia, with private school students exhibiting significantly higher
global competence. By contrast, no significant difference in global
competence was observed between public and private schools in South
Korea and Hong Kong. These regional distinctions may be attributed to
school choice systems influenced by market dynamics (Bellei, 2007).
Under the market-driven selection mechanism, students from more
affluent socioeconomic backgrounds tend to opt for private schools.
Compared to public schools, private schools often provide students with
enhanced opportunities for international interaction and engagement
(Lasten et al., 2022). Hence, when no significant performance gaps exist
between students in public and private schools within a region, particularly on global proficiency indicators, the overall schooling system is
favorably and likely to be more equitable (Goren and Yemini, 2017b).

Examining the factors contributing to student’s global competence,
the hierarchical linear modelling analysis based on the PISA 2018
datasets indicated that students’ gender and their self-efficacy in global
issues, ESCS, and teachers’ intercultural attitudes had widely significant
effects on students’ global competence in Chile, Korea, Croatia, Hong
Kong, Malta, and Panama. Further, the results revealed remarkable
regional differences in terms of the effect of different factors on students’
global competence among these countries/regions.
The findings provide insights for educators and researchers, particularly regarding equal and balanced social, family, and school conditions that contribute to education for global competence. On the one
hand, this study—rooted in the OECD PISA 2018 framework for global
competence—offers valuable insights for enhancing global competence
in school education. It highlights the significant positive impact of
teachers’ multicultural awareness and global consciousness on students,
emphasizing the need for comprehensive teacher training in global
competence. Furthermore, the research indicates that schools should
offer students a diverse range of global topics; student-driven learning
formats are insufficient, and the implementation of global-related activities necessitates professional guidance from teachers. On the other
hand, from a theoretical standpoint, particularly when viewed through
the lens of critical scholarship, the findings offer supplementary evidence of neoliberal inclinations within the PISA framework for global
competence (Cobb and Couch, 2022; Engel et al., 2019). Notably, it
reveals a strong correlation between students’ global competence and
their family’s ESCS in the six countries/regions and a higher performance of private school students than their peers in public schools in
Chile, Panama, Malta, and Croatia. Moreover, the distinctive characteristics of each of the six areas underscore the challenge of proposing a
one-size-fits-all educational strategy or model for global competence.
The findings highlight the importance of region-specific, context-aware
recommendations that respect the unique social, political, and historical
contexts of each locale in terms of education for global competence.
Some study limitations should be acknowledged. First, the scope of
this study was limited in terms of the conceptualization of global
competence. It is imperative to acknowledge that global competence is
“a relatively young construct” (Sälzer and Roczen, 2018, p.7). Although
global competence differs with other notions—such as intercultural
education, global awareness, global citizenship—in terms of underpinning value assumptions, convergences in their lineages exist invariably
(Mannion et al., 2011; Priestley et al., 2010). This study reviews existing
research using a broad sense of this notion in the literature review; incorporates literature on intercultural education, global citizenship education, and education for global awareness; and then generated the
hypothesis of the factors of personal attribute, family background, and
school context that might affect individual’s global competence. However, we relied on the data provided by the OECD PISA 2018 as the
benchmark for examining factors related to global competence. Consequently, the pedagogical implications derived can only hold relevance

4.3.2. Teachers’ intercultural attitudes
The findings underscored that among the school-level factors,
teachers’ intercultural attitudes exerted positive influence on students’
global competence (Chile, β = 32.91; Korea, β = 23.61; Hong Kong, β =
29.50; Croatia, β = 18.14; Panama, β = 19.97; Malta, β = 30.77).
Although some studies have highlighted the important role of various
school environments, curricula, and activities in students’ global
competence (Hameed, 2020; Tormey and Gleeson, 2012), we found that,
among all school-level variables, teachers’ intercultural attitudes had
significantly positive effects on student global competence. This could
be attributed to the fact that students derive advantages from the
globally relevant attitudes and behaviors performed by their teachers
(Aydin et al., 2019). Teachers possessing a comprehensive understanding of the history of specific cultural groups are more inclined to engage
in classroom discussions about global issues and impart their attitudes to
students—either consciously or subconsciously (Ganley et al., 2019).
4.3.3. Content and approaches to global competence activities at school
Additionally, school global activities contents had significant positive effects on students’ global competence in Korea (β = 18.41) and
Malta (β = 20.10). This is consistent with the conclusions drawn by
Tormey and Gleeson (2012) and Hameed (2020), who emphasize the
pivotal role of the school context in nurturing students’ global competence. The result showed that offering multicultural and global-issue
focused activities at schools, particularly those connected to global
economic development, various cultures, and environmental protection,
can improve the global competence of students. This finding suggests
that schools and teachers should prioritize the inclusion of diverse global
topics in their activities.
Surprisingly, the study revealed an unexpected negative relation
between school global activities’ approaches and students’ global
competence in all six countries/regions (Chile, β = − 31.30; Korea, β =
− 49.94; Hong Kong, β = − 16.61; Croatia, β = − 26.10; Panama, β =

within the confines of the PISA 2018’s value assumptions and conceptual frameworks.
Second, this study employed a correlational methodology rather
than a causal one. We investigated the impacts of various types of variables on students’ global competence and offered interpretations of the
observed correlations grounded in existing research. Nevertheless, the
study did not establish definite evidence of causality.
Third, we refrained from delving deeper into the unique cultural
characteristics of each region due to limited space. We only focused on
six countries or regions from the Americas, Europe, and Asia, excluding
countries that have a long history of discussion in the global competence
field, such as the United States and the United Kingdom, due to the
limited datasets.
We believe that the OECD’s decision to assess global competence as
an innovative domain in the PISA 2018 is a worthwhile initiative. As
demonstrated here, the study offers valuable evidence for further examination and reflection. These results can serve as references for subsequent cross-national comparative research. We also welcome critical
viewpoints based on the findings of this research. Continuing to enrich
the discourse surrounding global competence is an important endeavor.
Giving more attention to specific countries while considering their
unique social, political, and historical contexts would be a valuable
endeavor as well. For instance, as discussed earlier, the signified of
“immigrant” can vary significantly in different national contexts, and as
such, the approach to educating about immigration awareness and attitudes should also be tailored to these distinctions. Pursuing these
research directions can yield substantial insights to advance the development of global competence education customized for local contexts.
