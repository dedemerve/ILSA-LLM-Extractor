This study investigated the professional traits of mathematics teachers that affected mathematical
achievement for fourth-graders in the TIMSS 2015 study, including teaching experience, degree of
educational qualification, teacher specialization, and amount of professional development, all
broken down further by gender. This investigation compared TIMSS 2015 results of students in
four countries (Singapore, Hong Kong, Japan, and Saudi Arabia) and used a causal-comparative
design with data available through the IEA website. The findings indicated that the traits of
professional development, specialization, educational qualification, and teaching experience
among mathematics teachers were found to have various levels of impact on the achievement of
fourth-grade students in the participating countries, as will be stated in the discussion of the
results.

1. Introduction
Many countries seek indicators of the quality of their educational systems and utilize several quantitative and qualitative methods
for that. Evaluation studies are one of the methods helpful in making judgments about the quality of educational systems, using indicators to describe the inputs, contexts, and outputs of educational systems. However, many studies conducted by professionals in this
context do not provide sufficient indicators for educational policymakers, often due to small sample size, inadequate tools, or limited
capabilities.
Large scale international evaluation studies are the most important tools adopted by some developed countries because they
provide high-quality data that support education reform. They also provide data that identify student performance levels that can be
compared to counterparts in countries with similar or different levels of development. These evaluation studies aim to assess the
quality of an educational system intended to prepare students for the future by linking research, policy, and practice. They are based on
the idea of "opportunity to learn," and explore the links between the intended curriculum (what the policy requires), the implemented
curriculum (what the school teaches), and the achieved curriculum (what the student learns) (IEA, 2013). These studies also depend on

the fact that judging the suitability of an educational system should be based on data that go beyond indicators proving the integrity of
the educational system only, to the factors affecting its results (Al-Mikhlafi, 2010).
The Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) is considered one of the most famous and wide-ranging international evaluation studies, providing the largest database of student achievement in mathematics and science and the traits of their
teachers and schools. TIMSS is supervised by the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) and is
administered every four years. It aims to provide educational policymakers with high-quality periodic data, based on international
scientific standards, that increase their understanding of school and non-school factors that affect teaching and learning in an international context (Mullis, 2003), and that help them diagnose the strengths and weaknesses of their educational systems. The TIMSS
report compares student achievement in mathematics and science for the fourth and eighth grades for all participating countries, in
addition to information related to curricula, educational programs, and the traits of teachers and their professional and teaching
qualifications. This information enables educational policymakers to improve the quality of academic achievement by studying
comparisons between different educational contexts.
The TIMSS study measures mathematical achievement through a test that aims to reveal the level of mathematical achievement in
two areas, one of which focuses on mathematical content, and the other concerns the cognitive domains. As for the content area, It
defines the mathematical content branches to be evaluated. In the fourth grade, in particular, the test includes, as stated in the TIMSS
2015 assessment framework, the following (IEA, 2013):
• Numbers (representing 50%), which include: Whole numbers, fractions, decimals, expressions, simple equations, and relationships.
• Geometric shapes and measures (representing 35%), which include: points, lines, angles, and two-and three-dimensional shapes.
• Presentation of data (representing 15%), including reading, interpretation, and representing).
As for the cognitive domains, it defines the thinking processes to be evaluated, namely: knowledge (representing 40%), application
(representing 40%), and inference (representing 20%). We can say with statistical confidence that the content of the intended curriculum in most of the countries that participated in the TIMSS study, and what is reflected in the textbooks; It is generally taught in
classrooms in those countries (Schmidt, Houang, & Cogan, 2004), and that the content of the mathematics curriculum document may
not differ in any one of them from what is found in other countries (Dindyal, 2006).
The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has been a participant in TIMSS since 2003, but the performance of its students in mathematics for the
fourth and eighth grades during its successive courses (2003, 2007, 2011, 2015) was below expected levels and those students obtained
very low scores compared to the global average performance, as shown in Table 1.
Research has established that student achievement is affected by several contextual variables, such as school resources, teacher
attributes, training, students’ attitudes toward teachers, textbooks, family, school, and the social environment that surrounds them
(Skouras, 2014). Despite this multiplicity of factors affecting student achievement. the teacher and the related variables have a major
impact and many educators emphasize that the teacher is one of the resources determining the academic success of students, which
prepares them for the future (Chetty, Friedman, & Rockoff, 2014; Rivkin, Hanushek, & Kain, 2005; Rockoff, 2004). Because a teacher’s
practices and professional traits are important factors affecting student achievement (Fung et al., 2017), there has been a great
emphasis on improving teachers’ effectiveness as a way to enhance student learning and understand the “black box” of education
(Burroughs et al., 2019). Literature asserts that the traits of the teacher, including the level of academic and educational qualification,
training courses received, and years of experience, are related to the achievement of students (Rivkin et al., 2005; Swinton, Thomas,
Benjamin, & Howard, 2010). Studies also show, in general, that the variables associated with the teacher, such as gender, experience,
and academic level, have an impact on achievement in mathematics (Akyuz & Berberoglu, 2010). Internationally, high-quality
teachers are often seen as playing a key role in the good results of their students (Burroughs et al., 2019). Even though curricula
and textbooks are important, the teacher must be well-prepared, distinguished in her/his performance, and have high educational
competencies, translating into practices that enhance the performance of students.
Comparing the professional traits of Saudi mathematics teachers with those of teachers from countries with high achieving students
may help to reveal the reasons for the low performance of Saudi students in the TIMSS 2015. One or more professional traits may be a
factor behind the poor level of mathematical achievement in comparison to students from East Asian countries. For example, it is
believed that teachers’ traits, such as teaching experience and professional development, are linked to student achievement (Al-Zoubi,
Malkawi, & Mikdadi, 2018). In addition, comparing Saudi results to countries with high achievement may help identify the most
important professional traits of mathematics teachers that affect student achievement in those countries, in order to develop those
teacher traits in countries with low achievement. Hence, this study investigated the professional traits of Saudi mathematics teachers
that may affect mathematical achievement for fourth-graders in the TIMSS 2015, and compared those with the professional traits of
their counterparts in three countries with high achievement: Singapore, Hong Kong, and Japan.

1.1. Theoretical Background
Professionalism, as a teacher trait, has received great attention in the field of mathematics education. Professionalism is the latest
guideline added by the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics (NCTM) to the School Mathematics Guidelines in its 2014 edition
(NCTM, 2014), where these principles describe the special traits of high-quality mathematics education programs. The decisions that
teachers, principals, and other professionals make about the content and nature of school mathematics must depend on sound career
guidance, such as that provided by the School Mathematics Guidelines.
The principle of professionalism indicates that in the distinguished mathematics program, educators themselves and their colleagues are responsible for the mathematical success of each student, and for their professional development at the individual and
group level in effective mathematics education and learning (NCTM, 2014). Nonetheless, the professional mathematics teacher does
not accept the status quo, even if it is relatively good. Instead, he/she is keen to learn and achieve self-development, working
continuously to increase their influence on students’ learning of mathematics and aspiring to achieve continuous professional growth.
Teachers do not work in isolation from other colleagues, but instill and support a culture of professional cooperation with others, and
realize that by engaging in this profession, they become mathematics learners and practitioners throughout their careers (NCTM, 2014,
p. 99).
Although there is an abundance of research on the topic of teacher quality, evidence of the impact of teacher traits and gender on
students’ achievement remains limited. Along this line, a set of research studies indicated that high achievement of students is
associated with teacher’s gender and a number of other attributes, including experience (measured by years of teaching), professional
knowledge (measured by education and self-preparation for teaching mathematics), and availability of learning opportunities
(measured by time spent on learning mathematics and content coverage), with these variables providing the basis for a cross-country
comparison model (Burroughs et al., 2019).
Teaching experience refers to the number of years working as a classroom teacher. A number of studies show a positive relationship
between teachers’ experience and student achievement (Wayne & Youngs, 2003). Meanwhile, others have failed to identify consistent
and statistically significant correlations between student achievement and the experience of their teachers (Blömeke, Olsen, & Suhl,
2016; Gustafsson & Nilsen, 2016). Some studies have also indicated that teaching experience is important early in a teacher’s career,
but there are few or no gains in subsequent years (Boyd, Grossman, Lankford, & Loeb, 2006; Rivkin et al., 2005; Staiger & Rockoff,
2010). Pil and Leana (2009) noted a small difference indicating that teachers’ gained experience at the same classroom level over a
number of years was positively related to student achievement.
A teacher’s professional knowledge refers to specialized knowledge, knowledge of curricula, and educational knowledge (Collinson, 1999). This knowledge is affected by the teacher’s qualifications, university degree, attendance at the college, postgraduate
study, and opportunities to engage in in-service training, commonly referred to as “professional development” (Collinson, 1999; Rice,
2003; Wayne & Youngs, 2003).
As with teaching experience, the research is not conclusive about the impact of a teacher’s high marks, specialization in the subject,
and qualifications, with some studies indicating weak, inconsistent, or unimportant relationships with students’ achievement
(Blömeke et al., 2016; Harris & Sass, 2011; Luschei & Chudgar, 2011). However, several international studies comparing one country
to another have found that teacher qualifications were linked to the outcomes of their students (Gustafsson & Nilsen, 2016; Montt,
2011).
Similar findings have been reported concerning professional development, with some studies concluding that professional
development may affect the quality of education (Desimone, Porter, Garet, Yoon, & Birman, 2002; Desimone, Smith, & Phillips, 2013),
while other studies have found that teachers’ professional development experiences showed limited associations with their effectiveness as teachers. In the same vein, and based on an analysis of sixteen studies, it was found that there are important and positive
relationships between professional development and student achievement (Blömeke et al., 2016; Harris & Sass, 2011). Similarly,
Wallace (2009) indicated that professional development has a small, but sometimes statistically significant, effect on student
achievement. An analysis of mathematics data from the National Assessment of Educational Progress [NAEP] from 2000 found that 1.2
additional hours of professional development per year were associated with an increase of 0.62 points in the average score of students.
In general, Wallace concluded that professional development has an average effect on teacher practice and some small impact on
student achievement.
Teacher preparation and professional development in Saudi Arabia - like the rest of the world - has received great attention, as it is a
fundamental pillar of education. There have been continuous development processes to help develop teacher preparation programs in
a manner that meets the requirements of the recent development. Specifically, the process of mathematics teacher preparation at the
primary school in Saudi Arabia underwent several transformations, starting with intermediate teacher institute programs (two years
after middle school), then, secondary teachers’ institutes (three years after middle school), after that, intermediate colleges (two and
half years after high school), and finally the bachelor’s degree that used to be granted by teacher colleges affiliated to the Ministry of
Education or the faculties of education affiliated to the universities (Al-Sayegh, Al-Hujailan, & Al-Omar, 2004). The prevailing trend in
the period of implementing the TIMSS 2015 study was to prepare primary school teachers through the colleges of education affiliated
to universities, and for a period of no less than four years in the integrated preparation programs, in which the students study
educational and specialized courses at the same time. These colleges also provide a sequential program (one year) in which students
enroll after completing specialized, non-educational university studies, and focus on professional preparation with the aim of
obtaining an educational diploma (Sabry & Tawfiq, 2007).
In order for the graduate of these programs to occupy the position of a teacher, he/she must pass the "Teachers’ Competencies" test,
which is a test that measures the achievement of the minimum standards that should be met by applicants to the teaching profession. It

The Ministry of Education is also responsible for the professional development of a mathematics teacher after joining the teaching
profession through programs offered by the ministry or educational training centers in education departments. Additionally, there are
some programs and methods provided by the educational supervisors in the field of professional development for the mathematics
teacher. In this regard, Al-Balawi and Al-Rajah (2012) indicate that benefiting from what the educational supervisors provide is the
most-frequently occurring professional development activity among mathematics teachers.
The influence of the teacher’s gender on the educational process, and specifically on the achievement of students, has received the
attention of several studies, seminars, and conferences, which have produced different views (Boucherback, 1996). However, there is
still an ongoing debate about whether students’ results can be linked to the gender of their teachers. Generally, results from previous
studies on teacher gender are not consistent (Akyuz & Berberoglu, 2010). Some studies concluded that the teacher’s gender has no
effect on students’ mathematics achievement (Carrington, Tymms, & Merrell, 2008; Chudgar & Sankar, 2008; Lingard, Martino, Mills,
& Bahr, 2002). While other studies have shown that students taught by female teachers perform better in mathematics, as female
teachers tend to provide adequate support, a more positive academic atmosphere, and higher motivation levels for students (Hoque,
Razak, Zohora, & Islam, 2013; Krieg, 2005; Laird, Garver, & Niskodé-Dossett, 2011). Meanwhile, a third direction of studies indicated
that students’ performance is enhanced if they have a teacher of the same gender. This could be justified as teachers can act as role
models for their students (Carrington & Skelton, 2003; Dee, 2006; Gray & Leith, 2004; Holmlund & Sund, 2008).
Based on the previously discussed views, the effect of teacher gender and occupational characteristics on students’ achievement in
mathematics has continued to be the subject of widespread debate due to the varying results of different studies. Therefore, the TIMMS
study data are uniquely suitable for investigating these variables. This is due to the rich data provided by this study on areas of
teacher’s gender and occupational characteristics, and because students around the world are subjected to the same standardized test
(Mulji, 2016).
Analysis of the results of TIMMS studies has received the attention of researchers aiming to explore the relationship between
teacher variables (gender and professional traits) and students’ achievement. These researchers have gathered extensive information
about teachers’ backgrounds and practices, across countries, that can be used to address important questions about how teachers
influencing student achievement. For instance, Al-Hajjaji (2012) compared the traits of teachers in countries with high achievement
(Singapore and China) and countries with low achievement (Saudi Arabia) on the TIMSS 2007, to detect and identify those traits. The
study highlighted the most important traits explaining the variance in students’ achievement. It found that mathematics teachers in
high-achieving countries are more involved in training courses, devote more time during the week to giving lessons and solving
problems, allow calculators to be used more, have greater access to computers, do not have resource constraints, and are more focused
on time-consuming duties, including reports and applications. The tests prepared by them are based on questions that include understanding, application, explanation, interpretation, and reasoning, more than those created by teachers of mathematics in Saudi
Arabia. This study also found that the traits of mathematics teachers that most strongly account for variation in student achievement
are the sample questions prepared by the teachers and the use of computers connected to the Internet to search for ideas and information; together they accounted for 15% of the variance in students’ achievement.
Albursan and Tighizza (2012) analyzed the evaluation practices implemented by eighth-grade mathematics teachers in Saudi
Arabia for TIMSS 2007 and compared them with the evaluation practices of their counterparts in South Korea, as one of the countries
with high achievement in TIMSS. They also tried to determine the relationship between the evaluation practices carried out by
mathematics teachers in Saudi Arabia and mathematical achievement for their students. The study reported different assessment
practices between Saudi mathematics teachers and their Korean counterparts as follows: 1) Saudis assigned a greater number of exercises, while Koreans allotted more time for them; 2) Korean mathematics teachers were more likely to ask questions dealing with
exploring patterns and relationships, or questions that require explanation and justification, and 3) Korean mathematics teachers were
more likely to use authentic evaluation. Yildirim and Demir (2014) studied the relationship between traits of students and teachers and
students’ grades on TIMSS 2011, based on eighth-grade student data. They found a statistically significant relationship between the
students’ grades in mathematics and science and the student’s trust in them and a statistically significant relationship between the
students’ grades in mathematics and science and the teacher’s job satisfaction. Finally, there was also a statistically significant
relationship between students’ grades in mathematics and science and their level of awareness of these two topics.
Ker (2016) investigated the variance in achievement between Singaporean and American students in the TIMSS 2011. Hierarchical
linear modeling analysis was used and indicated common traits between Singaporean and American students influencing achievement
in mathematics, including students’ trust to learn mathematics, and teacher’s trust to teach mathematics. However, the learning resources had the greatest influence on the achievement of American students, while the students’ attitudes, levels of motivation, and
ambition were most important in explaining the achievement of Singaporean students.
In another context, Al-Zoubi et al. (2018) analyzed the evaluation practices included in the Jordanian sample in the TIMSS 2011.
They noted differences in the teachers’ and students’ ratings of the frequency of homework, where the student ratings came out higher
than the teachers’ ratings. However, there was consistency in their perception of the time spent doing homework in both mathematics
and science, and that the time they spend on homework exceeded the accepted international standard. The results also showed that
mathematics tests focused mainly on applying mathematical procedures, while in science, the focus was on applying theoretical
knowledge.
Al-Salouli (2018) aimed to identify the level of mathematical knowledge necessary for teaching mathematical subjects included in
TIMSS 2011 and to reveal the relationship between the variables of teaching experience and professional development and the level of

In the same Jordanian context, Ababneh (2019) explored the relationship among variables influencing the mathematical
achievement of Jordanian students in the TIMSS 2011. A linear hierarchical modeling analysis found that 29.49% of the total variance

were the most influential variables, explaining 13.9% of the total variance. However, there was no effect from the variable of the

1.2. Questions of the Study
This study seeks to answer the following questions:
1) Do teacher gender, teaching experience, and/or the interaction between them have an impact on the mathematical achievement of
fourth-grade students in the TIMSS 2015 in each of Singapore, Hong Kong, Japan, and Saudi Arabia?
2) Do teacher gender, the teacher’s educational qualifications, and/or the interaction between them have an impact on the mathematical achievement of fourth-grade students in the TIMSS 2015 in each of Singapore, Hong Kong, Japan, and Saudi Arabia?
3) Do teacher gender, specialization, and/or the interaction between them have an impact on the mathematical achievement of
fourth-grade students in the TIMSS 2015 in each of Singapore, Hong Kong, Japan, and Saudi Arabia?
4) Do teacher gender, amount of professional development, and/or the interaction between them have an impact on the mathematical
achievement of fourth-grade students in TIMSS 2015 in each of Singapore, Hong Kong, Japan, and Saudi Arabia?
5) Which professional traits of mathematics teachers are associated most closely with the variation in mathematical achievement for
the fourth-grade students in TIMSS 2015 in each of Singapore, Hong Kong, Japan, and Saudi Arabia?
1.3. Significance of the Study
The significance of this study stems from its focus on the mathematics teacher, an essential element in the educational process that
affects the attitudes and motives of students toward learning in general and learning mathematics in particular. The study relies on
data from TIMSS 2015 to: 1) identify the professional traits of a mathematics teacher that affect the achievement of students, and 2)
compare the traits of Saudi mathematics teachers with the traits of mathematics teachers in countries with high achievement. The goal
was to identify the professional traits of mathematics teachers that are related to high levels of student achievement, to benefit from
them in developing teacher preparation and professional development programs for in-service teachers in countries with low
achievement.
1.4. Terminology of the Study
Professional Traits of the Mathematics Teacher: Linguistically defined as a characteristic or temper, or what can be relied upon to
distinguish between one person and another. The “professional traits of the teacher” are defined in this study as the professional
teacher variables identified by TIMSS 2015 in the teacher questionnaire used within its tools. The current study limits it to teaching
experience, educational qualification, specialization, and professional development areas.
Trends of the International Mathematics and Science Studies (TIMSS): A large-scale international study supervised by the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA). It is administered every four years, to provide educational
policymakers with reliable and periodic data as a basis for understanding the effects of educational policies and practices within each
country separately, and across educational systems in a wide international context. It helps them to disclose the inputs of their
educational systems and their contexts and outputs related to the achievement of their mathematics and science students, to help them
improve it.
2. Method
For the purpose of this study, the comparative causal approach was used to explore teachers’ professional traits, gender, and
interaction between them that may have an impact on the studied behavior (i.e., the achievement of the fourth-grade students).
2.1. Population and Sample
The study population consisted of all mathematics teachers and their fourth-graders in Saudi Arabia, Singapore, Hong Kong, and
Japan who participated in the TIMSS 2015. The teachers are divided into three categories (mathematics, mathematics and sciences,
and integrated sciences), but this study was restricted to mathematics teachers only. This was due to the lack of “integrated sciences”
teachers in Saudi Arabia and the small number of teachers of "mathematics and science" in Hong Kong. Table 2 describes the study
sample.

2.2. Data Collection
The study relied on data collected from the results of the TIMSS 2015 and the IEA’s databases. These included the achievement test
results and the teacher questionnaire.
2.2.1. Mathematical Achievement Test for the Fourth Grade in TIMSS 2015
The math test consisted of 175 questions intended to reveal the level of mathematical achievement of the fourth-grade students.
This test uses two types of questions: multiple choice and compound responses. At least half of the total observed scores comes from
multiple choice questions, where one score is listed for each, while compound response questions have one or two scores listed,
depending on the nature of the task and the skills required to complete it.
2.2.2. Teacher Questionnaire
The questionnaire responses provided information about teacher traits, class contexts for teaching and learning mathematics, and
mathematical content topics taught. Each teacher participating in the study answered questions that required 30 minutes of teacher
time. Questionnaire items specifically explored some of the teachers’ environments, their views on opportunities to collaborate with
their colleagues, job satisfaction, qualifications, and training, as well as their professional development. The questionnaire also
collected specific information about the traits of the participating class being tested in TIMSS in terms of teaching time, materials,
teaching activities, enhancing student engagement, computer use, evaluation practices, and homework. It also included some basic
demographic information.
2.3. Statistical Methods
This study described the sample responses using means, frequencies, and percentages. Two-way analysis of variance (F-test) was
calculated to study the effects of teacher gender and professional traits, and their interaction, on mathematical achievement for fourthgrade students in the TIMSS 2015. In addition, Eta squared (2) was implemented to calculate the effect size.
3. Results
This study aimed to investigate the effects of teacher gender (male/female), his/her professional traits, and the interaction between
them on the mathematical achievement of fourth-grade students.
3.1. First Question
Do teacher gender, teaching experience, and/or the interaction between them have an impact on the mathematical achievement of
fourth-grade students in the TIMSS 2015 in each of Singapore, Hong Kong, Japan, and Saudi Arabia?
To answer this question, a two-way analysis of variance (2 × 5) was used to identify the influence of teacher gender (male, female)
and his/her teaching experience (less than 5 years, 5 to less than 10, 10 to less than 15, 15 to less than 20, 20 years or more) on
mathematical achievement for fourth-graders in the comparison countries, as shown in Table 3.
Regarding the impact of teachers’ gender (male, female), as seen in Table 3, there was no significant effect at α ≤ 0.05 on
mathematical achievement for students in Singapore, Hong Kong, and Japan. However, as shown in Table 4, influence was reported in
the case of Saudi Arabia, in favor of female teachers.
Regarding teaching experience, there was no significant effect at α ≤ 0.05 of teaching experience on the mathematical achievement
of fourth-grade students in the TIMSS 2015 in all countries of comparison.
There was no significant effect at α ≤ 0.05 for the interaction between teachers’ gender and teaching experience on the mathematical achievement of fourth-grade students in TIMSS 2015 from Hong Kong, Japan, and Saudi Arabia. However, a small impact was
found in the case of Singapore. Fig. 1 illustrates these interactions.
It is obvious from Fig. 1 that there was no effect from the interaction between teacher gender and teaching experience on the
achievement of students when the experience was less than five years, or 15 to less than 20 years. There was, however, an effect from
the interaction on the achievement of students when the experience was five years to less than 10, or 20 years or more, on the behalf of
female teachers, and behalf of male teachers when they have 10 years to less than 15 years of experience.

4. Discussion & Implementations
According to the variable of the of teachers’ gender and targeted professional traits, the study results indicated that students taught
by female teachers excelled in mathematical achievement over students who were taught by male teachers in both Japan and Saudi
Arabia, although there was no effect from gender in the rest of the countries. As for Saudi Arabia, this may be due to the quality of
students taught by female teachers, because the educational system limits the teaching of female teachers to female students only,
whose achievement exceeded that of male students in the results of TIMSS 2015. The intense competition among females for teaching
positions in Saudi Arabia results in highly qualified females being appointed, which does not apply to males. In Japan, this finding
might be justified as female teachers are more capable than male teachers to interact with elementary students and consider their
characteristics, which could be reflected in their mathematical achievement.
This result is supported by a number of studies that concluded that students taught by female teachers perform better in mathematics, as female teachers tend to provide adequate support, a more positive academic atmosphere, and higher motivation for students
12

International Journal of Educational Research 104 (2020) 101671

M.S. Alharbi et al.

(Krieg, 2005; Laird et al., 2011; Hoque et al., 2013).
In terms of teaching experience, the results showed that this variable had no effect on students’ mathematical achievement in all
comparison countries. Although this result is remarkable, it can be explained in light of the culture of new generations. New generations of teachers may develop their skills faster through social media and specialized websites, making the performance of less
experienced teachers similar to teachers with more experience. This is consistent with several previous studies that have indicated that
experience is not a critical feature (Blömeke et al., 2016; Boyd et al., 2006; Gustafsson & Nilsen, 2016; Hussein, 2011; Pil & Leana,
2009; Rivkin et al., 2005; Staiger & Rockoff, 2010).
There was an effect from the interaction of the teachers’ gender with teaching experience in the achievement of students in
Singapore only, when the teacher had 5 to less than 10, or 20 years or more experience, in favor of female teachers. This could be
explained as female teachers in Singapore are recent graduates who are more patient and able to deal with primary school students.
Besides, female teachers, who have more than 20 years of experience, are at an age when they treat their students as children, which
resulted in their academic achievement. When teachers had 10 to less than 15 years’ experience, differences emerged in favor of male
teachers. This may be attributed to the fact that this period is an intermediate stage between graduation modernity and possession of
significant experience, in which the teachers’ teaching quality may decrease, which is reflected in the achievement of their students.
The results also found no effect from the teacher’s qualification on the level of students’ mathematical achievement for all comparison countries. Perhaps this is due to the nature of the elementary stage that does not require highly specialized mathematics and
allows teachers with different qualifications to teach the fourth-grade. This is consistent with the results of several previous studies
concluding that research was inconclusive about the impact of the teachers’ qualifications, with weak, inconsistent, or unimportant
relationships between this variable and student achievement (Blömeke et al., 2016; Harris & Sass, 2011; Luschei & Chudgar, 2011).
While there was an effect of the interaction between teachers’ gender with academic qualification on the achievement of students in
Japan when the teacher has (Master’s or equivalent), for the benefit of female teachers. This may be since female teachers are more
able to interact with primary school students, and that their master’s degree and its equivalent (It is the highest level for candidates for
the education profession in Japan), making them more aware of the traits of students at this stage and how they are treated, which is
reflected in their academic achievement.
The findings also indicated that there was no impact on teachers’ specialization on the level of students’ mathematical achievement
in all comparison countries. This may be due to the nature of mathematical content in the fourth grade, which enables teachers to teach
it, regardless of their specialization. This is consistent with the results of several previous studies that concluded that research was not
conclusive about the impact of the teacher’s specialization, indicating weak, inconsistent, or unimportant relationships with students’
achievement (Blömeke et al., 2016; Harris & Sass, 2011; Luschei & Chudgar, 2011).
The results also indicated that there was an effect from the interaction of teacher gender and specialization on students’
achievement in Hong Kong when the teacher’s major was primary education, but not in math, on behalf of male teachers. This may be
because their educational qualification has enabled them to be able to deal with elementary students like their peers of female teachers,
and excelled in the ability to manage the class, which was reflected in the achievement of their students. When the teacher’s specialty
was math, but not in primary education or any other major, differences emerged in favor of the teachers. This may be since female
teachers are better able to deal with primary school students.
Results also showed no effect from professional development, or its interaction with teacher gender, on the level of students’
mathematical achievement, except for Hong Kong. This may be due to the lack of relevance of the areas of professional development of
the teacher in the comparison countries regarding the requirements of the TIMMS test and its questions and how to deal with it.
The results did show that professional development explained the variance in Hong Kong students’ achievement more than any
other variable. It could be due to the intensity of professional development experienced by a Hong Kong teacher, especially in the first
five years. For Japan, the variables of specialization and teaching experience had the most powerful effect on the variance in student
achievement. This may be due to the fact that experienced mathematicians are the ablest to teach it. Educational qualification was the
variable most likely to influence the achievement of the students of Saudi Arabia and Singapore. This may be due to the fact that the
holder of higher educational qualification is more familiar with the scientific subject and the methods of teaching and evaluating it.
According to the curriculum model in the TIMSS 2015 study, the results of the current study show how the intended curriculum
related to national and societal contexts, represented by the teacher’s gender and occupational characteristics, can affect the acquired
curriculum related to students’ learning outcomes and characteristics as represented by students’ mathematical achievement in the
TIMSS 2015 study.
4.1. Limitations of the Study
This study was limited to fourth-grade mathematics teachers in Singapore, Hong Kong, Japan, and Saudi Arabia. It was also limited
to the results of their students and the data extracted from the achievement test tools and teacher questionnaire in TIMSS 2015, to
study the effect of the gender of teachers and some of their traits (teaching experience, academic qualification, specialization, and
professional development fields) on the mathematical achievement of students.
5. Conclusions
Although the research literature emphasizes the importance of teacher traits and their impact on students’ achievement (especially
teaching experience, scientific qualification, specialization, and professional development), scientific research has not been conclusive. The evidence for the impact of these traits remains limited. Extensive international studies, with various tools, multiple variables,

wide samples, and the rich, high-quality data they provide on these traits are a valuable resource for use in studying these traits indepth. Such studies provide an acceptable basis to compare educational systems in many countries with similar levels of development. Differences in how countries measure relationships between teacher quality and student outcomes can make it difficult to share
educational ideas among countries. This is especially true if there is a lack of understanding about how (or why) policies are implemented in different national contexts, because it may be difficult to know whether (or when) a particular policy should be adopted
(Plucker, 2014).
Although the previous literature emphasizes the importance of teacher gender and the professional traits, especially the teaching
experience, scientific qualification, specialization, and professional development, and their impact on students’ achievement, scientific
research has not been conclusive. The evidence for the impact of teacher gender and the professional traits remains limited.
