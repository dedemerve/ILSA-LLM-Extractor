The effective use of Information and Communication Technology (ICT) is closely linked to improved academic
performance, yet global research on this topic remains limited. This study analyzes PISA 2018 data to explore the
relationship between ICT use in various contexts and reading, math, and science literacy among 248 224 15-yearolds from 52 countries/regions. The network analysis shows that students’ performance in reading, math, and
science is closely linked to ICT use in various contexts. Emphasis should be on developing students’ ICT
competence and supervising their use of ICT for learning outside school.

1. Introduction
Digital technology is increasingly ubiquitous in society, enabling
individuals to seamlessly access the Internet from virtually anywhere, at
any time, utilizing portable communication devices to retrieve a wealth
of information. This concept is envisioned as digital capability, and its
augmentation is intricately tied to the abundance of readily accessible
digital resources. The concept derived from this analytical framework is
the accessibility of digital resources in a global society, which can be
explained in terms of “digital capital”. It refers to the accumulation of
digital competencies and digital technologies that include ICT endowments, technological knowledge, content creation and problem solving,
and the skills needed to effectively use these digital tools, and which
may influence various outcome, including students’ academic performance. This is because ICT are increasingly present in students’ lives,
both inside and outside the classroom. However, it may not only be
access that matters, but also how students use them and how well they
are able to use these tools both from a technological perspective and in
terms of their intended purpose (Pitzalis & Porcu, 2024; Ragnedda,
2018). This is why studying the impact of these tools on academic
performance is crucial, as the results may vary depending on factors
such as the student’s circumstances. For example, without supervision,
students may use ICT primarily to watch entertaining videos or engage
in social media activities, rather than for activities that benefit their
learning. Additionally, students’ attitudes toward ICT play a role, with a
positive attitude toward ICT use (believing they have autonomy and

confidence in using ICT) being positively correlated with academic
performance (Courtney et al., 2022). Therefore, this highlights the
importance of exploring the relationship between ICT use and academic
performance. However, the findings of the International Computer and
Information Literacy Study (ICILS) underscore the existence of disparities in ICT literacy both among and within participating nations,
revealing that a substantial proportion of adolescents still lag behind
average levels of proficiency in this domain (Fraillon et al., 2014).
Previous research has shown that the effective use of ICT (such as autonomy in using ICT, confidence in using ICT, and attitudes towards ICT)
is associated with higher academic performance in students. This is
especially true for students who have been born into the digital age,
where mastering ICT-related skills efficiently is a key factor in
enhancing their academic achievements (Courtney et al., 2022).
Therefore, exploring more deeply the relationship between students’
ICT use and academic performance remains a key area.
Currently, many countries around the world are actively preparing
for digital education, which refers to the integration of digital technology with educational practices. For example, the Education Bureau of
Hong Kong, China, launched the fourth Information Technology in Education Strategy (ITE4) in 2016, aiming to harness the potential of IT to
enhance students’ learning abilities, which also includes initiatives to
promote the development of the ICT environment (Education-Bureau,
2016). In addition, European countries have also started to emphasize
the importance of improving ICT-based education, encouraging teachers

2.3. The relationship between ICT use in and out of classroom/school and
academic achievement
The availability of ICT resources at home/school emphasizes the
digital tools or resources that students can access in their home or school
environment. In contrast, the use of ICT in and out of the classroom/
school in this study refers to specific form, such as students using ICT for
leisure, social interaction, and subject-related ICT use both inside and
outside the classroom. Previous studies have found that when students
use ICT for out-of-school leisure, social interaction, and learning, there is
a “∩” pattern in academic performance. The peak of the “∩” indicates
that the relationship between ICT use in and out of the classroom/school
and academic performance is balanced. When students use ICT for social
purposes at a low to moderate intensity, it does not affect academic
performance (Zhu & Li, 2022). However, intensive use of ICT for
entertainment and social activities both inside and outside the classroom
increases cognitive and mental burdens, ultimately hindering students’
academic performance. Clearly, ICT use both in and outside the classroom/school is also interconnected with students’ academic performance. Therefore, this study intends to further incorporate it into a
network model to explore its relationship with students’ academic
performance.

2. Literature review and state of the art
2.1. The role of ICT-related use in academic performance
More specifically, the relationship between different forms of ICT use
and academic achievement follows an “∩” shape, with the correlations
varying over time (Borgonovi & Pokropek, 2021). However, the effects
of ICT use on achievement varied across different contexts, purposes,
and types of use, etc. It highlighted the need for further differentiation
between various forms of ICT use—such as out-of-school ICT use for
recreation or study, in-school general use, and students’ perceptions and
attitudes toward ICT use—in practical research, in order to fully understand the impact of ICT on student achievement (J. Hu & Yu, 2021).
These ambiguous results warrant further investigation within a global
network model to explore more deeply the relationship between
different types of ICT use, contexts, attitudes, and performance in
reading, mathematics, and science.

2.4. The relationship between cognitive and affective attitudes towards
ICT use and academic achievement
In addition to concerns about the application of ICT resources,
cognitive and affective attitudes towards ICT use are also of general
interest, as it directly determines, to some extent, the manner in which
individuals engage with ICT. In the context of the PISA 2018 survey, four
pivotal factors are encompassed: autonomy in ICT utilization, interest,
perceived ICT proficiency, and the employment of ICT for social
engagement. These factors collectively constitute the motivating
impetus for ICT usage, wherein students’ engagement with technology
fosters active access to learning resources via ICT tools, which, in turn,
enhances their ICT skills and proficiency (Park & Weng, 2020). These
factors align precisely with fundamental human psychological needs
(Deci & Ryan, 2000): the quest for autonomy, the drive for competence,
and the yearning for relevance. In the realm of ICT utilization, competence translates into an individual’s innate inclination towards
mastering ICT-related skills and tasks, thereby showcasing their abilities
and worth. Autonomy, on the other hand, unfolds as the capacity for
individuals to engage in self-directed and self-managed activities,
guided solely by their personal interests and aspirations. Lastly, relevance addresses the profound human need to forge social connections
and build relationships leveraging the power of ICT tools, fostering a
sense of belonging and interconnectedness (X. Zhang & Zhou, 2023).
Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that these indicators
serve as modest predictors of students’ academic achievement (Cerasoli
et al., 2016). For instance, students who possess greater autonomy in
employing ICT tend to excel in planning and overseeing their learning

leading them to engage in non-learning-related activities, such as
excessive online gaming (Chen et al., 2024; Gubbels et al., 2020).
However, another study found that family ICT resources are widely
regarded as a key contributor to student learning and development.
More abundant family ICT resources are typically associated with higher

2.5. The relationship between ICT use time and academic performance
Similarly, the number of hours spent on ICT resources is closely
related to students’ academic performance. A survey of Irish students
showed that, holding all other variables constant, those who reported
spending more than 30 min a day preparing reports and presentations
had reading scores that were about one-seventh of a standard deviation
higher than those who did not engage in such activities. While the survey did not differentiate between time spent at home or school, a clear
implication emerges: The long-term and correct use of ICT for learning
will yield positive outcomes for students (Gilleece & Eivers, 2018).
Secondly, another study found that although students ’ monthly and
weekly computer use was positively correlated with students ’ academic
performance, students ’ daily computer use was negatively correlated
with academic performance. Obviously, the length of ICT use will cause
the secondary return of the corresponding subject score, that is, when
the use of ICT reaches a certain threshold, the return will stagnate or
decline (Karlsson, 2022). Therefore, this study incorporates ICT use
duration (distinguishing weekdays and weekends) into the network
structure to explore its role in the network structure.

2.7. Research aim
Therefore, this study aims to answer the following research questions
from a global perspective using PISA 2018 data: First, to construct a
network structure model of students’ performance in reading, mathematics, and science in relation to ICT usage; second, to analyze, interpret, and compare the similarities and differences in centrality
indicators based on the constructed network structure models for
reading, mathematics, and science; and finally, to propose recommendations for improving students’ academic performance based on the
research findings and previous studies.
3. Methods
3.1. Data analyses
Academically relevant items covered by the PISA 2018 survey
include reading, math, and science literacy. But with the main focus on
assessing students’ reading literacy, it is defined as the “An individual’s
capacity to comprehend, utilize, assess, reflect upon, and interact with
texts for the purpose of goal attainment, knowledge enhancement, potential development, and active societal participation”(OECD, 2019b).
To embody this definition, PISA 2018 employs a diverse array of
subject-specific texts that mirror real-life situations, thereby aligning the
reading assessment with authentic cognitive demands, fostering
higher-order thinking skills (OECD, 2019a). The data employed in this
study originates from the 2018 PISA, administered by the Organization
for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). In this iteration,
the core area of inquiry centered on the reading proficiency of 15-year-olds, assessed primarily through simulated online reading exercises,
thereby providing an estimation of their digital reading literacy. Additionally, this research delves into the analysis of two other academic
domains: Mathematics (It is defined in the 2018 measurement: Mathematical literacy is an individual’s capacity to formulate, employ and
interpret mathematics in a variety of contexts. It includes reasoning
mathematically and using mathematical concepts, procedures, facts and
tools to describe, explain and predict phenomena. It assists individuals
to recognise the role that mathematics plays in the world and to make
the well-founded judgements and decisions needed by constructive,
engaged and reflective citizens.) and Science performance (It is defined
in the 2018 measurement: Scientific literacy is the ability to engage with
science-related issues, and with the ideas of science, as a reflective citizen.) (OECD, 2019a). For a comprehensive understanding of the sampling methodologies employed, kindly refer to the dedicated PISA
technical report (OECD, 2019a). The reasons for selecting the PISA 2018
database for this study are as follows: Firstly, PISA 2018 is the seventh
cycle of the assessment, and it focuses on reading in a digital context.
The reading assessment of PISA 2018 updated its assessment framework
by fully integrating the concept of digital literacy and further redefining
reading literacy, emphasizing the reading of dynamic texts in digital
environments (such as embedded hyperlinks). Secondly, PISA 2018 includes two new ICT usage variables (unique to PISA 2018), namely ICT
use inside and outside the classroom, as well as many other variables
related to ICT usage in various contexts. These indicators can better
support this study in exploring the network structure relationship between ICT usage and academic performance. Finally, the respondents in
the PISA 2018 survey come from 79 different countries/regions, with 52
countries/regions completing the ICT usage questionnaire. These
countries span different continents and have varying national income

2.6. The relationship between metacognition and ICT use, academic
performance
Another important factor in how ICT affects students’ academic
performance is the individual’s self-regulatory process, also known as
metacognition. Metacognition in reading encompasses the strategic
regulation of the reading process, which necessitates planning, standardizing, vigilant monitoring, and comprehensive evaluation of
reading activities (Veenman et al., 2006). Especially in digital reading, it
also involves the need to further master the relevant features of the
Internet, such as downloading relevant documents. Previous studies
have found a relationship between metacognition, ICT usage, and students’ academic performance. Specifically, appropriate ICT usage is
beneficial for students’ metacognition, while intensive or excessive ICT
usage can disrupt metacognition, which in turn affects students’ reading
literacy. This further indicates the mediating role of metacognition. The
implication is that students should engage in active ICT-related information processing, which is conducive to improving metacognitive
strategies, thereby influencing academic performance (M. Li & Wang,
2022). More specifically, the findings suggest that ICT use can have an
indirect effect on reading achievement through three metacognitive
strategies (understanding and remembering, summarizing and assessing
credibility). Additionally, ICT use plays a moderating role by mitigating
the negative impact of social motivation for ICT use on digital reading
achievement. These results further highlight the crucial role of metacognitive strategies in the relationship between ICT use and students’
academic performance (Cadamuro et al., 2019). Of course, this impact
also varies by gender. Previous studies have found that males tend to use
metacognitive strategies, such as summarizing and understanding/memory, less frequently than females. In other words, the
higher reading scores reported by females can be attributed to their use
of these metacognitive and control strategies. This difference is also
related to ICT-related skills. Proficient use of ICT can make it easier to
understand the content being read, which seems to benefit males more
(Callan et al., 2017; Xie et al., 2023). In addition, age is also a factor that
needs to be considered. Some researchers believe that younger learners
may lack the metacognitive knowledge or skills required for effective
metacognitive instruction, which leads to poorer learning outcomes for
this age group (Williams & Atkins, 2009, pp. 26–43). However, they may
be able to coordinate various cognitive strategies by improving their
ICT-related skills, thereby indirectly enhancing their academic

3.2. Measures
3.2.1. Metacognitive strategies
Metacognitive strategies are assessed utilizing three key indicators:
understanding and remembering (It consists of 6 items, corresponding to
the code: Undrem), summarization capabilities (It consists of 5 items,
corresponding to the code: Metasum), and the newly introduced aspect
of assessing credibility (It consists of 5 items, corresponding to the code:
Metaspam) in the 2018 research (OECD, 2020). The assessment utilizes
a 6-point Likert scale, spanning from 1 to 6, where higher scores signify a
greater level of knowledge among participants regarding metacognitive
strategies.
3.2.2. Content related to the use of ICT
Subject-related ICT use includes two main items, subject-related ICT
use in the classroom (labeled as “Ictclass”) and subject-related ICT use
outside the classroom curriculum (labeled as “Ictoutside”). This metric
aims to quantify students’ engagement with subject-specific ICT usage,
meticulously tracking the duration of time they devote to utilizing ICT
for activities pertinent to their academic subjects, both within and
beyond the classroom setting. It represents a novel addition to the PISA
2018 survey, offering fresh insights into students’ digital learning
practices.
The availability of ICT resources at home (labeled as “Icthome”) and
on school (labeled as “Ictsch”), as assessed by the PISA program by
measuring 11 digital devices (e.g., computers, smartphones) that provide students with digital resources, ranges from 0 to 11.
The duration of Internet usage both within and outside of school
premises, on both weekdays and weekends, was recorded through two
specific queries: “During a typical weekday, for how long do you use the
Internet at school? (labeled as “IC005Q01TA”)” and “During a typical
weekday, for how long do you use the Internet outside of school (labeled
as “IC006Q01TA”)?”. The relevant responses for each question were “No

the number selected indicates that the Internet was used for a longer

3.2.3. Control variables
Given previous research indicating that gender, age, and students’
economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS: is a complex composite
measure in the PISA test, derived from the combination of the following
three indicators: the highest occupational status of the parents, the
highest level of education attained by the parents over the years, and
home possessions) often significantly influence the relationship between
students’ ICT use and academic performance, these factors were
included as control variables in the network structure of this study (Chen
et al., 2021; X. Hu et al., 2018).
3.2.4. PISA 2018 data for math, reading, and science assessments
The three core variables involved in this study are reading, math, and
science literacy. The PISA 2018 survey prominently features reading as a
pivotal aspect, with approximately half of its assessments dedicated to
evaluating reading programs. This segment aims to gauge students’
proficiency in interacting with texts for diverse purposes pertinent to
their daily lives and social interactions. In contrast, mathematical literacy centers on assessing students’ capacity for mathematical
reasoning, whereas scientific literacy specifically targets their ability to
engage with scientific and technical readings. For the comprehensive
evaluation of the three fundamental variables mentioned earlier, each
participating student was presented with ten plausible values (PVs). This
study constructed a network structure for each PV value of reading,
science, and mathematical literacy. The results showed that the centrality metrics of the 10 network models for all three academic performances were identical. Therefore, in line with other studies, the first PV
value (i.e., PV1READ, PV1SCIE, and PV1MATH) for each academic
performance was selected as the final value for this study, which also
yielded valid results (J. Hu & Zhang, 2024).

3.3.2. Network centrality
In this study, centrality was evaluated using four indicators: strength,
betweenness, closeness, and expected influence (EI) index. Strength
centrality is the sum of the absolute weights of edges connecting a node
to others, reflecting its direct connectivity. Closeness represents the inverse of the sum of the shortest path lengths between a node and all
others, with a larger value indicating quicker access to other nodes.
Betweenness refers to the number of shortest paths between any two
nodes, highlighting a node’s role as a “bridge” and potential target for
intervention (Epskamp et al., 2018). Additionally, the EI was computed
in this study due to its enhanced stability compared to other centrality
metrics, particularly in networks characterized by both positive and
negative edges. Nodes with high EI values are considered as core
symptoms in the network (Robinaugh et al., 2016).

aiming to discover and identify the roles played by each node in the
network. In the science literacy network structure, the centrality indicators are roughly consistent with the centrality indicators in the math
literacy network structure.
Regarding the accuracy and stability of the constructed network,
Fig. SB1 shows the bootstrapped CIs of the edge weights and pairwise
comparisons between the edges are shown in Fig. SB2, the bootstrap 95
% CI for the edge weights are narrow, suggesting that the results of the
network model are reliable. Fig.SB3 demonstrates that the Bootstrapped
difference test for node EIs shows that the most central symptom is
significantly different from the other symptoms in the network model. In
addition, as shown in Fig. SB4, the CS coefficients of the centrality indexes of the three network structures calculated using the bootstrap
method are all 0.75 (>0.5), indicating good stability.

3.3.3. Network accuracy and stability
The stability and accuracy of the network were rigorously evaluated
utilizing the bootnet package. Specifically, the precision of edge weights
and strength centrality indices was scrutinized through nonparametric
bootstrapping (nboots = 2500) to derive bootstrap confidence intervals
(CI), thereby illuminating the accuracy of the estimated edge weights.
Furthermore, this study assesses the robustness of the centrality index by
computing the Correlation Stability Coefficient (CS-C). Typically, a CS-C
value exceeding 0.25 is deemed acceptable (n = 1000), with a preference for values above 0.5, indicating that the network structure can be
confidently considered stable (Epskamp et al., 2018).

all forms of ICT use related to students’ academic performance in the
network model, demonstrating the intricate network relationships between different forms of ICT use and each academic performance from a

themselves as proficient in using ICT tend to have more positive attitudes toward scientific literacy, including higher levels of self-efficacy
and greater interest in the subject (Areepattamannil & Santos, 2019;
Courtney et al., 2022), this may be related to the constant need to adapt
to the ever-evolving nature of the modern learning environment.
Regarding perceived ICT autonomy, on the other hand, it is consistent
with perceived competence and was positively and positively correlated
with students’ academic performance in this study. Of course, similar
nodes to the predicted effect of perceived ICT competence are the use of
ICT for social recreation, and the use of ICT for general learning tasks
within the school. Specifically, unlike previous studies, the use of ICT for
out-of-school recreation-related activities was weakly and positively
associated with students’ academic performance (Scientific and math
performance), although no curvilinear effect was tested in this study.
Previous research has suggested a possible U-shaped relationship, with
the key being to identify the optimal points of use and monitor them
effectively. The impact of this form of ICT use on students’ academic
performance remains ambiguous(Chen et al., 2021). Regarding the
other two factors, it is widely acknowledged that the use of ICT for
learning tasks in schools’ benefits students’ academic performance, as it
enhances their learning experience and is often viewed as a facilitator of
improved outcomes (Comi et al., 2017). However, this study found a
negative correlation between the frequency with which students use
digital devices for various activities on campus and students’ academic
performance. One possible explanation is that excessive use of ICT may
not be beneficial for students who are already heavy users, because, in
the absence of proper supervision, students may appear to be using ICT
for related learning tasks, but in reality, they may be browsing content
unrelated to the learning tasks. Therefore, this study suggests that future
efforts should address the potential paradox of over- or under-use of ICT
in school settings, ensuring a balanced approach to its integration in
general learning tasks (Zhu & Li, 2022).
Regarding closeness centrality, the use of ICT for socialization and
other related activities was found to be the node most closely connected
to other nodes in the reading network structure. However, it showed a
negative correlation with reading literacy. This can be explained using
the displacement effect, whereby if the time students spend on ICT is not
spent on activities that improve reading literacy, but rather on other
recreational or social activities, then the effect on reading achievement
is negative. Since students have a limited amount of time to use ICT
during the day, an increase in the amount of time spent on recreational
activities means a decrease in the amount of time spent on academic
achievement(Weis & Cerankosky, 2010). It maintains the closest
connection with other nodes in the entire network, meaning that a high
score for this node can quickly extend to other nodes. The takeaway is
that, while the social use of ICT in various environments provides students with opportunities to meet basic psychological needs, this form of
use should be limited to low-intensity engagement to avoid interfering
with students’ learning. Excessive use may lead to negative consequences in other areas as well (Zhu & Li, 2022). Meanwhile, another
closeness centrality node, related to the use of ICT outside of school for
school-related learning tasks, was found in the network structure of
academic performance and more pronounced in the math and science
literacy network structures. This commonality suggests that both subjects may benefit from logical thinking skills, which can be explained by
learning transfer theory (Hung, 2013). The theory posits that the two
disciplines overlap in certain areas, allowing students to build on their
existing knowledge base with varying degrees of adaptation and modification (Anderson et al., 1995). Previous research has shown that
reading plays a fundamental role in the overall academic system, with
improved reading skills enabling quicker access to information in other
subjects. Similarly, the same principle applies to math and science:
enhancing scientific literacy can, to some extent, also foster improvements in mathematical thinking skills (Hatlevik et al., 2018; L. Li et al.,
2023).

6. Limitation, conclusion and future research
There are also several limitations to this study, firstly although this
study constructs a network structure for academic achievement based on
a large sample of international surveys, it does not further differentiate
between the details of the individual countries/regions involved. Due to
the potential influence of response style (such as acquiescent, extreme,
and mid-point responding) biases when using multiple measurement
scales in the PISA survey on ICT usage, especially in cross-cultural
studies, measurement errors may increase. Future surveys could
consider combining multiple measurement methods and conducting
cultural adaptation checks to overcome this limitation. Secondly, the
nested nature needs to be considered regarding the impact of students’
academic achievement, as students live in a multi-level social structure.
Cross-sectional study designs do not allow causal inferences to be drawn,
and future research will require longitudinal study designs to determine
causality. Finally, future research should also incorporate more factors
related to ICT use to explore more complex network structures.
This study applies international data from the PISA 2018 survey for
the first time to construct network structures between global student
academic performance (reading, math, and science) and ICT use in a
variety of scenarios, and finds that the three network structures share
certain factors as well as have their own independent predictors. A
further distinction was also made between the characteristics of the
indicators that play a central role in the network, i.e., which indicator
plays a mediating or intimate role, and future interventions can be
tailored to the nature of this node. For example, used outside of school to
handle school-related learning tasks was found to be the strongest predictor of each of the three network structures, and therefore requires
reasonable supervision at home and on campus, as well as increased
autonomy of students’ ICT use to overcome the potential impact on
achievement.
Based on the insights garnered from the network structure constructed in this research, the following recommendations are proffered.
Firstly, our findings reveal that the networks underpinning reading,
mathematics, and science share common factors. Drawing upon the
theory of learning transfer, which posits that mastery in one subject can
positively influence performance in another due to shared predictors, we
suggest that the education sector should devise multifaceted scenarios
leveraging ICT that seamlessly integrate diverse subjects. This approach
fosters a mutually beneficial learning environment where competencies
acquired in one domain enrich and enhance those in another. Secondly,
it is imperative to fortify the supervision of students’ utilization of ICT
beyond school walls in addressing classroom content. While engaging
with ICT outside of school settings can foster a conducive atmosphere
that stimulates students’ intrinsic motivation through practical application, the allure and entertainment-driven nature of computerized
media content poses a latent peril. Abrupt distractions emanating from
such sources can disrupt cognitive processes and hinder focused
thought. Thus, enhanced oversight is necessary to ensure that these
learning experiences remain productive and free from undue
interference.
