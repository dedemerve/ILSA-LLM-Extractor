We analyse the effect of violent crime on students' results in Costa Rica. The combination of PISA data and highly
disaggregated information on crime at the district level is a main contribution of this article. Our methodological
approach provides also some novelties in the field, the most important being the use of a simultaneous equations
model solved with instrumental variables. This allows us to obtain unbiased and efficient estimators in the
presence of simultaneity between education and crime. We conclude that exposure to crime, measured by the
homicide rate, has a negative and significant impact on academic achievement. This effect is particularly important in the case of students attending schools situated in districts with a lower social development.

1. Introduction
The universalization of the education system has been one of the
most profound transformations in Latin American society in recent
decades and has had important consequences in terms of equality,
economic inclusion and social cohesion. However, despite the efforts
made, the results do not reflect the same success in quantitative as in
qualitative terms. This situation is highlighted by the results of the
Program for International Student Assessment (PISA) of the OECD: the
Latin American countries participating occupy among the lowest positions of the developed and emerging economies involved. Although
Latin American countries allocate increasing resources to education,
these efforts do not always guarantee an improvement in academic
achievement (Castro et al., 2017). For this reason, it is essential to
understand the endemic factors that contribute to poor performance at
schools. Weaknesses in infrastructures — especially of schools located
in disadvantaged environments — the obsolescence of educational
programs or deficiencies in teacher training are usually identified as
causes of these poor results (OECD, 2014a).
Surprisingly, among these occlusive factors, criminal violence has
received less attention in the theoretical and empirical literature, despite the importance of this phenomenon which especially affects
children and adolescents in a region characterized by high insecurity.
This paper focuses on this relationship and provides two principal
contributions. First, we combine geolocalized data from PISA-Costa
Rica 2012 and highly disaggregated information on crime at the district
level. The combination of PISA and local data constitutes a main

⁎

contribution, as a sizable body of studies relied on small samples or data
that do not offer the analytical possibilities of PISA. Second, this unique
multidimensional dataset allows us to benefit from a methodological
approach that tackles unobserved heterogeneity. The literature in the
field has explicitly recognized the difficulty of dealing with the unobserved heterogeneity issue and obtaining unbiased estimators, because exposure to violence may be correlated with other unobservable
factors that also affect educational outcomes (Caudillo and Torche,
2014). Previous empirical literature treats crime as exogenous and this
might result in the misinterpretation of the true effect of the variable, as
the estimations of the effect of crime on academic achievement could be
biased. In this study, the simultaneous equations model that we propose
considers crime endogenous and explicitly uses, as regressors, instrumental variables (IV). Finding suitable instruments is a difficult task
and these may have limitations. Even with these issues in mind, the use
of IV is particularly important for our analysis in order to calculate
causal effects, as this allows controlling for problems of endogeneity.
Previous research has rarely allowed any causal effects of violence on
educational outcomes to be identified. Our results show that exposure
to crime plays a key role in explaining the variability in the students′
performance. This effect is particularly important for students attending
schools situated in districts with a low level of social development.

In the case of Latin America, the literature that links violence and
academic achievement is heterogeneous and depends on the typology
of violence analysed. Countries in the region suffer many forms of
violence that have very different origins, characteristics and consequences1 . For example, Murillo and Román (2011) focus on the effect
of intra-school violence. In their study, which uses data from the Second
Regional Comparative and Explanatory Study (SERCE) for primary
school students, they emphasise that bullying is a serious problem
throughout Latin America. Students who suffer peer aggression yield a
significantly lower performance in reading and maths, and those in
classrooms with more episodes of physical or verbal violence perform
worse. Gómez Soler (2016) analyses the effect of a very different kind of
violence but that also has important effects on academic achievement:
civil armed conflict2 . Her results show a negative relation between the
intensity and presence of civil conflict in Colombia and performance in
the maths and language high school exit examinations (Saber11) from
2000 to 2007.
However, the most endemic form of violence in the region is that
associated with crime and drug trafficking. Unequal access to education, health, employment, and basic physical infrastructure with urban
poor′s living conditions are closely related to the incidence of crime and
violence, especially affecting the poorest (Fajnzylber et al., 2002;
Bourguignon et al., 2003; Rodgers, 2004; Soares, 2004). These factors,
visible and inherent to the Latin American region, are clearly connected
also with school failure. Countries that have a larger proportion of
students with socio-economic problems face more severe educational
challenges. According to PISA data, cited by Castro Aristizabal,
Giménez, and Pérez Ximénez-de-Embún (2018), 51% of Latin American
students live in unfavourable environments, and this has a negative
effect on school performance. The authors find that 32% of the variability in the results obtained by Latin American students in the PISA
math tests is associated with differences related to adverse circumstances. The large student performance gaps that exists within the same
town or city and even between schools located very close are, therefore,
a reflection of the high-income-inequalities that is often accompanied
by endemic violence (Hanushek and Woessmann, 2012; Murillo &
Martínez-Garrido, 2017).
Nevertheless, despite the fact that Latin America is the region with
the world′s highest crime rate (Jaitman, 2017), there is little empirical
research that has explicitly analysed the effect of criminal violence on
academic achievement.3 The few papers that do concentrate on narcoviolence. In recent years, the wave of extreme violence that Mexico has
suffered has led to an interest in this subject. Violence increased significantly after the government declared a "war against drugs" in 2006,
affecting not only individuals directly involved in illegal activities, but
also the civil population. Romano (2015) studies the consequences of
the increase in narco-violence. He combines data of the Evaluación Nacional del Logro Académico en Centros Escolares (ENLACE) with crime
rates at the municipal level to examine the effects of exposure to crime
on the educational outcomes of primary and secondary students from
2006 to 2012. Using fixed effects and IV, the author determines that an
increase in homicide rates is associated with a reduction in test scores.

2. Conceptual framework
Much of the empirical research in the field of crime considers
education to be an explanatory factor of violence (for a survey, see
Lochner, 2010). Improvements in education develop market skills,
which raise wages and, therefore, the opportunity cost of crime. Furthermore, education may socialize individuals, leading them to choose
not to engage in crime. Therefore, policies that subsidise schooling and
human capital investment have significant social benefits and can be a
key policy tool in the drive to reduce crime (Machin, Marie, & Vujić,
2011).
Unfortunately, the literature has paid less attention to the other side
of the issue, namely, the study of the effects of violence on education,
even though violence has important psychological and physical effects
on children. Osofsky et al. (1995) and Osofsky (1999:36) find that
children living in urban areas who are exposed to chronic community
violence present symptoms that include clinginess to parents or caregivers, fear of natural exploring beyond their immediate environment, a
numbing of affect, distractibility, intrusive thoughts, and feelings of not belonging. As a result, violence can disrupt children′s typical developmental trajectories and produce severe psychobiological effects. They
can develop posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), concentration and
sleeping problems, peer problems, aggressive behaviour and depression
(Gorman-Smith and Tolan, 1998; Margolin and Gordis, 2000; Ozer &
Weintsein, 2004; Heissel et al., 2017). All this deteriorates the psychological well-being of the student (Brück et al., 2014) and, in the
worst cases, the psychological disorders can lead to mental health
problems (Mazza and Overstreet, 2000).
For all these reasons, violence affects school attendance and completion, but can also condition the development of cognitive skills.
Empirical research has found that students who are exposed to crime
score lower in standardized tests. Most of the studies focus on urban
areas of the United States. They conclude that living in a violent environment is linked to a lower performance and behavioural problems
(Delaney-Black et al., 2002; Aizer, 2008; Milam et al., 2010; Sharkey,
2010; Burdick-Will et al., 2011; Burdick-Will, 2013; Sharkey, Schwartz,
Ellen, & Lacoe, 2014).
Several studies have focused on the heterogeneity of the effects
depending on socio-economic characteristics. Some key aspects considered have been gender and neighbourhood economic development.
Violence affects boys and girls differently. Young girls may be perceived
as more vulnerable to attacks and abuses, in particular, to rape
(Education for All, 2011 Cit. by Singh & Shemyakina, 2016). The heterogeneous effect of violence is especially pronounced in Latin America.
In the region, many boys are victims of homicide, often related to gang
activities and drug trafficking. Girls, on the other hand, are exposed to
more gender violence, particularly sexual violence, usually in a context
of human trafficking and unaccompanied forced migration. With respect to socio-economic contexts, criminal violence and disorder are
usually inextricably linked. Sampson and Groves (1989) point out that
neighbourhood structural disadvantage can lead to neighbourhood
disorganization (social disorganization theory). Merton and Merton
(1968) links crime with disadvantaged neighbourhoods where the
lower class lacks opportunities (relative deprivation theory). Wilson and
Kelling (1982) have indicated that exposure to disordered neighbourhoods —graffiti, broken windows, deteriorated buildings—could increase fear and the feelings of unsafety (broken window theory).
Mijanovich and Weitzman (2003) highlight that these disadvantaged
neighbourhoods are likely to have higher rates of delinquency and,
consequently, cause problems of youth behaviour linked to lower

The PISA dataset has some technical advantages. Besides the estimates of students′competencies, PISA collects data on the student, family and institutional factors that helps to explain differences in performance. Moreover, it is specifically developed with replicate sampling
weights and plausible values.5 As a consequence, the results of PISA
have a high degree of validity and reliability and enable international
comparisons (OECD, 2013).
Of course, like other programs that attempt to measure the performance of students, PISA is not immune to some problems of technical
nature. For example, the presence of missing observations, which stem
from the fact that students and directors leave questions unanswered,
can be a major problem when working with PISA. The combination of
unanswered questions in several sections represents a significant loss of
observations and can generate biases that affect statistical inference. It
is therefore important to identify and treat the missing data. Although
the percentage of missing observations in our PISA dataset is relatively
low (1.21%), the deletion of all the students that have a missing value
for at least one variable would have reduced the sample size by 14.6%
(from 4,602 to 4,017 observations). (See Table 1). To circumvent this
issue, we use the hotdeck procedure, specifically, the Bayesian bootstrap
method of Rubin and Schekeeach (1986) implemented in Stata by
Mander and Clayton (2007). As the latter explain, the missing values
are replaced in each stratum by values sampled from the most similar
cases of the observed data. As a robustness check, we have also obtained the estimations of the baseline model with missing values and
the results, available upon request, are similar to the ones obtained with
the treatment of missing data.
PISA microdata are complemented with official homicide rates at
the district level, the smallest administrative unit into which Costa Rica
is divided. A main concern of the study is to know the geolocation of the
schools participating in PISA. This information was provided by the
Ministerio de Educación and Estado de la Educación and allows us to link
the PISA dataset to homicide rates at the district level. Thus, we can
identify the district in which schools are located and, hence, analyse the
effect of violence on students′ achievement. All this data are available
for 2012, which makes requisite to merge the data with the 2012 version of PISA, and not with previous or later versions.
All in all, we have information about 4,602 students (2,460 girls and
2,142 boys) from 193 schools located in 138 districts. There are no
missing values for crime data. As we have the information of the location of every school participating in PISA and of the homicide rate in
every district in which the schools are situated, no observations were
lost in the matching process of the PISA and homicide datasets.
Nevertheless, when working with the socio-economic characteristics of
the districts that we will consider as explaining variables of homicide,
we lose 22 observations (leaving 4,580). This is because the Los Guido
district has no census data of the annual population growth.

Finally, we have to highlight that all this empirical research has had
to tackle a methodological problem. "A central difficulty is unobserved
heterogeneity: Communities more likely to be exposed to homicides
and other types of violence differ from nonviolent neighborhoods on
factors that may also affect educational outcomes" (Caudillo and
Torche, 2014:90). This makes assessing the effects of exposure to
community violence on children′s educational outcomes very challenging. In this study, the use of simultaneous equations solved by IV allows us to obtain an unbiased and consistent estimator of the effect of
violence on students′ achievement.
3. Case study
Our empirical study analyses the effect of criminal violence on
academic performance in Costa Rica, which is a novelty. There are a
number of reasons why Costa Rica is suitable for our empirical analysis.
First, it is located in a region where the issue of violence has a
singular importance in both social and economic terms. The levels of
violence of Costa Rica are far from those of other Central American
countries, such as El Salvador, Honduras or Guatemala (with 61.8, 41.7
and 26.1 intentional homicides per 100 000 inhabitants, see UNODC,
2020). However, in recent years, Costa Rica has experienced an
alarming increase in homicides. The rate of intentional homicides in
Costa Rica in 2017 (12.3 per 100 000 inhabitants) doubled both the
world average and the rate presented by the country a decade earlier
(UNODC, 2020). Young people and adolescents are especially vulnerable to this violence. The group of youths aged between 15 and 29 years
has one of the highest rates of intentional homicides (20.3 victims per
100 000 inhabitants) and almost one third of these deaths were related
to drug trafficking (Comisión Técnica Interinstitucional sobre
Estadísticas de Convivencia y Seguridad Ciudadana, 2017).
In addition, it shares, with other countries in the region, certain
endemic characteristics such as the incipient presence of transnational
organised crime and a pronounced inequality (Inter-American
Development Bank IDB, 2012).
Finally, the empirical analysis that we carry out requires homogeneous information with a high degree of geographical disaggregation
for a large set of variables that include demographic, social and economic data. Costa Rica is one of the countries in the region with more
accurate statistics, both with regards to the variety of the information
collected and in the methodological quality of its elaboration. For all
these reasons, Costa Rica is a highly suitable case for analysis.

4.2. Variables
Next, we describe the variables and sources we use in our empirical
study, that is based in a system of two equations. The PISA dataset is the
source from which we take the dependent variable and the predictors
for the first equation. The homicide rates and their socio-economic
predictors in the second equation are taken from official Costa Rican
sources. Table 2 provides the description of the variables, their sources
and years. Appendix A describes their summary statistics.

4.2.2. Second equation: the factors explaining violence
Homicide rate is the explained variable in the second equation of
the system. We measure violence through the homicide rate per 10 000
inhabitants in district k. 6 Although it is difficult to find an accurate
indicator of violence, the homicide rate is the most widely used in the
empirical literature (see Sharkey, 2010). This is because different legislations have a more homogeneous definition of homicide than of other
crimes and, thus, the data is more easily comparable. Additionally, its
measurement is more accurate, because other crimes, especially misdemeanours, are often not reported to the police for various reasons,
such as inconveniences in the presentation of complaints and the lack of
trust in the local police force and/or the judicial system.
The election of the explanatory variables of homicide rate follows
existing empirical research in criminology and is based on the empirical
study carried out by Gimenez, Svitková, Tkacheva, and Barrado (2020),
who analyse the determinants of violence measured by homicide rate in
Costa Rica. In their article, the reader can find a broad justification for
the election of the variables that we use as predictors for homicide rate
in this second equation. These are: number of police officers deployed
per 10,000 inhabitants, population density, annual population growth,
ratio of men to women, percentage of immigrants, a poverty index of
unsatisfied basic needs, the unemployment rate, the average years of
schooling (+15) and the percentage of population in a district living in
an urban area. It can be considered a standard model in the literature
on violence. The sources of these variables are the Instituto Nacional de
Estadística y Censos (INEC) and the Instituto Geográfico Nacional (IGN) of
Costa Rica.

4.2.1. First equation: the educational production function
Our empirical analysis is based on the use of the educational production function (EPF). This function establishes a statistical relation of
an empirical nature between a set of learning factors and academic
achievement.
The dependent variables in our study are students′ achievement in
maths, reading and science, measured by test scores in PISA. The
election of our control variables can be considered standard in the literature and follows the one proposed by Hanushek et al. (2013) and
that has been previously applied to study PISA results in Costa Rica (see
Gimenez, Martín-Oro, & Sanaú, 2018). The variables, whose source is
also the PISA database, can be grouped into three categories: 1) individual characteristics of the student; 2) socio-economic characteristics of the student′s household; and 3) characteristics of the school. In
addition to this model, we add a variable that quantifies violence in the
districts where the schools are situated.
As individual characteristics of the students, we include age, gender
and country of birth. Girls usually outperform boys in reading, but
underperform boys in technical subjects (OECD, 2015). Students born
abroad tend to obtain lower scores than national ones (Organization for
Economic Co-operation and Development OECD, 2012).
The variable used as a proxy of the household socio-economic and
cultural level is the PISA composite score ESCS (economic, social and
cultural status). Literature has found a positive association between
socio-economic status and students′ performance (Wossmann, 2005;
Tian, 2006; Schutz et al., 2008; Currie and Goodman, 2010; Rothstein,
2010).
The school characteristics included as control variables are number
of students, school ownership, school location, the lack of specialized
teachers, and school autonomy. A substantial proportion of the variation of test scores within countries participating in PISA is associated
with the school students attend and their teaching practices (Freeman
and Viarengo, 2014; Castro Aristizabal et al., 2018). Larger schools can
benefit from economies of scale but in the small ones is easier to build
social capital among children and parents (Carolan, 2012). Empirical
analysis with PISA data reveals a positive link between private education and results in cognitive tests (Fuchs and Wossmann, 2007; Castro
Aristizabal, Giménez, & Pérez Ximénez-de-Embún, 2017). Autonomy in
the management of the centre has a decisive importance on the students′ results (Clark, 2009). With respect to the school location, this

5. Exposure to crime and academic achievement: empirical results
Our results, shown in Table 4, are consistent with previous empirical evidence. The first-stage regressions of the LIML report the reduced form of the right-hand side endogenous variable, the homicide
rate. They show that the homicide rate is positively related to the percentage of immigrants, poverty, the unemployment rate and the percentage
of urban population. The number of police officers, population density, the
annual population growth, and the ratio of men to women are negatively
related to the homicide rate. Average years of schooling (+15) is not
significant. At the bottom of the table, the LM underidentification test
indicates that we can reject the null hypothesis of underidentification at
any standard level of significance because the model is identified. Additionally, we show the results of the weak identification tests. The
instruments used are exogenous and strong for all the standard critical
values of the tests.
The estimation of Equation (1) of the system of equations (solved
with the LIML estimator) shows that women score lower in maths and
science, and men in reading. Students born in Costa Rica obtain better
results than immigrant students. The coefficient of the PISA economic
social and cultural status index has a positive sign and is significant. With
respect to the school characteristics, the coefficients of school size,
shortage in teachers and school autonomy are positive and significant; 9
the coefficient of share of government funding is negative and significant;
and privately operated and schools′community location present mixed and
ambiguous effects. 10
With respect to our variable of study, exposure to crime, we note
that homicide rate has a negativeand significant effect on academic

The literature also shows that the impact of violence is heterogeneous and may depend on some socio-economic factors: gender and
differences in wealth are analysed in previous studies. Table 5 shows

the level of development of the districts (detailed results are available
in Tables B1 and B2 of Appendix B).
In the case of gender, we obtain evidence of greater effect of

exposure to crime on the academic achievement of male students in
science, but not in maths and reading. This is, ceteris paribus, there are
not clear differences in the effect of exposure to crime between boys
and girls.
Regarding the heterogeneous effects depending on the level of development, it is necessary to point out that the focus of the literature
has shifted over the years from a one-dimensional measure, based on

the United Nations Development Program approach, the focus has
changed from one that only considers income to one that considers
enhancing capabilities and enlarging human choices. Accordingly, we
use a multidimensional approach, summarized in Fig. 1, which covers

Table 5 shows that the interaction effect of the homicide rate and the
social development index is negative and significant in the three areas.
Students attending schools situated in districts with lower social development (less than the modal value) suffer a bigger impact of crime.
An increase of one standard deviation in the number of homicides per
10 000 inhabitants is associated with a reduction in the maths score of
11.9 points, 18.5 in reading and 11.8 in science, comparing with its
peers in highest development districts.
In sum, the results obtained in our research show that living in a
violent environment is linked to lower student performance. Our results
are in line with the empirical results found in the literature that focuses
on Latin America. In the case of Mexico, Michaelsen and Salardi (2015)
find that an increase of one homicide per 1 000 inhabitants decreases
the average score in ENLACE exams by 3 points, while Romano (2015)
finds, with the same source, that an increase of one unit in the number
of homicides per 10 000 inhabitants reduces average standardized test
scores by between 0.003 and 0.014 standard deviations. Finally, Monteiro & Rocha, 2017 observe that exposure to violence associated with
drug gangs leads to a reduction of 0.054 standard deviations in the
math test scores of the children of Rio de Janeiro.
With respect to the effects of exposure to violence depending on the
socio-economic factors, our results are also in line with previous studies
in international literature. First, in the existing evidence, the effect of
gender is inconclusive. While some studies find a significant effect of
violence on boys (Blattman and Annan, 2010; Kecmanovic, 2013,
Verwimp and Van Bavel, 2013; Gerardino, 2014), others show a pronounced effect in the case of girls (Swee, 2009; Shemyakina, 2011;
Monteiro & Rocha, 2017). This mixed evidence is conditioned by differences in the institutional environments and in the characteristics of
the violence studied (Singh & Shemyakina, 2016).
Second, we find that students who attend schools situated in districts with low social development are more affected by exposure to
crime. These results are consistent with Romano's (2015) which, in the
case of Mexico, shows that the impact of homicides is greater on schools
located in neighbourhoods with low socio-economic status. Also for
Mexico, Jarillo et al. (2016) find that both exposure to and persistence
of criminal violence reduce math test scores and this effect is more
intense for schools located in poor urban settings.

community violence is often correlated with many socio-economic
factors that, at the same time, have been shown to have harmful effects
on students′ outcomes (Aizer, 2008; Burdick-Will, 2013). As a result, it
is difficult to disentangle the effect of violence from its correlates
(Caudillo and Torche, 2014). In our article, focused on the case of Costa
Rica, the use of systems of equations solved using IV has allowed us to
capture the simultaneous relationship between violence and outcomes
and to obtain an unbiased and consistent estimator of the effect of
violence on students′ results. We conclude that the homicide rate has a
negative and significant impact on students′ achievement, measured as
their test scores in PISA. This effect is particularly important for students who attend schools situated in districts with a low level of social
development.
These results make it clear that crime prevention has to be a key
element in the design of a broad education policy: particular attention
should be given to the most vulnerable districts. Authorities in Costa
Rica clearly advocates for this comprehensive approach, aiming to
protect children and youths by incorporating civil society into a systematic campaign to counter violent crime. Some of the proposed
measures has a clear local orientation, being schools a main target of
the policies to control crime violence. They include strengthening local
governance structures, preventing gang violence, increasing the
number of security forces in key points where youths concentrate and
controlling light arms and firearms (Ministerio de Justicia y Paz, 2011).
Among the limitations of our study, we have to point out that data
on homicides refers to the districts where the schools are located. In
most cases these are the districts where the students live, but sometimes
this is not the case. However, violence close to the school has a decisive
influence on students, as students spend a significant part of their time
in their schools and carry out numerous extracurricular and leisure
activities in the area around the school.
With respect to lines for further research, our paper provides empirical evidence and econometric techniques that may be useful to other
countries in which violence is a key concern. Further research in this
area could apply the model and the analytical methodology to other
case studies in the region, as new databases with education, crime and
socio-economic variables become available. The study of the factors
through which crime affects academic achievement is, additionally, an
important area for future research, as is a more fine-grained extension
of the model that we propose. Among these factors, some key aspects to
focus on could be how exposure to crime affects the motivation, concentration and attendance of both students and teachers. Linked to this,
to introduce alternative measures of crime could be an important
contribution, because not all the effects may be linked to extreme
violence.
