Students’ reading enjoyment as a multilevel mediator of the relationship
between parental reading enjoyment, teachers’ stimulation of reading, ICT
factors, and reading literacy
Sojin Yoon , JiYoon Kim , Sehee Hong *
Department of Education, Korea University, Seoul, Republic of Korea

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Reading literacy
Enjoyment of reading
Multilevel structural equation modeling
PISA 2018

Background: Reading literacy is essential for educational success and effective functioning in modern society,
especially in an era dominated by digital technology. This study aimed to explore the relationships between
student-level and school-level variables and reading literacy. While prior research has highlighted various individual, household, and school-related factors contributing to reading competence, the structural relationships
among these variables have not been thoroughly examined.
Methods: This study utilized data from 6,650 Korean students who participated in PISA 2018. Multilevel
Structural Equation Modeling (MSEM) was applied to examine the relationships between student- and schoollevel variables and reading literacy. Variables analyzed included parental reading enjoyment, students’ ICT
interest, and teachers’ stimulation of reading, and school ICT capacity.
Results: At the student level, parental reading enjoyment, and students’ ICT interest were associated with both
students’ reading enjoyment and literacy. Furthermore, the relationships of parental reading enjoyment and
students’ ICT interest with reading literacy were mediated by students’ reading enjoyment. At the school level,
teachers’ stimulation of reading was positively related to students’ reading enjoyment, and schools’ ICT capacity
was associated with reading literacy. In addition, the relationship between teachers’ stimulation of reading and
reading literacy was mediated by students’ reading enjoyment.
Conclusions: The findings underscore the pivotal role of reading enjoyment in mediating the effects of parental
enjoyment of reading, students’ ICT interest and teachers’ stimulation of reading on reading literacy. These
results suggest that strategies to enhance reading literacy should prioritize fostering students’ enjoyment of
reading through collaborative efforts involving parents, teachers, and schools.

Introduction
Reading literacy is considered a fundamental proficiency not only for
overall educational achievement but also for individuals to function as
successful members of society in various aspects of life (OECD, 2019e).
In the modern information society, adolescents’ use of information and
communication technology (ICT) in their learning and daily lives has
become commonplace. As reported by the OECD (2019a), 95 % of adolescents use the Internet without difficulty at home. With the widespread availability of digital devices and the Internet, there is now an
environment where an abundance of information is easily accessible.
Therefore, in this flood of information, it has become increasingly
crucial for adolescents to cultivate reading competence that involves

properly selecting information aligned with their objectives and
assessing the reliability of selected information (Kim, 2012; Son et al.,
2020).
In recent years, several studies have emphasized that students’
reading literacy is influenced by a combination of psychological,
behavioral, and contextual factors, such as their enjoyment of reading,
confidence in reading ability, ICT familiarity, and parental or teacher
support (Kang & Yum, 2013; Son et al., 2020; Zhai et al., 2024). These
findings suggest that reading literacy cannot be understood solely as a
skill internal to the learner but rather as the outcome of multilevel
ecological factors. For instance, Son et al. (2020) found that students’
enjoyment of reading significantly predicted reading performance
across diverse school contexts, while Kang and Yum (2013)

demonstrated that both student- and school-level factors—including

In a computer-based extension of the Progress in International
Reading Literacy Study (ePIRLS) 2016 assessment report, the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA)
identified reading competence as the most significant influence on
digital reading skills (Mullis et al., 2017). They reported that reading
competence significantly contributes to Internet information exploration and understanding of content. Additionally, the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA) recognizes reading literacy as
essential for knowledge accumulation and potential capability development. PISA defines reading literacy as the ability to engage in various
reading activities, such as understanding, utilizing, and evaluating texts,
achieving personal goals, and participating in social activities (OECD,
2019a).
In other words, it goes beyond the mere ability to read and
comprehend texts, encompassing the capacity to think about and infer
from the content and express thoughts triggered by the reading. Based
on this definition, the OECD considers reading literacy a core competency of the 21st century, consisting of critical and creative thinking and
collaborative problem-solving skills (Griffin et al., 2012; OECD, 2005).
Moreover, the increasing digitalization of text consumption environments demands that educational research not only address
individual-level competencies but also examine broader contextual
variables, including those at the classroom and school levels, that shape
students’ reading practices and literacy development.
South Korea has maintained high PISA scores in reading literacy for
an extended period. However, there has been a decline in overall
reading literacy levels since the notable outcomes of PISA 2009,
particularly evidenced by a significant decrease in average scores of
reading achievement in PISA 2018 (Cho et al., 2019). Consequently, the
OECD categorized South Korea as a nation with continually decreasing
reading achievement (OECD, 2019b). One potential explanation for this
trend is the increasing prevalence of digital media-based reading activities driven by the development of an information society. Previous
studies indicated that reading through digital media may have an
adverse impact on overall reading performance compared to reading
printed material, potentially leading to a decline in reading literacy
(Clinton-Lisell, 2021; Eyre, 2017). Especially for South Korean students,
the accessibility of reading through digital media has increased significantly due to the high level of ICT development (International Telecommunication Union, 2017). In this context, it is necessary to consider
the reasons for the decline in reading literacy among South Korean
students and explore possible strategies to address this issue.
To identify effective factors associated with reading achievement, it
is essential to consider both student-level and school-level variables, as
previous research has demonstrated their unique and interactive effects
on literacy outcomes. For example, student-level indicators such as
reading enjoyment, ICT interest, and self-efficacy (Chung & Park, 2022),
and school-level factors such as teacher support and school ICT environment (Son et al., 2020) have all been shown to significantly influence
reading literacy. Accordingly, this study adopts a multilevel perspective
to investigate key predictors across individual and institutional
dimensions.
Furthermore, this study focuses specifically on Korean data from the
PISA 2018 assessment due to its distinctive national context. South
Korea presents a unique combination of historically high reading literacy performance and a recent statistically significant decline in scores,
making it an important case for investigating the underlying factors

contributing to literacy outcomes (OECD, 2019b). Moreover, Korea’s
intensive ICT integration, strong parental involvement, and early
adoption of digital learning environments (International Telecommunication Union, 2017; Kim, 2012) offer a particularly relevant setting for
examining the complex interactions between psychosocial and contextual variables that shape students’ reading literacy. While cross-national
comparisons may yield broader generalizability, this study aims to
provide context-specific insights that can inform future research and
policy directions.
Enjoyment of reading and reading literacy
With the increasing emphasis on the importance of reading competence, research on reading achievement has focused on individual
characteristics and on household, teacher, and school factors (Rowe,
1995; Son et al., 2020). For individual characteristics, among the many
features of students considered as influencing factors of reading
competence, enjoyment of reading has been suggested as a key predictor
of reading literacy in several studies (Lewis & Teale, 1980; Mantle-Bromley, 1995; McKenna & Kear, 1990). Lee and Kim (2013) found
enjoyment of reading to be a common factor at the student level among
six countries achieving the highest scores in reading literacy in PISA
2009. Also, in research that applied machine learning techniques to
identify critical predictors of reading literacy, reading enjoyment was
selected from PISA 2018 data (Chung, 2022; Son et al., 2020). Moreover,
Kang and Yum (2013) examined the effects of variables on reading
achievement in PISA 2009 using multi-level analyses and found that the
reading enjoyment was a significant variable of reading literacy at the
school level. Enjoyment of reading, or reading for pleasure, refers to the
current and future act of reading with one’s own free will based on an
interest in reading (Clark & Rumbold, 2006). From this point of view, it
is possible to conclude that enjoyment in reading at student- and
school-levels is strongly related to improving reading proficiency.
Relationship between student factors, enjoyment of reading, and reading
literacy
Adolescents’ positive attitudes and motivation toward reading are
not developed in a short period, and they are particularly influenced by
household factors, especially parental influences. In South Korea, where
there is a high level of parental interest in children’s education and
academic achievements, parents’ perceptions and attitudes can impact
children’s learning attitudes, achievements, and motivation. In the
reading context, Klauda (2009) found that parents’ attitudes, beliefs,
and values regarding reading can influence their children’s reading attitudes and motivation. Sonnenschein et al. (2000) reported that when
parents value reading, they can support their children in developing
positive attitudes and motivation towards reading. Notably, among
parental factors, parents’ enjoyment of reading has been shown to have
a positive and direct relationship not only with students’ cognitive
achievement, such as reading proficiency, but also affective achievement, including the enjoyment of reading and perception of reading
(Clark & Rumbold, 2006; Kim, 2023). Thus, this study hypothesized that
parents’ enjoyment of reading can affect children’s reading enjoyment
and reading literacy.
Although parental characteristics are commonly considered part of
the family-level context in ecological models (e.g., Bronfenbrenner,
1979), this study adopted the PISA framework, where such information
was derived from student background questionnaires and directly linked
to individual student responses (Klauda, 2009; OECD, 2019d). Since
each student in the dataset reported a unique response about their
parents’ reading behavior, this variable was treated as a student-level
indicator in accordance with multilevel modeling practice for nested
data structures (Hox, 1998).

importance of reading enjoyment on reading literacy has been supported
in previous studies, to date no study has explored the relationship between student factors, school factors, students’ reading enjoyment, and
reading performance simultaneously using multilevel analyses. Therefore, based on the data of PISA 2018, this study applied a multilevel

the factors explaining reading literacy at both the student and school
levels. In other words, this study aimed to verify the influence of individual characteristics (parents’ enjoyment of reading, students’ interest
in ICT), teacher characteristics (teachers’ stimulation of reading,
teachers’ efficacy), and school characteristics (school’s ICT capacity) on
reading literacy, with a particular focus on the mediating role of reading
enjoyment of students. As explained earlier, parental reading enjoyment
was modeled at the student level, following PISA data linkage and
multilevel modeling guidelines (Hox, 1998; OECD, 2019d). The research
questions reflecting this focus are as follows:
Research Question 1: At the student level, are parental enjoyment
of reading, students’ interest in ICT, and students’ enjoyment of
reading associated with students’ reading literacy?
Research Question 2: At the student level, does students’ enjoyment
of reading mediate the relationship between parental enjoyment of
reading and students’ interest in ICT on reading literacy?
Research Question 3: At the school level, are teacher characteristics
(teachers’ stimulation of reading, teachers’ efficacy), school characteristics (schools’ ICT capacity), and students’ enjoyment of
reading associated with reading literacy?
Research Question 4: At the school level, does students’ enjoyment
of reading mediate the relationship between teacher characteristics
(teachers’ stimulation of reading, teachers’ efficacy) and school
characteristics (schools’ ICT capacity) on reading literacy?

Relationship between school factors, enjoyment of reading, and reading
literacy
In their comparison of characteristics related to reading achievement
in PISA 2009, Cho et al. (2011) attributed the most significant variation
among nations to teacher influence. This finding indicates that teacher
factors, such as learning and psychological variables, play a crucial role
in influencing reading achievement. Previous studies reported that
teachers’ positive behaviors in class strengthen students’ engagement in
education and relevant courses (Sherab, 2013; Zepke & Leach, 2010). In
the reading context, according to Urfali-Dadandi and Dadandi (2022),
teachers’ behaviors encouraging students’ reading engagement significantly affected reading achievement, and students’ reading enjoyment
mediated significantly the relationship between teachers’ encouraging
behaviors and students’ reading achievement. Also, teacher’s efficacy,
defined as a self-belief about the ability to impact student learning, is
known as one of the main influencing factors on students’ achievement
(Ashton & Webb, 1982; Moore & Esselman, 1992). However, there is
little research to explore whether teachers’ efficacy is significantly
related to academic success through students’ academic interests,
especially in reading. Based on the results of previous studies, this study
hypothesized that teachers’ stimulation of reading and teacher efficacy
have a significant effect on students’ reading literacy through students’
interest in reading.
Moreover, in the digital environment, it is possible to increase the
high frequency of reading activities using ICT with the greater number
of ICT devices owned by the school. Previous research regarded
educational ICT resources in schools as crucial for student academic
performance (Naik et al., 2020; Zhong, 2011). In particular, Tracey and
Morrow (2006) found that a rich literary environment at the school level
providing sufficient opportunities to encounter various types of reading
materials can impact students’ intrinsic motivation in reading. It is
therefore possible that students with high intrinsic motivation in reading
influenced by class and school environment tended to engage in reading
activities, thereby increasing opportunities for improving reading proficiency. Since the high frequency of reading activities using ICT is likely
to increase with the greater number of ICT devices owned by the school,
the present study included the ICT capacity of schools as the influencing
factor on the enjoyment of reading and reading literacy. It is hypothesized that the possession of ICT devices in schools can impact on the
enjoyment of reading and reading literacy by improving students’
reading activities.

Method
Data
The study utilized the PISA 2018 dataset, a comprehensive international assessment evaluating academic performance in mathematics,
reading, and scientific and various social and environmental factors
among 15-year-old students across OECD and partner countries. It includes student and parent surveys regarding their backgrounds, as well
as school surveys completed by teachers or principals. This study
involved a secondary analysis of the publicly available PISA 2018 data,
which are fully anonymized, ensuring the privacy of individual students
and schools. Therefore, no ethical review by a research ethics committee
was required for this study. The analysis adhered to ethical standards for
secondary data research. Among 78 countries participating in PISA
2018, data from South Korea was retrieved to analyze the influence of
backgrounds and educational and environmental variables at the student and school level on students’ reading literacy in South Korea. Data
from 6,650 students (52.0 % male, 48.0 % female) from 188 schools
were analyzed.

Control variables
To address potential confounding effects, several control variables
were incorporated at both the student and school levels. First, gender
and ICT competency were controlled for at both student and school
levels, as prior research has reported gender differences in reading
achievement (Choi & Park, 2012; Kang, 2015; Lee & Ku, 2019), attitudes
toward reading (Kim, 2005), and engagement in reading as a leisure
activity (McKenna et al., 1995). Research has also shown significant
associations between ICT competency, reading enjoyment, and reading
achievement (Gubbels et al., 2020; Lee & Wu, 2012). Second, economic,
social, and cultural status (ESCS), as defined in the PISA data, was also
included at both levels. ESCE is a widely recognized crucial background
factor associated with reading performance (Chiu & Chow, 2015; OECD,
2019e). Finally, at the school level, school type (public vs private) was
added, given its established links to differences in reading achievement
and school resources such as peers and teachers characteristics
regarding the reading (OECD, 2019d). Both ESCS and ICT competency
were grand-mean centered.

2) Enjoyment of reading
Enjoyment of reading was measured with five self-report items: “I
read only if I have to,” “Reading is one of my favorite hobbies,” “I like
talking about books with other people,” “For me, reading is a waste of
time,” and “I read only to get information that I need.” The answers were
scored on a four-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree,” 2 =
“disagree,” 3 = “agree,” and 4 = “strongly agree”), and the same items
were administered to both students and parents. Three questions were
reverse coded so that a higher score means a higher reading enjoyment.
The reliability of students’ enjoyment of reading (Cronbach’s alpha) was
.819, and that of parents was .761.
3) ICT interest

Data analysis

The ICT interest of students was measured by six items: “I forget
about time when I’m using digital devices,” “The Internet is a great
resource for obtaining information I am interested in (e.g. news, sports,
dictionary),” “It is very useful to have social networks on the Internet,” “I
am really excited discovering new digital devices or applications,” “I
really feel bad if no Internet connection is possible,” and “I like using
digital devices.” Students answered with how much they agreed with the
statement on a four-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree,” 2 =
“disagree,” 3 = “agree,” 4 = “strongly agree”). The Cronbach’s alpha of
the ICT interest questionnaire was .826.

Since this study utilized multilevel data with students nested in
schools, a multilevel structural equation model (MSEM) was used to
analyze the structural relationship of reading literacy with selected
variables from the student and school levels. The sampling weight
provided in the PISA data was applied to account for the complex
sampling design and unbiased parameter estimates. The overall goodness of fit of the model was checked at both the student and school levels
using the four-step method proposed by Ryu and West (2009). In the first
step, a saturation model was set for the between-group level, and a base
model was analyzed for the within-group level. A saturated model assumes that all variables are correlated, while a baseline model assumes
that all variables are unrelated. In the second step, the saturation model
was analyzed at the between-group level and the theoretical model (the
research model) at the within-group level. The chi-square values and
degrees of freedom derived from these two processes were used to
calculate the goodness of model fit at the within-group level. The third
step analyzed the saturation model at the within-group level and the
base model at the between-group level. The final step analyzed the
saturated model at the within-group level and the theoretical model at
the between-group level, and the between-group fit was calculated using
the same values from the two processes as the within-group model fit.
Based on those four steps, this study calculated CFI, TLI, and RMSEA
indices at both levels using Rye and West’s method except for SRMR,
which the results of at both levels were provided in the results of the
Mplus statistical program (Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2017). When the
MSEM model was tested in Mplus, the parameters were estimated by
default through the Maximum Likelihood estimation with Robust (MLR)
method. However, since the method proposed by Ryu and West (2009) is
based on the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation method, the ML
method was used to estimate the parameters and the goodness of fit for
the overall research model in this study. In addition, missing values were
handled by the Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) method,
which is reported to perform excellently in multilevel structures (Larsen,
2011).
Mediation effects were examined using Monte Carlo (MC) 95 %

possible to differentiate the number of between-level groups and individuals per between-level group from the original sample. Conversely,

4) Teachers’ stimulation of reading engagement
Teachers’ stimulation of reading engagement was measured with
four items that asked students how often the following occurred in their
language-of-instruction lessons: “The teacher encourages students to
express their opinion about a text,” “The teacher helps students relate
the stories they read to their lives,” “The teacher shows students how the

The answers were scored on a four-point Likert scale from 1 = “never or
hardly ever” to 4 = “every lesson.” The Cronbach’s alpha of the teachers’
stimulation of reading engagement was .979.
5) Teachers’ self-efficacy in instructional settings
In the optional teacher questionnaire, PISA 2018 administered four
items to measure teachers’ self-efficacy in instructional settings: “Craft
good questions for my students,” “Use a variety of assessment strategies,” “Provide an alternative explanation, for example, when students
are confused,” and “Implement alternative instructional strategies in
[my] classroom.” Teachers responded on a four-point Likert scale (1 =
“not at all,” 2 = “to some extent,” 3 = “quite a bit,” and 4 = “a lot”). The
reliability of these items was .861.

The principal of each school answered the schools’ ICT capacity

devices connected to the Internet is sufficient,” “The number of digital
devices for instruction is sufficient,” “Teachers have the skills to integrate digital devices in instruction.” Those items were scored on a fourpoint Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree,” 2 = “disagree,” 3 = “agree,”
and 4 = “strongly agree”), with a reliability of .916.

In other words, as students’ interest in ICT increases, their enjoyment of
reading decreases, potentially leading to lower reading literacy. While
direct research on the relationship between interest in ICTs and reading
enjoyment is scarce, some of the ICT items in PISA used in this study (“I
forget about time when I’m using digital devices”, “I really feel bad if no
Internet connection is possible”) suggest a potential connection to
overuse or dependence on ICTs. This finding relates previous studies
showing that excessive use of ICTs or a preference for ICTs negatively
affects reading habits (Çizmeci, 2017; Gezgin et al., 2023). In essence, a
stronger preference for ICTs may shift attention to other online activities
(e.g., social media, online games) rather than reading in leisure time.
This shift could result in a diminished interest in reading, contributing to
a potential decline in reading literacy.
However, the direct effect of ICT interest on reading literacy was
positively significant. This seemingly contradictory finding can be
explained by the dual role of ICT interest. On the one hand, higher ICT
interest appears to reduce reading enjoyment, as students may devote
more time and attention to screen-based activities rather than traditional print reading (Sarimanah et al., 2022). On the other hand, ICT
interest may directly enhance reading literacy by fostering digital text
navigation, information search, and comprehension skills that are
increasingly relevant in today’s learning environments. It is also noteworthy that PISA 2018 was administered primarily in a computer-based
format (OECD, 2019b), suggesting that students with stronger ICT interest and greater familiarity with digital platforms may have been
better equipped to perform in this test environment including reading
literacy. Consistent with this interpretation, Lee and Wu (2012) reported
that subjective aspects of ICT, such as attitude and confidence, were
positively related to the reading literacy both directly and indirectly
through online reading. Similary, Lin et al. (2023) found that motivational aspects of ICT, including ICT interest, were positively related to
digital reading, wheras actural ICT use at school was negatively associated with digital reading. Taken together, these results suggest that
reading may not be students’ initial intent when working with computers, but due to positive attitudes toward ICT and frequent exposure to
online material, they may unconsciously spend considerable time
engaging in diverse reading contexts, which can contribute to higher
reading literacy (Lee & Wu, 2012).

This study applied MSEM analysis to examine the structural relationships of various student and school-level variables affecting
reading literacy. The results of the study are as follows.
First, at the student level, parents’ enjoyment of reading significantly
positively associated with both students’ enjoyment of reading and their
reading literacy. This finding aligns with previous research showing that
parents’ positive attitudes toward reading affects children’s reading
ability (Baker, 2003; Baker & Scher, 2002; Govorova et al., 2023; Ho &
Lau, 2018). If parents love to read, their children tend to imitate them
from an early age, and this high motivation to read may have helped
their children improve their literacy. This finding indicates the importance of the parent’s role as a mirror for their children’s reading habit
formation and development for overall reading literacy (Baker, 2003).
The significant positive effect of reading enjoyment on reading literacy
in this study is also consistent with previous research (Clark & Rumbold,
2006). Enjoyment of reading is related to motivation and has consistently been identified as a strong predictor of reading literacy (Boyask
et al., 2023; Clark & Rumbold, 2006; Tavsancil et al., 2019). Furthermore, this study found a significant mediating effect of children’s
enjoyment of reading on the effect of parents’ reading enjoyment on
children’s reading literacy. This mediation effect suggests that seeing
parents enjoying reading in the home shapes children’s view that
enjoyment is an essential reason for reading (Baker & Scher, 2002),
which in turn leads them to enjoy reading themselves, and that the
positive mindset and habits formed at an early age lead to increased
reading literacy (Baker, 2003). It is also possible that if parents enjoy
reading, they read to their children or engage in activities with books
more frequently. This high parents’ reading engagement may have
sparked an interest in reading in their children, leading to increased
reading frequency, which in turn helped improve literacy (Ho & Lau,
2018). In summary, the notable significance of the mediating effect of
students’ reading enjoyment on the relationship between parental
enjoyment of reading and reading literacy in this study implies that a
supportive home environment plays a crucial role in cultivating students’ reading enjoyment.
Second, students’ interest in ICT was identified negatively associated
with their reading enjoyment, and reading enjoyment significantly
mediated the relationship between interest in ICT and reading literacy.

Although our study focused on Korean students, its implications
extends globally. Worldwide evidence indicates that students’ reading
literacy has declined in recent years (OECD, 2019e), a phenomenon
closely related to digital distraction (Gezgin et al., 2023; Honma et al.,
2022), the rapid spread of short-form content (Chiencharoenthanakij
et al., 2025; Chiossi et al., 2023; Haliti-Sylaj & Sadiku, 2024),
video-based learning and entertainment (Gong & Tao, 2024), and
decreasing enjoyment of learning (Wilkinson et al., 2020). South Korea,
with exceptionally high adolescent smartphone ownership rate of about
97 % (Ryu et al., 2024), prolonged daily screen usage (Lee et al., 2024),
and more than 40 % in adolescent classified as being at risk of smartphone overdependence (Kim et al., 2024), serves as a critical case study
for educational challenges in the digital age. Our findings indicate that
enjoyment of reading plays a key role in enhancing reading literacy, and
that supportive family and school environments can foster this enjoyment. These results provide valuable lessons applicable to other countries facing similar reading literacy crises.

At the school level, the mediating effect of reading enjoyment on the
relationship between teachers’ stimulation of reading and reading literacy was significant. This result is in line with prior research indicating
that teachers’ support for students’ reading contributes to increasing
students’ reading enjoyment (Ho & Lau, 2018; OECD, 2019d). Teachers’
stimulation of reading leads students to engage in reading during class
actively, helping them recognize the importance of reading in their lives.
This, in turn, boosts their confidence, interest, and intrinsic motivation
to read, thereby positively influencing digital literacy (OECD, 2022).
On the other hand, teacher efficacy did not significantly affect both
students’ reading enjoyment and reading literacy, which is inconsistent
with previous studies (Guo et al., 2012; Varghese et al., 2016). This
result might be attributed to the possibility that the co-input variable,
stimulation of reading, has a more direct effect on reading than
reading-related variables. When compared to the teacher’s stimulation
variable that directly focuses on reading, the items of teacher’s
self-efficacy measured the overall use of teaching strategy. In addition,
the OECD (OECD, 2019d) also discovered that teachers’ stimulation of
reading had a more significant impact on students’ reading enjoyment
than teachers’ efficacy, even after controlling for students’ reading
performance and other teaching strategies. Therefore, to enhance students’ reading enjoyment and literacy, a more effective teaching strategy is needed to target and stimulate reading directly rather than solely
focusing on improving the overall quality of teacher-centered
instruction.
Moreover, schools’ ICT capacity had no significant effect on students’ reading enjoyment and no mediating effect. This result is inconsistent with previous studies that suggest a relationship between
schools’ ICT capacity and students’ reading enjoyment (Xiao & Hew,
2022). On the other hand, the direct negative effect of schools’ ICT capacity on reading literacy was significant. This result is similar to the
findings of Li and Petersen (2022), who reported that school ICT resources had a deleterious effect on students’ reading performance. This
finding may reflect digital distraction in well-resourced schools, which
means that students in well-equipped schools were more likely to use
ICT both in and outside the classroom (Gubbels et al., 2020), potentially
leading to a decline in linguistic achievement and literacy as they divert
attention away from school lessons and tasks (Wang & Wang, 2023). In
addition, ICT use in schools without effective classroom instruction or
regulations may further acclerate digitial distraction (Martin et al.,
2025). Therefore, rather than concentrating on ICT resources, the most
effective approach to enhance students’ reading literacy at the school
level might leverage teachers’ learning strategies to increase students’
enjoyment of reading.
Overall, the findings suggest that parents’ reading enjoyment at the
individual level and teachers’ stimulation at the school level positively
influence reading literacy by increasing students’ reading enjoyment,
highlighting the significant role of influential figures in students’ lives.
When the adults closest to students at home and school demonstrate
their enjoyment of reading, express their liking for reading, and
emphasize the importance of reading, they can instill in adolescents
positive perceptions of reading, consequently enhancing their reading
literacy. Based on this, governments and schools may consider establishing more robust connections between home and school to improve
students’ reading literacy. For example, schools could provide families
with a curated list of recommended books for parents and children,
fostering shared enjoyment and dedicating time for reading and discussion at home. At the school level, teachers could benefit from specific

Limitations and implications
This study has several limitations. First, as it was based on crosssectional data, it was impossible to test the long-term effects of individual, parental, and school variables on students’ reading literacy.
Since reading literacy is a skill that can be developed over a long period,
establishing the causal relationship between the variables by investigating the mediating effects at the same point in time requires further
research. Second, although we examined the mediating role of reading
enjoyment in the relationship between ICT interest, parental reading
enjoyment, and reading literacy, we did not test potential moderating
effects. Considering the contradictory direct and indirect effect observed
between ICT interest and reading litearcy, the inclusion of moderators
could allow for a more detailed interpretation. For example, the purpose
of ICT use (e.g., for academic learning vs. entertainment) may influence
whether ICT interest contributes positively or negatively to reading
outcomes. Future research should therefore extend our model by
incorporating such moderators to better capture the complexity of ICTrelated influences on reading literacy. Next, at the school level, items for
teacher stimulation of reading were rated by students, while teacher
self-efficacy in instruction was self-rated by teachers. Therefore, teacher
self-rated stimulation of reading and student-perceived teacher selfefficacy may differ in perspective. Future research should consider the
gap between students’ perceived teacher characteristics and teachers’
self-rated characteristics to better understand teachers’ impact on students. Finally, only Korean students were included in this study as it
aimed to examine the effects of ICT-related personal and school variables on Korean students’ reading enjoyment and reading literacy.
However, as mentioned previously, decrease in reading literacy and
enjoyment are global concerns; therefore, future studies could examine
whether the findings of this study can be replicated in different countries
and whether there are differences across countries, such as through
multiclass analysis.
Despite these limitations, this study demonstrated the mediating
effect of enjoyment of reading on the effects of student and school factors on reading literacy through MSEM analysis. By validating this
mediating effect, this study emphasizes that getting students excited
about reading is a priority for improving reading literacy. Among the
numerous factors influencing reading literacy, reading enjoyment is a
relatively easy variable to improve through active efforts by parents,
teachers, and schools compared to other socioeconomic factors (Boyask
et al., 2023). Considering the results of this study that parents’ enjoyment of reading, interest in ICT, and teachers’ stimulation of reading
affect reading literacy through reading enjoyment, it would be essential
to develop a plan to improve literacy through close collaboration between school and home. In other words, parents’ exemplary role at
