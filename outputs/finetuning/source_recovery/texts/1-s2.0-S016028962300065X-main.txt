Predicting cross-national sex differences in large-scale assessments of
students’ reading literacy, mathematics, and science achievement:
Evidence from PIRLS and TIMSS
Sandra Oberleiter *, Jonathan Fries, Laura S. Schock, Benedikt Steininger, Jakob Pietschnig
Department of Developmental and Educational Psychology, Faculty of Psychology, University of Vienna, Austria

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Sex differences
PIRLS
TIMSS
Student achievement
Reading literacy
Mathematics
Science
Macro indicators

Causes of sex differences in educational achievement have been controversially discussed in the extant literature.
It has been speculated that differing prosperity and equality of opportunities may be linked to these differences,
but conclusive empirical evidence for such effects is unavailable. Here, we present evidence for sex differences in
international large-scale assessments of reading literacy, mathematics, and science across 16 cohorts from 1995
to 2019. Our analyses of PIRLS and TIMSS reading literacy, mathematics, and science achievement data (N =
3,999,062; 90 countries) showed consistent advantages for girls in reading literacy (d range: − 0.02 to 0.66). For
mathematics and science this pattern was less unambiguous, yielding non-trivial effects in both directions (d
ranges: − 0.44 to 0.36 and − 0.50 to 0.46, respectively). Sex differences in all three domains were more pronounced in more egalitarian countries (β range 0.16 to 0.20). Higher national prosperity and educational investment predicted larger sex differences favoring fourth grade boys in mathematics and science (β range: 0.07 to
0.39) and became less meaningful with increasing student ages (β range for eighth graders: 0.17 to 0.21). In all,
our findings suggest that influences of economic macro-indicators on sex differences in educational achievement
are differentiated according to subject, indicating larger sex differences in mathematics and science in more
egalitarian and prosperous countries.

Sex differences have been observed in a majority of researched domains, spanning a range of effect sizes from small (e.g., object location
memory, negative emotions) to medium (e.g., anxiety disorders,
impulsivity) and even large (e.g., social interests, sexual violence) effects, depending on the scrutinized trait (Archer, 2019). The accumulated evidence about sex differences in cognition over the past century
suggests that they are robust in specific domains, although they do not
seem to map onto psychometric g (Reynolds, Hajovsky, & Caemmerer,
2022; for some contrasting evidence see Johnson, Carothers, & Deary,
2008). This corresponds to the ideas of the Gender Similarities Hypothesis (GSH; Hyde, 2014), which states that there are no sex differences in general cognitive ability, but that there are consistent
differences in specific cognitive domains.

4. Discussion
Here, we show evidence for cross-temporally persisting differences
in educational achievement between girls and boys that appear to be
differentiated according to the examined cognitive domain. Importantly, these achievement differences seem to be linked to different
macro-indicators of national prosperity, economic and gender (in-)
equality, and educational investment. These findings present several
points of interest, as we detail below.
Girls showed better performance in reading literacy whilst for
mathematics and science, a less unambiguous pattern emerged. Whilst
girls outperformed boys in reading across all cohorts in all countries, for
mathematics and science, no consistent pattern of sex differences in

either direction emerged. Sex-specific advantages appeared to be
differentiated according to the investigated country. Our results indicate
that these sex-related differences are linked to country-level socioeconomic factors.
First, girls showed remarkably consistent advantages compared to
boys in reading literacy across cohorts in all examined countries,
yielding merely a single data point out of 168 with an inconsistent sign
which yielded a trivial effect. This overwhelming evidence for girls’
reading literacy advantages supports results of prior findings indicating
higher girls reading achievement (e.g., Lynn & Mikk, 2009; Voyer &
Voyer, 2014). National prosperity and equality negatively predicted
girls’ advantages, indicating smaller advantages of girls in more prosperous and less egalitarian countries.
Nonetheless, girls’ reading performance was positively influenced by
higher equality in terms of (i) the gender gap in health, education,
economy, and politics and (ii) relative access to the educational system
between boys and girls. However, effects of these socioeconomic indicators were predominantly small and considerably less powerful than
their effects on mathematics and science. Furthermore, we did not find
any associations of performance differences in reading literacy with
governmental expenditure in education (see Reilly, 2012 and Giofre
et al., 2020, for similar results).
Second, on average, sex differences in mathematics and science
across cohorts were trivial in size. However, sex differences appeared to
be substantially differentiated according to the examined countries
which supports prior results of cross-national sex differences (Stoet &
Geary, 2013): Whilst in many countries, boys outperformed girls in
mathematics, effect sizes in several countries yielded girls’ advantages
across cohorts.
Our presently observed between-country variations thus support the
idea of socioeconomic macro indicators as important drivers of sex
differences in mathematics and science. In fourth graders, sex difference
variations in signs and strength were meaningfully predicted by national
prosperity and societal equality indicators. Boys’ advantages seemed to
be larger in more prosperous nations as well as more economically and
gender equal societies. The amount of public investment in education
predicted boys’ advantages in both mathematics and science positively,
yielding a medium-sized effect.
This is consistent with previous research, indicating that socioeconomic factors may be associated with sex differences in mathematics
(Giofre et al., 2020). Furthermore, individual differences are typically
observed to be more pronounced in more egalitarian environments due
to reduced environmental variability (e.g., increases in intelligence
heritabilities in more egalitarian countries; Pietschnig, Traub, Siegel, &
Pellegrini, 2021).
In contrast to our expectations, prosperity-related macro-indicators
showed less meaningful systematic influences for eighth graders in
either mathematics or science compared to fourth graders. Conceivably,
these results are a consequence of children in more prosperous and
egalitarian nations to specialize earlier in their areas of interest, thus
mitigating the impact of socioeconomic factors at older ages. We speculated, that the observed associations in eighth grade could be explained
by the interaction between a country’s prosperity or equality and the
enrollment of students in secondary schools. In comparison to fourth
graders, there are fewer countries that have compulsory schooling for
eighth graders, which leads to a restriction of variance and therefore
potentially smaller effects. However, when we reanalyzed our results
without such countries, the effects remained virtually unchanged.
Effects for grade eight were smaller in size, in most cases they yield
similar implications as those in grade four. However, whilst mathematics achievement advantages of boys appeared to be mostly driven by
more public investment in the educational system, boys’ science
achievement advantages were mostly related to higher economic
equality. Interestingly, no consistent effect was found for GDP per capita
in the eighth grade. However, this is not unexpected because GDP per
capita can be assumed to have smaller effects on scholastic achievement

compared to other prosperity-related measures such as educational
spending. Countries with the highest GDPs are not necessarily those that
invest most in their educational systems. However, based on our current
findings, we cannot be entirely certain if different changes would
manifest when school grades with different in-between intervals or
initial assessment ages were to be examined.
Our results show that national prosperity and economical as well as
gender equality appear to play an important role for achievement differences between boys and girls, particularly when it comes to mathematics in fourth graders. Consistent with previous research, sex
differences, especially in mathematics and science, seem to become
more pronounced, the wealthier and more egalitarian a country is (e.g.,
Giofre et al., 2020). The most consistent influences of any of our
investigated predictors were observed in mathematics and science for
public investment in education as well as equality for grade four, which
showed unequivocally larger sex differences in nations with larger
economic and gender equality and higher educational spending. Moreover, reading literacy seemed to be further affected by the relative access
of boys and girls to education.
The present findings of national macro-indicator effects on sex differences in reading literacy, mathematics, and science may be attributed
to differing possibilities in pursuing one’s interests depending on socioeconomic circumstances in a given environment. In other words,
individuals may have better opportunities to engage in what they are
interested in, when they are living in a rather prosperous and egalitarian
society. In this sense, more developed and prosperous countries may
allow for a greater expression of individual interests which may in turn
exacerbate sex differences, because boys and girls may differ therein
(Giofre et al., 2020).
Sex-dependent interest differences have often been explained according to biopsychosocial explanations (Miller & Halpern, 2014). In
this vein, biological differences (e.g., in brain structure and function)
can affect a student’s learning, perception, and information processing
but also cultural factors such as gender norms and stereotypes (which
were argued to be stronger in more prosperous nations; see Charles &
Bradley, 2009; however there is no consensus on this and the available
empirical evidence is weak; Howell & Klein, 2018) may influence how
boys and girls are encouraged to pursue certain subjects or activities.
Moreover, our findings suggest that national prosperity as well as
economic and gender equality are strong facilitators of mathematics and
science performance among boys in fourth, but to a considerable smaller
extent, in eighth grade.
4.1. Limitations
In the context of the present study, potential influences of countryspecific curricula on reading literacy (e.g., van Hek, Buchmann, &
Kraaykamp, 2019) were not investigated. However, we could presently
show that country-level macro indicators (e.g., GDP per capita, GGI) can
be considered to be meaningful predictors of reading literacy. It needs to
be kept in mind when interpreting effects on reading literacy, that PIRLS
subscales include a measure on reading attitudes, which is arguably not
an objective measure of reading achievement.
Of note, TIMSS-science results in the two initial cohorts (i.e., 1995
and 1999) showed a markedly different sex difference pattern than in
later cohorts. This may indicate that revisions of the original assessment
instrument may have led to a change in the properties of the observed
science (sub-)domains. Interestingly, these sex difference-specific between-countries asymmetries were more substantial for science than for
mathematics in the first two assessments, but began to behave in a
similar fashion starting from the third assessment.
5. Conclusion
We show here that the malleability of sex differences in educational
achievement is differentiated according to domain. Whilst mathematics
