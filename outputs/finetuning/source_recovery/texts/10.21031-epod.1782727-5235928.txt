Comparison of Statistical and Machine Learning Approaches for
Predicting Mathematical Literacy: Evidence from PISA 2022
Türkiye
Taner YILMAZ *

Kübra ATALAY KABASAKAL **

Abstract
This study compares statistical and machine learning methods for predicting mathematical literacy among students
in Türkiye who participated in PISA 2022. Using data on 6,427 students and 13 standardized predictors capturing
cognitive, affective, and contextual dimensions, we evaluated multiple linear regression, least absolute shrinkage
and selection operator, random forests, extreme gradient boosting, artificial neural networks, and a stacking
ensemble within a 10-fold cross-validation design. Ensemble approaches outperformed linear methods: the
stacking model achieved the highest accuracy (out-of-fold R² = .319; RMSE = .777), followed closely by extreme
gradient boosting (R² = .313) and random forests (R² = .304). Linear models yielded weaker results (multiple linear
regression R² ≈ .270; least absolute shrinkage and selection operator R² = .273). Mean absolute error values were
nearly identical across models (≈ .633–.658), with minimal between-fold variation due to rounding at three
decimals. Residual analyses indicated that ensemble models produced more stable error structures, whereas linear
methods showed stronger heteroskedasticity. Across all approaches, socioeconomic status consistently emerged
as the strongest predictor, followed by mathematics self-efficacy and disciplinary climate, underscoring the dual
roles of student beliefs and classroom environment. These findings highlight the advantages of ensemble methods
for predictive performance and variable-importance estimation, emphasizing the ongoing impact of socioeconomic
inequalities on educational outcomes.
Keywords: mathematical literacy, PISA 2022, machine learning, predictive modeling, educational data mining

Introduction
A diverse array of cognitive, social, and emotional competences is essential for individuals to participate
in society and the workforce in today's digitally altered and constantly evolving environment. The
Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD, 2024) has published publications
emphasizing these competencies, commonly known as twenty-first-century skills. Mathematical
literacy is an essential ability that empowers individuals to identify, comprehend, and apply numerical
information to address real-world challenges. Mathematical literacy is not a standalone ability; it is
intertwined with other forms of literacy, including computer, media, and visual literacy. A robust
association has been identified between mathematical and computer literacy (Ic & Tutak,
2018). Moreover, connecting mathematics literacy with information and communication technologies
(ICT) augments individuals' proficiency in utilizing technology successfully, a crucial skill in the digital
era (Moloi, 2011).
The OECD used three interrelated dimensions—content, procedure, and context—to define
mathematical literacy in the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) (OECD, 2021).
The content dimension encompasses numbers, algebra, and geometry; the process dimension
emphasizes formulating, implementing, and interpreting; and the context dimension situates tasks within
personal, social, and professional realms. This comprehensive paradigm illustrates that mathematical

Türkiye has consistently underperformed in mathematical literacy compared to the OECD average since
its initial participation in PISA in 2003. Turkish students achieved notable advancements in the 2018
and 2022 cycles, enhancing their average scores and international standings (OECD, 2023). According
to the Ministry of National Education’s report on PISA 2022, Türkiye scored 453 points in mathematics,
above the OECD average of 438, and showed one of the most consistent improvements among
participating countries since 2003. Socioeconomic status, measured through the Economic, Social, and
Cultural Status (ESCS) index, remains a key determinant of student performance. Although Türkiye’s
mean ESCS level (-1.19) is lower than the OECD average, the relationship between socioeconomic
background and achievement is weaker (12.6%) than the OECD mean (15.5%). These findings highlight
the persistent impact of socioeconomic disparities on mathematical literacy and support the need for
advanced predictive analyses that integrate cognitive, affective, and contextual factors (Ministry of
National Education [MEB], 2023; OECD, 2023).
These improvements underscore the need to employ rigorous scientific tools when analyzing the
determinants of mathematical literacy. Assessing performance predictors and the comparative benefits
of different analytical methods is crucial alongside monitoring achievement levels.
A growing corpus of research has discovered multiple predictors of mathematical performance. Shine
et al. (2023) assert that self-efficacy is a significant predictor of success, while problem-solving
approaches are directly affected by metacognitive processes (Desoete & Veenman, 2006; Flavell, 1976).
Anxiety in mathematics negatively impacts performance (Ashcraft, 2002; Karakolidis et al., 2016),
while motivation boosts engagement (Bandura, 1986). A critical factor influencing achievement is
confidence, which signifies students' perceptions of the accuracy of their responses (Morony et al.,
2013). Moreover, socioeconomic status and previous academic performance are regularly identified as
two of the most reliable predictors of educational outcomes (Duff, 2004).
In educational research, baseline models have conventionally been developed utilizing classic statistical
methods, particularly Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) (Kovacs, 2008). MLR is effective for
analyzing correlations among variables; yet, it often exhibits suboptimal performance with intricate and
high-dimensional datasets, resulting in elevated error values and diminished coefficients of
determination (Hussain, 2020). These limitations have highlighted the need for more advanced
methodologies.
In educational data mining, modern techniques such as the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection
Operator (LASSO; Tibshirani, 1996), ensemble methods like Random Forests (RF; Breiman, 2001) and
Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost; Chen & Guestrin, 2016), along with Artificial Neural Networks
(ANN; Rumelhart et al., 1986), have demonstrated enhanced predictive efficacy. LASSO improves
model parsimony by employing variable selection and reducing the hazards of overfitting. XGBoost
frequently surpasses alternative methods by enhancing gradient boosting and yielding elevated R2 values
in numerous applications, whereas RF attains robust prediction accuracy via bootstrapping and feature
randomness (Yan, 2021). Although often lacking interpretability, ANNs are proficient in modeling
nonlinear relationships across extensive and intricate datasets. Ensemble methods such as stacking seek
to mitigate these constraints—particularly the limited interpretability and potential overfitting associated
with deep learning models—by integrating robust base learners, often yielding significant enhancements
in predictive accuracy (Liu & Jia, 2022; Menahem et al., 2009).
Despite these advancements, comparative research in education remains limited. Limited research
systematically compares machine learning with traditional approaches in predicting student
achievement, particularly in mathematical literacy, but they have been examined in other fields (Lou &
Colvin, 2025). Comparisons are essential to elucidate the merits and drawbacks of each option, thereby
informing methodological and policy decisions, particularly in light of the structural complexity of
educational data (Padilla, 2023; Reinsel et al., 2022; Schubert & Gertz, 2017).
The primary purpose of this study is to conduct a comparative analysis of various statistical and machine
learning methods in predicting mathematical literacy. In this regard, the study has three main objectives:

The study evaluates model performance using three widely recognized metrics: the coefficient of
determination (R²), the root mean square error (RMSE), and the mean absolute error (MAE). R² captures

prediction errors. RMSE emphasizes larger errors more heavily, whereas MAE offers a clearer sense of
the average error across predictions.
To achieve these aims, the study specifically addresses the following research questions:
1.

In predicting mathematical literacy, how do the predictive performances of MLR, LASSO,

which approach produces the best results?

2.

In describing mathematical literacy, how do the rankings of predictor importance differ
among these approaches?

To address these questions, the study applies a range of statistical and machine learning techniques,
beginning with classical regression models.
Regression
Regression analysis is a crucial statistical instrument for investigating the connection between a
dependent variable and several predictors. MLR, first utilized almost a century ago, provides a thorough
explanation of outcomes by evaluating the combined effects of variables (Fisher, 1922; Padilla, 2023).
Its strength is in its ability to concurrently evaluate multiple elements, leading to its widespread use in
disciplines such as engineering, economics, health sciences, and education (Talib & Midi, 2009).
However, multiple linear regression relies on specific assumptions, including homoscedasticity,
linearity, independence of observations, and normality of residuals. The model's dependability decreases
when specific assumptions are breached. Abd El-Salam (2013) and Rousseeuw (1984) contend that
factors such as multicollinearity and outliers frequently skew coefficient estimates, reduce predictive
accuracy, and hinder data interpretation. Notwithstanding the efficacy of diagnostic instruments like
residual plots, variance inflation factors (VIF), and modified R², effectiveness is constrained in highdimensional or noisy data scenarios.
Robust regression methods were created to improve robustness against outliers and assumption
violations. The Huber M-estimator reduces the impact of substantial outliers while utilizing the leastsquares approach for minor errors (Huber, 1981; Huber & Ronchetti, 2009). In instances of moderate
outlier contamination, the MM-estimator—so named because it combines an initial robust M-estimator
with a subsequent efficient M-estimator—ensures accurate estimates by combining high efficiency with
robustness (Maronna et al., 2006; Yohai, 1987). While not part of the primary comparative analysis in
this study, robust regression offers methodological context and demonstrates the enhancements of MLR
intended to address its shortcomings.
Regression using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)
LASSO regression utilizes an L1 penalty in the regression framework to diminish some coefficients
toward zero, thereby producing concise and comprehensible models (Friedman et al., 2010). The
integration of regularization with variable selection is very beneficial for analyzing high-dimensional
data. Moreover, LASSO improves stability by reducing multicollinearity and enhancing generalization
performance (Efron et al., 2004; Zou & Hastie, 2005).
However, LASSO's reliance on specific optimization methods, such as coordinate descent, can make it
computationally demanding and frequently leads to the selection of only a single variable from groups
of highly correlated predictors, thereby omitting others that may still be valuable (Zou & Hastie, 2005).

The choice of the regularization parameter greatly affects performance and must be carefully adjusted
to prevent underfitting and overfitting.
Random Forest (RF)
Breiman (2001) developed RF, an ensemble technique that uses random feature selection and bootstrap
sampling to generate several decision trees (Breiman, 1996, 2001). In regression, predictions are
consolidated, while in classification, they are established through majority voting. This approach
enhances generality, minimizes volatility, and mitigates overfitting.
Ziegler and König (2014) claim that RF is characterized by its exceptional predictive accuracy,
robustness against overfitting, and appropriateness for high-dimensional problems. Its unique feature is
the capacity to generate variable importance measures, making it advantageous for both forecasting and
the identification of pertinent predictors (Genuer et al., 2010; Rothacher & Strobl, 2024). Nevertheless,
RF exhibits multiple limitations: imbalanced datasets may skew predictions towards majority classes
(Patra et al., 2021), high-cardinality categorical variables pose significant management challenges (Zhu,
2020), model interpretability declines as the number of trees increases, and training costs rise with larger
datasets (Manzali & Elfar, 2023).
Hyperparameters, including the number of trees (ntree), the number of predictors per split (mtry), and
the maximum tree depth (max_depth), substantially affect performance. Although augmenting the
quantity of trees typically reduces inaccuracy, the advantages eventually diminish (Probst & Boulesteix,
2018). Thus, cross-validation (CV) is essential for determining optimal configurations.
Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)
Chen and Guestrin (2016) created XGBoost, an exceptionally efficient version of gradient boosting.
XGBoost builds trees sequentially, with each consecutive tree correcting faults from previous iterations,
while RF generates trees simultaneously. The algorithm achieves enhanced predicted accuracy on large
and complex datasets as a result of this incremental refining.
Its benefits encompass precision, computational efficiency, and inherent regularization, all of which aid
in mitigating overfitting (Bao & Wen, 2024; Chen & Guestrin, 2016). Similar to RF, XGBoost provides
both predictive and interpretative advantages by producing changeable significance ratings. However,
the methodology is susceptible to hyperparameter selection, and poorly calibrated models may overfit
or require excessive computational resources (Priscilla & Prabha, 2020).
Grid and random search are frequently used for hyperparameter optimization in boosting methods,
though they often require substantial computational resources. Studies demonstrate that sophisticated
techniques, such as Bayesian optimization (Bergstra & Bengio, 2012; Putatunda & Rama, 2019) and
tools like Optuna (Akiba et al., 2019), improve efficiency.
Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs)
Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) are computer structures including interconnected nodes arranged in
layers, simulating biological brain systems (LeCun et al., 2015). ANNs are proficient at modeling
complex and nonlinear interactions because of their ability to rapidly discern patterns from data. They
have been efficiently employed across various sectors, including speech recognition, biotechnology,
healthcare, and finance. The ANN model used in this study was implemented using the caret and nnet
packages, which employ a single hidden layer feed-forward architecture. This design was selected to
balance model complexity and interpretability, as the study’s aim was to compare distinct modeling
approaches under similar conditions rather than to develop deep neural networks.
Nonetheless, there are drawbacks linked to ANN models as well. Comprehensive network training
necessitates considerable time and computational resources (Knisleya et al., 2007), while smaller
datasets heighten the risk of overfitting (Verma et al., 2018), and their "black-box" architecture hinders
interpretability (LeCun et al., 2015). The structural design and hyperparameters, including learning rate
(lr), number of hidden layers (n_layers), activation function (activation), and regularization parameter
(λ), affect prediction accuracy.

Stacking
Wolpert (1992) introduced the ensemble technique known as stacking, or layered generalization. It
integrates the forecasts of numerous base learners with a sophisticated meta-learner (Sagi & Rokach,
2018). Stacking frequently produces more reliable predictions by using the unique advantages of many
algorithms (Baskin et al., 2017). In practice, regression-based learners are often used as the meta-model,
whereas tree-based learners like RF and XGBoost are generally applied as base models (Zhang et al.,
2022).
Stacking can exceed the efficacy of singular models (Chatzimparmpas et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2024).
However, it necessitates careful preprocessing, prudent selection of base learners, and resourcedemanding validation techniques like k-fold CV. Stacking may result in overfitting if the base learners
exhibit insufficient diversity or if the meta-models are excessively complicated (Zheng et al., 2020;
Wang & Yue, 2019).
Method
Research Design
Using observational data, the researchers in this study employed a predictive correlational survey design
to investigate the factors that influence mathematical literacy. Multiple independent variables can be
included simultaneously in this cross-sectional design, allowing for the evaluation of their predictive
power on the target variable as well as the determination of their relative importance levels (Karasar,
2022; Sullivan, 2024). The design, which is based on statistical learning principles, facilitates the
development of highly accurate predictions while allowing the interpretation of relationships among
variables (James et al., 2021).
Participants and Data Source
Researchers looked at data from Turkish students who took the PISA 2022 exam. These students were
15 years old and were in formal secondary school. PISA (OECD, 2023) employed a two-stage stratified
sampling method to collect 7250 student questionnaires. To address potential nonresponse biases and
unequal selection probabilities, the analyses utilized student weights provided by the OECD. The
findings can be generalized to the 15-year-old student demographic in Türkiye, as these weights indicate
the population representation of each student (OECD, 2024).
Since 2015, PISA has been administered via computer. The assessment design primarily adheres to the
principles of conventional test administration, despite its limited adaptivity and partial resemblance to a
Multi-Stage Test (MST) paradigm (OECD, 2024).
This study utilized international microdata files provided to the scholarly community by the OECD. To
focus on students’ individual and contextual characteristics, they exclusively included data from student
surveys, omitting school and parent questionnaires.
Variables
In this study, the researchers derived the PVORT variable by calculating the arithmetic mean of the ten
plausible values (PVs) and employed it as the dependent variable. The OECD (2024) advocates separate
analyses of each plausible value followed by averaging the outcomes; nevertheless, some prior research
opted for a more straightforward method by averaging the plausible values to create a singular indicator
(Wu, 2005). This method yields an impartial estimate of the population mean at the group level;
however, it fails to adequately reflect the uncertainty linked to individual scores. By employing this
approach, the researchers harmonized methodological rigor with analytical practicality and expressly
recognized this constraint when interpreting the findings.
The investigation began with 24 variables extracted from the PISA 2022 Türkiye student survey. This
set was reduced to 13 predictors by integrating theoretical reasoning with empirical evidence. Initially,
the variables were reviewed in light of prior research in educational psychology and sociology, and those

Data Preprocessing
The dataset included 7,250 Turkish students, which was reduced to 7,116 after removing cases with
more than 30% missing data. Variable-level missingness was below 3% and assumed to be Missing at
Random (MAR). Missing values were imputed using the missForest algorithm. Outlier analyses
indicated heavy tails for several socio-emotional indices; after testing multiple scenarios, 5% trimming
yielded the most stable performance, resulting in a final analytic sample of 6,779 students. To ensure
scale consistency, all predictors and the dependent variable (PVORT) were re-standardized (z-scores)
based on the Türkiye subsample. Thirteen predictors were retained for all models to guarantee
comparability. Further diagnostic checks confirmed acceptable linearity, low multicollinearity (VIF =
1.05–1.34), and approximately normal residuals. Full preprocessing details, including missing data
mechanisms, trimming scenarios, and hyperparameter optimization, are provided in Appendix A.
Modeling Strategy
All analyses were conducted using R (Version 4.5.0; R Core Team, 2025). All models were trained on
the same set of 13 standardized predictors within a 10-fold CV framework implemented in the caret
package. Out-of-Fold (OOF) predictions were generated for each method, which allowed the researchers
to compare models fairly while avoiding any risk of data leakage.
As a starting point, the researchers evaluated MLR with several robust variants. Among these, Ordinary
Least Squares (OLS) model with heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors, type 3 (HC3)
adjustments emerged as the most stable reference. Building on this foundation, the researchers applied
machine learning approaches with distinct strengths: LASSO focused on variable selection, RF
contributed ensemble-based accuracy, XGBoost added boosted decision trees, and Artificial Neural
Networks (ANN) captured nonlinear relationships. Finally, the researchers developed a stacking
ensemble that combined MLR, RF, and XGBoost, which delivered the most balanced performance
across R², RMSE, and MAE.
Hyperparameter tuning was carried out within carefully defined search spaces for each machine learning
model. For RF, the researchers varied mtry values between 3 and 11, set minimum node sizes to either
5 or 10, and fixed the number of trees at 1,000. For XGBoost, the researchers used random search to
explore a wide range of parameters, including nrounds (50–500), max_depth (2–6), eta (0.01–0.3),
gamma (0–5), colsample_bytree (0.3–0.8), min_child_weight (1–20), and subsample (0.5–1.0). For the
ANN, the search space included 3 to 9 hidden neurons and weight decay values ranging from 0 to 0.01,
all tested within a single hidden-layer structure. Both grid and random search procedures in caret were
employed, and the best hyperparameters for each model were retained for analysis.
Comparison of Model Performance
Using the final analytic sample (n = 6,427) and the same set of 13 predictors, the researchers evaluated
the performance of all models under identical conditions. To ensure fairness, the same CV folds were
applied across methods, and OOF predictions were used to assess generalization. The primary evaluation
metric was R², while RMSE and MAE served as secondary indicators. Models were ranked primarily
by OOF R²; in cases of ties, the model with the lower RMSE was considered superior, followed by MAE
as a final criterion. To capture variability, CV means and standard deviations were reported for each
performance metric.
All reported outcomes are based on models optimized within the predefined hyperparameter search
spaces, minimizing the risk of bias from suboptimal configurations. This procedure ensured that
performance differences reflected the intrinsic strengths of the models rather than artifacts of parameter
tuning.

provided in Appendix B.

Determination of Variable Importance
The researchers defined variable importance in line with the logic of each method. In MLR (OLS), they
considered the absolute values of standardized beta coefficients, while in LASSO they focused on the
absolute values of coefficients at the optimal λ (Friedman et al., 2010; Tibshirani, 1996). For RF, they
relied on permutation importance scores (Wright & Ziegler, 2017), whereas in XGBoost they used gain
and cover metrics provided by the model through the caret::varImp function (Chen & Guestrin, 2016).
In the ANN model (nnet), predictor importance was derived from connection weights (Garson, 1991;
Goodfellow et al., 2016; Venables & Ripley, 2002).
To make comparisons meaningful across approaches, all importance scores were rescaled to a 0–1 range,
with the complete set of rescaled values for the 13 predictors reported in Appendix C. Each method also
generated a ranking of predictors from most to least important, which the researchers summarized in
Table 3. To evaluate how consistent these rankings were across models, they computed Spearman’s rank
correlation coefficient (Spearman, 1904). Table 3 highlights these results and shows how far patterns of
variable importance converged across methods.
Assumption Checks and Model Limitations
For MLR, diagnostic checks suggested that the relationships between predictors and the outcome were
broadly linear, with only slight deviations in the Q–Q plot. The Breusch–Pagan test (Breusch & Pagan,
1979), however, pointed to significant heteroskedasticity (BP = 222.2, df = 13, p < .001).
Multicollinearity was not an issue, as VIF values ranged from 1.05 to 1.34. Cases flagged as influential
through Cook’s D (> 4/n) and leverage (h > 2p/n) accounted for about 5.19% of the sample and were
removed. Following this adjustment, the sample size dropped to 6,427, and model performance
improved: R² increased from .2072 to .2725, RMSE fell by 9.77%, and MAE decreased by 8.58%. To
adjust for heteroskedasticity, all OLS inferential results were reported with HC3 robust covariance
estimates.
For methods such as LASSO, RF, XGBoost, ANN, and stacking—which do not rely on parametric
assumptions—the researchers nonetheless implemented several safeguards to ensure comparability.
They inspected residuals from OOF predictions, standardized predictors within each CV fold, and fixed
random seeds to enhance reproducibility (Hastie et al., 2021; Wolpert, 1992).
Despite these precautions, some design and measurement limitations remain. Because the study drew
on cross-sectional data, the findings are predictive rather than causal. National-level generalization is
constrained, as design-based weights could not be fully integrated into predictive models given PISA’s
complex sampling design. Representing mathematical performance with the mean of plausible values
(PVORT) also does not reflect individual-level uncertainty, since Rubin-type combination rules were
not applied. Moreover, variable importance scores should be interpreted cautiously, as OECD-derived
indices may contain measurement error.
There are modeling limitations as well. The search spaces for hyperparameters were necessarily
restricted, the ANN model was limited to a single hidden layer, and while stacking proved effective, it
was less interpretable than the individual models. Finally, since different methods define variable
importance differently, direct numerical comparisons across models should be approached with caution.
To improve robustness overall, the researchers applied HC3 covariance corrections, excluded influential
cases, trimmed 5% of outliers, and imputed missing data under the MAR assumption using missForest.
By evaluating performance on OOF predictions, they reduced overfitting bias and strengthened the
reliability of the reported results.

Stacking and XGBoost outperformed linear approaches (LASSO and MLR), as evidenced by
significantly higher R² values and lower RMSE values. However, differences among Stacking,
XGBoost, and RF were minimal. Appendix B provides the full details of the Friedman and Wilcoxon
comparisons.

Discussion
To model mathematical literacy, the researchers compared the predictive performance and variable
importance rankings of several approaches and drew three key conclusions.

First, the analysis showed that ensemble techniques outperformed conventional regression methods.
XGBoost also delivered strong results, but the Stacking (GLMNET) model achieved the highest
accuracy, supporting the idea that combining diverse learners enhances generalization. By contrast,
MLR and LASSO produced the weakest outcomes. These findings align with earlier studies that
documented the limitations of linear models for complex, high-dimensional educational data (Li et al.,
2016). Additional evidence from large-scale assessment contexts confirms that boosting and bagging
strategies often surpass linear regression in predictive accuracy (Chen & Guestrin, 2016; Cutler et al.,
2007). Both standard deviations and mean values highlighted these performance differences: ensemble
methods demonstrated greater stability across folds, while MLR and LASSO showed higher variability,
indicating less reliable performance.
Second, residual-fit analyses revealed systematic differences between models. Tree-based methods (RF,
XGBoost) and the ANN produced balanced residual distributions with only slight tail deviations, while
classical regression approaches (MLR, LASSO) showed stronger heteroskedasticity and deviations from
normality. The Stacking (GLMNET) model maintained histogram symmetry and avoided systematic
residual patterns, demonstrating the most stable error structure. This outcome indicates that ensemble
models not only provide superior predictive metrics but also produce more reliable error structures.
Zounemat-Kermani et al. (2021) similarly reported that ensemble methods generated more consistent
and predictable error patterns in flood prediction. Statistical tests supported these interpretations: the
Friedman test identified significant overall differences, and Wilcoxon post hoc comparisons showed that
Stacking and XGBoost significantly outperformed linear approaches. No substantial differences
emerged among RF, XGBoost, and Stacking, confirming the broadly comparable benefits of ensemble
methods.
Third, the predictor importance analysis confirmed that socioeconomic status (ESCS) remains the
strongest determinant of student achievement. This result aligns with the extensive literature on the role
of socioeconomic background in shaping educational outcomes (Agasisti & Longobardi, 2014).
Evidence from PISA further shows that disadvantaged students encounter systematic barriers (OECD,
2019; Sirin, 2005). In this study, cognitive and classroom-level variables such as mathematics selfefficacy (MATHEF21) and disciplinary climate (DISCLIM) also ranked highly, underscoring the
importance of both individual beliefs and learning environments. Affective factors (e.g., mathematics
anxiety [ANXMAT], general sense of belonging [BELONG]) and contextual variables (e.g., peer
cooperation [COOPAGR], emotional support from peers [EMOCOAGR], and sense of safety at school
[FEELSAFE]) exerted less influence overall, although the ANN model uniquely identified Family
Support (FAMSUP) as the most important predictor. This divergence illustrates how nonlinear models
can capture interaction patterns that linear and tree-based methods may overlook.
A comparison of rankings across models further underscored the importance of methodological choice
for interpretation. MLR and LASSO generated identical rankings, while RF and XGBoost produced
very similar prioritizations. ANN, by contrast, offered distinct rankings that reflected a different
perspective on variable contributions. These results highlight the value of comparing multiple
approaches to gain a comprehensive understanding, whereas relying on a single method risks providing
only a partial picture. Consistent with prior research, tree-based models such as RF and XGBoost
frequently delivered dependable importance scores (Epifanio, 2017; Janitza et al., 2016; Sotiroudis et
al., 2020). At the same time, other studies have shown that ANN and related nonlinear methods can
generate divergent rankings, reflecting their ability to capture unique interactions (de Oña & Garrido,
2014; Vecoven et al., 2020). Recent work also emphasizes the potential of combining variable
importance scores across methods to build more reliable indicators (Rusyana et al., 2024; Zheng et al.,
2021).
Taken together, the findings contribute both theoretically and practically. From a theoretical perspective,
the study demonstrates that ensemble methods serve as powerful alternatives for analyzing complex
educational data, offering higher predictive accuracy and more stable error structures than linear
regression. From a practical standpoint, socioeconomic status consistently emerges as the most critical
predictor, reinforcing the need for policies that address educational inequality and provide targeted
support to disadvantaged students. Finally, the evidence suggests that combining statistical and machine
learning models can generate complementary insights that benefit both educational research and
policymaking.

Beyond these theoretical and methodological contributions, the findings of this study also have direct
implications for educational practice in Türkiye. The strong predictive power of socioeconomic status
(ESCS) underscores the continuing influence of social and economic disparities on students’
mathematical literacy, highlighting the need for policies that promote equity across schools. Likewise,
the importance of mathematics self-efficacy and disciplinary climate points to the value of classroomlevel interventions aimed at fostering students’ confidence in mathematics and maintaining orderly,
supportive learning environments. In practice, the machine-learning models developed in this study
could serve as diagnostic tools for educators and policymakers to identify students at risk of low
performance early in the academic year. Such predictive insights would allow schools to design timely,
data-driven interventions—such as targeted tutoring, parental engagement, or socio-emotional support
programs—to narrow learning gaps and promote more equitable outcomes.
This study has several methodological limitations that also provide directions for future research.
Because the analyses were based on a cross-sectional dataset, causal inferences cannot be drawn; future
work could therefore employ longitudinal designs to explore causal relationships. In addition, the
complex sampling weights of PISA could not be fully integrated into the models, which restricts the
generalizability of the results to the national level. Future comparative analyses across countries would
strengthen the robustness of the findings. The PVORT variable, created by averaging ten plausible
values, does not fully capture individual-level uncertainty; subsequent studies could analyze each
plausible value separately and combine them using Rubin’s rules. From a modeling perspective, the
artificial neural network was restricted to a single hidden layer, and the hyperparameter search space
was limited. Exploring multi-layer architectures and broader parameter ranges could further improve
model performance. Although the stacking model demonstrated strong predictive power, its
interpretability remained limited. Future studies could apply advanced explainability tools, such as
SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP; Lundberg & Lee, 2017), to enhance interpretability across
methods. Finally, measurement errors in derived indices should be considered when interpreting
variable importance scores, as these indicators may contain systematic bias.

Appendix A. Data Preprocessing Details
A1. Initial Screening
• Continuous and numerical variables (dependent and independent) were checked to ensure that
observed ranges matched the expected scale ranges.
• Descriptive statistics indicated that most variables had distributions with means near zero,
consistent with OECD standardization, and minimum/maximum values within the expected zscore range.
• Some variables, such as ESCS and COOPAGR, displayed broad ranges of extreme values.
Others showed modest missingness: EMOCOAGR (2.95%), FAMSUP (2.66%), and ESCS
(1.16%). Special codes (e.g., 9.99, 999, 9999) representing systematic missingness were
recoded as NA.
A2. Missing Data Analysis
• Row-level screening removed students with more than 30% missing responses, reducing the
dataset from 7,250 to 7,116 cases.
• Variable-level missingness was below 3%, with EMOCOAGR showing the highest rate
(2.95%).
• Missing data patterns were visualized using VIM::aggr() (Kowarik et al., 2016) and
mice::md.pattern() (van Buuren et al., 2011).
• Little’s MCAR test applied via the naniar package (Tierney et al., 2023) rejected the MCAR
assumption (χ²(731) = 1273.47, p < .001). The mechanism was assumed to be MAR because
missingness was concentrated in specific student groups and socio-emotional indices.
• Missing values were imputed with the missForest algorithm (Stekhoven & Bühlmann, 2012),
which can model nonlinear relationships. After imputation, overall missingness remained below
10% (see also Raglin & Moraffah, 2023).
A3. Outlier Detection and Treatment
• Outliers were initially flagged using the Interquartile Range (IQR) method for variables such as
COGACRCO, EMOCOAGR, BELONG, ANXMAT, and COOPAGR.
• Boxplots confirmed heavy tails for ESCS, COOPAGR, and EMOCOAGR, and a few extremely
high values were noted in PVORT.
• The Minimum Covariance Determinant (MCD) method based on Mahalanobis distance
(Rousseeuw & Van Driessen, 1999) identified 3,134 cases (43.92%) as outliers, which was
deemed excessive.
• Four trimming scenarios were tested:
1. 2% trimming
2. 3% trimming
3. 3% trimming + 2% Winsorization
4. 5% trimming
• Performance of MLR models across these scenarios was compared using RMSE, R², and MAE.
The 5% trimming scenario produced the most stable results (RMSE = 75.76, R² = .223, MAE =
61.27) and reduced the sample to 6,779 cases. No missing data remained after trimming.
A4. Correlation Analysis
• Correlations with PVORT identified ESCS (r = .379), MATHEF21 (r = .254), DISCLIM (r =
.180), and ANXMAT (r = –.149) as the strongest predictors.
A5. Re-standardization
• Although OECD composite indices are standardized internationally, the Türkiye subsample
showed differences in mean and standard deviation at the pooled level, raising concerns of scale
inconsistency.
• Because algorithms such as MLR, LASSO, and ANN are scale-sensitive (de Amorim et al.,
2023), all predictors and PVORT were re-standardized (z-scores) using the Türkiye subsample
to ensure a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 for each variable.
A6. Diagnostic Checks
• A baseline MLR model was fit using stats::lm with the 13 predictors.
