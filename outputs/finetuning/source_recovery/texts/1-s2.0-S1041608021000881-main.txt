Research rooted in self-determination theory (SDT) has shown that need-supportive teaching, which involves
support for students' needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness, is associated with better motivation,
engagement, and achievement. However, there is a lack of research on how need-supportive teaching is associated with student well-being. In addition, past studies on SDT have mostly confined themselves to a limited
range of cultural contexts, usually Western cultures. To address these gaps, we examined the association between
need-supportive teaching and students' well-being across eight distinct cultural groups (Western Europe, EastCentral Europe, East Europe, Latin America, English-speaking, Confucian, Southeast Asia, African, and the
Middle East). We analyzed secondary data from the Program for International Student Assessment which
included responses from 513,295 students nested in 70 societies. Data were analyzed using hierarchical linear
modeling. Results indicated that need-supportive teaching was associated with better subjective, eudaimonic,
and social well-being. Results held across the eight cultural groups and were robust to the inclusion of covariates
at the individual (gender, socioeconomic status, immigration status, and parent support) and school (school
socioeconomic status and percentage of local students within a school) levels. Our study provides strong support
to the SDT assumption that need-supportive teaching is associated with optimal functioning across different
cultural contexts. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

1. Introduction
Self-determination theory (SDT) is a macro theory of human motivation that postulates the importance of three basic psychological needs,
including autonomy, competence, and relatedness for optimal functioning (Ryan, 2009; Ryan & Deci, 2017). In the educational context,
research on SDT has shown that teachers who teach in ways that support
students' needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness (i.e., needsupportive teaching) can facilitate students' autonomous motivation,
engagement, learning, and achievement (Coudevylle et al., 2020; Reeve
et al., 2020; Stroet et al., 2013).
However, there are also key gaps in the literature that need to be
addressed. First, much of the existing research on need-supportive
teaching has focused on its association with learning-related outcomes
(see Stroet et al., 2013). Less work has been done to examine how needsupportive teaching is associated with student well-being. This gap is
surprising given that SDT emphasizes the fulfillment of the three basic

needs to promote optimal well-being and not just learning (Ryan & Deci,
2017).
A second critical gap pertains to the cross-cultural universality of
SDT. SDT asserts that support for the three basic needs is a universal
requirement for well-being in all cultures (Deci & Ryan, 2000). Though
SDT researchers have conducted studies that seem to support the universality claims, most of these have been done in a limited set of cultural
contexts (Chirkov et al., 2005; Liu & Flick, 2019; Sheldon et al., 2011).
Hence, more cross-cultural research is needed.
The third gap is that prior studies have only focused on one aspect of
well-being, mostly subjective well-being (Church et al., 2013; Yu et al.,
2018). Well-being, however, is a multi-faceted construct consisting of
subjective, eudaimonic, and social components (Keyes, 2002).
To address these critical gaps, we used secondary data from the 2018
Program for International Student Assessment (PISA, OECD, 2018) and
examined how need-supportive teaching is associated with different
components of student well-being (i.e., subjective, eudaimonic, and

A noteworthy limitation of previous studies is that most of them only
considered one or two dimensions of well-being (e.g., subjective wellbeing; Orkibi & Ronen, 2017). The current study, therefore, differs
from previous studies by using a multidimensional conceptualization of
well-being which includes three key components: subjective well-being
(Diener, 2009; Myers & Diener, 1995), eudaimonic well-being (Ryff,
1989; Sheldon, 2002; Waterman et al., 2010), and social well-being
(Keyes, 1998).
Subjective well-being is defined as an individual's overall evaluation
of subjective wellness in his/her life or emotional experience (Diener,
1984). Subjective well-being can be further divided into two components: affective well-being and cognitive well-being. Affective wellbeing refers to individuals' positive and negative affect, while cognitive well-being focuses on life satisfaction (see Diener et al., 2018). The
impact of need satisfaction on students' subjective well-being has been
explored in past studies. For instance, Tian et al. (2014) reported that
students who experienced a higher sense of competence scored higher in
subjective well-being. Jin and Kim (2017) found that autonomy was
significantly associated with lower levels of depression, while feelings of
competence were positively associated with life satisfaction.
Eudaimonic well-being derives from Aristotle's (1985) conceptualization of well-being of the good life, encapsulating being true to oneself
and working towards personal growth. Eudaimonic well-being has been
considered as a complement and contrast to subjective well-being. In
this study, we focused on meaning in life, a key aspect of eudaimonic
well-being (Ryff, 2017). Meaning in life is defined as “the sense made of,
and significance felt regarding, the nature of one's being and existence”
(Steger et al., 2006, p. 81). Based on SDT, we hypothesized that students'
eudaimonic well-being could be facilitated by need-supportive teaching.
Social well-being is defined as the appraisal of an individual's circumstances and functioning in society (Keyes, 1998). Keyes (1998)
characterized social integration as an important element of social wellbeing, which pertains to the evaluation of the quality of ones' relationship with others, and sense of belonging to a group. In this study, we
focused specifically on school belonging as an aspect of social wellbeing. School belonging occurs when students feel accepted, respected, included, and supported by others from the school social environment (Goodenow & Grady, 1993).

1.1. Basic psychological needs and need-supportive teaching
To reiterate, SDT assumes that there are three basic psychological
needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. The need for autonomy
relates to the inherent desire to feel volitional and free in engaging in
actions (Ryan & Deci, 2000). The need for competence pertains to the
need for efficacy and control (Deci & Ryan, 2000). The need for relatedness pertains to a person's desire to connect with others and feel a
sense of belonging (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Ryan & Deci, 2000). When these
three needs are satisfied, individuals flourish.
In the school context, students' needs for autonomy, competence, and
relatedness can be facilitated through need-supportive teaching. Needsupportive teaching is defined as teachers' provision of autonomy support, structure, and involvement which help satisfy students' needs for
autonomy, competence, and relatedness respectively (Stroet et al.,
2013). There is a solid empirical base showing that need-supportive
teaching is associated with positive academic outcomes (e.g., Hornstra
et al., 2018; Kurdi et al., 2018; Niemiec & Ryan, 2009; Stroet et al.,
2015).
Autonomy support pertains to teachers' actions to promote students'
identification, nurturing, and building of their inner motivational resources (Connell & Wellborn, 1991; Reeve, Deci, & Ryan, 2004).
Autonomy-supportive teachers accept and welcome students' emotions
and thoughts and provide choices for students. Numerous studies have
demonstrated the benefits of autonomy-supportive teaching for
increasing students' intrinsic learning motivation and experiences of
self-determination (e.g., Hospel & Galand, 2016; Kiemer et al., 2018).
Teachers give students competence support by providing clear expectations and giving appropriate feedback (Vansteenkiste et al., 2012).
For example, teachers can provide structure by setting goals depending
on the students' capabilities. Past studies (e.g., Hospel & Galand, 2016;
Taylor & Ntoumanis, 2007) have shown that a well-structured classroom
environment enables students to obtain their desired outcomes and
consequently increase their feelings of competence.
Last, students who have high-quality relationships with their teachers are more likely to have their need for relatedness or involvement met
(Connell & Wellborn, 1991). Teachers support their students' need for
relatedness by showing affection and care towards their students (Stroet
et al., 2013).
The roles of the three dimensions of need-supportive teaching have
been examined in several studies. For instance, autonomy support has
been shown to be positively associated with students' intrinsic motivation and willingness to learning (Hospel & Galand, 2016; Kiemer et al.,
2018; Ryan & Deci, 2017). Competence support was found to be
significantly enhanced by the teacher's implementation of a structured
teaching environment (Hospel & Galand, 2016; Taylor & Ntoumanis,
2007). Moreover, empirical studies reported that perceived structure
had a positive relationship with student's motivation and engagement
(Nie & Lau, 2009). In the same vein, several studies indicated that
relatedness support has a positive impact on students' learning engagement (Daly et al., 2009; King, 2015; Murray, 2009).

1.3. Cross-cultural universality
SDT maintains that the satisfaction of individuals' basic psychological needs is a universal necessity for fostering well-being (Ryan et al.,
2017). Past studies have supported this assumption (Chen et al., 2015;
Chirkov et al., 2005; Yu et al., 2018). For instance, Chen et al. (2015)
found that need satisfaction was equally important for optimal functioning across four cultures (i.e., Belgium, China, USA, and Peru). In
another study, Chirkov et al. (2005) reported that need satisfaction was
associated with better well-being in both Brazil and Canada.
However, some cultural researchers have also challenged the validity
of SDT's claims, especially concerning the relevance of autonomy in nonWestern contexts (Chirkov et al., 2005). Perhaps, this controversy between SDT's universality claims and cross-cultural challenges may be
caused by the different definitions of autonomy. Cultural researchers
tend to define autonomy as interpersonal independence and individualism (Markus & Kitayama, 2003). Conversely, SDT claims that autonomy presents the self-endorsement of an individual's behavior and the
experience of personal volition (Chirkov & Ryan, 2001). Therefore, SDT
assumes that individuals in collectivist cultures will also have high levels
of well-being when they perceive their actions to be autonomous and
self-endorsed. In this study, we use SDT's definition of autonomy.
SDT researchers have tried to test the universality of its assumptions
across different cultural contexts. Nonetheless, most of these studies
have been confined to a single non-Western cultural context (e.g., Korea

1.2. Subjective, eudaimonic, and social well-being
Several studies have demonstrated the benefits of satisfying the three
basic needs for students' well-being (e.g., Orkibi & Ronen, 2017; Tian
et al., 2014). For instance, perceived autonomy-support has been shown
to predict greater well-being in a sample of adolescents from Russia and
the United States (Chirkov & Ryan, 2001). In another study among
Spanish middle school students, researchers found that students who
were highly connected with their teachers had a higher level of well2

in Jang et al., 2010; China in Zhou et al., 2009; Japan in Nishimura &

could only be done when several distinct cultural groups are examined
simultaneously. To our best knowledge, no prior study has tested how
need-supportive teaching is associated with well-being across these
eight distinct cultural groups.
To address this research gap, we followed Schwartz's (2009) cultural
values theory which posits eight distinct cultural groups based on how
they score in terms of three dimensions: autonomy versus embeddedness, hierarchy versus egalitarianism, and mastery versus harmony. In
other words, we tested the need-supportive teaching to well-being
linkage across eight cultural groups. The value orientations of each
culture are discussed below.
People in autonomous cultures view themselves as independent and
autonomous, while people in embeddedness culture recognized themselves as collective. The dimension of hierarchy refers to hierarchical
systems with an unequal legitimate distribution of resources, roles, and
powers. Egalitarianism refers to cultures wherein people are treated as
moral equals. People in mastery cultures value competence to change
the environment and shape it according to their own goals, whereas
people in harmony cultures value peace and protection of the environment (Schwartz, 2006). Based on these value orientations, Schwartz
(2009) classified the different countries into eight cultural groups:
Western Europe, East-Central Europe, Eastern Europe, Latin America,
English speaking, Confucian, Southeast Asia, and Africa and the Middle
East.
According to Schwartz (2009), compared with Western Europe, regions in both Eastern Central Europe and Eastern Europe are lower on
embeddedness and hierarchy. Eastern Central Europe (e.g., Albania,
Croatia, and the Czech Republic) is higher on harmony and intellectual
autonomy, and lower in hierarchy than Eastern Europe (e.g., Azerbaijan,
Belarus, Bosnia, and Herzegovina). Latin America, which includes
countries such as Argentina, Brazil, and Chile is higher in hierarchy and
embeddedness, and lower in intellectual autonomy. Regions in Englishspeaking cultures, including Australia, Ireland, and New Zealand,
emphasize autonomy and mastery. Confucian cultures, which include
Greater China regions such as Mainland China, Hong Kong SAR, Macau
SAR, and Taiwan, emphasize hierarchy, embeddedness, and mastery.
Cultures in Africa and the Middle East (e.g., Jordan, Morrocco, and
Egypt) emphasize meaning in life through social relationships (i.e., high
embeddedness) rather than individual differences (i.e., low autonomy).
Southeast Asian cultures, including Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei
Darussalam, emphasize the fulfillment of one's obligations within a hierarchical system.

Gender differences in subjective well-being have been repeatedly
examined over the past decades (see the review by Batz & Tay, 2017).
For example, Liu et al. (2016) found that in general, girls have a higher
level of life satisfaction than boys. Furthermore, girls reported higher
scores in learning-related satisfaction (Casas et al., 2007). As for positive
and negative affect, OECD (2019) reported that girls tended to have
higher levels of negative affect than boys though no gender differences
were found in terms of positive affect. An international study conducted
by Joshanloo (2018) that included participants from 166 countries
revealed that males generally scored higher in eudaimonic well-being
than their female counterparts. Regarding social well-being, Watson
et al. (2020) documented that boys had lower school belonging
compared to girls.
Prior studies have found a positive association between SES and
satisfaction with school and psychological well-being, indicating that
students from advantaged families had higher levels of well-being than
their less advantaged peers (e.g., Loft & Waldfogel, 2020). Furthermore,
as shown in a recent review by Tan et al. (2020), family SES was positively associated with subjective well-being.
In terms of eudaimonic well-being, lower SES was reported to be
significantly associated with lower levels of purpose in life (Ryff &
Singer, 2008). O'Connor and Kirtley (2017) found that individuals from
a disadvantaged socioeconomic background had lower levels of
belonging (social well-being). Moreover, low school SES was also found
to be significantly linked to mental illness, such as depressive symptoms
(Yue, 2017).
A negative association between immigration background and psychological well-being was documented in previous studies (e.g.,
Alivernini et al., 2020). For example, Oppedal and Røysamb (2004)
investigated the relationship between adolescents' mental health and
their immigration status. They found that immigrants were at greater
risk of suffering psychological distress and feeling less supported than
their local peers in school. Furthermore, it was also found that students
with immigrant backgrounds tended to report more negative outcomes,
such as a low level of social relatedness (Cherng, 2015), negative attitudes (Cavicchiolo et al., 2016), and being bullied (Alivernini, Manganelli, et al., 2019), as compared with local students.
The number or percentage of immigrant vs. local students within a
school is also an important factor to consider. Immigrant school
composition has been suggested as a critical factor (Vervoort et al.,
2010). Studies have shown that schools with a significant number of
immigrant students had worse disciplinary climates and social outcomes, like bullying and physical fighting (Strohmeier et al., 2011;
Walsh et al., 2016).
Parent support is also an influential factor in students' well-being.
Empirical studies have revealed the powerful role of parent support in
children's mental health and well-being (Duineveld et al., 2017; Ratelle
et al., 2013).
To summarize, gender, student SES, school SES, immigrant status,
percentage of local students within a school, and parent support have all
been found to be closely associated with student well-being. To control
for these alternative explanations, we included them as covariates in the
current study. This allows us to test the unique predictive effects of needsupportive teaching on well-being outcomes.

1.4. Controlling for alternative explanations
SDT assumes that psychological needs are universal and relevant for
individuals regardless of their demographics and background (Vansteenkiste et al., 2020). Thus, to test the impact of need-supportive
teaching on students' well-being, we also took important covariates
into account. These covariates that have been documented in past
studies as factors being closely associated with well-being. Specifically
in the educational context, sociocultural factors such as gender and socioeconomic background were found to play an important role in students' well-being (e.g., Alivernini, Cavicchiolo, et al., 2019; Girelli et al.,
2018; Reeve et al., 2018). A study showed that students from disadvantaged families and girls with fewer friendships reported a lower level
of positive affect (Alivernini, Cavicchiolo, et al., 2019). Similarly,
Alivernini et al. (2020) found that females, immigrants, and low socioeconomic status (SES) students were more likely to experience psychological distress at school. Therefore, it is necessary to consider the role of
sociocultural factors when investigating the patterns of student wellbeing. In the current study, the covariates included sociodemographic
factors (i.e., gender, SES, school SES, immigration status, and percentage of local students within a school) and parent support.

The current study is a secondary analysis of the 2018 Programme for
International Student Assessment (PISA; OECD, 2018), which aims to
provide information about the capacity of 15-year-old students around
the world to face the challenges in their future lives. PISA in 2018
assessed students' knowledge and skills in reading, mathematics, and
science among 37 OECD countries and 42 partner countries and
economies.
In the present study, we used data from 70 countries with a total
sample size of 513,295 students. We divided all economies into eight
transnational cultural groupings (Schwartz, 2009), the sample size,
percentage of females, and countries in each culture were listed out in
Table 1. Across the eight cultures, the sample size ranged from 40,000 to
110,000, and the percentages of females ranged from 48% to 52%.

2.2. Measures
2.2.1. Subjective well-being
Subjective well-being consists of students' life satisfaction, positive
affect, and negative affect. PISA operationalized life satisfaction using
the item, “Overall, how satisfied are you with your life as a whole these
days?”. This item was rated from 0 to 10. Respondents were questioned
about how they normally feel in their lives. Participants reported their
positive affect by five adjectives: joyful, cheerful, happy, lively, and proud.
Negative affect was reported by four adjectives, containing afraid,
scared, sad, and miserable. Each adjective was rated on a 4-point Likert

scale ranging from 1 (never) to 4 (always). The reliabilities for positive
affect (Cronbach's α = 0.80), and negative affect (Cronbach's α = 0.75)
are both high.

2.3.2. Invariance
PISA addressed the issue of cross-country comparability of constructs/items when developing scales/items. Interested readers can find
the internal consistency information and cross-country comparability of
constructs in Chapter 16 of the PISA 2018 Technical Report (OECD,
2020). We also tested the validity of the scales used across the eight
cultural groups.
Before running the main analysis, we tested a measurement model of
need-supportive teaching and well-being variables. A 6-factor model
was posited with need-supportive teaching, positive affect, negative
affect, life satisfaction, meaning in life, and school belonging as correlated factors underpinned by their respective items. Except for life
satisfaction, all constructs were measured by several items. We also
tested the invariance of the measurement model across the eight cultural
groups. Model fit was assessed by comparative fit index (CFI), TuckerLewis index (TLI), and Root mean squared error of approximation
(RMSEA). CFT and TLI above 0.90, and RMSEA below 0.08 were interpreted as acceptable fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999; Marsh et al., 2004).
Meanwhile, we used ΔCFI ≤ 0.010, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, ΔSRMR ≤ 0.030
as the cutoff values for configural invariance, and ΔCFI ≤ 0.010,
ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, ΔSRMR ≤ 0.010 for metric and scalar invariance
(Chen, 2007).

2.2.2. Eudaimonic well-being
Eudaimonic well-being was operationalized in terms of students'
responses to the three-item meaning in life scale (e.g., “My life has clear
meaning or purpose”). Participants rated each item on a 4-point scale
from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). This scale had good internal reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.85).
2.2.3. Social well-being
Social well-being was operationalized in terms of responses to the
school belonging scale. It was measured by 6 items. A sample item is “I
make friends easily at school”. All the items were rated on a 4-point
Likert scale (1 = strongly agree to 5 = strongly disagree). This scale had
good internal reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.74).
2.2.4. Need-supportive teaching
Three items were utilized to measure student's perceived basic psychological needs support. Need-supportive teaching is captured by items
on competence support (i.e., “The teacher made me feel confident in my
ability to do well in the course.”), autonomy support (i.e., “The teacher
listened to my view on how to do things.”) and relatedness support (i.e.,
“I felt that my teacher understood me.”). All the items were rated
ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). This scale also had
good internal consistency reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.86) in this study.
These items closely match prior measures of need-supportive teaching
(Ahn et al., 2019; Iglesias García et al., 2020; Pitzer & Skinner, 2017).

2.2.5. Covariates
We used three items in the PISA 2018 student questionnaires to
measure parent support, including “My parents support my educational
efforts and achievements”; “My parents support me when I am facing
difficulties at school.”; “My parents encourage me to be confident.” All
items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly
disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). This scale showed good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.89).
We also included sociocultural variables as covariates. These factors
included gender (female = 0, male = 1), student SES, and school SES. An
index of economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS) was used to indicate student SES in PISA (OECD, 2016). School SES was operationalized
as the average ESCS of all the students within the target school. This
operationalization is consistent with prior research on school SES (Chiu
et al., 2016). The PISA index of immigration status was used to operationalize students' immigration background (immigrant = 0, local = 1).
The percentage of local students within the school was also calculated.

p < .001), meaning in life (ß = 0.12, p < .001), and school belonging (ß
= 0.10, p < .001). Need-supportive teaching negatively predicted
negative affect (ß = − 0.04, p < .001).
As the measure of need-supportive teaching used above focused on
the omnibus construct, we conducted a robustness check to test whether
each component of need-supportive teaching (autonomy, competence,
and relatedness support) was associated with the well-being outcomes in
a similar way. Results showed that each component of need-supportive
teaching (representing competence, autonomy, and relatedness) was
associated with well-being in a similar way. For the sake of brevity, we
did not include the information here but placed them in the Supplementary Files (see Table S1).
Table 5 shows the standardized coefficients after controlling the
covariates in the final model of HLM across eight cultural groups. The
results showed a significant association between need-supportive
teaching and subjective well-being, supporting H1. Students who had
perceived higher need-supportive teaching predicts higher levels of life
satisfaction (H1a) and positive affect (H1b) and lower levels of negative
affect (H1c).
Need-supportive teaching positively predicted eudaimonic and social well-being supporting H2 and H3. After controlling the demographic factors, the association between need-supportive teaching
and well-being remained across the eight cultural groups, which supported H4.

3. Result
3.1. Descriptive statistics
Before performing the HLM analysis, descriptive statistics and
bivariate correlations for the overall sample were reported in Table 2.
The results showed a positive association between need-supportive
teaching and positive affect (r = 0.17, p < .001), life satisfaction (r =
0.15, p < .001), meaning in life (r = 0.18, p < .001), and school
belonging (r = 0.14, p < .001). The correlation between need-supportive
teaching and negative affect was significant but the value is very low (r
= − 0.05, p < .01). The Cronbach's alpha coefficients were higher than
0.7 across all eight cultures.
3.2. CFA and measurement invariance
Measurement invariance results are shown in Table 3. In M1, singlegroup CFA was conducted. The 6-factor measurement model with needsupportive teaching and the five dimensions of well-being (positive
affect, negative affect, life satisfaction, meaning in life, and social wellbeing) had a good fit to the data in the overall sample and yielded the
following fit indices: CFI = 0.945, TLI = 0.935, and RMSEA = 0.042
(90% CI [0.042, 0.043]). In the second step, we estimated configural
invariance (M2), wherein the factor structure was set to be equal across
cultures. In the third step, we constrained factor loadings to be equal
(M3) to test the metric invariance. Next, we constrained factor loadings
and intercepts to be equal (M4) to test scalar invariance.
Multiple group invariance testing indicated that configural invariance was achieved with CFI = 0.935, TLI = 0.923, and RMSEA = 0.046
(90% CI [0.046, 0.046]). Metric invariance was also achieved with CFI
= 0.933, TLI = 0.925, RMSEA = 0.045 (90% CI [0.045, 0.045]), ΔCFI =
0.002, ΔTLI = 0.002, and ΔRMSEA = 0.001. The criteria for metric
invariance was met. However, scalar invariance was not achieved (ΔCFI
= 0.025, ΔTLI = 0.020, and ΔRMSEA = 0.004). Thus, the model
exhibited measurement invariance of factor loadings, but intercepts
varied across groups. The fit indices for each model are reported in
Table 3.

and well-being across the eight cultures, we conducted a supplementary
analysis on each of the 70 participating countries/economies. The results are presented in Table 6.
Table 6 reports the standardized coefficients in each country. The
coefficients are statistically significant for all countries for positive
affect, life satisfaction, meaning in life, and school belonging. However,
the results were more varied for negative affect. In particular, the
parameter estimates in East-Central Europe are mostly significant, while
the estimates in Southeast Asia, Latin America, and West Europe were

4. Discussion

results indicated that need-supportive teaching was associated with
negative affect in East-Central Europe, but not in Southeast Asia, Latin
America, and West Europe. One explanation is that it might be due to
cultural differences in how people view negative emotions. Some cultures view negative emotions as more acceptable than others. For
instance, Americans consider negative feelings as more harmful, while
Japanese see these negative emotions as more natural (Curhan et al.,
2014). Other studies have shown that emotions such as anxiety and guilt
lead to more positive learning outcomes among Chinese but not Western
students perhaps because Chinese see learning as difficult and effortful,
making negative emotions more acceptable (Li, 2012).
Another potential explanation for why the results with regard to
negative affect were less consistent is that we only measured teachers'
need-supportive teaching but not their need-thwarting teaching styles.
Early SDT research did not empirically distinguish between support for
and thwarting of basic psychological needs. However, a recent development in the SDT literature is the bifurcation of need-support and needthwarting experiences as overlapping yet distinct dimensions of needsupport (Bartholomew et al., 2011; Rocchi et al., 2017; Van den
Berghe et al., 2016). Need-thwarting teaching is characterized by the use
of controlling strategies, a chaotic style, and cold interpersonal interactions that could frustrate students' basic psychological needs (Bartholomew et al., 2011; Van den Berghe et al., 2014, 2016). Studies have
shown that need-support is more related to positive outcomes, while
need-thwarting teaching style is more associated with negative outcomes. Perhaps, negative affect might be more consistently related to
need-thwarting teaching styles than need-supportive teaching. Future
studies can distinguish need-supportive and need-thwarting teaching
styles.
A key strength of our study was that we not only investigated subjective well-being but also included other two key components of wellbeing: eudaimonic and social well-being. The results indicated that
need-supportive teaching is positively associated with eudaimonic wellbeing, i.e., meaning in life (H2). Furthermore, there was a significant
positive relationship between need-supportive teaching and social wellbeing, i.e., school belonging (H3). These results are in line with previous
studies on the relationship between need satisfaction and school

The purpose of the current study was to examine the association
between need-supportive teaching and students' well-being across eight
cultural groups. Our study showed that need-supportive teaching was
positively associated with subjective, eudaimonic, and social well-being
which supported H1, H2, and H3, respectively. To a large extent, these
results held across the eight cultural groups when controlling for the
different covariates (supported H4).
Need-supportive teaching and well-being. Our findings contribute to the
literature on SDT and well-being. Much of the SDT research has focused
on individuals' learning outcomes (Benlahcene et al., 2020; Ganotice
et al., 2020; Reeve et al., 2020) with less attention paid to well-being.
The fact that need-supportive teaching is also linked to well-being outcomes as suggested by the present study, could be an important extension of the SDT literature.
In terms of subjective well-being, we found support for H1a and H1b
with need-supportive teaching positively predicting positive affect and
life satisfaction. More specifically, we found that need-supportive
teaching positively predicted positive affect and life satisfaction. In
terms of negative affect, H1c was also supported as need-supportive
teaching negatively predicted negative affect. These findings corroborate past studies that have shown the important role of basic need
satisfaction in promoting students' subjective well-being (Jin & Kim,
2017; Orkibi & Ronen, 2017; Tian et al., 2014; Yu et al., 2018). For
example, Ratelle et al. (2013) found that autonomy support is associated
with higher subjective well-being. Similarly, Yu et al. (2018) observed
the positive association between the need for autonomy and subjective
well-being in both Eastern and Western contexts. Some other studies
also documented the impact of teachers' competence support on fulfilling students' need for competence, and on promoting students'
autonomous motivation (Guay et al., 2017; Olivier et al., 2020).
Our supplementary analysis in each of the 70 countries/regions
indicated that the association between need-supportive teaching and
positive indices of subjective well-being such as life satisfaction and
positive affect were consistent in all of the 70 economies. However, the
results for negative affect were not always that consistent. For example,

presented the positive impact of basic psychological needs on eudaimonic and social belonging, relatively little work has been conducted in

teaching plays an important role in facilitating students' eudaimonic

As suggested by SDT (Ryan & Deci, 2001), eudaimonic well-being is
an objective indicator of psychological functioning, while hedonic wellbeing is a subjective indicator. In the same vein, Keyes et al. (2002)
suggested that hedonic well-being, as measured by the dimensions of
subjective well-being (i.e., positive affect, negative, and life satisfaction), is different from eudaimonic well-being (i.e., meaning in life).
Although conceptually distinguishable, these two types of well-being are
strongly related (Church et al., 2013). Our study showed that need-

supportive teaching was associated with a greater sense of meaning.
Social well-being reflects the extent to which individuals are functioning well in societies and communities (Keyes, 1998). In this study,
we operationalized social well-being as school belonging. Previous
studies have mostly focused on the impact of SDT needs on subjective
well-being (Sheldon et al., 2011) and eudaimonic well-being (Ryan
et al., 2008), but neglected social well-being. In this study, we found that
need-supportive teaching was significantly and positively associated
with social well-being.
The findings of the current study supported SDT's cross-cultural
universality and applicability. The associations between needsupportive teaching and well-being were consistent across the eight
cultures even after controlling for the role of different covariates (H4). In

4.1. Effect sizes
Despite broad support for our key hypotheses, the effect sizes were
mostly small. Perhaps this is because teachers' need-supportive teaching
is only one contributor towards adolescent well-being among many
other factors (Žukauskienė, 2014). The small effect sizes we found,
however, were consistent with past SDT research (e.g., Froiland & Oros,
2014). Though most researchers use Cohen's (1988) effect size criteria to
judge the magnitude of the effect, recent studies have urged caution in
unilaterally applying Cohen's criteria for small, medium, and large effects. Meta-analyses have found that “small” effect sizes are actually
more common in social science, psychology, and education research
(Gignac & Szodorai, 2016; Hattie, 2008). For example, Gignac and
Szodorai (2016) claimed that a Pearson's r of 0.20 is more common in
psychological studies. This number is more closely aligned with the
correlations we found (rs ranging from 0.14 to 0.17 for the positive dimensions of well-being).

5. Implications, limitations, and future directions
Although our study was cross-sectional, it has important practical
implications. Our findings suggest that teachers' need-supportive
teaching is positively associated with students' well-being across eight
cultures even after accounting for a large number of covariates. Thus,
practitioners are encouraged to adopt need-supportive teaching strategies, such as providing choice, giving structure, and demonstrating
affection, to improve students' psychological functioning. This study
also has implications for teaching training interventions. Perhaps,
teacher preparation and professional development programs should
emphasize the importance of need-supportive teaching, especially in
what ways and aspects it would benefit students' well-being.
Some limitations should be noted. First, we used PISA-provided
items to operationalize our variables of interest rather than using
more well-established questionnaires. Second, we relied on self-reported
data, which might be prone to self-report bias (Lanyon & Goodstein,
1997). Third, the research design was limited to a cross-sectional design
making it impossible to form causal conclusions. Therefore, experiments
and longitudinal studies are needed to make stronger causal conclusions. Fourth, we only focused on need-supportive teaching styles but
not need-thwarting teaching practices. Future studies which make an
empirical distinction between need-support and need-thwarting teaching styles might be needed to present a fuller account of how SDT could
shed light on the relationship between teaching styles and student wellbeing. Fifth, the current study was mainly interested in testing the
universality claims of SDT across the eight cultural groups. Future
studies can explore the possible distinctions among the eight cultural
groups.

4.2. Covariates
In addition, we observed that all covariates (i.e., gender, SES, school
SES, immigration status, percentage of local students within a school,
and parent support) were significant predictors of students' positive
well-being, i.e., positive affect, life satisfaction, and school belonging,
while only gender and parent support were significantly associated with
negative affect. In particular, boys showed a higher level of negative
affect and lower life satisfaction than girls, which is consistent with the
results of a meta-analysis conducted by Chen et al. (2019). Besides, boys
were more likely to experience negative affect and feelings as compared
to girls. Similar findings were reported by Liu et al. (2016). A plausible
explanation for this is that compared to girls, boys are more easily
criticized by teachers in school. As a consequence, boys may have lower
life satisfaction in school than girls.
In line with prior studies (Costa et al., 2016; Duineveld et al., 2017;
Ratelle et al., 2013), the current study suggested that parent support was
a robust predictor of students' well-being, indicating that students who
received more support from their parents had higher well-being.
Moreover, consistent with previous studies (Loft & Waldfogel, 2020;
Ryff & Singer, 2008; Tan et al., 2020), our results demonstrated that SES
at both the student- and the school-level had a significant and positive
impact on well-being (except for negative affect).
Regarding immigration status, we found that local students had
higher levels of positive affect, life satisfaction, and school belonging.
These findings echo previous studies reporting that immigrant students
were at a disadvantage compared to their local peers in school (Alivernini, Cavicchiolo, et al., 2019; Cavicchiolo et al., 2016; Cherng, 2015;
Oppedal & Røysamb, 2004).
Schools with higher percentages of local students also had higher
levels of positive affect, life satisfaction, meaning in life, and school
belonging, while at the same time having lower levels of negative affect.

6. Conclusion
The present study underscores the importance of need-supportive
teaching in fostering students' subjective, eudaimonic, and social wellbeing. The effects of need-supportive teaching held even after controlling for other potentially confounding variables at the individual
(gender, student SES, immigration status) and school (school SES and
percentage of locals vs. immigrants in a school) levels. Our findings are
generalizable across the eight cultural groups and demonstrated the
universal importance of need-supportive teaching. Perhaps, as revealed
by this study, encouraging teachers to teach in need-supportive ways
could be an important pathway to improving student well-being. (6724
