A B S T R A C T
This study examines the construct validity of the mathematics confidence scale in the Swedish TIMSS 2019 
fourth-grade data, addressing a methodological gap in educational effectiveness research, conducting secondary 
analyses of international large-scale assessments (ILSAs). Previous research has operationalised the mathematics 
confidence construct in various ways. Although ISLA data provide robust measures, self-report scales require 
validation for method effects and linguistic equivalence across cultures. Using confirmatory factor analysis and 
bifactor exploratory structural equation modelling, this study examined the factorial structure and translation 
equivalence of the mathematics confidence scale to validate its operationalisation for further analyses. Results 
indicated method effects associated with mixed-worded items, suggesting that linguistic complexity may 
compromise validity. Structural equation models confirmed a significant relationship between mathematics 
confidence and achievement, yet model fit, and strength of relationships varied across operationalisations. The 
findings underscore the importance of construct validation in ILSA-based secondary analyses and call for greater 
attention to translation equivalence and item wording.
1. Introduction
Educational effectiveness research often investigates factors 
explaining differences in student learning outcomes through secondary 
analyses of international large-scale assessments (ILSAs) data. These 
analyses typically use contextual questionnaire data to identify variables 
that may account for variation in student achievement. Although ILSAs 
provide high-quality data with valid and reliable measures, issues of 
reliability and validity must still be addressed when latent constructs are 
derived from questionnaire items (Hooper et al., 2013; Steinmann, 
S´anchez, et al., 2022; Steinmann, Strietholt, et al., 2022). Questionnaire 
items are usually combined into scales representing underlying con­
structs, such as motivation, that cannot be directly observed (Messick, 
1989). Many of these scales include mixed-worded items, both favour­
able and unfavourable statements, to maintain respondent attention and 
reduce acquiescence bias (Marsh, 1994; Roszkowski & Soven, 2010). 
However, mixed-worded items often differ in cognitive difficulty. 
Younger students or those with lower reading ability may find 
negatively-worded statements particularly challenging (Steinmann, 
Strietholt, et al., 2022). Research has shown that mixed-worded scales 
may introduce response bias and method effects (Chyung et al., 2018; 
DiStefano & Motl, 2006). Yet, reducing item numbers can compromise 
content validity (Messick, 1989). Consequently, when using latent 
constructs in secondary analyses, researchers must discuss validity is­
sues and justify their operationalisation choices. However, previous 
secondary analyses have rarely addressed content and construct validity 
explicitly.
Despite the extensive use of ILSA data, few secondary analyses 
evaluate the construct validity of questionnaire scales, consider cross- 
linguistic differences, or provide a clear rationale for how latent con­
structs, such as mathematics confidence, are operationalised. The 
Swedish scale measuring student-perceived mathematics confidence in 
TIMSS has not been examined regarding its mixed-worded scale, nor 
have potential threats to construct validity or cross-linguistic differences 
in interpreting negations been explored (Holmberg, 2013). This meth­
odological gap is significant given ongoing concerns about response bias 
and method effects in mixed-worded scales (Chyung et al., 2018; 
Michaelides, 2019; Steinmann, S´anchez, et al., 2022). There is therefore

This study seeks to address that need.
Drawing on a cross-linguistic approach, the present study uses 
Swedish fourth-grade data from TIMSS 20192 to assess the construct 
validity of the mathematics confidence scale and to identify potential 
method effects arising from its mixed-worded design. The Swedish 
sample allows for a semantic validation of the translated items, assessing 
their linguistic equivalence with the original English version and 
examining differences in syntax, idiomatic usage, and cultural bias. 
Structural contrasts between Swedish and English, particularly 
regarding the use of negations and word order, may increase the 
cognitive complexity of negatively worded items. For instance, Swedish 
uses a negation similar to English (He is not/han ¨ar inte) but omits the 
auxiliary verb (He does not speak/han talar inte). Furthermore, whereas 
English maintains a direct word order (subjet-verb-object), Swedish 
places the verb before the subject and the negation after it (On Tuesdays 
he does not work/på tisdagar jobbar han inte).
Given that mathematics confidence strongly predicts mathematics 
achievement (Stankov & Lee, 2017), this study applies structural 
equation modelling (SEM) to compare four alternative operationalisa­
tions of the construct. The analyses illustrate how different measure­
ment models influence the observed relationships between mathematics 
confidence and achievement and highlight the importance of rigorous 
validation when using ILSA data for secondary analyses.
1.1. Validity
Validity refers to the extent to which an instrument measures the 
intended construct (AERA, 2014). For questionnaire data to achieve 
high reliability and validity, items should be clearly worded, concise, 
and unambiguous (Messick, 1989). To enhance construct validity, 
mixed-worded Likert scales are often used to maintain respondents' 
attention and reduce acquiescence bias, respondents’ tendency to agree 
indiscriminately with items (Marsh, 1994; Roszkowski & Soven, 2010). 
A mixed-worded scale can appear in four different modes: a direct 
positive (‘Mathematics is fun’), a negated positive (‘Mathematics is not 
fun’), a direct negative (‘Mathematics is boring’), and a negated nega­
tive (‘Mathematics is not boring’).
1.2. Item scale in TIMSS and previous research
For secondary analyses, TIMSS provides both raw scale scores 
(continuous) and transformed scales with three categories (high, me­
dium, and low) (Mullis & Martin, 2017). Although the transformed scale 
facilitates secondary analyses (Foy et al., 2020), Marsh et al. (2013)
warn that it may reduce reliability and predictive validity compared 
with raw scores. These transformed scores assume that respondents 
consistently interpret both positively and negatively worded items, 
which may not hold for respondents with lower reading of cognitive 
skills (Steinmann, Strietholt, et al., 2022).
Operationalisation of the mixed-worded mathematics confidence 
scale varies widely in previous research using TIMSS data. Some studies 
include all nine items, both positive and negative (Lee & Chen, 2019), 
while others divide them into two subsets (Roszkowski & Soven, 2010; 
van Sonderen et al., 2013). Several studies use only the positively 
worded items (Chen & Lu, 2022; Min et al., 2016; Wang, 2020), 
single-item measures (Kim & Sidney, 2024; Stankov et al., 2014), or the 
transformed scale provided by TIMSS (Berger et al., 2020). These diverse 
operationalisations rarely include a discussion of construct validity, 
which complicates cross-study comparisons and replications. This 
highlights the need for further studies that systematically evaluate the 
construct validity of such scales and justify their operationalisation 
choices.
In secondary analyses, mixed-worded items are typically recoded so 
that agreement with positive items receives higher values, and agree­
ment with negative items receives lower values (van van Sonderen et al., 
2013). However, Hooper et al. (2013), analysing the TIMMS 2011 
‘students confident in mathematics’ scale, found that merely reversing 
the negatively worded items did not make them psychometrically 
equivalent to the positive ones. Including negatively worded items 
introduced method effects and construct-irrelevant variance. Similarly, 
studies using TIMSS data have differed in how they operationalise 
mathematics confidence, often without providing clear justifications (e. 
g., Berger et al., 2020; Chen & Lu, 2022; Ivanova & Michaelides, 2022; 
Lee & Chen, 2019). Such inconsistencies underscore the need for 
transparent reporting of conceptualisation, operationalisation, and 
measurement modelling decisions in secondary analyses.
1.3. Method effects and response bias
Method effects arise from the way items are worded or combined to 
reflect a latent construct, potentially compromising validity. In sec­
ondary analyses, mixed-worded scales may threaten construct validity if 
positive and negative items tap into different underlying dimensions or 
constructs (Marsh, 1994; van Sonderen et al., 2013). This can introduce 
construct-irrelevant variance (Messick, 1989), weaken factorial and 
statistical validity (DiStefano & Motl, 2006), and reduce internal con­
sistency (Roszkowski & Soven, 2010; Ye & Wallace, 2014).
Although ILSAs use mixed scales to reduce acquiescence bias and 
broaden construct coverage, including negatively worded items may 
cause method effects. Such items often lower internal consistency, 
sometimes producing factor loadings below the commonly accepted 
threshold of 0.4 (Field, 2018). Measurement invariance studies using 
ILSA data show that negative items can contribute to non-invariance in 
attitudinal scales (Reynolds et al., 2022), distort factorial structure, and 
generate method effects (Michaelides, 2019; Wang et al., 2018). 
Conversely, excluding these items has been shown to improve internal 
consistency (Hooper et al., 2013; Roszkowski & Soven, 2010; Ye & 
Wallace, 2014).
Mixed-worded scales risk introducing response bias, particularly 
when inconsistent respondents are not identified (Steinmann, S´anchez, 
et al., 2022). Response bias refers to systematic deviations in answering 
patterns, which may stem from several sources. First, social desirability 
bias may lead respondents to disagree with unfavourable ones to 
represent themselves positively (e.g., ‘I am not good at mathematics’) 
(Spector, 1992). Second, response style bias refers to tendencies such as 
acquiescence, inattentiveness, attrition, or misunderstandings when 
interpreting complex or negatively worded items (Steinmann, S´anchez, 
et al., 2022; van van Sonderen et al., 2013). Third, negative wording 
increases the linguistic complexity, causing cognitive challenges 
(Michaelides, 2019; Steinmann, Strietholt, et al., 2022; Wang et al., 
2018).
Steinmann, Strietholt, et al. (2022) examined four mixed-worded 
questionnaire scales from various ILSA datasets: TIMSS and PIRLS 
2011(Grade 4, Australia), PISA 2018 (Grades 8 and 12, US), and a 
German national panel study (Grades 5 and 9) using Rosenberg’s 
self-esteem scale. They found that students with weaker reading or 
cognitive skills were more likely to respond inconsistently to negatively 
worded items, undermining construct validity and introducing system­
atic bias (Marsh, 1994; Steinmann, Strietholt, et al., 2022). Similarly, 
Steinmann, S´anchez, et al. (2022) reported that 20 % of the fourth-grade 
respondents responded inconsistently to mixed-worded self-concept 
scales in TIMSS 2011. These inconsistent respondents scored lower on 
the achievement tests, suggesting that lower reading or cognitive ability 
contributes to the observed response bias.

1.4. Linguistic equivalence across translations
Translation procedures used for ILSAs are essential for ensuring 
cross-linguistic comparability. TIMSS applies standardised translation 
and verification procedures, including back translation, to detect dif­
ferences in meaning between the source and translated versions (Ebbs 
et al., 2020). However, back translation has been criticised for over­
looking subtle semantic shifts, and the ‘double translation’ method, 
translating from two source languages (e.g., English and French), has 
been proposed as a more valid approach for capturing linguistic equiv­
alence (Upsing & Rittberger, 2018). Ros´en and Nilsen (2024) advocate 
for validation studies that explicitly examine phrasing and translation 
choices and how these may influence respondents’ interpretations 
across cultures.
In summary, previous research indicates that mixed-worded scales 
may capture different latent constructs and be susceptible to response 
and cultural bias. To date, few studies have examined the construct 
validity of the mixed-worded scales in TIMSS in secondary analyses or 
examined how the translations, conceptualisations, and operationali­
sations of the latent construct influence the relationships found in such 
analyses (Ros´en & Nilsen, 2024). Addressing this gap, the current study 
aims to contribute empirical information on these validity aspects.
1.5. Mathematics confidence
Mathematics confidence serves as a proxy for academic self-concept, 
referring to individuals’ beliefs about their ability to succeed, which 
are shaped by prior experiences, social interactions, and peer compari­
sons (Marsh & Craven, 2006). Consequently, students’ self-perceptions 
may be positively or negatively influenced by the extent to which they 
feel competent, autonomous, and connected to the teacher and class­
mates. Academic self-concept is a multidimensional construct that has 
been operationalised through various items across studies. Likewise, 
secondary analyses using ILSA data have employed varying operation­
alisations of the latent construct of mathematics confidence, as previ­
ously discussed.
A substantial body of evidence identifies academic self-concept as a 
strong predictor of student achievement (Lee & Stankov, 2018; Marsh 
et al., 2013; Marsh & Craven, 2006; Shavelson et al., 1976; Stankov & 
Lee, 2017). For instance, analysis of Grade 4 and Grade 8 data from 60 
countries in TIMSS 2015 showed mathematics confidence to be a robust 
predictor of mathematics achievement across cultures (Lee & Chen, 
2019). Similarly, Marsh et al. (2005) found in a longitudinal study of 
5659 German 7th graders that mathematics interest and achievement 
were reciprocally related to academic self-concept. In a study analysing 
three waves of TIMSS (2003, 2007, 2011), Min et al. (2016) used the 
average self-concept (measured by four positively worded items) as the 
dependent variable. They identified potential cultural bias in how 
teachers’ feedback and students' self-assessments differ; East-Asian stu­
dents tended to downplay their abilities relative to Western students 
(Min et al., 2016). They raised concerns about translation and differ­
ential interpretation of questionnaire items and Likert scales across 
cultures and languages (Min et al., 2016).
Overall, prior research underscores the need to scrutinise the 
construct validity of the mathematics confidence scale in TIMSS, as well 
as potential method effects arising from negatively worded items and 
item translations.
1.6. Purpose
The aim of this study is twofold. First, it examines the construct 
validity of a mixed-worded scale and the linguistic equivalence of its 
translation. Second, it explores the relationships between mathematics 
confidence and mathematics achievement, investigating whether 
different operationalisations of the latent construct influence this rela­
tionship. The specific research questions are: 
1. To what extent is there linguistic equivalence between the original 
and the translated items in the TIMSS 2019 mathematics confidence 
scale?
2. To what extent are there indications of method effects or bias in the 
mixed-worded mathematics confidence scale?
3. What are the relationships between mathematics confidence and 
mathematics achievement, depending on the operationalisation of 
the measurement model?
2. Methods and materials
2.1. Data
Data were drawn from the Swedish Grade 4 sample of TIMSS 2019. 
Conducted on a four-year cycle since 1995, TIMSS assesses students’ 
mathematics and science achievement in Grades 4 and 8 across 
participating countries. TIMSS employs a two-stage stratified sampling 
design. First, schools are randomly selected; second, one or two intact 
classrooms are chosen within each school. All TIMSS data are publicly 
available on the IEA website.3
The Swedish sample comprised a total of 3965 students nested 
within 224 classrooms across 145 schools. The average class size was 18 
students, and the average student age was 10.8 years.
2.2. Measures
The scale ‘student confident in mathematics’ comprised nine mixed- 
worded items, four positively worded and five negatively worded. For 
example, “I usually do well in mathematics”; “Mathematics is harder for 
me than for many of my classmates”; “I am good at working out difficult 
mathematics problems”; “Mathematics makes me confused”. Students 
rated their agreement with statements on a 4-point Likert scale ranging 
from Agree a lot (1) to Disagree a lot (4). Because higher response values 
indicated lower mathematics confidence, the positively worded items 
were recoded so that higher values consistently reflected higher confi­
dence. Thus, all items were scaled from 1 (Disagree a lot) to 4 (Agree a 
lot), ensuring conceptual consistency across items.
Mathematics achievement served as the outcome variable. TIMSS 
uses a rotated matrix-sampling design, in which each student was 
administered a subset of items from a larger item pool sufficient to yield 
a reliable measure (Martin et al., 2017). TIMSS employs item response 
theory (IRT) to generate five plausible values, which provide accurate 
estimates of each student’s proficiency (Foy et al., 2020). All five 
plausible values were included in the analysis to ensure unbiased and 
reliable measurement (von Davier, 2020).
2.3. Analytical methods

(Upsing & Rittberger, 2018).

scale introduced method effects or response bias. To address this ques­
tion, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed (Model A1) to 
evaluate the inter-item correlations among the nine items and detect 
potential wording effects (Brown, 2015). Previous research has shown

(Model 5) had the best model fit and was retained for further analyses.
Next, the bifactor exploratory structural equation modelling (ESEM) 
was applied to the nine mixed-worded items (Model A2). The bifactor 
ESEM approach was preferred over the correlated traits-correlated 
methods (CT-CM) model as it provides a more realistic representation 
of complex, multidimensional data and is less susceptible to being 
empirically underidentified and failing to converge as the CT-CM model 
(Brown, 2015).
2.3.2. Phase 2: Structural equation modelling
The first research question was a pragmatic semantic validation, 
conducted to assess conceptual and linguistic equivalence between the 
English source text and the Swedish translations (Upsing & Rittberger, 
2018).
The second research question examined whether the mixed-worded 
scale introduced method effects or response bias. To address this ques­
tion, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed (Model A1) to 
evaluate the inter-item correlations among the nine items and detect 
potential wording effects (Brown, 2015). Model B1 included all nine 
items. Model B2 included eight items (excluding item 7), and Model B3 
comprised three positively worded items (items 1, 4, and 6). Model B4 
used the target-rotated bifactor ESEM model, including one general 
factor representing overall mathematics confidence and two specific 
factors (reflecting positive and negative subscales. Mathematics 
achievement was regressed on the latent mathematics confidence factor 
(s) in each model to assess how different operationalisations influenced 
the observed relationships (Models B1-B4) (see Table 1).
2.4. Statistical analyses
All analyses were conducted in Mplus version 8.6 (Muth´en & 
Muth´en, 2017). To account for the hierarchical data structure, the 
cluster variable IDCLASS and the Mplus option TYPE = COMPLEX were 
used to adjust for clustering effects and the TOT-weight. Intra-class 
correlation coefficients (ICCs) for mathematics confidence were all 
below 0.05, indicating minimal between-class variance and justifying 
single-level SEM (Hox et al., 2011). Missing data were handled using 
full-information maximum likelihood (FIML). The robust maximum 
likelihood estimator (MLR) was applied, consistent with extensive evi­
dence that MLR performs well with Likert scales containing four or more 
categories and treating categorical data as continuous, especially in 
large samples (DiStefano & Motl, 2006; Lee & Stankov, 2023; Marsh 
et al., 2013; Teig et al., 2018; Yang Hansen et al., 2022; Yang et al., 
2012; Zhang & Bae, 2020). All five plausible values for mathematics 
achievement were included to ensure unbiased parameter estimation, 
using the Mplus option ‘TYPE = IMPUTATION.
Model fit was evaluated using multiple criteria: good model fit was 
indicated by CFI and TLI values above .95, RMSEA and SRMR below .05; 
acceptable model fit corresponded to CFI and TLI values above .90, 
RMSEA and SRMR below .08 (Brown, 2015). Chi-square statistics were 
reported but not used as strict criteria for model rejection due to the 
influence of the large sample size on these values (Brown, 2015).

3.2. Phase 1: Validating the construct of mathematics confidence
3.2.1. Semantic validation
To address the first research question, whether there was linguistic 
equivalence between the original and translated items, the study 
examined the validity of the scale of mathematics confidence. A prag­
matic semantic validation was conducted to ensure conceptual and 
linguistic equivalence between the English source text and the Swedish 
target text (see Table 3). Considering the items' directions, four items 
had a direct positive mode (items 1, 4, 6, and 7), four out of the five 
negative items had a direct negative mode (items 2, 5, 8, and 9), and one 
had a negated positive mode (item 3 = I am just not good at mathe­
matics). Where notable translation differences are observed, the literal 
translations of the opposite wording are presented in square brackets or 
described as ‘identical’ when no differences occur.
Five items exhibited notable translation differences, marked with an 
asterisk in Table 3, and discussed below. In item 1, the grammatical 
subject changed from “I” in English to “it” in Swedish. This shift moves 
the focus from the student’s ability and performance to the general 
experience of the situation, subtly distancing the respondent from the 
action. In item 6, the English phrase ‘working out’ emphasises the 
calculation process involved in solving a mathematics problem. The 
Swedish translation uses the verb ‘solving’ and replaces ‘problem’ with 
‘task’, which has a broader meaning encompassing exercises, activities, 
or problems, thereby emphasising a more general problem-solving skill. 
For item 7, the English phrase ‘good at’ is translated with the preposition 
‘i’ instead of the preposition ‘på’. Although both prepositions can be 
used interchangeably, ‘i’ implies competence and results within a spe­
cific field, such as the subject matter of mathematics, whereas ‘på’ em­
phasises ability or aptitude in mathematics more broadly. In item 8, the 
English version compares the difficulty of mathematics with all other 
school subjects, while the Swedish version states that mathematics is the 
hardest subject without making explicit comparisons. Both versions 
focus on the student’s experience, but the English phrasing includes a 
comparative element. Finally, item 9 differs in subject focus. In the 
English version, the subject is ‘mathematics’, suggesting that mathe­
matics is the cause of confusion, actively rendering the student confused, 
whereas the Swedish version uses ‘I’, highlighting the student’s personal 
experience and reaction when working with mathematics. A more literal 
translation would have been acceptable.

worded scale of mathematics confidence revealed indications of 
method effects or bias. The mathematics confidence scale was examined 
through CFA, focusing on inter-item correlations (Model A1; see 
Table 4). Positively worded items were recoded so that higher values 
indicate higher mathematics confidence. The overall model fit was poor

items, and correlations between the positively worded and negatively

were positively skewed, and the negatively worded items were nega­

ently to item wording.

3.2.3. Bi-factor ESEM
To further explore the validity of the mathematics confidence items, 
a bifactor ESEM (Model A2) was estimated (see Table 5). Before anal­
ysis, the positively worded items were reverse-coded so that higher 
values consistently indicated higher mathematics confidence. All nine 
items loaded significantly on the general factor, representing overall 
mathematics confidence. The positive specific factor included signifi­
cant loadings from seven items, the four positively worded items (items 
1, 4, 6, and 7) and three negatively worded items (items 2, 5, and 8). The 
negative specific factor showed significant loadings from one positively 
worded item (item 4) and three negatively worded items (items 5, 8, and 
9). Two negatively worded items (items 2 and 3) did not load signifi­
cantly on the negative specific factor, suggesting that they primarily 
reflect the general factor rather than the specific negative factor. Model 
fit was good.
However, several items demonstrated low factor loadings (< 0.40), 
indicating limited validity. Item 7 showed problematic loadings, a low 
and negative factor loading on the general factor (-.29) and a moderate 
loading on the positive specific factor (.36). This suggests that item 7 
may tap into a different latent construct, such as the ‘supportive 
climate’, reflecting the teacher’s actions rather than the student’s self- 
belief in mathematics confidence.
3.3. Phase 2: Structural equation modelling
The third research question addressed the relationships between 
mathematics confidence and mathematics achievement, depending on 
the operationalisation of the measurement model. This was examined in 
a validation process assessing the model fit in four models (Models B1- 
B4). In each model, mathematics achievement was regressed on 
different operationalisations of the latent construct of mathematics 
confidence. Model B1 comprised all nine items from the mathematics 
confidence scale. Although the standardised coefficients were signifi­
cant, the overall model fit was poor. Model B2 comprised eight items, 
excluding item 7, which appeared to reflect a different construct 
(possibly related to a supportive teacher rather than student confi­
dence). The model fit was not acceptable. Model B3 operationalised the 
latent construct using three positively worded items (items 1, 4, and 6). 
The model fit was good. In each of the three models (B1-B3), the items 
included had significant factor loadings positively loading onto the 
latent construct. All three models revealed significant positive relations 
between mathematics confidence and mathematics achievement, with 
standardised regression coefficients ranging from.322 (Model B3) to 
.393 (Model B2) (see Table 6). Models B1 and B2 explained 15 % of the 
variance in mathematics achievement, and Model B3 accounted for 
10 %.
The final model (Model B4) tested the target-rotated bifactor ESEM 
model with orthogonal rotation, consisting of one general factor (G- 
factor) onto which all items loaded, a specific positive factor and one 
negative factor. Model fit was good: X2/df = 70.747/18; CFI = .994; TLI 
= .984; RMSEA = .027; SRMR = .011. However, standardised correla­
tions between mathematics achievement and the factors revealed un­
expected patterns. A significant negative relationship for the G-factor 
(-.45), a small but significant positive relationship for the negative s- 
factor (.10), and a non-significant relationship for the positive S-factor 
(.07, p = .054). Several negatively worded items had negative or 
nonsignificant factor loadings (e.g., items 3 and 8), suggesting hetero­
geneity in how students responded to these items. All three latent factors 
were modelled orthogonally, and the residual variances were significant 
across all indicators, suggesting no overfitting. These counterintuitive 
findings may reflect complex interactions between perceived mathe­
matics confidence and actual ability, inconsistencies related to reverse 
coding, or suppression effects. This would suggest method effects related 
to item valence that influence the scale’s predictive validity.
Among Models B1-B4, the best model fit is observed in Models B3 
and B4. While Models B1-B3 show similar standardised coefficients for 
the relationships between student mathematics confidence and mathe­
matics achievement, the contradictory signs and significance in Model 
B4 complicate interpretations. Accordingly, inferences from Model B4 
should be made cautiously. Subsequently, the model with the best model 
fit is found in the most parsimonious model (Model B3), with a stand­
ardised coefficient easily interpreted. Therefore, this study suggests that 
the operationalisation of Model B3, including the three positively wor­
ded items 1, 4, and 6 (see Appendix A, Table 8), is the most valid and 
reliable measurement model to reflect students’ mathematics confi­
dence in further secondary analyses.

4. Discussion
This study examined the construct validity and the linguistic equiv­
alence of the mixed-worded mathematics confidence scale in TIMSS 
2019, drawing on Swedish TIMSS grade 4 data to address three research 
questions concerning translation, method effect, and the relationship 
between mathematics confidence and achievement. The first research 
question explored the extent of the linguistic equivalence between the 
original and the Swedish translations of the mathematics confidence 
scale. Although TIMSS provides rigorous translation guidelines, the 
findings indicate that further research on the linguistic equivalence is 
warranted (Ercikan et al., 2015). Consistent with previous research 
(Ercikan et al., 2015; Upsing & Rittberger, 2018), results suggest that 
not all items were linguistically and culturally equivalent. For example, 
in the Swedish version, the shift from the first-person pronoun (“I”) to an 
impersonal form (“it”) may distance the respondents from the action, 
potentially altering item meaning. Addressing such translation dis­
crepancies could involve implementing double translation procedures or 
qualitative interviews in single-country secondary studies to explore 
how both younger and older students interpret items. As suggested by 
Ros´en and Nilsen (2024), secondary analyses using ILSA data should 
include validation studies that explicitly consider translation and item 
phrasing to identify and reduce cross-cultural bias.
The second research question examined potential method effects or 
response bias associated with the mixed-worded scale of mathematics 
confidence. In line with previous research (Michaelides, 2019; Stein­
mann, S´anchez, et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2012), this study found in­
dications of inconsistent responders and possible method effects. Some 
students appeared to agree with both positively and negatively worded 
items rather than adjusting their responses according to the item di­
rection. Particularly, item 7 (My teacher tells me I am good at mathe­
matics) showed weak inter-item correlations and the highest rate of 
missingness, and low factor loadings in the bifactor ESEM model, sug­
gesting that it may reflect perceptions of teacher support rather than 
students’ mathematics confidence. Overall, the validation process 
revealed issues related to inconsistent responders, wording effects in the 
mixed-worded scale (DiStefano & Motl, 2006; Steinmann, Strietholt, 
et al., 2022), and possible translation-related biases (Min et al., 2016). 
Analyses using latent constructs that fail to represent respondents’ true 
attitudes risk producing biased or misleading results. While excluding 
negatively worded items can improve psychometric consistency, it may 
narrow the conceptual scope of the construct, thus reducing content 
validity. Nevertheless, given the linguistic complexity and potential to 
increase cognitive load, particularly for younger students (Michaelides, 
2019; Wang et al., 2018), negatively worded items were excluded in the 
final operationalisation of the latent construct in this study, aligning 
with previous research (Hooper et al., 2013; Michaelides, 2019; Stein­
mann, Strietholt, et al., 2022).
The third research question examined the relationship between 
mathematics confidence and mathematics achievement across different 
operationalisations of the construct. Consistent with previous studies 
(Lee & Stankov, 2018; Marsh & Martin, 2011), the results revealed 
positive relationships between mathematics confidence and mathe­
matics achievement in all SEM models. When construct validity was 
considered and only the three positively worded items were retained, 
the reduced model showed good model fit and a significant positive 
relationship with mathematics achievement. Across the three SEM 
models, no evidence emerged of the negative relationship between ac­
ademic self-concept and achievement reported in several cross-cultural 
studies (Marsh et al., 2019; Min et al., 2016). This divergence may be 
due to the single-country context and the use of single-level SEM. By 
contrast, a study of Hong Kong TIMSS 2019 (Chen, 2022) using multi­
level SEM found a negative relation between mathematics confidence 
and mathematics achievement at the classroom level but a positive one 
at the student level. This study’s target-rotated bifactor ESEM model 
produced a similar complex pattern, with the general factor showing a 
negative relationship with mathematics achievement, making the 
interpretation of these results more complex.
Overall, the findings highlight that careful validation of mixed- 
worded scales and translated items is important for secondary analysis 
drawing on translated ILSA data. This single-country study highlights 
the importance of assessing construct validity and translation equiva­
lence before constructing latent factors and interpreting their relation­
ships with learning outcomes. While the reliance on Swedish student 
questionnaire data may introduce mono-method bias and limit gen­
eralisability, and the cross-sectional nature of TIMSS excludes causal 
inferences, the findings provide valuable insights into the psychometric 
and linguistic challenges of mixed-worded scales. Further research could 
extend this work by validating other affective scales and translated items 
in ILSA questionnaires across cultural contexts using both quantitative 
and qualitative methods.
5. Conclusion
Latent constructs employed in secondary analyses must be grounded 
in both statistical and semantic validation processes, combining ap­
proaches such as CFA, bifactor ESEM, SEM, and linguistic analyses. 
Mixed-worded scales, although intended to maintain respondent atten­
tiveness and mitigate acquiescence bias, may compromise validity if 
they are linguistically complex and increase cognitive load, particularly 
for younger respondents or less proficient readers. The findings that 
different operationalisations of the same latent construct produce 
divergent results underscore the need for transparent reporting and 
careful justification of conceptualisations and measurement decisions. 
Ultimately, this study contributes to methodological discussions on 
construct validity in secondary ILSA research.
