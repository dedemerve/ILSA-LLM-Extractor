We examined how teacher support and disciplinary climate may have additional benefits for students from low
(or high) socioeconomic status (SES) families in high and low globally competitive economies. Multilevel
regression analyses on 184102 students from 6397 schools from diversified globally competitive economies in
the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) showed that: (a) students in high SES families
benefited more in reading in higher than lower globally competitive economies; (b) greater teacher support
improved reading in high than low globally competitive economies; (c) teacher support would alleviate the
negative impact of low SES on reading; and (d) a positive disciplinary climate enhances reading performance in
both high and low globally competitive economies, with SES effects being more pronounced in schools with a
positive disciplinary climate, especially in low globally competitive economies. Practical implications, limitations, and future research directions are discussed.

Reading proficiency is a foundational skill critical for academic
success (Dolean et al., 2019). Reading achievement is influenced by
multiple factors, including individual attributes, family background, and
school environment (Chiu & Khoo, 2005). Among these, family socioeconomic status (SES) is a significant determinant and central to discussions on educational equity (Coleman, 1968). Extensive research
have indicated that children from economically disadvantaged backgrounds often lag behind their more affluent peers in academic performance (Kim et al., 2019; Lam & Zhou, 2022; Sirin, 2005). Given the
substantial impact of family SES on academic outcomes, it is essential to
examine external environmental factors that can mitigate its effects.
This examination is crucial for educators and policymakers to develop
targeted, effective interventions that can help socioeconomically
disadvantaged children succeed academically, break the cycle of
poverty, and promote greater educational equity.
Empirical evidence shows that, in addition to family SES, the school
environment significantly impacts reading achievement (Aikens & Barbarin, 2008). Grounded in the ecological systems theory, children’s

development is influenced by dynamic interactions within multiple
environments (Bronfenbrenner, 1979, 1989). These contextual spheres
can either enhance or hinder a student’s developmental trajectory. For
instance, students’ learning patterns and school engagement are shaped
by their social milieu, highlighting the interaction effect of external
environment and family background (Chiu & Khoo, 2005). Recognizing
these diverse effects underscores the need for a holistic view that includes societal and school environments to understand disparities in
students’ reading achievement. Besides, Creemers and Kyriakides
(2006) have proposed a dynamic model of educational effectiveness,
which considers these interrelated sociological and psychological variables at each level in explaining student learning outcomes. Kyriakides
et al. (2018) also emphasized the importance of teaching quality within
this dynamic model.
Therefore, the present study focused on exploring how social and
school contexts affect the impacts of family SES on reading achievement.
We also hypothesized the varying effects of the social environment on
reading achievement across economies linked to the schooling

1. Theoretical backgrounds

2.1. Direct and moderation role of teacher support

Bronfenbrenner’s (1979, 1989) ecological systems theory underpins
this study, emphasizing the complex nature of children’s development
influenced by multiple layers of interaction, from immediate family and
school settings to broader cultural norms and societal practices. The
theory comprises five interconnected components: the microsystem (e.
g., family, friends, neighbors, school), the mesosystem (e.g., relationships between the groups from the microsystem system), the exosystem
(e.g., extended family, media, local governments), the macrosystem (e.
g., culture, social norms), and the chronosystem (e.g., time)
(Bronfenbrenner, 1979, 1989). Therefore, a comprehensive exploration
of child development necessitates examining not only the children and
their immediate environment but also the intricate interplay between
the broader environmental contexts. In the present study, we incorporated critical tenets of Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory as
foundational pillars for designing strategies that benefit students and
education practices. Specifically, we addressed three core inquiries: (a)
discerning the influence of proximal relationships (microsystem, e.g.,
family SES) on students’ reading achievement; (b) the link and impact
between a student’s immediate relationships (e.g., family SES) and
external relationships (mesosystem, e.g., teacher support, disciplinary
climate) that might potentially affect student reading achievement; (c)
how the global backdrop (macrosystem) steered students’ educational
progress.
Besides, other theories, such as the family investment model and the
risk and resilience model, would uniquely contribute to understanding
the SES-reading achievement relationship and the moderating roles of
teacher support and disciplinary climate.

Teacher support encompasses to various dimensions, including
informational, instrumental, emotional, and appraisal support, tailored
to address students’ diverse needs (Tardy, 1985). Supportive teachers
always demonstrate a keen interest in each student’s learning, providing
personalized feedback, addressing quires, and adapting practices to individual learning requirements (Bl!
omeke et al., 2016). Teachers can
positively influence students through meaningful interactions, timely
responses, academic and emotional support, and supportive classroom
environments (Crosnoe et al., 2004). Prior research has established a
direct correlation between teacher support and enhanced reading proficiency (Ma et al., 2021), forming the current study’s foundational
premise for moderation analysis.
The risk and resilience model provides a framework to understand
and address the complex interactions between risk factors and protective
factors that influence students’ adaptation. The model posits that the
protective elements within a child’s environment can facilitate adaptive
development and diminish adverse outcomes, even in challenging circumstances (Zimmerman & Arunkumar, 1994). “Risk” refers to factors
increasing the likelihood of unfavorable outcomes, while “resilience”
denotes the accumulation of developmental assets that can buffer the
negative impacts of risk exposure (Brooks, 2006). Previous studies have
shown that familial and contextual risks can impede positive outcomes
for many youths, and schools offer a potential resource that can facilitate
the resilience processes that scaffold healthy outcomes (Christle et al.,
2007; Sharkey et al., 2008). Teachers are uniquely positioned to support
positive outcomes for youth in adverse contexts, suggesting that teacher
support could function as a moderator to alleviate the negative impact of
socioeconomic disadvantage on students. From this perspective, effective and sufficient teacher support may mitigate the detrimental consequences of growing up in financially constrained families or low
competitive economies.
Existing literature consistently demonstrates the critical role of
teacher support, particularly within economically disadvantaged student cohorts (Chi et al., 2018; Haw et al., 2021; Ma, Jiao et al., 2022).
For example, Haw et al. (2021) showed that teacher support significantly enhances students’ reading proficiency while mitigating the influence of SES on academic achievement using the 2018 Philippines
PISA dataset. Similarly, Ma et al. (2022) found that teacher support alleviates the negative effect on reading achievement among economically
disadvantaged students in China. Accordingly, we hypothesize that
teacher support positively moderates the relationship between SES and
secondary students’ reading achievement.

2. The relationship between family SES and reading
achievement
SES denotes an individual’s or family’s social and economic position
(Marks, 2014). In educational contexts, SES is typically evaluated
through a composite index involving income, home possessions,
parental educational attainment, and occupation (OCED, 2019; Sirin,
2005). The family investment model provides mechanisms explaining
the association between SES and academic outcome; specifically, low
family SES is associated with less financial investment in activities (e.g.,
reading activities) with children, while high SES families provide children with rich cultural capital that contributes to their learning (Conger
& Donnellan, 2007). Substantial empirical research and meta-analyses
have established a strong positive correlation between family SES and
achievement, particularly in reading (Hart et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2020;
Sirin, 2005).
For instance, Chiu et al. (2012) found a positive relationship between
SES and reading achievement across diverse cultures using PISA data. Su
et al. (2017) found that preschool linguistic skills and family factors
predicted later literacy skills in Chinese children. Moreover, Lervåg et al.
(2019) highlighted the significant impact of SES on the development of
reading abilities among Roma children, an economically disadvantaged
group.4 Recent research by Ma et al. (2023), using PISA 2018 data,
reaffirmed the strong link between SES and reading achievement in both
China and the United States. Research also indicated that SES-related
disparities in reading achievement are considerable in most countries
(Chmielewski, 2018; Hanushek et al., 2022; Lam & Zhou, 2022).
Prior research, theoretically and empirically, highlights the significant role of SES in reading development across diverse economies.

2.2. Direct and moderation role of disciplinary climate
Disciplinary climate is a well-researched aspect of school characteristics (Atlay et al., 2019). It pertains to students’ perceptions
regarding the stability and effectiveness of classroom rules and the frequency of disciplinary incidents (e.g., interruptions) among peers
(Cheema & Kitsantas, 2014). A sound disciplinary climate characterized
by clear expectations, consistent rule enforcement, and a focus on positive behavior can create a safe and supportive learning environment
that encourages students’ engagement and motivation to learn (Wang &
Degol, 2016). Substantial research has shown a positive correlation
between classroom disciplinary climate and students’ reading performance (Ma Xiao et al., 2022; Ning et al., 2015), providing a foundation
for further moderation analysis.

2.4. The present study
The link between family SES and academic achievement is well
established in the literature. According to the risk and resilience model,
schools could also offer potential resources, such as teacher support and
good classroom discipline, which facilitate resilience processes and
compensate for the negative impact of low-income family resources on
students’ academic performance. We postulated that in disadvantaged
economies (a risk factor), teacher support and a positive classroom
discipline would more effectively buffer the negative impact of disadvantaged family backgrounds than in advantaged economies. Hence,
this research aimed to compare the moderating role of teacher support
and disciplinary climate on the SES and reading relationship across
varied economies.
Compared to the sole reliance on GDP as a metric in previous studies,
we added an economic dimension at the country level by incorporating
global competitiveness as measured by the Global Competitiveness
Index (GCI; Schwab, 2018), which provided a comprehensive framework to assess national competitiveness, encompassing not only economic dimensions but also institutions, policies, and factors influencing
productivity (Schwab, 2018). In summary, this study expanded on the
effect of SES, teacher support, and disciplinary climate on reading
abilities across high and low GCI economies. Specifically, the following

2.3. Cultural context considerations
According to Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory, the relationship between SES and reading achievement and the moderating
roles of teacher support and disciplinary climate may vary across
different economies. The family investment model suggests that lowincome families may struggle to invest adequately in activities that
promote child development, such as reading books with their children.
Wealthier economies generally have greater human, financial, and social capital reserves than less developed economies (Lee & Borgonovi,
2022), so parents may provide more economic and emotional support to
their children. As a result, these children’s developmental outcomes
often surpass those of their less advantaged peers (Becker & Tomes,
1986; Schofield et al., 2011), making the SES-reading relationship more
pronounced compared to less developed economies. Previous empirical
research indicated that high-income countries or those with higher GDP
tend to perform better academically in large-scale international assessments than middle- or low-income countries (Baker et al., 2002; Chiu &
Khoo, 2005; Chiu & McBride-Chang, 2006). The finding also implies the
possibility of differing SES-reading relationships across economies.
In addition, previous studies also emphasize the crucial role of the
cultural context in comprehending the dynamics of teacher-student interactions within school settings (den Brok & Levy, 2005; Ma et al.,
2023; Ma et al., 2022). For example, in developed economies with
abundant resources, teacher-student interactions may be enhanced due
to the successful implementation of learner-centered teaching (Shah &
Kumar, 2021). Conversely, lower- and middle-income economies adhere
less to the student-centered methods (Sakata et al., 2022), potentially
reducing teacher support. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the
cross-economy disparities in the direct impact of teacher support.
Simultaneously, empirical inquiry is warranted to explore the varying
extent to which teacher support moderates the SES-reading link across
different economies.
Furthermore, different cultural and behavioral norms across nations
may lead to varying effects of disciplinary climate on reading. For
example, higher-income countries prioritize cultivating positive
teacher-student relationships and establishing a secure, supportive
learning environment through the widespread adoption of studentcentered approaches (Shah & Kumar, 2021). However, resource constraints and limited teacher training in low-income countries may hinder

3. Method
3.1. Data sources and participants
The data used in this research were drawn from the database of the
2018 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) (OECD,
2019) The target PISA samples were selected with Probability Proportionate to Size Sampling (PPS) (OECD, 2019). Students were nested in
sampled schools for each economy. Based on the GCI index presented in
the World Economic Forum report (Schwab, 2018), we selected 15
high-GCI economies and 11 low-GCI economies, totaling 26 economies
representing 184102 students from 6397 schools for analysis. The GCI
ranges from 0 to 100, with a high value indicating greater global
competitiveness. Integrating educational indicators with the GCI

3.7. Covariates
At the student level, gender consistently appears as a significant
predictor of reading achievement, necessitating its inclusion as a
controlled variable in prior studies (Chiu & McBride-Chang, 2006).
Moreover, Lopez-Agudo et al. (2021) reported that students who undertook tests in languages other than their home language performed
worse in both reading and mathematics, a phenomenon previously
addressed by Marchant and Finch (2016). Therefore, we incorporated
this linguistic factor as a covariate at the student level. Specifically,
gender is dichotomously coded, with “1” representing “female” and “0”
representing “male.” Likewise, the index of language used at home is
binary, assuming the value ‘‘1′’ when the test is taken in the student’s
native language and “0′’ otherwise. In addition, school SES significantly
influences students’ reading performance in elementary and middle
schools (Marchant & Finch, 2016). Therefore, school SES, aggregated by
the mean individual SES score of all students in a school, was also
controlled for in the present analysis. Higher values indicate higher
school SES.

3.2. Measures
In the present study, we tested the proposed hypotheses with
weighted likelihood estimates (WLE) scores in data analyses as in previous studies (Ma et al., 2022). The WLE scores were obtained from the
IRT scaling procedure (OECD, 2019).
3.3. Socio-economic status (SES)
In PISA 2018, SES was presented by the economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS). This composite score incorporates three key indicators: the highest parental occupational status, educational
attainment, and household possessions. Each component was standardized to achieve a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. The
ESCS index is the arithmetic mean of the three standardized components
(OECD, 2019). Higher values indicate better family SES.

3.8. Statistical analyses
Mplus 8.8 was used for the analysis. The percentages of missing data
for the language used at home, SES, disciplinary climate, and teacher
support were 2.86 %, 3.04 %, 4.08 %, and 4.16 % of the total samples,
respectively. As the missing data were less than 5 %, the missing data in
this study were analyzed in Mplus by default, Full Information
Maximum Likelihood (FIML) estimation, which unites all variable information in the data to estimate model parameters and account for
missing values (Muth”en & Muth”en, 2017).

3.4. Teacher support
Teacher support, defined by various dimensions, including informational, instrumental, emotional, and appraisal support (Tardy, 1985),
was measured.in PISA 2018 by four items representing students’
perceived emotional, instrumental, appraisal, and informational support
from their teachers in reading class. Students were asked how often (1 →
every lesson, 2 → most lessons, 3 → some lessons, and 4 → never or hardly
ever) specific supportive behaviors occurred, such as “the teacher shows
an interest in every student’s learning” and “the teacher gives extra help
when students need it”. The reliability of student-perceived teacher
support ranged from.773 to.922 across participating economies (OECD,
2019). The school-level teacher support index was the average score for
each school, with higher scores indicating more teacher support in the
school.

3.9. Analytical model
The multilevel model can estimate the interaction between variables
across levels and explore the effects at different levels (Krull & MacKinnon, 2001). Thus, we employed four two-level hierarchical models for
data analysis in four steps (M0 to M3). M0 was a null model to test the
reading performance variance explained at the school level; M1 was a
direct effect model testing the effect of family SES on reading achievement; M2 tested the moderation effect of school teacher support(M2a)
and disciplinary climate (M2b); M3 was a final model to test teacher
support and disciplinary climate effect together. Instead of reporting
regression coefficient, effect size, which measures the magnitude or
strength of the relationship between variables, independent of sample
size, was considered in addition to statistically significant. The details of
the analytical models, effect size, and interpretation of the results are
shown in the Appendix.

3.5. Disciplinary climate
As mentioned earlier, disciplinary climate pertains to students’ perceptions regarding the stability and effectiveness of classroom rules and
the frequency of disciplinary incidents (e.g., interruptions) among peers
in the class (Cheema & Kitsantas, 2014). In PISA 2018, students reported
the frequency of five disruptive events during language lessons, such as
“students don’t listen to what the teacher says” and “there is noise and
disorder,” using a four-point scale (1 → every lesson, 2 → most lessons, 3
→ some lessons, 4 → never or hardly ever). The reliability of the disciplinary climate scale for all participating economies ranged from.755
to.930 (OECD, 2019). The school-level disciplinary climate index was
the mean value of student-level responses, with higher scores indicating
a better disciplinary climate.

3.10. Interpretation of the interaction
When a significant interaction is detected, interpreting it becomes
critically important. We used the Johnson-Neyman (J-N) method to
calculate regions of significance, exploring the range over which the
moderator affects the focal predictor as significantly positive, nonsignificant, or significantly negative (Bauer & Curran, 2005; Preacher
et al., 2006). We plotted the 95 % confidence interval (CI) and confidence bands over the full range of the moderating variable, illustrating
the association between the independent and dependent variables.
Readers interested in technical details about these procedures can refer
to Bauer and Curran’s (2005) work.

3.6. Reading achievement
Reading was the major domain of PISA 2018, which assessed the
skills of information location, comprehension, evaluation, and reflection. Reading achievement was examined using both computer-based
assessment and traditional paper-pencil assessments, along with a variety of item types, texts (single tests and multiple texts), and scenarios.
PISA 2018 measured reading achievement using 10 plausible values
(PVs) following the PISA data manual. The 10 PVs were assigned to 10
separate datasets, and each model was analyzed 10 times. The 10 results
from each analysis were then integrated using Rubin’s (1987) rules to
obtain an average estimate and corresponding standard error (Ma et al.,
2022; OECD, 2019).

5. Discussion
Using PISA 2018 data from 15 high GCI economies and 11 low GCI
economies, this study examined the relationship between SES and
reading achievement. Employing multilevel regression analysis, we
accounted for the moderating roles of teacher support and disciplinary
climate in both high and low GCI economies. Results highlighted the
substantial impact of SES on students’ reading performance. Additionally, teacher support and disciplinary climate exhibited distinct influence patterns on the SES-reading achievement relationship across
different economies. Theoretically, this study integrates Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory with the risk and resilience framework and the family investment model, providing new insights into the
moderating roles of teacher support and disciplinary climate. Practically, it underscores the critical importance of addressing educational
disparities and promoting equity in schools.

economies
Our findings underscored the affirmative impact of family SES and
school SES on reading achievement, highlighting inequalities in reading
outcomes. The consistent association between SES and reading
achievement aligns with existing research, reinforcing the strong predictive capacity of SES (Chiu et al., 2012; Hart et al., 2013; Liu et al.,
2020). The explanation for the association between SES and reading
literacy is multifaceted. First, lower SES students may experience

moderating effect of school disciplinary climate on academic achievement, particularly in low GCI economies. While the observed effect size
may be small, it is important to note that minor effects can accumulate
and have significant long-term implications (Abelson, 1985). Academic
achievement measured at a single point in time represents only a
snapshot of educational success or outcomes. Therefore, the long-term
impact of these small effects should not be overlooked. As Matz et al.
(2017) have stated, even small effects can still matter significantly in the
context of big data and large-scale educational assessments.

paradoxically amplifies educational inequity, a trend predominantly
discernible in economies with lower GCIs. The phenomenon finds its
roots in the cultural capital theory: students from privileged backgrounds benefit from enriched educational settings, including highquality educators and a conducive disciplinary climate. international standards (Heyneman & Loxley, 1983). In contrast, in less
competitive economies, limited resources, overcrowded classrooms,
lack of qualified teachers, outdated teaching materials, and insufficient
funding can impact the quality of education (Tikly, 2011). Therefore, in
these less competitive economies, students from wealthy families may
be more likely to study in high-quality schools with sound disciplinary
climates, increasing SES-achievement gaps under a positive disciplinary
climate context.

5.2. The role of teacher support and disciplinary climate
5.2.1. Direct effect of teacher support and disciplinary climate
Our findings revealed that students perceived more teacher support
but a poorer disciplinary climate in low GCI economies than high GCI
economies. The risk and resilience model can explain the higher
perception of teacher support in low GCI economies. Students from
lower-income or disadvantaged backgrounds may rely more heavily on
the emotional and social support provided by their teachers, even if the
actual level of teacher support is not significantly higher. This is due to
fewer resources and social safety nets available outside school. The
poorer perceived disciplinary climate in low GCI economies could be
related to systemic factors such as larger class sizes, limited school
infrastructure, and less effective school management and policies.
For the direct effect of teacher support, findings suggested that the
positive effect of teacher support on reading performance was stronger
in high GCI economies compared to low GCI economies. This discrepancy can be attributed to several factors. Wealthier nations are more
likely to employ student-centered teaching approaches (Sakata et al.,
2022), fostering stronger teacher-student interaction and resulting in
improved reading outcomes. Conversely, economies with lower GCI
scores exhibit limited integration of student-centered methods, yielding
negligible positive effects of teacher support on reading outcomes.
(Sakata et al., 2022). An analysis of PISA 2018 school data reveals that
high GCI economies typically have smaller class sizes and lower
teacher-student ratios, allowing teachers greater individualized attention and support opportunities. With fewer students to manage, teachers
in high GCI economies can devote more time and resources to improving
each student’s academic achievement, including their reading
proficiency.
Regarding the direct effect of the disciplinary climate effect, a positive disciplinary climate would enhance students’ reading performance.
The finding was consistent across economies regardless of the global
competitiveness level. This underscores the paramount significance of
cultivating an optimal classroom disciplinary climate. Such an environment fosters a conducive and orderly classroom environment where
students feel safe, respected, and engaged. As a result, students are inclined to develop a favorable attitude toward learning, thereby exerting
a constructive influence on their reading performance (Kutsyuruba
et al., 2015). Moreover, a positive disciplinary climate reduces disruptive behaviors and classroom disruptions, allowing teachers to allocate
more instructional time to reading instruction and practice, thereby
improving reading outcomes (Blank & Shavit, 2016).

5.2.3. Practical implications
First, our results underscore the substantial impact of SES on students’ reading performance, particularly in high GCI economies, and
highlight the influence of teacher support on reading proficiency in
these economies. Therefore, ensuring high-quality education at the
school level for all students should be a top priority, alongside promoting educational equity. Numerous studies on educational effectiveness and improvement have also emphasized the critical importance of
quality education at the school level (Kyriakides & Creemers, 2018;
Kyriakides, Creemers & Charalambous, 2018). By focusing on quality
teaching practices, targeted interventions, and equitable allocation of
educational resources, schools and education systems can help mitigate
the achievement gap associated with socioeconomic disparities.
The impact of teacher support on reading proficiency in high GCI
economies highlights the need for effective strategies in lower GCI
economies. Inadequate teaching systems in these regions may contribute
to this disparity, with limited professional development opportunities
hampering teachers’ ability to improve reading skills and support students. To enhance students’ perceived teacher support for lower GCI

5.3. Limitation and future directions
One fundamental limitation of the current research is that it relies on
cross-sectional data, meaning it was collected at a single time point. This
data type does not allow conclusions about the observed correlations’
causal direction. In other words, we cannot determine whether the
factors influence each other or if the relationship is simply correlational.
To address this, future studies should utilize longitudinal data, which
tracks the same individuals or groups over an extended period. This
would enable researchers to understand the dynamic nature of the relationships better and potentially infer causality.
Additionally, the research indicates that the moderation effect of
disciplinary climate was relatively small. While this finding provides
insight, qualitative methods such as interviews could offer a richer and
more nuanced understanding of the underlying dynamics. Interviews
would allow researchers to delve deeper into the participants’ perspectives and experiences, potentially uncovering additional factors or
contextual elements that influence the relationships being studied.
Finally, the current study collected data at the school level, which
provides a broad overview of the phenomena. However, future research
could benefit from gathering data at a more granular level, such as the
classroom or specific learning groups. This would enable researchers to
better identify and analyze the specific dynamics within these smaller,
more defined units, potentially leading to a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing the outcomes of interest.
By addressing these limitations in future studies, researchers can
build upon the existing knowledge and provide a more robust and
multifaceted understanding of the complex relationships and dynamics
within the educational context.
6. Conclusions
Our findings revealed that improved family economic status,
including income and assets, is associated with higher grades of reading
achievement in high GCI economies, supporting the family investment
model. Greater perceived teacher support improved reading performance in high GCI economies but not in low GCI economies. A positive
disciplinary climate boosted reading performance in both high and low
GCI economies, and family SES effect on reading is more pronounced in
schools with a positive climate, especially in lower GCI economies.
These findings support an integrative model combined Bronfenbrenner’s
ecological system with combining Bronfenbrenner’s ecological system
with a risk and resilience framework and family investment model in
explaining students’ academic success in reading.
