ABSTRACT

ARTICLE HISTORY

This study explores teachers’ instructional activities to ask students
provide evidence-based explanations in science lessons. The
sample of this study is 8739 eighth-grade students, 456 science
teachers, and 273 schools in the US that participated in TIMSS
2019. Utilising a hierarchical linear modelling approach, the study
found a signiﬁcant correlation between teachers asking students
to provide evidence-based explanations and student performance
in answering reasoning questions on TIMSS 2019. The study
suggests that promoting career growth that emphasises science
content, science pedagogy, and critical thinking is closely
associated with teachers’ encouragement of students using
evidence-based explanations. Furthermore, the study indicates
that teachers who undertook professional development (PD) in the
last two years are more likely asking students to provide evidencebased explanations. However, the lack of science resources
signiﬁcantly inﬂuences teachers’ decisions to asking students to
provide evidence-based explanations in science lessons.

Introduction
Previous studies advocated inquiry-based learning activities for science teachers in their
classrooms (Marshall et al., 2017; Miranda & Damico, 2015). This approach improves
students’ scientiﬁc skills by allowing them to explore concepts and phenomena independently before providing formal explanations (Marshall et al., 2017). Inquiry-based learning engages students in the genuine process of scientiﬁc discovery, promoting scientiﬁc
thinking and reasoning skills (Pedaste et al., 2015). Various classroom activities are
included in this approach as a student-centered pedagogy that promotes active learning,
scientiﬁc thinking, and reasoning skills (Adey & Shayer, 1990; Gerber et al., 2001;
Lawson, 1995; Marek & Cavallo, 1997). A critical component of eﬀectively implementing
inquiry-based learning is the professional development (PD) of teachers. PD programmes are designed to enhance teachers’ knowledge and competencies, enabling
them to create productive classroom activities that support students’ use of evidence
in providing explanations (Borko, 2004; Biggers et al., 2013; Luft & Hewson, 2014).

According to Duschl and Grandy (2008), understanding the association between evidence and knowledge is essential for students to grasp the nature of science as a systematic method of inquiry. This coordination involves critical thinking, reasoning, and the
ability to construct and evaluate scientiﬁc explanations based on empirical data. Research
by Osborne et al. (2003) underscores the signiﬁcance of this coordination in fostering
students’ scientiﬁc literacy. They argue that students who can eﬀectively link evidence
to scientiﬁc concepts are better equipped to understand and engage with scientiﬁc
issues. Additionally, Wilson et al. (2010) highlight that inquiry-based learning can signiﬁcantly enhance students’ knowledge, reasoning, and argumentation.
Despite its importance, there is a noted gap in the literature regarding how this coordination is eﬀectively taught and assessed in classrooms. Kite et al. (2021) pointed out that
many educational practices fail to fully integrate the epistemic processes of science and
hardly discuss computational thinking in terms of beyond data analysis. This gap underscores the need for deeper exploration into instructional strategies that can bridge this divide.
This study examines the relationships between teachers’ instructional activities and students’ performance in answering reasoning questions on TIMSS 2019. Speciﬁcally, we investigate the association between the frequency at which teachers ask students to provide
evidence-based explanations in class and teachers’ participation in Professional Development (PD). Additionally, we assess the moderating role of school resources in this relation.
Our study aims to lay the groundwork for future, more comprehensive research by
ﬁrst examining the current practices of teachers ask students to provide evidencebased explanations in science lessons. By identifying the frequency and context in
which teachers ask students to provide evidence-based explanations, we can gain valuable
insights into the existing educational practices. This knowledge is essential for developing
targeted interventions and professional development programmes that can more eﬀectively address and promote best practices in science teaching. Moreover, this initial
focus on teachers’ responses provides a necessary baseline that can inform subsequent
studies. The future studies can then examine deeper into the complex relationship
between instructional practices, reasoning skills, and the various factors inﬂuencing both.
We analyzed data from TIMSS 2019 to gain insights into the links between teachers
asking students to provide evidence-based explanations as an instructional activity, students’ performance in answering reasoning questions on TIMSS 2019, teachers’ participation in PD, and science resources. Speciﬁcally, three questions are of our particular
interest: (1) Is there a signiﬁcant correlation between teachers’ asking students to
provide evidence-based explanations and the performance of 8th graders in answering
reasoning questions on TIMSS 2019? (2) What types of teacher PD are signiﬁcantly
associated with the frequency of teachers asking students to provide evidence-based
explanations? (3) How do school resources aﬀect the relationship between PD and teachers asking students to provide evidence-based explanations?

Theoretical framework
Pedagogical content knowledge (PCK)
This study draws on the framework of the teacher pedagogical content knowledge
(PCK) model. Teachers apply their PCK to make decisions when planning, conducting,

and evaluating lessons. Shulman (1987) proposed the concept of pedagogical reasoning
as a PCK model to depict a teacher development process for attaining new comprehension. Pedagogical reasoning consists of ﬁve aspects: (1) the comprehension of a subject
matter, (2) the transformation of the subject matter into appropriate representations,
(3) instructions, (4) evaluation, and (5) reﬂection. These actions lead to teachers’
new comprehension of the learning goal, the subject matter, students’ characteristics,
teaching strategies, and self-reﬂection. In principle, a subject matter should encompass
both its content and educational purposes. In other words, it pertains not only to the
structure of the subject matter but also to the ideas within and outside the discipline.
As for the transformation of the subject matter, it includes the process of preparing,
using examples, analogies or demos to create powerful presentations, selecting instructional strategies, and adapting presentations for students of various characteristics.
Regarding instruction, it involves teacher performance in the classroom, such as
eﬀective classroom management and student-teacher interactions. The aspect of evaluation includes formative assessment, checking of student understanding during learning, and summative assessment of student performance at the end of teaching.
Together, the two types of evaluation lead to teachers’ reﬂection, where teachers
review student performances, reﬂect on their own experiences, and learn from them
to improve teaching.
The PCK model developed over time, and the Consensus Model (CM) of science
PCK proposed by Gess-Newsome (2015), conﬁrmed PCK as an individual category
of knowledge within the overall teachers’ knowledge system. Gess-Newsome deﬁned
PCK as both knowledge and skills that teachers know about what they teach, what
they do in planning and implementation, and the reasoning behind their actions
(Baxter & Lederman, 1999). Here, PCK is particularly important to a subject matter.
Teachers’ PCK is individual, unique, and associated with students’ outcomes. Most
recently, Park (2020) further proposed a Reﬁned Consensus Model to consider multiple
factors that can impact the development of PCK, factors other than teaching experiences, students, and professional development programmes. These include federal
policy, school culture, national standards, and curriculum. This model provides a
deeper understanding of designing learning opportunities and assessing science teachers’ PCK.
The present study is based on the Reﬁned Consensus Model, which highlights
several factors that aﬀect teachers’ PCK. This model places particular importance
on teaching experiences gained through implementing various instructional activities
in science classrooms, as well as student outcomes, such as their performances on
science tests, problem-solving skills, reasoning, critical thinking skills, and science
process skills. However, this study is focused solely on students’ performance in
answering reasoning questions on TIMSS 2019 as an outcome. The model also recognises professional development as a key factor in enhancing teachers’ PCK and takes
into consideration other factors outside the classroom environment, such as school
culture, national standards, and the curriculum. Accordingly, we examine how
school resources impact the implementation of speciﬁc instructional activities in
science classes and students’ performance in answering reasoning questions on
TIMSS 2019.

Reasoning skills
Lawson (2004) deﬁned reasoning skills as mental plans, strategies, or rules to process
information and derive conclusions beyond direct experiences. Further, Kuhn (1991)
deﬁnes reasoning skills as the capability to construct and critique arguments, which
is fundamental for scientiﬁc thinking. Reasoning is one of the most critical skills students should achieve. Research indicates that students who are proﬁcient in using evidence to support explanations and possess strong reasoning skills are better equipped
to understand scientiﬁc concepts and engage in informed decision-making (Duschl &
Osborne, 2002; NRC, 2012). Therefore, exploring these relationships provides valuable
insights into how educational practices can be improved to foster the development of
students’ reasoning skills. Reasoning in science involves the application of scientiﬁc
inquiries and requires several types of thinking, such as correlational, inductive, and
causal reasoning (Hejnova et al., 2018). Fostering reasoning skills means oﬀering students opportunities to explore an issue, formulate and test a hypothesis, control and
manipulate variables, evaluate evidence, and provide logical argumentation. To
improve students’ reasoning skills, teachers can use various strategies, such as
problem-solving strategy (Gillies & Khan, 2008), classroom discourse (Furtak et al.,
2010), and scientiﬁc investigation (Gillies et al., 2011). In addition, Kuhn (1991)
emphasised the development of argumentation skills as a critical component of cognitive development, advocating for educational practices that encourage debates and evidence-based discussions. Similarly, Zohar and Nemet (2002) found that integrating
content knowledge with argumentation tasks signiﬁcantly enhances students’ understanding and critical thinking skills, suggesting that using real-life dilemmas in the
classroom can foster deeper learning.

Instructional activity: asking students to provide evidence-based explanations
In science, premises are tested through investigation. From experimental results, these
premises may be accepted, rejected, or revised (Ding, 2018). A recent study suggests
(Banilower et al., 2010) that teachers can help students develop the ability to evaluate
and critique claims by asking them to use evidence to support their explanations. This
coordination of evidence and explanations has been extensively studied by scholars
such as Longino (1990) and Giere (1999), who emphasise its importance in scientiﬁc
inquiry. In the context of science education, researchers like Osborne et al. (2004) and
Sandoval and Reiser (2004) have explored how students can develop these essential
skills. Osborne et al. (2004) found that explicitly teaching argumentation signiﬁcantly
enhances students’ ability to construct and critique arguments, thereby improving
their understanding of the role of evidence in science. Similarly, Sandoval and Reiser
(2004) demonstrated that integrating conceptual and epistemic scaﬀolds in explanation-driven inquiry helps students develop a deeper understanding of scientiﬁc concepts and practices, fostering greater engagement and motivation in science learning.
This approach encourages students to view science as a process of knowledge construction. To support or refute a claim, students need to ﬁnd credible evidence and have
exposure to multiple experiences. By connecting new experiences with their pre-existing
ideas, students can revise their cognitive frameworks and reﬁne prior conceptions.

Classroom activities like debates, open-ended questions, discussions, and journal entries
allow students to use evidence to construct explanations.
Teachers’ decisions on asking students to provide evidence-based explanations in class
are complex because teachers must take into consideration the availability of resources,
suitable learning activities, assessment types, and student needs. In addition, eﬀective
delivery of pedagogical content in class requires teachers to have solid PCK and the
ability to accommodate their students’ unique backgrounds. A proven strategy to
enhance a teacher’s PCK is participation in PD.
Professional development (PD)
Well-designed PD programmes improve in-service science teachers’ knowledge and
teaching practices (Borko, 2004). Studies have found that PD can increase teachers’
knowledge and teaching competencies (Borko, 2004; Biggers et al., 2013; Luft &
Hewson, 2014). Further, Biggers et al. highlighted the importance of science teachers’
opportunities to develop their knowledge and proﬁciency. Their ﬁndings suggested
that continual, formal support is necessary for teachers to use science curricula eﬀectively
and create productive classroom activities for students’ sense-making. Additionally, Luft
and Hewson (2014) discussed that the goal of PD is to facilitate teacher learning processes
while considering local needs, such as students’ cultural background or teacher content
knowledge, and the national orientation toward the design of science curriculum in a
broad sense. Recent studies also revealed that school resources are associated with the
quality of instruction (Barro & Lee, 2010; Greenwald et al., 1996; Lay & Chandrasegaran,
2016).
The conceptual framework of professional development as introduced by Desimone in
2009 (Desimone, 2009). The framework highlights the key features of professional development, which include content focus, active learning, coherence, duration, and collective
participation. The content of professional development can vary depending on the
subject matter, pedagogy, curriculum, technology integration, critical thinking, assessment, and students’ needs (Avci et al., 2020; Christian et al., 2021; Dolﬁng et al., 2021;
Gerard et al., 2011; Gore & Rosser, 2022; Huang et al., 2022; Kelter et al., 2021; Lee &
Perret, 2022; Sancar et al., 2021; Smets et al., 2022; Unger & Tracey, 2013).
The duration of the professional development programmes has also been proven to be
an eﬀective factor in improving students’ outcomes (Ajani, 2022; Sancar et al., 2021). The
framework suggests that teachers’ participation in professional development will enhance
their knowledge and skills while changing their attitudes and beliefs, leading to improved
instructional practices and learning outcomes for students (Lee & Perret, 2022; Lee &
Zuze, 2011; Sancar et al., 2021).
Drawing on the above theoretical foundation, teachers can enhance students’ learning
experiences by aligning instructional activities with their cognitive developmental stages.
PD plays a key role in this process by providing teachers with a diverse range of pedagogical strategies for instruction. In class, teachers can use these strategies to cultivate students’ reasoning skills by, for example, asking them to provide evidence-based
explanations. The PCK framework serves as a guiding force in this process, seamlessly
integrating PD, instructional activities, and students’ progressive development of reasoning skills.

Methods
Data source and participants
We analyzed data from Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study
(TIMSS) 2019. Our sample is 8739 eighth-grade students, 456 science teachers, and
273 schools in the US that participated in TIMSS 2019. We examine the relationships
among students’ performance in answering reasoning questions on TIMSS 2019, the
frequency at which teachers ask students to provide evidence-based explanations,
and teachers’ participation in PD. Here, we analyzed the impact of seven diﬀerent
types of PD and their durations on the frequency of teachers asking students to
provide evidence-based explanations. These PD types included science content,
science pedagogy, science curriculum, technology integration, critical thinking,
science assessment, and students’ needs.
In our analysis, we include the variable lack of school resources, refers to the absence
or insuﬃciency of essential educational materials, access to technology, and adequate
funding for science programmes. This includes both general resources and sciencespeciﬁc resources as collected by TIMSS 2019 through the school questionnaire completed by principals (Mullis et al., 2020). General resources encompass instructional
materials, supplies, school building and grounds, heating/cooling and lighting systems,
instructional space, technology-competent staﬀ, audio-visual resources, and computer
technology for teaching and learning. Science resources include teachers specialising
in science, computers and software for science instruction, library materials relevant to
science, calculators, and science equipment and materials.
This variable is crucial for examining how resource constraints impact teachers’ ability
to engage students in evidence-based learning activities. The lack of these resources can
critically impede a teacher’s ability to ask students to use evidence for explanations. For
instance, inadequate instructional materials and science equipment can limit hands-on
activities and experiments, which are essential for teaching scientiﬁc inquiry and evidence-based reasoning (Banilower et al., 2010). Furthermore, insuﬃcient technology
can hinder access to digital tools and resources that facilitate interactive learning and
data analysis, crucial for modern science education (Schneider, 2003).
Table 1 provides a demographic analysis of the sample. Students’ performance in
answering reasoning questions on TIMSS 2019 is measured by science items in the
TIMSS 2019 booklet. Teachers’ responses to a questionnaire in TIMSS 2019 provided
data on how often they ask students to provide evidence-based explanations. Additionally, the TIMSS school questionnaires oﬀered information on school-level resources.

Data analysis
We used IBM SPSS Statistics 9 version 27 to answer the ﬁrst research question and HLM
version 8.2 to answer the other two research questions. HLM is a linear model that considers the nested structure of data neglected in regular ordinary least squares (OLS)
regression analysis (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002; Woltman et al., 2012). We used REML
estimations for the analysis to answer RQ1, with Level 1 containing student variables
and Level 2 containing teacher variables. As for RQ2 and RQ3, we applied multilevel
logistic regression Penalized Quasi Likelihood (PQL) to analyze the categorical data of
teachers’ responses. The teacher variables are at Level 1, and the school variables are
at Level 2. All student-related variables are group-mean-centered, while teacher-level
and school-level variables are grand mean-centered. To ensure equal representation
and avoid sampling issues, we applied a weighted variable, following the guidelines of
The TIMSS 2019 (Mullis et al., 2020).
To address the research questions, we employed hierarchical linear modelling (HLM),
a statistical technique well-suited for analyzing data with nested structures. In answering
these research questions, we applied (1) a fully unconditional model to assess the variability of students’ performance in answering reasoning questions on TIMSS 2019, (2) a
multilevel mixed model to test the signiﬁcance of the main predictor, which is the frequency of teachers asking students to provide evidence-based explanations, and (3) multilevel mixed models to test cross-level moderation between level 1 and level 2. Our Level1 model examines individual student characteristics, such as speaking English at home
and parental education, while the Level-2 model considers classroom-level practices,
speciﬁcally teachers asking students to provide evidence-based explanations. The

second research question explores the relationship between teachers asking students to
provide evidence-based explanations and their participation in professional development
(PD). Here, our model analyzes whether PD participation at the individual teacher level

By incorporating school resources into our Level-2 model, we can determine if these
resources enhance or diminish the eﬀect of PD on instructional practices.
These methods were chosen because HLM allows us to account for the hierarchical
nature of educational data, where students are nested within classrooms and schools,
providing a more accurate and detailed understanding of the relationships among variables. In these models, students’ performance in answering reasoning questions on
TIMSS 2019 (RQ1) and teachers’ instructional activities that involve asking students to
provide evidence-based explanations (RQ2 & RQ3) are the dependent variables and
hence are at level 1. The signiﬁcance level was deﬁned as p < .05, which is a standard
threshold in educational research. Relationships between variables were considered signiﬁcant if the p-value was below this threshold. This approach ensures that our ﬁndings
are robust and reﬂective of the diﬀerent levels of inﬂuence in education.

Control variables
Our analysis takes into consideration control variables at the student level, parents’
highest level of education and English as a primary language at home. Literature has
shown that parents’ education level (Eckert, 2008; Xu, 2011; Thomson et al., 2017;
Zhang et al., 2020) and the match of students’ primary language with the language
used on tests can signiﬁcantly impact student science achievement (Lay & Chandrasegaran, 2016; Thomson et al.).

Research question 1
Control variables
Results show that control variables, such as speaking English at home and parents’ education levels, were signiﬁcantly associated with students’ performances on the TIMSS
2019 reasoning items. The intercept coeﬃcient from the RQ1 model is g00 = 492.30
(SE = 4.40, t = 112.00, p < .001), indicating an average score of 492.30 out of 1000 on
the reasoning items. As deﬁned by NCES, the score scale can be divided into four
ranges, each representing advanced (>625), high (550), intermediate (475), and low
(<400) levels respectively. A signiﬁcant correlation was found between speaking
English at home and student performance on the reasoning items (g01 = 10.25, SE =
1.16, t = 8.84, p < .001). Speciﬁcally, students who always speak English scored the
highest on the reasoning questions with an average of 523.04, followed by those who
almost always speak English (512.80) and those who sometimes speak English
(502.55). Also, a signiﬁcant correlation was detected between parents’ highest level of

Discussion
Teachers asking students to provide evidence-based explanations (RQ1)
When students use evidence to create their explanations, they apply their reasoning skills
to provide support or refute their claims. According to studies by Banilower et al. (2010),
McNeill and Krajcik (2008), Yao and Guo (2018), this is an eﬀective way to enhance students’ cognitive abilities. Additionally, research by Kolodner et al. (2003), Lombrozo and
Vasilyeva (2007), and Wellman and Liu (2007) suggests that constructing evidence-based
explanations can activate students’ causal reasoning abilities. In line with these ﬁndings,
our study revealed that teachers asking students to provide evidence-based explanations
is a crucial predictor of their performance on reasoning items on TIMSS 2019. By engaging in this type of activity in (almost) every science lesson, students are more likely to
perform better than their peers. (g01 = 12.84, SE = 3.05, t = 4.21, p < .001).
The results suggest that teachers’ asking students to provide evidence-based explanations is positively and signiﬁcantly associated with students’ performances on the
TIMSS 2019 reasoning questions. Various ways can be used to carry out this instructional
strategy, for example, through classroom discussions, supporting and critiquing claims,
debates, open-ended questions, and students’ journal entries (Banilower et al., 2010). It is
important to note that teachers may have diﬀerent criteria to assess student performance
in this aspect.

This aligns with Longino’s (1990) emphasis on the epistemic importance of evidence in
scientiﬁc practice and Osborne et al.’s (2004) ﬁndings on the role of argumentation in
science education. Additionally, the observed relationship between evidence-based explanations and reasoning skills supports Kuhn’s (1991) theory on the development of cognitive skills through argumentation. Furthermore, Sandoval and Reiser’s (2004) work on
explanation-driven inquiry underscores the importance of scaﬀolding techniques in
helping students construct scientiﬁc explanations, which parallels our approach to
encouraging evidence-based explanations in the classroom. Zohar and Nemet’s (2002)
research further supports the idea that integrating content knowledge with argumentation
tasks can signiﬁcantly enhance students’ critical thinking and understanding, suggesting
that real-life dilemmas and evidence-based discussions are eﬀective classroom practices.
Further, a recent study by Cabello et al. (2021) examined how fourth-grade students in
Chile used pictures to explain the causes and eﬀects of an earthquake after learning the
Tectonic Plates Theory. Their conclusion also aligned with our ﬁndings on the instructional advantage of engaging students in evidence-based explanations. Similarly, in her
study, Keys (1994) found that joint report-writing with structured prompts could
improve students’ reasoning by explicitly encouraging them to connect evidence and
claims. However, Klahr et al. (1993) reported noticeable diﬃculties among seventhgraders in using evidence from various sources to create coherent explanations. To
address this issue, Wu and Krajcik (2006) proposed to train students to provide evidence-based explanations, including oﬀering prompts that reminded them to identify,
clarify, and support their claims with data sources. In the same vein but from a larger
context, our study conﬁrmed the beneﬁts of asking students to provide evidence-based
explanations of their reasoning skills.
Professional development (RQ2)
Teachers develop their instructional knowledge, skills, and practices by attending PD
(Biggers et al., 2013; Borko, 2004; Desimone, 2009; Pella, 2015; van Driel et al., 2001).
Additionally, PD can transform teachers’ attitudes and beliefs (Desimone, 2009;
Guskey, 2002). Various content, contexts, and processes have been designed to create
diﬀerent PDs (Luft & Hewson, 2014), but collaboration among participants or
between participants and PD facilitators is invariantly a crucial component. According
to Gutierez (2016), a lesson study – a Japanese model of teacher-led classroom research
experiences – can facilitate between-teacher collaborations. Pairing teachers with education researchers can promote teachers’ instructional eﬀectiveness.
The outcomes of this study reveal that the quality and length of teacher PD programmes are closely linked to teachers’ instructional activities. Teachers who participated
in PD programmes with a focus on science content, science pedagogy, and critical thinking would more likely ask students to provide evidence-based explanations. Conversely,
those who had not attended such programmes in the last two years were less likely to do
so. Further, teachers who participated in longer hours of PD in the last two years would
more likely ask students to provide evidence-based explanations than those who had
shorter PD experiences.
Additionally, PD has been found to be positively correlated with the frequency at
which teachers ask students to provide evidence-based explanations. This, perhaps, is

because PD can enhance teachers’ knowledge about eﬀective teaching methods, technology, and science content. Various PDs are available for teachers, aﬀording them ample
opportunities to collaborate with peers on lesson study, to receive individualised guidance from experts for improving teaching skills, and to participate in research. As
suggested by recent research (Thompson et al., 2019; Watkins et al., 2020), teachers
are in need of collaboration and reﬂection to enhance their instruction.
School resources shortage (RQ3)
Having suﬃcient resources in school is crucial for allowing teachers to choose from a
variety of instructional methods. This can signiﬁcantly aﬀect the quality of education
provided to students (Barro & Lee, 2010; Greenwald et al., 1996; Lee & Zuze, 2011).
For instance, teachers can conduct science experiments with their students if they
have the necessary equipment and materials. Other school resources, such as good air
quality and proper lighting, can all contribute to a conducive learning environment
(The National Renewable Energy Laboratory, 2002).
A study by Elliott (1998) indicated that students perform better on science tests
when their teachers have adequate resources to implement inquiry-based learning.
In general, students in schools with access to science resources had higher achievement on science tests than those without access to such resources. (Lay & Chandrasegaran, 2016). In our study, we conﬁrmed that a shortage in science resources is
signiﬁcantly linked to teachers’ tendency of asking students to provide evidencebased explanations. Teachers who worked in schools with limited resources tended
not to ask students to provide evidence-based explanations, regardless of their PD
experiences. Interestingly, the content of PD can be signiﬁcantly associated with
student performance in reasoning questions in TIMSS 2019. This is shown to be
true for PD on science content, science pedagogy, critical thinking, and the duration
of the PD. Speciﬁcally, teachers who received PD on these topics would more likely
ask their students to provide evidence-based explanations. Also, longer PD programmes were associated with an increased likelihood of a teacher asking students
to provide evidence-based explanations. At the school level, more resources for
science instruction were linked to teachers’ more frequent attempts in asking their
students to provide evidence-based explanations.

Limitations
The study signiﬁcantly enhances our understanding of instructional activities in
science classrooms, with the aim of improving student performance in reasoning
items within TIMSS 2019. By leveraging the reliability of TIMSS as an international
assessment tool, it oﬀers valuable insights into global science achievement. Examining
factors inﬂuencing instructional practices, including teachers’ backgrounds, professional development, and school resources, the study sheds light on their impact
on student performance on reasoning questions in TIMSS 2019. Hierarchical Linear
Modeling strengthens the analysis by considering within-group similarities and
between-group diﬀerences among teachers and schools. However, limitations arise
from relying solely on TIMSS 2019 questionnaire data, which restricts exploration

into professional development content and instructional strategies. Additionally, the
small cluster size of teachers and schools limits variable inclusion and testing of
random slopes, while the study’s generalizability is conﬁned to TIMSS 2019 participants. Further, the focus on general instructional activities rather than speciﬁc
science content and the absence of longitudinal analysis and exploration of causality
underscore areas for future research and reﬁnement.

Future direction
Future research directions can enhance our understanding of the relationship among students’ performance in reasoning tests, instructional activities, and school resources.
Longitudinal studies can provide insights into dynamic interactions among students, teachers, and schools over time, addressing causality and validity concerns. Extensive classroom observations and qualitative methods like interviews can oﬀer detailed insights into
instructional strategies. Reﬁnement of data collection tools, such as revising TIMSS 2019
questionnaires, can capture contextual information, particularly regarding professional
development. Additionally, future research should diversify samples beyond TIMSS
2019 participants to include various educational settings, like private or religiously
aﬃliated schools, to uncover unique instructional practices. Such eﬀorts will contribute
to a deeper understanding of eﬀective instructional activities and their impact on student
performance across diverse contexts.

Conclusion
This study explored the relationships between teachers asking students to provide evidence-based explanations, students’ performance on reasoning questions in TIMSS
2019, teacher PD, and school resources. Drawing on the reﬁned consensus model of
teacher PCK, we holistically examined student learning outcomes, teacher professional
development, and school cultures through the lens of teachers asking students to
provide evidence-based explanations. As Park (2020) pointed out, any classroom practice
is inﬂuenced by teachers’ PCK. In this study, we analyzed data from TIMSS 2019 using a
multilevel model to capture the interactions among these key constructs.
Our results clearly demonstrate several key relationships. Firstly, the frequency at
which teachers ask students to provide evidence-based explanations in class is signiﬁcantly associated with student performance on the reasoning items on TIMSS 2019
(RQ1). Also clear is the positive impact of speaking English at home and parents’ education levels on student reasoning skills measured by TIMSS 2019.
In addition, teachers who attended PD on science content, science pedagogy, and critical thinking would frequently ask students to provide evidence-based explanations, more
so than their colleagues who did not attend such PD (RQ2). Note that the duration of PD
appears to be positively linked to teachers’ frequency of doing so.
Moreover, our study highlighted a positive relationship between school resources and
teachers asking students to provide evidence-based explanations in science class. Teachers at schools with limited science resources would less likely ask their students to
provide evidence-based explanations, which underscored the role of adequate resources
in teachers’ pedagogical decisions during classroom teaching.
