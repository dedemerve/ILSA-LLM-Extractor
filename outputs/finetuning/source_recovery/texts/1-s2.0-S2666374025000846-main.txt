This study investigates the relationship between school leadership support and educational outcomes, focusing
on student academic achievement and teacher job satisfaction, utilizing a meta-analytic approach with data from
the TIMSS 2015 survey. The dataset comprises responses from eighth-grade mathematics and science teachers
across 45 countries. Meta-analytic integration reveals that leadership support has a small-to-moderate positive
effect on teacher job satisfaction, with pooled effect sizes of 0.20 (95 % CI: [0.18, 0.22]) for mathematics
teachers and 0.22 (95 % CI: [0.19, 0.24]) for science teachers. However, leadership support demonstrates a small
negative association with student achievement in both mathematics (-0.05, 95 % CI: [-0.07, -0.03]) and science
(-0.04, 95 % CI: [-0.05, -0.02]). These findings highlight the complex interplay between leadership practices,
teacher well-being, and student performance, underscoring the need for nuanced strategies that address these
dynamics to enhance both teacher satisfaction and student outcomes.

Introduction
The relationship between school leadership and educational outcomes has been a central theme in educational research over recent
decades (Liebowitz & Porter, 2019; Robinson & Gray, 2019; Ertem,
2021). High-quality leadership within schools is increasingly viewed as
a key determinant of both teacher job satisfaction and student academic
success (Day et al., 2020; Liebowitz & Porter, 2019; Lunenburg and
Ornstein, 2021). Studies have highlighted that strong school leadership
is fundamental in cultivating an environment that promotes teaching
excellence and enhances student learning (Grissom et al., 2021; Tan
et al., 2024). Additionally, leadership support has been closely associated with boosting teacher morale and job satisfaction—crucial elements for creating an engaging and effective learning environment
(Liebowitz & Porter, 2019).
Despite the recognized significance of leadership in educational
contexts, there is limited research exploring its influence across diverse
cultural and educational settings, particularly through comparative
analyses between countries. The Trends in International Mathematics
and Science Study (TIMSS) 2015 offers a valuable dataset for such an
investigation, providing insights into how variations in school leadership support impact educational outcomes on a global scale. This study

addresses this research gap by leveraging the TIMSS 2015 data to
examine the relationship between school leadership support and its
connection to teacher job satisfaction and student achievement in
mathematics and science across 45 countries. Through this analysis, the
research aims to enrich the discourse on educational leadership by offering evidence-based insights that can guide policy development and
practices across varied educational systems.
Literature review
School leadership and teacher job satisfaction
In the context of schools, leadership is generally understood as the
practices of principals and administrators that shape teaching and
learning environments. Key characteristics include setting instructional
direction, supporting and motivating teachers, fostering professional
collaboration, and building trust within the school community. The
literature typically distinguishes between different models such as
instructional leadership (focused on teaching and learning), transformational leadership (emphasizing vision, motivation, and change),
and distributed leadership (sharing responsibilities across staff)
(Leithwood et al., 2019; Liebowitz & Porter, 2019). These frameworks

provide the foundation for understanding how leadership practices can

Effective leadership in educational settings is characterized by
collaborative and supportive practices, including open communication,
the provision of adequate resources, and opportunities for professional
growth and career advancement (Boyd et al., 2011). Teachers are more
likely to experience job satisfaction when they perceive their principals
as competent, approachable, and supportive, fostering a positive and
encouraging work environment (Eryilmaz et al., 2025; Skaalvik &
Skaalvik, 2011). Supportive leaders not only recognize and celebrate
teachers’ accomplishments but also cultivate a culture of trust, teamwork, and shared commitment among the teaching staff. In contrast, a
lack of clear guidance, adequate resources, or a supportive environment
from school leaders can result in teacher dissatisfaction, disengagement,
and a decline in morale and job fulfillment (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2011).
Extensive academic research highlights the significant impact of
school leadership on teacher job satisfaction, establishing it as a key
factor within the educational work environment (Boyd et al., 2011;
Cogaltay et al., 2016; Johnson et al., 2012; Kraft et al., 2016; Ladd,
2011; Sims, 2020; Toropova et al., 2021). Boyd et al. (2011) examined
how school contextual factors influence teacher retention in New York
City by isolating the effects of teacher characteristics from
school-specific features. Their findings reveal that teachers’ perceptions
of school administration play a critical role in retention decisions, as
evaluations of school context by one group of teachers significantly influence their colleagues’ turnover decisions within the same school.
Similarly, Johnson et al. (2012) explored the relationship between
school working conditions and teacher satisfaction, career intentions,
and student achievement growth in Massachusetts using data from the
MassTeLLS survey. The study identified nine key components of the
work environment and concluded that social factors, including school
culture, principal leadership, and collegial relationships, are primary
predictors of teacher satisfaction and career plans.
Kraft et al. (2016) investigated the influence of organizational
context on teacher turnover and student outcomes in New York City
middle schools. By developing measures for school climate dimensions,
they found that improvements in leadership, academic expectations,
teacher collaboration, and safety were associated with reduced teacher
turnover. Ladd (2011), using administrative data from North Carolina
and a statewide teacher survey, demonstrated that working conditions,
particularly leadership quality, are strong predictors of teacher retention, independent of other school demographics such as student racial
composition.
Sims (2020) analyzed the relationship between working conditions
and teacher satisfaction in England’s secondary schools using TALIS
2013 data. The study highlighted the critical role of school leadership,
subject-specific training, and career advancement opportunities in
shaping teacher satisfaction and reducing turnover intentions. Toropova
et al. (2021), drawing on the TIMSS 2015 dataset, investigated the link
between leadership support and teacher job satisfaction in Swedish
mathematics education. They identified leadership support as the most
influential factor driving job satisfaction among mathematics teachers,
underscoring its importance in fostering a supportive teaching environment. Drawing on the findings of Liebowitz and Porter (2019),
principal behaviors have a notable impact on teacher well-being, with
effect sizes ranging from 0.34 to 0.38 standard deviations. These results
highlight the critical role of school leadership in fostering a supportive
environment that enhances teacher morale and professional satisfaction.
Collectively, these studies emphasize the vital role of school leadership in shaping teacher job satisfaction, particularly within the United
States and England. However, there remains a gap in understanding how
these dynamics operate in diverse cultural and educational contexts. To
address this gap, our study utilizes the TIMSS 2015 dataset to provide
cross-cultural insights into the relationship between school leadership
and teacher job satisfaction, aiming to expand the knowledge base
across international settings.

In this research, school leadership is conceptualized as the collaborative interaction between school administrators and educators,
particularly in areas such as curriculum development and decisionmaking. This multifaceted relationship underscores the essential role
of effective leadership in boosting teacher job satisfaction and fostering
a productive learning environment.
School leadership and student achievement
Early research suggested that the direct involvement of educational
administrators in leadership activities had a clear and straightforward
effect on student learning outcomes (Brookover et al., 1979; Karadag
et al., 2020; Leithwood et al., 2020; Marzano et al., 2005; Rutter et al.,
1979; Witzier et al., 2003). Over time, it has become evident that these
effects are more indirect and shaped by various internal factors within
schools (Ozdemir et al., 2022; Scheerens, 2012). Contemporary studies
highlight that the influence of school leaders on student achievement is
primarily mediated through their impact on teachers’ beliefs, practices,
and emotional well-being, as well as the broader organizational structure and school climate (Leithwood et al., 2019). Furthermore, the
effectiveness of school leadership is often context-dependent, influenced
by factors such as the socioeconomic backgrounds of students, underscoring the nuanced nature of leadership effectiveness (Eryilmaz and
Sandoval Hernandez, 2024; Eryilmaz and Sandoval-Hernandez, 2023;
Tan, 2018).
Despite the progress in understanding leadership’s role, there remains a lack of studies employing moderation analyses—that is, examining whether the effect of leadership on student outcomes differs
depending on contextual factors such as socioeconomic conditions,
policy environments, or cultural norms. Even fewer studies have used
combined moderation–mediation approaches, which test both how
leadership exerts its influence (mediation) and under what conditions
these influences are stronger or weaker (moderation). Such approaches
are crucial for understanding how instructional leadership impacts
student performance under varying conditions (Gümüş et al., 2022;
Sebastian & Allensworth, 2019). A systematic review by Özdemir et al.
(2022), synthesizing findings from 144 journal articles across five databases, revealed that instructional leadership remains the dominant
framework in recent studies. Importantly, the review highlighted an
increasing focus on direct effect models—that is, studies that estimate
the impact of leadership practices on student achievement without
explicitly modeling the mediating role of teacher practices, school
climate, or other contextual variables. This trend was especially visible
in non-English-speaking Western contexts. While such models provide
straightforward evidence of association, they risk oversimplifying the
mechanisms through which leadership influences learning. These findings point to the need for further exploration of mediating and moderating mechanisms underpinning the relationship between school
leadership and student learning, offering a valuable framework for
advancing future research and informing educational practices.
The relationship between leadership practices and their impact on
student outcomes has been the focus of extensive research, underscoring
the complexity of this domain Karadag et al. (2020); Leithwood et al.
(2019); Pietsch et al. (2023); Seashore Louis et al. (2010); Witziers et al.
(2003). Various analytical strategies have been developed to explore this
intricate relationship. For instance, Karadag (2020) synthesized findings
from 151 research articles and dissertations published between 2008
and 2018, encompassing data from 131,498 participants. This study
utilized a random effects model and found that educational leadership
has a medium-level impact on student achievement. Interestingly, the
effect was more pronounced in vertical-collectivist cultures, such as
those in Asia, compared to horizontal-individualistic cultures like the
United States.
Building on this, Pietsch et al. (2023) conducted a comprehensive
3-level individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis to examine the
correlation between instructional leadership and student achievement.

Their analysis, involving data from 1.5 million students across 75
countries from OECD’s PISA datasets (2009, 2012, and 2015), revealed
that the average correlation between instructional leadership and student achievement was nearly zero. However, the study identified significant variations across cultural contexts, with human development
levels playing a key role in moderating these effects. These findings
underscore the complexity of the global interplay between instructional
leadership and educational outcomes.
Leithwood et al. (2019) approached the topic by modeling variables
directly influencing learning that are amenable to leadership practices.
Drawing on survey responses from 1779 teachers in 81 Texas schools,
the study highlighted the intricate interactions within these variables,
emphasizing the necessity of targeted school improvement efforts and
further investigation. Similarly, Seashore Louis et al. (2010) explored
the influence of specific leadership behaviors—such as shared leadership, fostering trust among professionals, and supporting instructional
improvements—on teacher collaboration and classroom practices in the
United States. The study, using national teacher survey data, confirmed
a positive relationship between these leadership behaviors and student
achievement, particularly through shared leadership and a focus on
instruction.
The impact of direct and indirect leadership practices was further
examined by Witziers et al. (2003) through a quantitative meta-analysis.
Their findings confirmed that direct leadership has a small but positive
effect on student outcomes, echoing earlier research and paving the way
for future studies. However, more recent investigations, such as those by
Smith and Gümüs (2022), have challenged these assumptions. Using
longitudinal data from Danish public schools, their analysis revealed no
significant relationship between teachers’ perceptions of leadership
competency and dialogue about student outcomes and sixth-grade student achievement in reading or mathematics.
Similarly, Gumus et al. (2022) utilized cross-national PISA 2015 data
to examine the effects of instructional leadership on student achievement in reading, science, and mathematics. Their findings revealed no
significant relationship between instructional leadership and achievement in these subjects, further complicating the narrative around leadership effectiveness. Collectively, these studies align with Hallinger’s
(2011a, 2011b) assertion that the connection between leadership and
student outcomes is complex and not easily defined. Wu and Shen
(2022) conducted a multivariate random-effects meta-meta-analysis to
synthesize the relationship between principal leadership and student
achievement, revealing a statistically significant positive effect (Cohen’s
d = 0.34). Their findings highlight the lack of conclusive evidence favoring any specific leadership model while emphasizing the moderating
influence of educational contexts on leadership effectiveness. Shen &
Wu (2025) conducted a multivariate meta-analysis of 42 empirical
studies examining the effects of principal leadership on student
achievement in the United States. They found that principal leadership
positively impacts student achievement, with effect sizes of 0.25 SD for
direct effects and 0.22 SD for indirect effects, while identifying substantial heterogeneity influenced by student outcome measures as a
significant moderator.
In this study, we aim to contribute to this body of research by
investigating how school leadership support relates to students’ mathematics and science achievement on a global scale. By analyzing data
across diverse educational contexts, this research seeks to illuminate the
multifaceted dynamics underlying this critical aspect of education, offering insights that could inform both policy and practice.

Methodology
Data and sample
This study utilizes data from TIMSS 2015, the only large-scale
assessment providing nationally representative samples that examine
student and teacher performance in mathematics and science at the 4th
and 8th-grade levels. TIMSS employs a two-stage stratified cluster
sampling approach. In the first stage, schools are randomly selected with
probability proportional to size (PPS) from a comprehensive list of
eligible schools. The second stage involves selecting one or more intact
classes from the target grade within each participating school (Martin
et al., 2020). By sampling entire classrooms rather than individuals,
TIMSS enables the exploration of factors contributing to variability between classrooms, focusing on shared curricular and instructional
experiences.
TIMSS gathers extensive contextual data from a variety of stakeholders, including teachers, school leaders, students, and parents. This
includes information on early literacy and numeracy activities, home
learning resources, school discipline and safety, academic focus, curriculum implementation, technology integration, and teacher education
and professional development. Such comprehensive data allow TIMSS to
analyze the interplay of contextual factors that shape student learning.
This investigation focuses on 8th-grade mathematics and science
achievement as assessed in TIMSS 2015. The sampling process involves
selecting schools to represent diverse educational contexts, followed by
identifying specific classrooms where students undergo assessments in
mathematics and science. Teachers of these students complete detailed
questionnaires on their professional qualifications and instructional
practices. A unique school-level identifier links student outcomes to
teacher attributes and classroom strategies, enabling an in-depth analysis of these relationships. This systematic approach provides insights
into the factors driving global student achievement and contributes to a
better understanding of educational systems worldwide. For detailed
methodologies, assessment frameworks, and procedures, refer to the
official TIMSS website https://timssandpirls.bc.edu/timss2015/

Teacher job satisfaction (BTBGTJS): The Teacher Job Satisfaction
scale in the TIMSS assesses teachers’ feelings towards their profession
through responses to five key statements. Teachers rate how often they
feel content, find meaning and purpose, are enthusiastic, inspired, and
proud of their work, from "Never" to "Very often." This concise approach
captures the essence of job satisfaction, reflecting the emotional and
psychological rewards of teaching and providing insights into teachers’
professional well-being and motivation (Hooper et al., 2013).
School Leadership support (LEADERSHIP): The "School Leadership
Support" scale, crafted from teacher responses on collaboration,
instructional support, and professional development encouragement, is
a bespoke measure designed to gauge the quality and extent of leadership support within schools. By aggregating teachers’ perceptions across
three dimensions—collaborative planning efforts, the level of instructional guidance, and the promotion of teachers’ professional growth
—on a spectrum from "Very low" to "Very high," this scale provides a
nuanced understanding of school leadership’s concept. This approach
allows for a targeted analysis of how leadership practices contribute to
fostering a supportive and productive educational environment, offering
insights not originally detailed in the dataset but vital for evaluating and
enhancing the school’s teaching and learning climate.
School Conditions and Resources (BTBGSCR): The "School Conditions and Resources" scale within the TIMSS framework was constructed
as a categorical latent variable, based on teachers’ agreement with
statements assessing key aspects of their working environment. This
included evaluations of the sufficiency of workspace, teaching materials,
and technological resources among other concerns. By analyzing
teachers’ responses to these seven statements, the scale aimed to capture
the broader dimension of job satisfaction as it relates to the conditions
and resources available in schools, providing insights into how such
factors influence teachers’ professional well-being and effectiveness
(Hooper et al., 2013).
Safe and Orderly School (BTBGSOS): The "Safe and Orderly School"
scale is conceptualized as a continuous latent variable, constructed from
teachers’ responses based on their level of agreement with eight specific
statements. These statements are designed to evaluate perceptions of the
school’s safety policies, the adequacy of practices in place, and the

clarity of rules governing student behavior, among other aspects related
to maintaining a safe and orderly school environment. This approach
allows for a nuanced assessment of how teachers view the effectiveness
of their school’s efforts to create and uphold a secure and well-regulated
learning atmosphere, which is critical for both teaching and learning
success (Hooper et al., 2013).
Teaching experience (BTBG01): It is a continuous variable that expresses the years of teaching experience of teachers in their profession.

Analytical strategy
Adopting the methodology of integrative data analysis as outlined by
Brunner et al. (2022) and Munk et al. (2024), this study was conducted
in two primary phases. Initially, analyses were carried out separately for
each participating country to generate country-specific results. We also
examined model assumptions, including linearity, homoscedasticity,
and normality of residuals. Visual inspection of residual plots did not
reveal serious violations, suggesting that linear regression models were
appropriate for the purposes of this study. Subsequently, these individual findings were synthesized through meta-analytic integration to
construct an overarching perspective. The statistical analyses were
executed using R (Version 4.3.0; R Core Team, 2024).

emphasizing the critical role of leadership in creating a supportive and
productive work environment for teachers. Principals who are accessible
and actively involved in instructional planning foster an atmosphere
that enhances teacher morale and professional fulfillment. These results

These findings reinforce the assertions of Johnson et al. (2012) and

transformative role in fostering a positive school environment and
improving teacher job satisfaction globally.

Our analysis reveals an unexpected and non-significant negative
correlation between school leadership support and students’ achievement in science and mathematics. This finding contrasts with earlier
studies that emphasized the positive role of leadership in fostering academic achievement (Louis et al., 2010; Sun & Leithwood, 2017).
However, our results align with more recent research by Gumus et al.
(2022) and Smith and Gumus (2022), who also identified
non-significant or negative relationships between leadership and student achievement. These findings suggest that the dynamics of leadership support and its influence on academic performance are more
intricate than previously assumed, highlighting the need for a deeper
investigation into specific dimensions of leadership support and their
differential effects on student outcomes.

practices, job satisfaction, and school climate, rather than through immediate direct effects (Leithwood et al., 2019). Finally, the variability

producing positive effects in some settings and null or negative ones in
others (Karadag, 2020; Pietsch et al., 2023). Taken together, these
considerations suggest that our results should not be viewed as contradicting prior studies but as highlighting the complexity and
context-dependency of leadership effectiveness.
One potential explanation for the absence of significant positive effects may lie in the nature of the TIMSS dataset, which employs a crosssectional design capturing data at a single point in time. As prior
research indicates, the impact of leadership on academic performance
may unfold gradually, accumulating over an extended period (Heck &
Hallinger, 2010). Consequently, the cross-sectional approach might
obscure the incremental benefits of leadership practices that are more
apparent in longitudinal studies. This limitation underscores the
importance of utilizing longitudinal data to capture the nuanced and
time-dependent effects of leadership on student achievement.
Moreover, the specific aspects of leadership support assessed in the
TIMSS dataset may not comprehensively represent the full spectrum of
effective leadership practices. For instance, instructional leadership,
transformational leadership, and distributed leadership might exert
varying degrees of influence on academic outcomes, depending on
contextual factors such as cultural norms, socioeconomic conditions,
and school-level resources (Wu & Shen, 2022). The substantial heterogeneity observed in leadership effects across different countries further
supports this notion, suggesting that leadership practices must be
tailored to the unique needs of each educational context (Karadag et al.,
2020; Pietsch et al., 2023).
Extending upon existing research, our study offers a global
perspective by analyzing the science and mathematics achievement of
eighth-grade students across 45 countries. This international scope
provides valuable insights into the interplay between leadership support
and academic performance across diverse cultural and educational
contexts. While earlier meta-analyses (Shen & Wu, 2020; Wu & Shen,
2022) highlighted statistically significant positive effects of leadership
on student outcomes, they also noted substantial variability moderated
by contextual factors. Our findings contribute to this discourse by
illustrating the nuanced and context-dependent nature of leadership
support’s impact, underscoring the importance of exploring moderating
factors such as socioeconomic conditions, teacher-student relationships,
and school climate.

One limitation of this study is that the measurement of school
leadership support was based on only three items, as the TIMSS data
provides limited information about school leadership. This restricted
measurement may not fully capture the multifaceted nature of leadership support, which is often conceptualized through dimensions such as
instructional leadership, transformational leadership, and distributed
leadership (Liebowitz & Porter, 2019; Leithwood et al., 2019). By
relying on a narrow set of items, the study may overlook critical aspects
of leadership practices that could differentially influence teacher job
satisfaction and student achievement. As a result, the assessment of
school leadership support in this study provides only a partial representation of the broader construct, highlighting the need for future
research to employ more extensive and multidimensional measures to
capture the complexity of leadership and its nuanced impact on
educational outcomes.
A further limitation of this study is that, although we integrate
findings across 45 countries, our analyses do not explicitly incorporate
country-level contextual factors such as socioeconomic conditions,
educational policies, or cultural norms. Prior research has shown that
leadership effectiveness is shaped by these broader contextual influences (Eryilmaz & Sandoval-Hernandez, 2023; Karadag, 2020;
Pietsch et al., 2023). While our meta-analytic approach accounts for
statistical heterogeneity across countries, it does not explain why effect
sizes differ across contexts. Future research should therefore combine
teacher- and student-level data with macro-level indicators (e.g., GDP,
education policy frameworks, cultural dimensions) to capture the
context-dependent nature of leadership support and its impact on
educational outcomes.
Although our models included several teacher- and school-level
covariates (e.g., gender, experience, professional development, school
resources, and school safety), they did not capture other potentially
important mediators such as teacher workload, student demographics,
or instructional quality. These unmeasured factors may influence both
teacher job satisfaction and student achievement, thereby confounding
the associations observed in this study.
Moreover, while this study has provided valuable insights into the
relationship between student academic achievement, teacher job satisfaction, and school leadership support, it did not investigate the role of
teacher job satisfaction in the relationship between school leadership
support and students’ academic achievement through a mediation or
moderation approach. Understanding whether teacher job satisfaction
mediates or moderates this relationship would contribute to a more
comprehensive account of the mechanisms through which leadership
affects student outcomes. Therefore, future studies should consider
adopting mediation or moderation analysis frameworks to better capture these dynamics.
Finally, while our random-effects meta-analytic approach accounts
for between-country heterogeneity, the results revealed moderate to
substantial variation (with I² values up to 75 %). This indicates that the
associations between leadership support, teacher job satisfaction, and
student achievement are not consistent across all contexts. Our study
does not explore the reasons behind this heterogeneity, which limits the
generalizability of the pooled estimates. Future research should therefore investigate potential moderators at the country level—such as socioeconomic conditions, education policy frameworks, or cultural
norms—to explain cross-national variation and provide a more contextsensitive understanding of leadership effectiveness.

may influence teacher satisfaction, it is equally possible that more
satisfied teachers perceive their leaders as more supportive. We partially
address this issue by controlling for important teacher covariates, but we
