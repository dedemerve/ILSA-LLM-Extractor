A B S T R A C T
The relationship between private tutoring and mathematics learning outcomes remains unclear in the existing 
literature. To address this issue, a three-level meta-analysis was conducted of 76 international empirical studies 
published between 2000 and 2024, examining the association between private tutoring and students' cognitive 
and non-cognitive learning outcomes in mathematics. Results revealed a small positive correlation between 
private tutoring and mathematics learning outcomes in primary and secondary schools, primarily in cognitive 
learning outcomes. The relationship with non-cognitive learning outcomes was nonsignificant. Additionally, test 
type, school stage, and geographical location moderated the relationship between private tutoring and cognitive 
learning outcomes in mathematics.
Educational relevance statement
This three-level meta-analysis systematically examines the relation­
ship between private tutoring and mathematics learning outcomes in 
primary and secondary education. The results revealed a small positive 
association between private tutoring in mathematics and cognitive 
learning outcomes, moderated by school stage, geographical location, 
and test type. These findings highlight significant variations in the 
effectiveness of private tutoring in mathematics across different 
educational contexts, providing evidence-based insights to help parents 
make rational choices tailored to their children's individual needs and 
cognitive development, while preventing excessive or inappropriate 
practices. Additionally, the study offers direction for future research, 
particularly in exploring the relationship between private tutoring in 
mathematics and non-cognitive outcomes.
1. Introduction
Private tutoring, as a form of after-school instruction, has drawn 
considerable attention in the field of education (Bray, 2017 Byun et al., 
2018 Zhang & Bray, 2019). Private tutoring is particularly prevalent in 
highly competitive Asian countries such as China, South Korea, Japan, 
and Singapore (Bray, 2013 Choi & Park, 2016 Ghosh & Bray, 2020
Joki´c, 2013 Wang & Guo, 2018). Among academic disciplines, mathe­
matics, a high-stake subject, is one of the most frequently tutored (Wang 
& Guo, 2018). Parents invest considerable resources, hoping that private 
tutoring can provide their children with more learning opportunities 
and help them improve their mathematics learning outcomes (¨Ozdere, 
2021).
According to the school learning model and social cognitive theory, 
private tutoring may affect mathematics learning outcomes (Carroll, 
1963 Bandura, 2001 Guill et al., 2020). The school learning model 
emphasizes that a student's learning outcome depends on the ratio of 
actual study time to the time required by the individual (Carroll, 1963). 
Private tutoring can provide students with additional time and oppor­
tunities to study mathematics (He et al., 2021 Zhang et al., 2022), which 
may lead to improvement in their mathematics learning outcomes. In 
contrast, social cognitive theory posits that human behavior is the 
product of the interaction among behavior, environmental variables, 
cognition, and other personal factors (Bandura, 2001). The effect of

Previous studies have found that different sample characteristics, 
tutoring characteristics, and research characteristics may cause differ­

This also explains why the results are inconsistent in prior empirical

tively synthesizes the main empirical research results in this field to 
enhance people's understanding of the true correlation between private 
tutoring and mathematics learning outcomes, and explores variables 
that potentially moderate this correlation.
1.1. Definition of private tutoring and mathematics learning outcomes
Private tutoring is a form of paid assistance that mimics and sup­
plements the mainstream curriculum of schools. It takes place outside of 
the school curriculum and changes along with the evolution of the 
school curriculum (Bray, 2013, 2014 Zhang & Bray, 2020). This study 
focused exclusively on private tutoring in mathematics.
Mathematics learning outcomes refers to the assessable and expected 
changes in students' knowledge, skills, attitudes, values, behaviors, or 
abilities after they complete the process of learning mathematics (Walsh, 
2006). This study draws on Heckman and Kautz's (2012) framework for 
classifying human abilities, defining mathematical learning outcomes as 
students' comprehensive performance following educational in­
terventions in mathematics, encompassing the two dimensions of 
cognitive and non-cognitive learning outcomes.
Cognitive learning outcomes refer to the quantifiable results that 
students acquire through intellectual efforts during the teaching process 
and various educational activities (Shi et al., 2020). When reviewing the 
literature, it was found that in most studies on cognitive learning out­
comes, mathematical achievements are used to measure students' 
cognitive learning outcomes in the mathematics subject (Hong & Hong, 
2016 He et al., 2021 Zhang et al., 2023 Zhang et al., 2024b), while a 
small number of studies have specifically measured abilities such as 
problem-solving skills, creative thinking, focusing on the mastery and 
application of mathematical knowledge in real life (Han & Suh, 2023). 
This aligns with the detailed classification of cognitive learning out­
comes by Kyllonen et al. (2011), who pointed out that cognitive factors 
include the mastery of subject-specific knowledge and skills, and the 
outcomes are often reflected in academic indicators such as test scores 
and average grade, and also involve integrating knowledge in complex 
situations, logical reasoning, abstract thinking, problem-solving, and 
language expression abilities. From a practical perspective, although 
mathematical achievements are often used as a reference for students' 
mathematical abilities, the two are not equivalent. The abilities an in­
dividual possesses and the actual abilities they demonstrate often differ, 
and the achievements may only reflect a part of the abilities rather than 
the entire range. Based on this, this study further classifies the related 
research involving cognitive learning outcomes into two categories for 
analysis: mathematics achievement and mathematics ability.
Non-cognitive learning outcomes encompass the psychological, 
emotional, and attitudinal changes that influence the experience and 
behavior of mathematics learning (De Raad & Schouwenburg, 1996; 
Lipnevich & Roberts, 2012; Messick, 1979). Self-determination theory 
provides a basis for classifying the positive and negative non-cognitive 
learning factors in mathematics learning (Deci & Ryan, 1993). During 
the process of mathematics learning, when psychological needs such as 
autonomy and relevance are met, positive outcomes such as enhanced 
intrinsic motivation will occur; otherwise, negative effects will arise 
(Bray, 2003 Krapp, 2005). Positive factors include those that can 
enhance students' sense of control over learning and the perceived value 
of tutoring, while negative factors have the opposite effect. In this study, 
positive factors represent learning interest, learning motivation, and 
perception of a supportive atmosphere during teacher-student in­
teractions and peer collaboration in mathematics learning. Negative 
factors include mathematics anxiety, academic burden, and negative 
emotions when facing mathematical challenges (Haase et al., 2019
Rameli & Kosnin, 2016).
Mathematics learning outcomes are framed in terms of cognitive and 
non-cognitive dimensions, considering both the acquisition of knowl­
edge and skills, and the development of appropriate learning psychology 
and attitudes. Together, these two aspects define students' comprehen­
sive mathematics learning outcomes, providing a multi-dimensional 
context for assessing such outcomes (Fig. 1).
1.2. Associations between private tutoring and mathematics learning 
outcomes
Among the numerous empirical studies that have explored the rela­
tionship between private tutoring and mathematics learning outcomes, 
some indicate that private tutoring may enhance both the cognitive and 
non-cognitive aspects of students' mathematics learning outcomes (Li, 
2021 Zhang et al., 2023 Zhang et al., 2024c Zheng et al., 2020). In terms 
of cognition learning outcomes, it was primarily observed that students 
who participated in private tutoring showed significantly greater 
improvement in their mathematics achievement compared to those who 
did not receive such tutoring (Li, 2021 Zheng et al., 2020). This result is 
consistent with the school learning model. In terms of non-cognitive 
learning outcomes, some studies found that private tutoring in mathe­
matics may promote positive non-cognitive factors such as students' self- 
confidence (Liang et al., 2022 Zheng et al., 2020), stabilize or enhance 
their overall well-being, and reduce the frequency of negative emotions 
(Sun et al., 2020). This phenomenon can be explained by self- 
determination theory, which posits that the social environment can 
enhance human internal motivation by supporting the satisfaction of 
three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relation­
ships (Deci & Ryan, 1993). Private tutoring, as a supplementary 
educational form, may be able to compensate for deficiencies of formal 
school education in meeting students' basic psychological needs.
However, other studies have reached the opposite conclusion, 
finding that private tutoring does not improve students' mathematics 
achievement in terms of cognition learning outcomes (Cole, 2017
Oemerogullari et al., 2020), and may even reduce students' creative 
thinking (Han & Suh, 2023). In terms of non-cognitive learning out­
comes, students who participate in private tutoring in mathematics may 
exhibit greater helplessness and lower interest in the subject (Guill et al., 
2020 Benckwitz et al., 2022), and may even have a negative effect on the 
mental health of middle school students (Kim et al., 2022 Li & Liu, 2022
Zhang & Gao, 2022). As such, it is clear that there remain contradictions 
in the empirical research on the relationship between private tutoring 
and the mathematics learning outcomes. Therefore, a meta-analysis is 
needed to systematically explain these contradictions.
1.3. Previous meta-analyses and research gap
Three meta-analyses have examined the relationship between pri­
vate tutoring and overall achievement in multiple subjects (Liu & Yang, 
2015 Zhang & Liu, 2022 Zhang, 2024). Liu and Yang (2015) conducted a 
traditional meta-analysis of 25 studies published between 1990 and 
2014, finding that the effect size of private tutoring on multi-disciplinary 
comprehensive academic achievement was relatively low, and influ­
enced by how academic achievement and grade variables were 
measured. Zhang (2024) analyzed 21 empirical studies of primary and 
secondary school students from 2000 to 2023, finding that private 
tutoring was not related to students' academic achievement. Zhang and 
Liu (2022) conducted a quantitative synthesis of 22 empirical studies 
from 2001 to 2020, finding that the subject variable had a moderated 
relationship between private tutoring and academic achievement. Given

cognitive and non-cognitive aspects of mathematics learning outcomes,

depth exploration of the relationship between private tutoring and 
mathematics learning outcomes uses a relatively novel three-level meta- 
analysis, which can better explain the interdependence of effect sizes as 
compared with simpler approaches.
1.4. Exploration of moderator variables
The school learning model states that students' learning outcomes are 
influenced by their ability and the quality of teaching (Carroll, 1963). 
Previous studies have found that different student abilities, types of 
private tutors, and forms of private tutoring may cause differences in the 
effectiveness of private tutoring in mathematics. For example, after 
tracking longitudinal changes among 1500 secondary school students 
following private tutoring in mathematics, Zhang et al. (2024b) found 
that students with lower ability gained more from private tutoring than 
those with higher ability levels. Oemerogullari et al. (2020) discovered 
that different private tutors produce different mathematics learning 
outcomes. Teachers are the key factor in the quality of private tutoring 
(Hawrot et al., 2024). Some studies also show that there are differences 
in students' mathematics learning outcomes according to whether pri­
vate tutoring is conducted in a group or individual setting (Wiseman, 
2021 Zhang & Liu, 2016). However, some studies have found that there 
is no significant difference between these two forms of mathematics 
private tutoring in primary school mathematics (Pellegrini et al., 2018). 
This contradiction may stem from differences in educational stages. 
Therefore, the current study considers school stage, student level, type 
of teacher, and form of tutoring as potential moderators of the correla­
tion between private tutoring and mathematics learning outcomes.
According to cognitive load theory, the limited capacity of human 
working memory determines that learning outcomes are largely 
dependent on the reasonable regulation of cognitive load (Paas et al., 
2004; Sweller, 2008). When students receive private tutoring in math­
ematics during both semesters and vacations, differences in their 
cognitive load during these two different periods may affect the corre­
lation between private tutoring and mathematics learning outcomes; 
this has been confirmed in empirical studies (He et al., 2021). Moreover, 
as the duration of private tutoring increases, students may have more 
time to study and practice problem-solving skills, thereby reducing ac­
ademic stress (Benckwitz et al., 2022). However, increasing tutoring 
duration may also increase external cognitive load, leading to distrac­
tion, fatigue, and a reduced correlation between private tutoring and 
learning outcomes (Han & Suh, 2023). Thus, the duration and period (i. 
e., semester versus vacation) of private tutoring also affect the correla­
tion between private tutoring and mathematics learning outcomes.
It is worth noting that differences in test type can also lead to 
inconsistent research conclusions regarding the relationship between 
private tutoring and mathematics cognitive learning outcomes. For 
instance, some studies used school final test scores as the dependent 
variable, finding that private tutoring in mathematics could improve 
mathematics achievement (Zhang et al., 2021 Zhang et al., 2023). 
However, using test data from the Program for International Student 
Assessment, Wiseman (2021) discovered that private tutoring in math­
ematics was negatively correlated with students' academic achievement. 
Therefore, the current study examines the moderating effects of 
different test types.
Extant empirical studies primarily used cross-sectional and longitu­
dinal research designs. Students who attend private tutoring and those 
who do not often exhibit systematic differences in terms of their initial 
academic achievement, learning motivation, and other aspects, all of 
which may lead to biases in research results. Cross-sectional studies 
typically survey different student groups at a specific point in time (Ren 
et al., 2023). Although this method can quickly collect data, it is difficult 
to control for inter-group differences, and the results may be biased 
(Zhang et al., 2022). In contrast, longitudinal studies follow the same 
group of students over multiple time points, focusing on the dynamic

1.5. Research objectives and hypotheses
Given that extant empirical studies of the correlation between pri­
vate tutoring and mathematics learning outcomes have not reached 
consensus, this research adopted a three-level meta-analysis to quanti­
tatively integrate and assess empirical studies conducted internationally 
from 2000 to 2024. Specifically, this research had the following three 
questions: (1) To quantitatively assess the overall correlation between 
private tutoring and mathematics learning outcomes by integrating 
existing empirical data; (2) To separately explore the correlations be­
tween private tutoring and cognitive and non-cognitive learning out­
comes in mathematics; and (3) To use moderation analysis to identify 
the variables that explain heterogeneity among studies. Based on the 
existing research and the theoretical framework, it was hypothesized 
that private tutoring would be positively correlated with overall 
learning outcomes in mathematics (Hypothesis H1), positively corre­
lated with cognitive learning outcomes (Hypothesis H2), and positively 
correlated with non-cognitive learning (Hypothesis H3). It was further 
hypothesized that the correlation between private tutoring and learning 
outcomes of mathematics would be affected by student characteristics 
(e.g., student ability, region), private tutoring characteristics (e.g., 
teacher type), and research characteristics (e.g., test types; Hypothesis 
H4).
2. Method
2.1. Data collection
2.1.1. Literature search strategy
This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Report­
ing Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guide­
lines (Page et al., 2021). No human subjects approval was required as 
the study did not involve direct collection or analysis of individual-level 
human subject data. The R package and the literature data used in this 
study can be accessed via the following website: https://www.labxing. 
com/lab/1318/data. Interested readers may contact the corresponding 
author to obtain detailed data. We conducted a comprehensive search 
across multiple authoritative databases (EBSCO, ProQuest, ScienceDir­
ect, SpringerLink, Web of Science Core Collection, PsycINFO, SCOPUS, 
CNKI, WanFang, VIP), employing Boolean logic operators to construct 
search formulas, with the aim of retrieving literature encompassing the 
two primary themes of “private tutoring” and “mathematics.” The term 
“shadow education” is widely used in the context of private tutoring. 
However, there is diversity in common expressions across different re­
gions of the world. These include “cram school,” “extracurricular pro­
grams,” “summer schools,” “holiday programs,” and “out of school time” 
in the United States (Bray, 2014 Zhang & Liu, 2022). Consequently, to 
ensure comprehensive acquisition of information, the following search 
terms were employed to retrieve relevant studies from titles, abstracts, 
and keywords. The search terms included: “out of school tutoring” OR 
“OST” OR “extra tutoring” OR “Private tutoring” OR “Private tuition” 
OR “Shadow education” OR “Private supplementary tutoring” OR “Ed­
ucation remediation” OR “Cram school” OR “summer school” OR 
“vacation” OR “after school programs”.
2.1.2. Inclusion criteria
In an initial search of multiple databases, 7199 relevant literature 
records were identified. Using Zotero software, 2315 duplicate studies 
were eliminated. To ensure the accuracy of the screening process, two 
researchers meticulously reviewed the titles, abstracts, and keywords of 
the literature and excluded documents that were not relevant to the 
theme of this study. Subsequently, the two researchers conducted a full- 
text search, careful reading, and two rounds of review of the 479 doc­
uments that were highly relevant to the topic, ensuring that the studies 
met the following criteria: 
1. The study was empirical in nature.
2. The study needed to focus on private tutoring in mathematics, which 
is a form of paid, out-of-school learning that imitates the formal 
mathematics curriculum taught in schools.
3. The topic needed to pertains to the relationship between private 
mathematics teaching and students' mathematics learning outcomes, 
or the relationship between private mathematics teaching and non- 
cognitive learning outcomes.
4. The research sample needed to include ordinary students from pri­
mary through high school.
5. The study needed to provide relevant statistical measures for calcu­
lating effect sizes (r values, t values, F values, d values, and so forth, 
that can be converted into correlation coefficients).
6. The study was published between the years 2000 and 2024.
After rigorous selection and verification by two researchers, 76 
studies were included in the final analysis. The study retrieval, 
screening, and inclusion processes are depicted in the PRISMA flow di­
agram (Fig. 2).

2.3.2. Three-level meta-analysis model
The conventional meta-analytic framework is predicated on the 
assumption of mutual independence among effect sizes; should corre­
lations exist between sizes, the shared, overlapping information could 
potentially engender overestimation of the reliability of the outcomes 
(Borenstein et al., 2021). However, research on intrinsic complexity and 
variability may yield effect sizes subject to three forms of interdepen­
dence: interdependence due to sampling variability, reliance on the 
magnitude of the true effect, and nested interdependence of multiple 
effect sizes within a single investigation (Cheung, 2019 Cheung, 2015). 
It is often difficult to attain an ideal state of complete independence 
among effect sizes. With sufficient data one can compute the conditional 
sampling covariance matrix for the effect sizes, thereby effectively 
managing the first and second types of interdependencies using 
advanced multivariate meta-analytic techniques. Nonetheless, it must 
be acknowledged that for most of the studies incorporated into this re­
view, obtaining adequate data to estimate the covariance matrices of the 
samples was not feasible. Consequently, a three-level meta-analysis 
model was introduced that addressed the issue of effect size indepen­
dence and elucidated the third form of dependency more effectively 
than a traditional multivariate meta-analysis (Van den Noortgate et al., 
2013, 2015).
In the three-level meta-analysis model, sources of variance were 
partitioned across three levels: Level 1 consisted of sampling variance

2.3.3. Publication bias and sensitivity analysis
Assessing publication bias is a crucial step in ensuring the objectivity 
and scientific rigor of meta-analysis results. The most commonly used 
methods in research are funnel plots and Egger's test (Ran et al., 2022
Soga & Gaston, 2024 Wu & Yu, 2024). However, the three-level meta- 
analysis model involves a more complex random-effects structure. 
Traditional funnel plot methods and Egger's tests are primarily used for 
publication bias testing in simple random-effects or fixed-effects models, 
often assuming that effect sizes originate from independent studies and 
potentially overlooking their inherent correlations. In this context, an 
extended Egger's test method based on a three-level meta-analysis model 
using sampling error or sample size as a moderating variable has been 
proposed (Viechtbauer, 2017) and applied (Habeck & Schultz, 2015
Zhang & Liu, 2022). Therefore, building on previous research, we 
adopted the extended Egger's test to analyze publication bias based on 
the overall three-level meta-analysis model, using sampling error as a 
moderating factor. If the null hypothesis (H0 : β1 = 0) is rejected at a 
90% confidence level, publication bias is present (Sterne & Egger, 2005
Zhang & Liu, 2022).
Sensitivity analysis for a multivariate meta-analysis model is still 
under development (Habeck & Schultz, 2015). Currently, most studies 
use inter-sample Cook's distance (CD) and DFBETAS values to assess the 
sensitivity of the analysis (Lai et al., 2023 Zhang & Liu, 2022). CD re­
flects the impact of an observation on the overall model fit and is 
calculated based on the degree of change in model parameter estimates 
after the removal of the observation (Cook, 1977). A larger CD value 
indicates that the corresponding observation has a significant impact on 
the model estimation. DFBETAS measures the degree of change in each 
regression coefficient estimate after the deletion of an observation 
(Belsley et al., 2005). If the CD value exceeds 0.45 (Cook & Weisberg, 
1983) or the DFBETAS value exceeds 1 (Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010), 
the effect size is considered to have an influence on the average effect.
3. Results
3.1. Summary of the study
The meta-analytic synthesis encompassed a corpus of 76 research 
studies, with 116 participant samples extending from N = 47 to N =
248,308, and a timeline of publication traversing the period between 
2005 and 2024. From the included studies, 202 effect sizes were sys­
tematically retrieved and analyzed. Among them, 166 effect sizes cor­
responded to investigation of the relationship between private tutoring 
and cognitive learning outcomes in mathematics, while 36 effect sizes 
were related to investigation of the relationship between private tutor­
ing in mathematics and non-cognitive learning outcomes. Within the 
research pertaining to students' mathematical cognitive learning out­
comes, a substantial majority (92.1%) centered on mathematics 
achievement, with a mere 7.9% explicitly examining mathematics 
ability such as mathematical reasoning, proficiency, or creative apti­
tude. Additionally, research foci in private mathematics tutoring were 
notably concentrated within the geographical confines of Asian nations 
and predominantly targeted the secondary school stage.
The moderator variables encompassed test type, region, geograph­
ical position, school stage, student level, form of tutoring, type of 
teacher, tutoring period, tutoring time, and publication year. Specif­
ically, test type consisted of 13 large-scale international tests, 29 large- 
scale national tests, 36 school tests, and 12 independently developed 
tests. In terms of region, 9 participant samples originated from North

3.3. Association of private tutoring with mathematics learning outcomes
In this study, we adopted a significance level of 0.05. The model 
results are presented in Table 2. Private tutoring exhibited a statistically 
significant positive association with overall mathematics learning out­
comes (r = 0.110,p < 0.01). A statistically significant positive correla­
tion was observed between private tutoring and cognitive learning 
outcomes in mathematics (r = 0.144,p < 0.01), whereas no significant 
correlation was detected for non-cognitive learning outcomes in math­
ematics (r = 0.041,p > 0.05).
In accordance with the three-level meta-analytic model, the variance 
in heterogeneity among studies was expressed as I2 = τ2(2) + τ2(3)
(Cheung, 2015). As delineated by Higgins and Thompson (2004), I2 
thresholds at 25%, 50%, and 75% correspond to low, moderate, and 
high levels of heterogeneity, respectively. The between- and within- 
study heterogeneity for the cognitive group were 52.92% and 47.05%, 
respectively, with a mere 0.03% attributed to sampling error. Within the 
non-cognitive group, the between- and within-study heterogeneities 
were 14.69% and 85.01%, respectively, with a minimal 0.30% attrib­
uted to sampling error. Consequently, it is possible that underlying 
moderating effects may have influenced the observed outcomes.
Further analysis of the overall data (Table 3) revealed that the cor­
relation between private tutoring and students' cognitive learning out­
comes in mathematics was primarily reflected in mathematics 
achievement (r = 0.145,p < 0.01), but not in mathematics ability (r =
0.082,p > 0.05).
Among non-cognitive factors, private tutoring in mathematics was 
not significantly correlated with either positive factors (r = 0.073, CI =
[ −0.038, 0.184], p > 0.05) or negative factors (r = 0.104, CI = [ −
0.002, 0.210],p > 0.05).

4. Discussion
4.1. Main findings
In this study, a three-level meta-analytic model was constructed to 
quantitatively synthesize 76 studies (202 effect sizes) published since 
2000, examining the relationship between private tutoring and mathe­
matics learning outcomes. The results indicated a significant positive 
correlation between private tutoring and overall mathematics learning 
outcomes, supporting Hypothesis 1. However, it is noteworthy that the 
magnitude of this positive correlation was small (Cohen, 1992). When 
analyzing cognitive and non-cognitive dimensions separately, the model 
yielded divergent results.
Regarding the cognitive dimension, private tutoring had a small 
positive correlation with mathematics achievement (Cohen, 1992), but 
no significant correlation with mathematical abilities, partially sup­
porting Hypothesis 2. This finding aligns with the school learning model, 
which suggests that the additional learning time provided by private 
tutoring may help students enhance mathematics achievement (Carroll, 
1963 Boaler & Zoido, 2016). However, this finding also reveals that 
private tutoring may focus excessively on exam-oriented training (e.g., 
mechanical algorithmic teaching, exam “tricks”; Zhang et al., 2024a), 
making it difficult to facilitate the transition from shallow learning to 
deep learning (Marton & S¨alj¨o, 1976). Therefore, we recommend that 
parents carefully weigh the costs and expected benefits of private 
tutoring before making decisions. Meanwhile, tutors should refine 
instructional strategies to better support the development of students' 
mathematical abilities.
Regarding the non-cognitive dimension, private tutoring in mathe­
matics exhibited no significant correlation with either positive or 
negative non-cognitive learning outcomes; Hypothesis 3 was not sup­
ported. These results indicate that the relationship between private 
tutoring in mathematics and learning outcomes is limited to the cogni­
tive dimension and does not promote students' non-cognitive develop­
ment. It is noteworthy that although this study did not find a significant 
association between private tutoring in mathematics and non-cognitive 
learning outcomes, conflicting results exist in the literature (Guill et al., 
2020 Kim et al., 2022 Li & Liu, 2022 Liang et al., 2022 Zhang & Gao, 
2022). This inconsistency may arise from either the currently limited 
research on the relationship between private tutoring in mathematics 
and non-cognitive learning outcomes, or the substantial heterogeneity 
in students' initial psychological traits (Benckwitz et al., 2022 Zhang 
et al., 2024b). Therefore, we suggest that subsequent research should 
pay more attention to the relationship between private tutoring in 
mathematics and non-cognitive learning outcomes, and use diverse 
samples and comprehensive measurement tools to more deeply examine 
the correlation between the two.
4.2. Moderating effects
To address the third research objective of identifying variables that 
may influence the relationship between private tutoring in mathematics 
and learning outcomes, we analyzed 11 potential moderator variables 
across three dimensions (student characteristics, tutoring characteris­
tics, and research characteristics) based on relevant theories and exist­
ing literature. The results indicated that test type, school stage, and 
geographical location significantly moderated the relationship between 
private tutoring and cognitive mathematics learning outcomes, while no 
variables showed moderating effects on non-cognitive outcomes, 
providing partial support for Hypothesis 4.
Regarding the test-type moderator, a significant positive correlation 
was observed between private tutoring and cognitive mathematics 
learning outcomes (r = 0.289) when school test scores were used as the 
evaluation metric, whereas no significant relationships were found 
when other tests were employed. This pattern likely stems from the 
inherent characteristics of private tutoring, namely its high degree of 
alignment with and supplementation of mainstream school curricula, 
resulting in substantial content consistency between private tutoring 
materials and school tests (Bray, 2013, 2014). In contrast, international/ 
national standardized tests or independently developed tests typically 
employ different evaluation frameworks and item design approaches, 
with assessment priorities that diverge from school test content. Only 
when the test content is highly consistent with the teaching content does 
the relationship between private tutoring and mathematical cognitive 
learning outcomes become significant. Consequently, we recommend 
implementing diversified assessment methods in private tutoring to 
emphasize students' flexible knowledge application and mitigate exam- 
oriented effects.
The school stage variable was also an important moderating factor. 
The relationship between private tutoring and mathematics cognitive 
learning outcomes was stronger for secondary school students (r =
0.203, p < 0.01) than for primary school students (r =
−
0.063,
p > 0.05). This pattern may stem from the relatively foundational nature 
of primary school mathematics content (Guill & Lintorf, 2019), whereby 
regular classroom instruction may be sufficient to promote student 
comprehension. In contrast, the increased complexity of the secondary 
school mathematics curriculum creates greater potential for private 
tutoring to address cognitive learning gaps. These findings suggest that 
private tutoring interventions should align with students' cognitive 
development stages, with particular caution against excessive tutoring 
during primary education.
This study also identified geographical location as a significant 
moderator of the relationship between private tutoring and mathematics 
cognitive learning outcomes. This moderating effect may be attributed 
to urban-rural disparities in cultural and socioeconomic contexts. For 
instance, urban students may derive greater benefits from more 
competitive academic environments and greater household investments 
in education (Liu, 2012), whereas rural students face constraints due to 
socioeconomic factors and educational resource limitations. These 
constraints not only reduce rural students' access to high-quality private 
tutoring but may also diminish its potential effectiveness when 
obtained.
Furthermore, this meta-analysis did not identify any moderating 
variables that could explain the heterogeneity in the relationship be­
tween private tutoring and mathematics non-cognitive learning out­
comes. This may be attributed to both the limited number of existing 
studies that focus on non-cognitive dimensions, and the even smaller 
number of studies that provide detailed reports on relevant moderating 
variables. Further, compared to the measurement of cognitive learning 
outcomes, assessing non-cognitive dimensions (e.g., emotional and 
psychological changes) requires more sophisticated variable control, 
greater specialized knowledge, and extensive experimental validation 
(Messick, 1989). Therefore, to clarify the source of the heterogeneity in 
the relationship between the two, as mentioned earlier, we suggest that 
subsequent research should pay more attention to the relationship

4.3. Limitations and future research
This study has some limitations. First, the limitation of sample size 
within studies is a common issue when conducting meta-analyses (Shen 
et al., 2020). We found that the sample size was limited when examining 
the effects of moderators such as teacher type and tutoring duration. 
Future studies should report as comprehensively as possible the char­
acteristics of private tutoring, including its form, quality, and student- 
level features. This will help further clarify the factors that may mod­
erate the effects of private tutoring in mathematics. Second, although we 
comprehensively considered the moderating effects of student charac­
teristics, tutoring characteristics, and research methodological vari­
ables, we recognize that other factors (such as educational equity and 
students' learning motivation before tutoring) may also explain the 
heterogeneity of the observed results (Wiseman, 2021) and require 
further investigation. Third, non-cognitive factors play an indispensable 
role in the learning process. (Khine & Areepattamannil, 2016), but 
research on the relationship between private tutoring in mathematics 
and students' non-cognitive aspects is relatively scarce. This may also be 
one of the main reasons why our study did not identify variables that 
could moderate the relationship between tutoring and students' non- 
cognitive aspects. Future research should pay more attention to stu­
dents' non-cognitive factors in mathematics. Fourth, although we 
analyzed the correlation between private tutoring and mathematics 
cognitive learning outcomes, we were unable to explain the relationship 
between private tutoring and different cognitive abilities. Researchers 
often focus on students' academic mathematics achievement, with few 
studies making detailed distinctions among different cognitive abilities 
(Zhang et al., 2023). Future research should strengthen the focus on the 
relationship between private math tutoring and different cognitive 
abilities, which would improve research on tutoring effectiveness and 
better inform policy-making.
5. Conclusion
The data results show that there is a positive correlation between 
private tutoring in mathematics and students' mathematics cognitive 
learning outcomes, especially mathematics achievement, but the effect 
size is small, and the correlation is also moderated by geographical 
location, school stage and test types. There is no significant correlation 
between private tutoring in mathematics and non-cognitive learning 
outcomes. These results show that private tutoring can be used as a 
supplementary educational resource when students have insufficient 
knowledge in the classroom, but parents need to look at it objectively 
and rationally and choose carefully, and should not over-rely on it. To 
better explain the sources of heterogeneity among studies in non- 
cognitive dimensions, it is suggested that future studies further 
explore the mechanism behind the relationship between private tutoring 
in mathematics and students' non-cognitive learning outcomes., Poli­
cymakers and educators should consider these findings when designing 
interventions to support student learning, especially in areas where 
private tutoring is prevalent.
