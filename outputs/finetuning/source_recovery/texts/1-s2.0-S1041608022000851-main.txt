This longitudinal study aimed to investigate how motivational transitions of adolescents in the domain of
mathematics from Grades 9 to 10 were related to student-perceived mathematics teacher support and studentoriented teaching. Data were drawn from the 2012 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) and
its German national extension called PISA Plus 2012–2013. We used a subsample of 2605 students (51.0 % girls)
from 198 classrooms. Using latent profile analyses, we identified three motivational patterns based on
expectancy-value theory that were meaningfully associated with students' mathematics test scores and work
ethics. Latent transition analyses showed that these patterns were mostly stable across time. Occurring changes
were characterized by a decrease in mathematics motivation across time. Student-oriented teaching as reported
by students in Grade 9 impeded maladaptive motivational transitions. Students with particularly low interest and
utility value benefitted from teachers who direct their instruction at students' motivational characteristics.

1. Expectancy-value profiles in math: How are studentperceived teaching behaviors related to motivational
transitions?
Students are differently motivated to learn — some students feel
competent in certain domains but at the same time do not enjoy learning
in these domains, others feel competent, enjoy to learn but, at the same
time, associate high psychological costs with academic learning processes (Lazarides, Dietrich, et al., 2019; Watt, Bucich, & Dacosta, 2019).
Motivational characteristics thus differ across groups of students (interindividual differences) as well as within students (intra-individual differences) (Marsh, Lüdtke, Trautwein, & Morin, 2009). Such motivational patterns have been shown to be relevant for students' domainspecific educational and occupational choices (Aunola, Selänne,
Selänne, & Ryba, 2018; Lazarides, Dicke, et al., 2019; Oppermann,
Vinni-Laakso, Juuti, Loukomies, & Salmela-Aro, 2021). Theoretical
frameworks on motivational development, such as Eccles's situated
expectancy-value theory (SEVT; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) describe that

such differences in motivational characteristics occur because each adolescent's' motivation is differently influenced by learning situations and
context. There is, however, a scarcity of research that investigates how
such intra-individually different motivational characteristics change
across time and whether and how such changes relate to characteristics
of the person and of the learning situation. Such knowledge would,
however, allow better understanding of how individual's motivational
development differs depending on different learning contexts. The present study addresses such questions and investigates (a) the identification of profiles based on SEVT, (b) students' motivational transitions
from Grade 9 to Grade 10 in secondary mathematics classrooms, and (c)
how student-oriented teaching practices and teacher support as
perceived by the students are related to such motivational transitions
across time. We focus on the domain of mathematics because it is widely
recognized that mathematics competence represents an important life
skill (e.g., OECD, 2020).

1.1. Motivational profiles based on situated expectancy-value theory

different domains. Because of social stereotypes, it is, for example,
assumed that teachers and parents perceive girls to have lower competences than boys in mathematics (Watt, 2016). In line with such theoretical assumptions, person-centered studies have shown that girls are
less likely than boys to be in groups with high task values and competence beliefs in STEM domains, and that boys were less likely to fall into
groups with low task values and competence beliefs in such domains
(mathematics: Oppermann et al., 2021; mathematics: Watt et al., 2019).
Other studies show that girls tend to be overrepresented in patterns with
low perceived talent and intrinsic value (interest value), high utility value
and costs (Watt et al., 2019), and underrepresented in profiles with low
interest but high self-concept in mathematics (Lazarides, Dietrich, et al.,
2019). Taken together, girls seem to be less likely to be in profiles with
high expectancy beliefs, independent from the level of task value in
those profiles.
Regarding the consequences of membership, on a theoretical level,
we assume that the interindividual patterns of motivational beliefs
relate to students' achievement because SEVT describes motivational
beliefs as most proximal influences on achievement (Eccles, Wigfield, &
Schiefele, 1998). The specific value of person-centered approaches is
that such relations are expected to vary across different groups of individuals (Laursen & Hoff, 2006). Previous person-centered research
indicated, for example, that students in a ‘Moderate All’ profile had a
significantly lower STEM GPA than students in a ‘High Competence/
Values-moderate Low Costs’ profile in science (Perez, Wormington,
et al., 2019), or that groups with low self-concept in reading often reported low reading skills, whereas some groups with high interest also
reported low reading skills (Viljaranta et al., 2017). Thus, personcentered research points out that achievement seems to be more
strongly associated with expectancies of success than with interests.
Focusing on achievement-related behaviors, Watt et al. (2019) found
that students in a ‘Positively engaged’ group with high expectancies,
interest, utility value, and low perceived cost reported the highest academic effort followed by students in a ‘struggling ambitious’ group with
high positive motivational beliefs but high perceived cost. In this study,
we focus on achievement-related behaviors in terms of work ethics,
which is defined as an effortful and conscientious engagement in activities in a given domain (Mang et al., 2018).

Situated expectancy-value theory (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) describes the motivational beliefs of individuals, defined as task-specific
values and success expectancies, as proximal influences on their
achievement and achievement related behaviors. Success expectancies
are defined as the beliefs about one's own ability to achieve well on an
upcoming task. Task values are the subjective value of a given task for an
individual and are differentiated into four subjective value facets
(Eccles, 2005). In this study, in line with other SEVT-based studies (e.g.,
Fong, Kremer, Cox, & Lawson, 2021), we focus on success expectancies
in terms of self-efficacy beliefs. Previous research has shown that success
expectancies and self-efficacy are empirically not distinguishable
(Wigfield & Eccles, 1994). Self-efficacy is defined as ‘beliefs about one's
capabilities to organize and execute the courses of action required to
produce given attainments.’ (Bandura, 1997, p. 3). Furthermore, we
focus on two task value facets — on intrinsic value, which is defined as
the interest that individuals perceive when engaging in an activity, and
on utility value, which is defined as the perceived usefulness of activities
for an individual's goals (Eccles, 2005). We focus on these two value
facets because of their contrasting character — intrinsic value is
intrinsically characterized, utility value is similar to extrinsic motivation. Applying a person-centered approach, we are interested in identifying profiles of motivational beliefs. On a theoretical level, SEVT
proposes strong relations between intrinsic value, utility value, and
success expectancies, which would be represented by profiles with leveldifferences in motivational beliefs — for example, profiles of ‘high’,
‘low’ and ‘moderate’ expectancy-values. Although the number of identified profiles varies across studies, prior work based on SEVT has shown
consistently profiles characterized by level-differences. Chow, Eccles,
and Salmela-Aro (2012), for example, showed in a sample of U.S. Grade
10 high school students three profiles in math and science: high math
and physical science value, moderately low math and physical science
value, and a low math and physical science value. Watt et al. (2019)
identified three profiles of expectancy-values in math in a sample of
Grade 10 students in Australia: a ‘Positively engaged’ (high intrinsic and
utility value, high perceived talent, and low perceived cost), a ‘Disengaged’ (low perceived talent and intrinsic value, high utility value and
costs), and a ‘Struggling ambitious’ profile (high intrinsic and utility
value, high perceived talent, and high perceived cost).
Despite the high intercorrelations among motivational belief constructs, based on SEVT, it might however also be theoretically assumed
that groups of students exist with diverging levels of motivational beliefs. Already in elementary school, students are able to differentiate
success expectancies and task values (Wigfield et al., 1997) — thus, they
might feel competent in domains in which they do not enjoy learning
and which they do not consider as useful, and they might enjoy learning
in domains and consider them as useful in which they do not feel
competent. Lazarides, Dicke, et al. (2019), for example, identified such a
divergent profile in a sample of U.S. high school students — among the
four expectancy-value profiles which they identified was a ‘low intrinsic
value’ profile with low interest, moderate utility value, but high selfconcept (along with medium/high/low motivational beliefs profiles).
Using another sample of Grade 9 students in Germany, Lazarides, Dietrich, et al. (2019) identified four similar profiles, including a ‘highly
confident, hardly interested’ profile characterized by low interest,
moderate utility value and high levels of self-concept.

1.3. Motivational transitions
Students' expectancies and values decline on average over the course
of the school years (Wigfield et al., 1997). Possible causes of this decline
have been described on a theoretical level by stage-environment fit
theory (Eccles & Roeser, 2009), that propose that particularly in
adolescence, students experience a greater need for autonomy and selfdetermination, which is often not addressed in learning environments in
schools. Developmental theorists further describe developmental tasks
in adolescence such as building close relationships to peers or detachment from parents (Havighurst, 1948; Seiffge-Krenke, Kiuru, & Nurmi,
2010), which might cause that adolescents focus on other priorities than
their academic development. Based on the SEVT framework, it can be
assumed that the average decline in students' academic motivational
may not pertain to all students equally (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020).
Interindividual differences in developmental trends of motivational
beliefs are reflected in different changes in students' patterns of motivational beliefs. Person-centered studies that aim to capture such
interindividually different developmental processes are rare. The few
existing studies propose that adolescents' patterns of motivational beliefs are relatively stable over time (Marcoulides, Gottfried, Gottfried, &
Oliver, 2008), but that there is also a certain proportion of adolescents
for whom changes in motivational patterns occur. Using a latent transition approach, Oppermann et al. (2021), for example, showed in a
sample of Finnish elementary students a moderate stability of the

1.4. Student-perceived teaching behaviors and motivational transitions

Hypothesis 3b. Previous research has shown that the effect of mathematics self-concept on test scores is stronger than the effect of mathematics interest on subsequent test scores (Marsh, Trautwein, Lüdtke,
Köller, & Baumert, 2005). Hence, for mathematics competence, we expected that students in profiles with high competence beliefs and low
interest would still show high mathematics test scores. On the other
hand, interest is an important drive for school engagement. For work
ethics, we thus expected that students with low levels of interest – even if
coupled with high self-efficacy – might report low levels of work ethics.

Hypothesis 3a. Regarding profiles characterized by level differences,
we assumed that students in profiles with overall high motivation would
report high levels of test scores and work ethics and vice versa for students in profiles with particularly low motivation (Watt et al., 2019).

Socio-cognitive frameworks such as SEVT propose that the motivational beliefs of individuals are shaped by their interpretations and
perceptions of their socializers' beliefs and behaviors (Eccles et al.,
1998). Particularly teachers are central socializers in adolescence whose
beliefs and support behaviors influence the developmental changes of
adolescents' motivational beliefs (Brophy, 1985; Hamre & Pianta, 2006).
Many variable-centered studies have shown that teachers' supportive
behaviors are relevant for the development of adolescents' motivation
(e.g., Dietrich, Dicke, Kracke, & Noack, 2015; Hettinger, Lazarides, &
Schiefele, 2022; Ma, Luo, & Xiao, 2021). Referring to the idea of personcentered approaches that such relations between variables vary for
different groups of individuals within a population, it might be assumed
that teaching behaviors are differently relevant for changes in motivational beliefs for different groups of students. Accordingly, Lazarides,
Dietrich, et al. (2019) showed that adolescents' self-reported instructional clarity and teaching for meaning enhanced adaptive motivational
changes for students with overall low motivation (low interest, low selfconcept, low perceived importance of learning) by enhancing changes to
groups with overall moderate motivation. Xie, Vongkulluksn, Cheng,
and Jiang (2022) showed that high school students in a ‘balanced
demotivated’ group with low levels of motivation particularly benefit
from high perceived school belonging, which was associated with
changes to a group with moderate levels of external regulation one year
later. However, there are only few studies that examine the theoretical
assumption that student-perceived teaching behaviors are differently
relevant for students' motivational change depending on their motivational pattern.

Hypothesis 4a. Overall, we expected that profile membership in
adolescence would be stable across time.
Hypothesis 4b. For those with changing profile membership, we expected that apart from the average trend of a decline in motivation, we
would be able to identify students who were able to increase their low
motivation and to move to profiles with rather moderate levels of
motivation.
Hypothesis 5. We expected that student-oriented teaching practices
(H5a) and teacher support (H5b) as perceived by students would
enhance changes from patterns with low motivation to more adaptive
patterns of motivation.
3. Method
3.1. Sample and design
The data for this study stem from the German participation in the
2012 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA, Prenzel
et al., 2013) and its national extension called PISA Plus 2012–2013,
which assessed students competencies again one year later (Reiss,
Klieme, Köller, & Stanat, 2017). All students were administered a twohour competency assessment; in addition, they answered a questionnaire covering contextual information including their perception of
teaching behaviors in class. For PISA Plus 2012–2013, a sample of two
Grade 9 classes per school were tested once more a year later in 2013,
when the students were in Grade 10 (see Heine, 2017). Since PISA Plus
2012–2013 combined a mandatory baseline survey (at t1, 2012) with n
= 9998 students with a voluntary “follow-up” survey in the subsequent
year, there were substantial sample dropouts at both the school and
student levels at the second measurement time point (t2, 2013). In
addition to a structurally induced decrease in sample size to n = 8874
students due to schools and students for whom a 10th grade level is not
provided for curricular reasons, self-selection due to voluntariness at t2
reduces the longitudinal sample to n = 4610 students who participated
at both measurement time points (cf. Heine, Nagy, Meinck, Zühlke, &
Mang, 2017). The sampling failures at both levels were modelled by
Heine et al. (2017) using existing information from the first measurement time point so that the biases in sample and representativeness of
the remaining n = 4610 students can be compensated for by appropriately adjusted design weights available in the data set.
From this initial longitudinal sample (n = 4610), we used a subsample of n = 2605 students (51.0 % girls) from 198 classrooms in 121
schools. To keep constant any adverse effects of a potential different
quality of teaching at t1 and t2, the selection criterion was that there was
no change of mathematics teacher from grade 9 to grade 10. Approximately two-thirds of the students in our subsample visited non-

3.2. Measures
We based our latent profile analyses on motivational constructs
assessed in Grades 9 and 10. Measures and data both stemmed from the
PISA 2012 data set (Mang et al., 2018). The exact wording of all items is
reported in Appendix A.

3.2.3.2. Teacher support. Student-perceived teacher support was
assessed with six items, for example “Our mathematics teacher explains
mathematics tasks and content until we have understood”. The response
scale ranged from 1 (in each lesson) to 4 (never or almost never) and items
were reversed in polarity for this study. Reliability was α = 0.84 (Grade
9).

3.2.1. Motivational constructs
3.2.1.1. Mathematics self-efficacy. Self-efficacy was assessed with an
eight-item scale. The introductory item was “How certain are you that
you can solve the following mathematical task?”; example items were
“Solving an equation such as 3x + 5 = 17” and “Understanding diagrams
in newspapers.” The response scale ranged from 1 (very confident) to 4
(not at all confident) and the scoring of items was reversed in polarity for
this study. Reliabilities were α = 0.80 (Grade 9) and α = 0.83 (Grade 10).

3.3. Missing data on student background questionnaire
Data were missing for two reasons: a) at Grade 9 because of the
rotated questionnaire design, and b) at Grades 9 and 10 because of
missing answers for single items, for example when students did not fill
in questionnaires in time or skipped specific measures. Due to the
rotated design at Grade 9, of the n = 2605 students in our subsample
who participated in Grades 9 and 10, n = 1752 students reported on
motivational constructs and n = 1721 students reported on teaching
behaviors in mathematics class at Grade 9 — a total of n = 791 students
reported on both, motivational constructs and perceived teaching behaviors. To deal with missing data, we used multiple imputation which
yields more accurate estimates and increased test power compared to
traditional techniques for handling missing data (Enders, 2010). In the
imputation model, we included auxiliary variables from the common
part of the questionnaire that provided relevant background information
(e.g., socioeconomic background, gender, school type). We created m =
20 imputed datasets using Mplus version 8.6 (Muthén & Muthén, 19982019).

3.2.1.2. Mathematics interest. Interest was assessed with a four-item
scale. Example items were “I like books about mathematics” and “I am
looking forward to my mathematics lessons.” The response scale ranged
from 1 (strongly agree) to 4 (strongly disagree) and items were also
reversed in polarity for this study. Reliabilities were α = 0.89 (Grade 9)
and α = 0.89 (Grade 10).
3.2.1.3. Mathematics utility value. Utility value was assessed with a
four-item scale. Example items were “I work hard in mathematics
because it will help me in my future job” and “Learning mathematics is
worth it because it improves my occupational and career perspectives.”
The response scale ranged from 1 (fully applies) to 4 (does not apply), and
again items were reversed in polarity for this study. Reliabilities were α
= 0.85 (Grade 9) and α = 0.84 (Grade 10).

2017). The interpretation of transitions is valid under the condition of
partial structural similarity (Muthén, 2011).

4.2. Latent profile analyses
4.2.1. Motivational belief profiles at Grade 9
For Grade 9, a comparison of fit indices, reported in Table 2, showed
that the BIC and aBIC values consistently decreased across profile solutions. Entropy pointed to a three-profile solution. The BIC-difference
scores plotted in Fig. 1 indicated that the BIC declined most strongly
from the two- to the three-class solution and then levelled out. The plot
indicates that the three-class solution has a substantial better fit than the
two-class solution. Beginning with the four-class solution, the profile
solutions do not decisively contribute to a better model fit. Thus, the BIC
difference scores point to the three-profile solution. When exploring the
profile solutions, results showed that the solutions from the three-profile
solution onwards did not contain clearly distinguishable profiles (see
Appendix D for four-profile solution). Also, beginning with the fiveprofile solution, the group sizes were small with sample sizes of only
7 % of the full sample. Taking all of the information above into account,
we selected the three-profile solution. Average latent class probabilities
(p = .81–p = .88) indicated a high classification quality.
The mean values of profile indicator variables are reported in Table 3
and profile solutions are depicted in Fig. 2. Multivariate analyses of
variance revealed that all between-profile differences in motivational
variables were highly significant (see Appendix E). Additionally, we
calculated Cohens' d values as a measure of effect sizes for mean differences between profiles (Table 3).
Our interpretation of the profiles does not only rely on mean differences between profiles. We also took the response format of the scales
into account. This way, the meaning of the scales that students were
required to answer can be fully captured. Referring to the response scale,
students can be differentiated into groups who reject the statements of
the scales (response area 1–2), who moderately agree with the statements (response area: 2–3) and who show agreement with the given
items (response area: 3–4; see Fig. 2). Furthermore, we selected short
labels for the profiles which are not referring to specific variable names
but rather represent the character of the profiles.
The first profile was characterized by low mathematics utility value
and interest combined with moderate mathematics self-efficacy beliefs.
We labelled this profile accordingly as ‘Disengaged: Moderate selfefficacy, low utility and interest’ (20.9 %, n = 545). The second profile was characterized by high self-efficacy, utility value, and high interest. Accordingly, it was labelled as ‘Highly motivated: High selfefficacy, utility and interest’ profile (24.7 %, n = 646). Students'
average mean scores on all three motivational variables in this profile
were located in the upper section of the response scale indicating that
students in this profile, on average, felt self-efficacious, interested and
perceived that mathematics was of high value for their future goals. The
third profile was characterized by high self-efficacy, and by moderate
utility value and interest in mathematics. The profile was labelled as
‘Confident, but more or less engaged: High self-efficacy, moderate utility
and interest’ profile (54.3 %, n = 1414).

5. Discussion
This study contributes to research on inter-individual patterns of
motivation by examining changes of adolescents' motivational patterns
in secondary school mathematics classrooms and their relations to
student-perceived teaching behaviors. Key findings were that we found
three similar motivational groups in Grades 9 and 10. Changes in
motivational pattern membership were mostly maladaptive, but a small
number of students also transitioned to more favourable motivational
patterns. Student-oriented teaching prevented maladaptive motivational transitions, emphasizing the need for individualized teaching to
hinder motivational decline in class.

5.2. Motivational profiles: gender, test scores, and work ethics
SEVT proposes that due to gender-related socialization processes,
girls would report lower motivational beliefs in mathematics and science domains than boys (Eccles et al., 1998). According to such theoretical assumptions and in line with our expectations (Hypothesis 2a),
girls were less likely to be in the ‘Highly motivated’ profile as compared
to boys in Grades 9 and 10. Furthermore, in line with our assumptions
(Hypotheses 2b), more girls than boys were in the ‘Disengaged’ profile in
Grade 9, but not in Grade 10. Because in the ‘Disengaged’ profile low
interest and utility value was coupled with moderate self-efficacy beliefs, it might be assumed that girls are of particular risk to lose their
valuing of mathematics in adolescence even at high levels of perceived
competence. In future work, to overcome such gender disparities, we
need to examine carefully the basis for such gender-related motivational
beliefs.
According to theoretical assumptions of SEVT about direct relations
between motivational beliefs and achievement, and in line with our
expectations (Hypothesis 3a), mathematics test scores and work ethics
were particularly high in the ‘Highly motivated’ profile. Further developing the theoretical assumptions, our results suggest that a combination of moderate/high mathematics self-efficacy and low interest and
utility value may be particularly detrimental for students' achievement
and achievement-related behaviors because – contradicting our expectations (Hypothesis 3b) – students in the ‘Disengaged’ profile had the
lowest test scores and work ethics of all groups.

5.1. Motivational profiles
Our contribution to theory development in the context of SEVT is
that, in line with our assumptions (Hypothesis 1a and 1b), we found
distinct profiles of motivational beliefs among learners that were characterized by quantitative (Hypothesis 1a: ‘Highly motivated’ profile)
and qualitative (Hypothesis 1b: ‘Disengaged’, ‘Confident, but more or
less engaged’) interindividual motivational differences. Our findings
thus indicate that the levels of motivational beliefs vary between persons
(interindividual differences) but also within persons (intraindividual
differences). These finding corresponds to the theoretical assumption
that motivational beliefs of individuals are differently pronounced
depending on the individual's developmental and situational processes,
individual differences, and individual by context processes (Eccles &
Wigfield, 2020). In the context of SEVT, although it is theoretically
assumed that close relations exist between an individual's interest,
utility value and success expectancies (Eccles, 2005), our results show
that some individuals feel competent in domains in which they are not
interested. This finding has implications for future choice behaviors and

Grade 10 which made it necessary to exclude all students whose mathematics teacher changed between the school years. This selection process might have led to potential biases. On the other hand, it must be

Third, when interpreting the transitions in this study, it needs to be
acknowledged that we only imposed partial structural similarity because
the mean value of self-efficacy in Grade 9 in the “Confident, but more or
less engaged” profile differed from the mean value of the same profile at
Grade 10, which was slightly higher than in the Grade 9 profile.
Consequently, this profile was only partially similar across time and
students who remained in this profile experienced apparently a slight
increase in self-efficacy instead of not changing their motivational
pattern.
To conclude, our findings inform research on multidimensional
learning goals by showing that intra-individual differences in students'
motivation are closely linked to mathematics test scores and work
ethics, affecting future academic pathways in adolescence. The theoretical contribution of our findings thereby is (a) to show that groups of
students with distinct combinations of motivational beliefs exist within
the population (interindividual differences) and that the levels of
motivational beliefs also vary within individuals (intraindividual differences) despite the theoretically assumed linear positive relations
among the constructs; (b) that the average decline in student motivation
does not apply to all students equally and that changes in motivational
beliefs differ for different groups of students; and that (c) relations between student-perceived teacher support and motivational beliefs vary
across groups of students. It is important to note that student-oriented
teaching as perceived by students was most relevant for changes toward a more adaptive pattern of motivation, calling for more studies
that examine the interrelations between perceived teaching behaviors
and motivational patterns in class.

One of the main contributions of this study to existing research was
the examination of inter-individual motivational changes and their relations to student-perceived teaching behaviors in mathematics classrooms. Although based on developmental theorists (Havighurst, 1948;
Seiffge-Krenke et al., 2010), a motivational decline in adolescence might
be expected, our findings show that motivational changes vary across
different groups of individuals. Profile membership was relatively stable
across time, confirming our expectations (Hypothesis 4a). However,
there were also changes in student motivation that differed across
groups. Confirming our expectations (Hypothesis 4b), there were groups
that experienced a decrease in motivation, but there were also groups
that experienced adaptive motivational changes. Some students who
were in the ‘Confident, but more or less engaged’ profile at Grade 9
changed, for example, to the ‘Highly motivated’ profile at Grade 10 (n =
66) and some students who were in the ‘Disengaged’ group at Grade 9
increased their interest and changed to the ‘Confident, but more or less
engaged’ profile at Grade 10 (n = 73). These findings extend our understanding of how motivation develops by showing that the average
decrease in motivation does not apply to all student and by identifying
specific patterns of motivational changes.
Confirming our expectations (Hypothesis 5a), we showed that
student-oriented teaching was related to motivational change. However,
the specific theoretical value of this study is that we were able to show
that student-oriented teaching was only important to motivational
change of students with specific combinations of motivational beliefs.
High levels of perceived student orientation in Grade 9 prevented
changes of students in the ‘Highly motivated’ groups to the ‘Confident,
but more or less engaged’ group at Grade 10, and enhanced changes
from the ‘Disengaged’ group to the ‘Highly motivated’ instead of the
‘Confident, but more or less engaged’ group. Thus, student-oriented
teaching prevented changes to patterns of motivation characterized by
low interest. We confirmed thus that the provision of adaptive learning
support is beneficial for students' academic learning and motivation
(Corno, 2008). It is highly interesting that – contrasting with our
expectation (Hypothesis 5b) – we did not find effects of studentperceived general teacher support on motivational transitions across
time. One explanation might be related to the measures that were used
in this study: The items that assessed perceived teacher support captured
general learning support with no reference to a specific support of students' interest or valuing of learning.
