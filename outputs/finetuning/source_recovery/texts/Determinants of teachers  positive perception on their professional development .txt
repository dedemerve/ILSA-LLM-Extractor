ABSTRACT

ARTICLE HISTORY

Given the complex nature of teachers’ professional development (PD)
processes, it is crucial to examine how various factors surrounding teachers are associated with the evaluation of their PD experience. By applying a machine-learning technique, least absolute shrinkage and selection
operator (LASSO), we were able to include numerous factors in an integrated model to create a data-driven, parsimonious predictive model that
is readily applicable. Using TALIS 2018 U.S. data (n = 2,418), we identified
16 important explanatory variables (out of 132 variables) in determining
teachers’ positive perception on their PD. We found that teachers’ PD
experience depends on multiple layers of factors such as features of PD
activities (10 variables), teachers’ individual characteristics (four variables),
and school organisational environments (two variables). Theoretical and
practical implications are also discussed.

Introduction
It is crucial for teachers to continuously develop their expertise in teaching to stay abreast of
constant changes in social situations and expectations. For example, recent COVID-19 pandemic
abruptly required teachers to become an expert of teaching in a virtual setting for students who
cannot come to schools. Considering these abrupt and overwhelming demands, it is not reasonable
to expect teachers to be fully equipped with complete knowledge and skills from the very early stage
of teaching career. In this context, the importance of systemic efforts to constantly improve
teachers’ professionalism, or professional development (PD), has been acknowledged in both
research and practice (Desimone 2009, Jacob and McGovern 2015). Teacher PD is a broad concept
that explains teachers’ learning activities to improve students’ learning and development through
enhancing teachers’ knowledge and expertise (Avalos 2011). Teacher PD may take various forms
from formal learning activities (e.g. participation in courses or graduate programmes) to informal
activities (e.g. collaboration with colleagues, observations of other teachers’ instruction).
While participating in PD, teachers are not passive learners; rather, they actively organise
their own learning based on their individual characteristics and surrounding environments
(Postholm 2012, Imants and Van der Wal 2020). Thus, to get a better understanding of
teachers’ PD experience, it is critical to consider various factors that exist in multiple layers
at personal level, programme level, and broader school organisational level. However, prior
research on teacher PD has limited the focus to a single layer and/or selective factors due to the
methodological constraints. Without fully considering the interrelated and dynamic

With the recent development of computational methods and machine learning algorithms, more

learning approach was adopted to produce the optimal prediction model which can accurately

relative importance of 132 potential factors from three different layers (i.e. individual teacher

integrated model. In the following section, we provide a review of the relevant literature that guided
this study.

Literature review
Teachers’ evaluation on their professional development experience
Many endeavours have been devoted to evaluating the effectiveness of PD activities to determine
whether they have achieved their aims or not. According to the representative training evaluation
model suggested by Kirkpatrick and Kirkpatrick (2016), it is necessary for evaluators to consider the
multiple levels of the training impacts on professionals and organisations: (1) participants’ reaction
level, (2) learning level, (3) behaviour level, and (4) result level. For reaction level, evaluators should
check trainees’ perceptions (e.g. satisfaction, perceived usefulness, happiness) regarding their
training experience. For learning level, evaluation should be focused on knowledge and skills that
the participants gained from their training experience. For behavioural level, the effect of training
on work performance is considered as the crucial outcome. Lastly, for result level, the productivity
gains of the whole organisation are mainly evaluated. By adopting Kirkpatrick’s training evaluation
model and applying it to teacher PD context, Guskey (2000, 2002) suggested the teacher PD
evaluation model by focusing on five critical levels of information, which are, teachers’ reaction,
teachers’ learning, organisational support and changes, teachers’ use of new knowledge and skills,
and student learning outcomes. This model for teacher PD evaluation has been widely adopted
from teacher PD research so far (e.g. Reeves and Pedulla 2011, Earley and Porritt 2014).
The evaluative models for training and teacher PD commonly emphasise the importance of
participants’ perceptions regarding their training experience as the fundamental aspect. This
is because perceptions of the participants can influence their knowledge, skills, job performance, and eventually organisational outcomes such as student learning and development
(Pajares 1992, Guskey 2002). In other words, teacher perception, which is situated at the very
first level (i.e. reaction level), determines the subsequent outcomes of the PD participation.
This means that changes in teachers’ knowledge, skills, and performance throughout their
participation in PD activities depend on whether they found the PD experience to be useful,
enjoyable, and/or satisfying. In fact, recent studies have provided empirical evidence supporting that teachers’ positive perceptions or reactions on their PD experience are critical to
support the entire effectiveness of PD (Liu et al. 2019, Powell and Bodur 2019). Additionally,
considering the accessibility, low cost, and relatively easy data collection (Wu et al. 2016),
much research examining the impact of teacher PD has widely focused on teachers’

Factors associated with teachers’ PD perceptions
Based on the constructivism perspective, Postholm (2012) indicated that recognising teachers as
active learners is essential to correctly understanding their PD experiences. Postholm (2012)
explains that teachers’ PD experience dynamically interacts with individual characteristics, conditions of PD activities, and the surrounding environment. In fact, several theoretical frameworks for
teacher PD have acknowledged the existence of various factors which can influence the quality of
teachers’ PD experience (Clarke and Hollingsworth 2002, Merchie et al. 2018, Sancar et al. 2021).
For example, Merchie et al. (2018) extended the traditional evaluation frameworks for teacher PD
by incorporating the programme level aspects (i.e. core and structural features), macro- and microlevel contextual factors (e.g. policy environment, school supports), and teachers’ personal characteristics (e.g. basic demographics, professional features). They argued that it is necessary to
consider the potential moderating roles of these factors when evaluating the causal path from
teachers’ PD experience to students’ learning outcomes.
In their interconnected model of professional growth, Clarke and Hollingsworth (2002) suggested that teachers’ development as a result of participation in PD can be influenced by not only
their personal factors such as knowledge, beliefs, and attitude, but also external factors such as PD
programme characteristics and supports from schools and districts. Likewise, Sancar et al. (2021)
conducted an extensive review of recent literature regarding teacher PD and suggested the integrated framework to conceptualise teachers’ PD experience. They explained teachers’ PD experience as a continuing process from preservice teacher education to retirement. Teachers’ PD
experience is formed and influenced by multi-layer factors including educational policies and
reform efforts, school contexts, PD programmes’ topics and curriculum, and individual teachers’
professional characteristics (Sancar et al. 2021). In sum, these conceptual frameworks commonly
acknowledge the potential impacts of multi-layer factors (i.e. individual teacher characteristics,
features of PD experience, and school organisational environments) on teachers’ PD experience.
Next, we summarise findings from previous empirical studies that support the associations between
various factors from the multi-layers and teachers’ PD experience.
Individual teacher characteristics
Teachers’ various individual characteristics (e.g. socio-demographics, professional features, and
beliefs or attitudes as an educator) are known to be meaningfully linked to what and how teachers
experience throughout their PD (Opfer and Pedder 2011, Sancar et al. 2021). This is because
teachers as adult learners tend to organise their own courses of professional learning based on their
personal needs and expectations (Nir and Bogler 2008, Zepeda et al. 2014). Specifically, empirical
evidence from prior studies supports that teachers may differentially experience their PD based
upon their unique demographical characteristics, such as race, gender, and teaching experience
(Kwakman 2003, Anderson and Olsen 2006, Zhang et al. 2021, Yoon and Kim 2022). Additionally,
prior research shows that teachers’ PD experience is closely associated with their professional
features such as preservice teacher education experience (Tack et al. 2018, Guberman and
Mcdossi 2019), induction training experience (Hobson et al. 2009), and subject matters that they
currently teach in schools (Kwakman 2003).
Among various individual features of teachers, the impacts of teachers’ belief systems on
their PD experience have also been rigorously examined in the prior literature. Kwakman
(2003) found that teachers’ personal beliefs as educators (e.g. professional attitudes, emotion
exhaustion, appraisal of meaningfulness) were substantially associated with the positive
recall on their PD experience. Similarly, researchers have found that teachers’ perceptions

et al. 2019), perceived usefulness and control over PD experience (Méndez et al. 2017), and
stress (Meriläinen and Pietarinen 2007).
Features of professional development experience
Vast interest in maximising the effects of PD on teacher outcomes has been paid to identify
the optimal characteristics of PD programmes and activities. Specifically, researchers have
rigorously investigated the optimal PD structural and configural features that can enhance
teachers’ knowledge, beliefs, practices, and in turn, student learning outcomes (VillegasReimers 2003, Desimone 2009, Darling-Hammond et al. 2017). Based on these discussions,
there is a consensus in that effective PD activities must have content-focused curriculum,
collaborative participation with their co-workers, active learning environment, coherent
structures, and sustained duration (Desimone 2009, Darling-Hammond et al. 2017).
Additionally, prior studies also examined the potential influence of various features of PD
activities such as formats (e.g. face-to-face, online), topics (e.g. content knowledge, evaluation
practices), and perceived barriers (e.g. lack of relevance, lack of financial supports) on
teachers’ PD experiences (Badri et al. 2016, Zhang et al. 2020, Fang et al. 2021, Yoon and
Kim 2022, Liu and Du 2022).
School organizational environments
School contextual environments have been noted as important factors that can influence teachers’
PD experience (Merchie et al. 2018). Among various school-level aspects, the crucial roles of school
leaders (i.e. leadership and management practices) for promoting teachers’ PD are well examined in
previous studies (Nir and Bogler 2008, James and McCormick 2009). Especially, Bezzina (2006)
focused on the importance of constructive feedbacks provided by school leaders to enhance not
only teachers’ satisfaction with their PD, but also their impacts on various teacher outcomes.
Furthermore, various forms related to the school climate (also known as ‘school capacity’ or ‘school
culture’) are also noted as important contextual factors that affect teachers’ PD experience (Loxley
et al. 2007, Opfer and Pedder 2011, Postholm 2012). For example, prior empirical studies suggest
that collaboration among teachers can promote their active engagement in professional learning
activities by sharing common but challenging goals, which in turn, improves teachers’ uses of
ambitious instructional practices and student learning outcomes (de Jong et al. 2019, Admiraal et al.
2021).
LASSO-based machine learning approach
The extant teacher PD literature that adopted quantitative analytic approaches has mainly relied on
the ordinary least squares (OLS) regression-based techniques (e.g. stepwise regression, structural
equation modelling) (Yin and Huang 2021). However, clear limitations of OLS-based techniques lie
in their lack of generalisability as well as multicollinearity and overfitting issue when including
many predictors in a single model (Hastie et al. 2009, McNeish 2015). Given the complex and
multiplicative natures of teachers’ PD experience, it is crucial to take many factors into consideration simultaneously to identify the relative importance of predictors that lead to teachers’ positive
PD experience. By doing so, education leaders and policy-makers can set proper priorities to design
better supporting systems for teacher PD.
Indeed, research methodologists have suggested alternative techniques to overcome the limitations of OLS regression-based techniques. One of the alternatives is the least absolute shrinkage and
selection operator (LASSO) (Tibshirani 1996). LASSO, as a part of penalised regression methods, is
a supervised machine-learning statistical technique, which automatically selects only the important
predictors for classification or prediction by shrinking unnecessary predictors’ coefficients towards
zero. The basic formula for LASSO is presented below.

When the dependent variable y is estimated by predictor x, λ becomes the tuning parameter
which determines the shrinkage estimation. If the tuning parameter λ is large, most of coefficient
estimates tend to shrink towards zero, which means that it leaves only a limited number of
predictors in the model. In contrast, when the tuning parameter λ is close to zero, it yields similar
parameter estimates with OLS regression model without shrinking unnecessary predictors. Thus,
the estimation of the optimal value of the tuning parameter λ is critical in LASSO. To do that,
LASSO usually adopts cross-validation (CV) process (Hastie et al. 2009). In case of 10-fold CV,
which is the most common approach in studies adopting LASSO technique, the data is randomly
divided into 10 groups with the 90% of the data for building a predictive model (i.e. training data)
and 10% of the data for testing the model performance (i.e. test data). As a result, the 10-fold CV
process produces several model fit statistics (e.g. binomial deviance, mean squared error) to
evaluate the predictive performance depending on each of the tuning parameter λ values.
A binomial deviance is mainly used for the LASSO model with a binary dependent variable as an
evaluation metric for the cross-validated error (Hastie et al. 2009, McEligot et al. 2020). Thus, the
best value of the tuning parameter λ, which yields the minimum binomial deviance, can be
determined through the CV process. Overall, the LASSO-based machine learning technique allows
researchers to explore big datasets and reach the most parsimonious, optimised, and generalisable
predictions.
The utility value of LASSO-based machine learning technique has been widely noted in many
different fields such as statistics, psychology, and medical science, given its advantages including (1)
the affordance for inclusion of numerous variables in one model, (2) the provision of parsimonious
model result, which is good for model interpretability, and (3) the freedom from the firm theoretical
backgrounds to build a model (Fan et al. 2014, McEligot et al. 2020). Several recent studies that
adopted the LASSO technique have been started to be published in the field of education as well
(e.g. Justice et al. 2019, Polikoff and Silver 2021). However, to our knowledge, this study is the first
attempt to examine teacher PD experience with the LASSO technique.

Present study
Given the potential influences of various multi-layer factors (i.e. individual teacher characteristics,
features of PD experience, school organisational environments) on teachers’ PD experience, this
study was designed to identify which explanatory factors can (or cannot) predict teachers’ positive
perceptions about their PD experience. Specifically, by adopting the LASSO-based machine learning technique, we included 132 potential explanatory factors and investigated their relative importance in teachers’ perceptions on their PD experience. The guiding research question for this study
is presented below:
● Which factors from multiple layers (i.e. individual teacher characteristics, features of PD

experience, and school contextual environments) are significant in determining teachers’
positive perception of PD experience?

Methods
Data
In this study, we used 2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) U.S. teacher data,
which was administered by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development
(OECD). We determined to use the TALIS because this large-scale secondary dataset provides

a variety of information regarding teachers’ professional practices, learning experience, and school
environments (OECD 2019). Specifically, after dropping the cases of teachers who reported that
they did not participate in any PD activities during the past academic year (n = 142), we used the
survey reports from 2,418 teachers in 165 lower secondary-level schools. Demographically, the final
sample was composed of 1,634 female teachers (67.6%) and 1,497 teachers who had graduate-level
degree (61.9%). The average teaching experience was 13.96 years (SD = 9.27, range = 0–48) and the
average number of teachers in each school was approximately 16 (range = 2–22).

Dependent and explanatory variables
Based on PD evaluation models that commonly emphasised the importance of professionals’
positive reaction about their training experience (Guskey 2002, Kirkpatrick and Kirkpatrick
2016), we decided to use teachers’ perceptions regarding their PD experience as our main dependent variable. Specifically, we used a dichotomous item (‘TT3G25’ in 2018 TALIS) asking,
‘Thinking of all of your professional development activities during the last 12 months, did any of
these have a positive impact on your teaching practice?’ (no = 0, yes = 1). In our final sample, 2,126
teachers (87.9%) perceived their PD experience as positive, while 292 teachers (12.1%) did not
perceive their PD experience as positive.
For explanatory variables, we used 132 variables from 2018 TALIS data which consisted of 78
dichotomous variables (i.e. no = 0, yes = 1) and 54 continuous variables which were all standardised (i.e. mean = 0, SD = 1). Based on our review of the literature regarding the multi-layer
factors that can influence teachers’ PD experience (e.g. Opfer and Pedder 2011, Merchie et al.
2018, Sancar et al. 2021), we grouped these 132 explanatory variables into three layers: (1)
individual teacher characteristics, (2) features of PD experience, and (3) school organisational
environments. Table 1 displays the layers and categories of the 132 variables. A total of 72
variables were categorised into the first layer, individual teacher characteristics. These variables
include two socio-demographic variables (i.e. gender, graduate degree), 58 variables related to

Analytic approaches
The purpose of this study is to identify factors that predict teachers’ positive perceptions of their PD
experiences across a range of layers such as teacher individuals, PD programmes, and school
organisations. For this purpose, we utilised the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator
(LASSO) (Tibshirani 1996). As explained in the previous section, LASSO is a machine learningbased penalised regression method that automatically selects explanatory variables that are important for classification/prediction of the dependent variable by shrinking coefficients of unimportant
variables to zero. LASSO has the advantage of overcoming the overfitting and multicollinearity
issues of traditional approaches such as OLS regression to identify only important factors from
a large number of predictors in an integrated model (Tibshirani 1996, Hastie et al. 2009, McNeish
2015).
Before conducting the LASSO with one dichotomous dependent and 132 explanatory variables,
we transformed all continuous variables into Z-scores (i.e. mean = 0, SD = 1) to consider the high
sensitivity of variable scale in LASSO and to make the coefficients comparable (Hastie et al. 2009).
We handled variables with missing values at random by using EM algorithms. These preliminary
analytic processes were conducted with SPSS 28.
With the processed dataset, we used the LASSO-based machine learning technique with ‘glmnet’
package in R Statistical Software (version 4.1.3.). We identified a parsimonious model with variables
that demonstrated higher relative importance in classifying teachers who perceived their PD
experience as positive. To determine the optimal tuning parameter λ value for the final LASSO
model, we conducted the 10-fold CV procedure. We used the binomial deviance as a criterion to
select the best λ value given that our main dependent variable is a dichotomous variable. Basically,
the smaller value of binomial deviance indicates the better performance model. In the following
section, we summarised our main findings.

their PD experience if they had high workplace stress. Additionally, teachers tended to perceive
their PD experience positively when they reported high job satisfaction. The results also showed
that when teachers perceived that they were positively contributing to the community through their
teaching profession or when they believed that teachers were well valued from society, they tended
to perceive their PD experience more positively.
Features of PD experience had the most survived variables among the three layers: two formatrelated variables, five topic-related variables, one need-related variable, and two barrier-related

variables. Notably, predictors related to topics of PD activities (e.g. PD activities which were
designed to improve subject knowledge, individualised learning, and pedagogical competencies)
were strongly associated with teachers’ positive perception on their PD experiences. Specifically,
teachers were more likely to have a positive perception on their PD experience when they reported
that they participated in PD activities with formats of in-person course/seminar or worked in
a network of teachers. When it comes to topics, positive PD perception was strongly associated with
their participation in PD activities focusing on knowledge about subject matter or individualised
learning approaches. Teachers’ PD participation for pedagogical competencies, cross-curricular
skills, and other non-listed topics were also shown to play important roles in forming teachers’
positive perception on PD experience. Teachers who felt high needs in PD related to student
assessment practices tended to have positive perception on PD experience. Furthermore, teachers
tended to have less positive perceptions on their PD experience when they perceived irrelevance in
PD activities or when there was no incentive for PD participation.
There were two explanatory variables related to school organisational environments: positive
impact of feedback and informal induction training experience. Teachers’ positive evaluation
regarding the impact of feedback from colleagues and school leaders on their teaching practices
was the strongest predictor to explain teachers’ positive perception on their PD experience.
Teachers’ positive perception on their PD experience was also substantially linked to their experience of participating in informal induction training.

Discussion
Using a cutting-edge machine-learning analysis, we identified the 16 important explanatory variables out of 132 variables to explain how teachers differentially perceive their PD experience. These
‘survived’ variables were distributed across all three main layers, which we conceptualised as
individual teacher characteristics, features of PD experience, and school organisational environments. To our knowledge, this is the first attempt to examine teachers’ PD process by adopting
LASSO-based machine learning technique. Traditional approach largely focused on verifying
a hypothetical model with a limited number of researcher-selected variables based on existing
theories or research findings. Our approach is unique in that we considered all of the potential
variables from the dataset and captured data-driven association patterns to build a generalisable
prediction model. By doing so, we were able to maximise the potential of the secondary datasets
such as TALIS through utilising the full information that is readily available. Furthermore, we
provide new perspectives and insights that traditional theories or studies may have neglected,
enabling researchers to identify gaps (or new starting points) in the literature. In the following
section, we discuss the specific significance of our main findings.
Out of 16 survived explanatory variables, 10 of them were directly related to the key features of
PD experience. This is in line with previous literature supporting the fact that characteristics of PD
activities themselves are the most direct and powerful factors influencing teachers’ PD processes
(Villegas-Reimers 2003, Desimone 2009, Darling-Hammond et al. 2017). Specifically, our findings
demonstrated the importance of participating in face-to-face formatted PD activities such as inperson courses and teacher networks. In contrast, other formats of PD activities, especially online
PD activities, were not significantly related to teachers’ positive perception on their PD experience.
This implies that teachers still tended to perceive traditional format of in-person PD activities as
more useful compared to other formats of PD. To ensure genuine opportunities for in-person
interactions while also increasing accessibility, education leaders or policy-makers may consider
promoting resources and supports to activate school-based PD activities (Bredeson 2000, Cheng
2017). Moreover, despite the recent expansion of the access of online PD activities and programmes, our findings indicate that the mere provision of online PD opportunities may not
guarantee their effectiveness. Especially, given the recent situations (e.g. COVID-19 pandemic),
which requires pervasive virtual learning system for teacher PD (Means et al. 2013, Powell and

Bodur 2019), more studies are in need to understand how online PD activities can be effectively
designed and implemented.
We also found that teachers’ PD participation for better understanding of content knowledge is
a strong predictor of their positive perception on PD experience. This finding confirms previous
literature which commonly supports the importance of providing PD activities that are designed to
improve teachers’ content knowledge on subject areas (Garet et al. 2001, Desimone 2009, DarlingHammond et al. 2017). In addition to the focus on content knowledge, our findings indicate that
PD topics related to general teaching skills (such as individualised learning, pedagogical competences, and cross-curricular skills) can also be critical in promoting teachers’ positive evaluation on
their PD experience. This finding is in line with previous studies suggesting that teachers are
motivated to engage in PD activities to equip themselves on how to effectively convey content
knowledge to students (Borko 2004, Garet et al. 2016), taking a step further than gaining the content
knowledge itself. Notably, participating in PD activities related to implementation of individualised
learning in class was an especially strong predictor of positive evaluation of their PD. Recent studies
have started to note the value of incorporating individualised learning strategies into teaching
practices for better student learning and development as well (Knauder and Koschmieder 2019,
Zhang et al. 2020). In this sense, our study points to the need for increasing various PD programmes
and encouraging teachers to learn what individualised learning entails and how it can be incorporated into the classroom practices.
Teachers’ perceived barriers for PD participation negatively predicted their positive perceptions
on PD. For example, when teachers recognised a shortage of relevant PD opportunities, they tended
to have less favourable PD evaluation in general. Relatedly, although the effect size was small,
participating in PD topics that were not included in the provided list (i.e. selecting ‘others’) served
as one of the important explanatory variables in predicting positive PD perceptions. This may
indicate the importance of providing an array of PD topics that can satisfy teachers’ unique needs
and expectations, which in turn improves teacher and student outcomes (Nir and Bogler 2008,
Yoon and Kim 2022). While designing PD programmes, education leaders can consider increasing
teacher involvement and supporting active engagement between a network of teachers who share
similar interests (Boylan 2018, Imants and Van der Wal 2020). Another perceived barrier in PD
participation was the lack of proper incentives. Although research suggests that internal motivation
(e.g. desire to grow, improved self-efficacy beliefs) is more powerful than external motivation (e.g.
incentive pay, promotion) to encourage teachers’ engagement in PD (Knowles et al. 2014, Gregson
and Sturko 2007), we argue that offering incentives might be regarded as more critical for certain
teachers in particular situations. Further research can examine whether the effect of incentives
differs based on teachers’ goals, values, and backgrounds.
Now we turn to the role of individual teacher characteristics in predicting positive PD perceptions. Compared to features of PD experience, only four individual teacher characteristics survived
in the final LASSO model. We found that teachers’ personal beliefs regarding their well-being (e.g.
workplace stress and job satisfaction) matter for determining their PD experience. This finding
provides additional justification for supporting teachers’ well-being (Prilleltensky et al. 2016,
Banerjee et al. 2017). Considering that most of the previous studies mainly investigated the link
from teachers’ PD participation to their beliefs as teachers, our findings point to the need to
investigate the potential reverse direction.
Additionally, our findings indicate that teachers’ desires to make meaningful contributions to
the society (e.g. developing children and youth, benefiting the socially disadvantaged) are strong
predictors of PD perceptions. Encouraging and maintaining teachers’ perceived values in their
profession can promote their continued efforts for improving their expertise as educators.
Relatedly, considering that value and policy influence was another survived explanatory variable
in the final model, it is critical for the society to acknowledge and respect teachers’ efforts and their
contributions. School leaders and policy-makers should cultivate respectful cultures and listen to
teachers’ voices and incorporate their ideas into the decision-making processes.

In addition to PD features and individual teacher characteristics, school organisational
environments can also shape teachers’ PD perceptions. Notably, the strongest explanatory
variable in terms of positive PD perception was the impact of feedback from school members
on their teaching practices. Abundant prior studies have emphasised the crucial role of feedback
on improving teaching practices and students’ learning and development (Bezzina 2006,
Tuytens and Devos 2017). Our study adds to the literature by pointing out that receiving highquality feedback that is readily applicable to the classroom practices can also strongly impact
their PD processes. Given that teachers receive various types of feedbacks from school leaders
and colleagues (e.g. observation, discussions, peer review), more attention should be given to
understanding what the high-quality feedback consists of and how it can be conveyed to
maximise their impacts.
Our findings also showed that taking part in informal induction activities was associated with
positive PD perceptions. Teachers who received informal supports from their colleagues (rather
than through formal/structured training sessions) during their early career stage (i.e. first 3 years)
were more likely to be satisfied with their PD experience. Despite the extant literature which
focused on the importance of providing formal induction training to facilitate teachers’ PD
processes (Hobson et al. 2009), our study highlights that informal induction experience can be
also important for teachers, especially in their early years. School leaders can facilitate teachers’
informal induction experiences by building a collaborative school climate where senior teachers (as
mentors) can easily interact with novice teachers (Goddard et al. 2015, Holzberger and SchiepeTiska 2021). In this sense, we suggest taking a broader perspective and considering both formal and
informal induction training opportunities for teachers to promote their active participation in PD
throughout their careers.
Limitations and future directions
Several limitations can be noted to guide future research. First, although we included more than 100
variables and used a LASSO-based machine learning technique to explore meaningful predictors of
PD perceptions, the dataset only consisted of self-report responses. Triangulating with objective,
behavioural information (e.g. administrative data, observations) may provide further support to the
current findings. Although machine learning approaches (including LASSO) can be a powerful tool
to identify the gaps in the literature by adopting a data-driven exploration (bottom-up approach),
cautions need to be taken when interpreting the findings. Theory-driven investigation (top-down
approach) should be accompanied to understand the underlying mechanisms of the findings and
further improving the theoretical foundations. Second, TALIS dataset does not have enough
teachers’ individual and school level demographic information. Even though we did not find any
links between teachers’ socio-demographics and their PD perceptions, it would not be appropriate
to draw a general conclusion that teachers with different backgrounds experience PD similarly.
Third, this study focused on examining the foundational level of PD evaluation; that is, we
specifically examined the antecedents of teachers’ perceptions of their PD experience. Future
studies can expand our findings by exploring and including the subsequent levels of PD evaluation
such as teachers’ knowledge, practice, and student outcomes. Fourth, this study is based on the
U.S. context and thus, it needs to be expanded to other nations and/or regions before generalisation.
Finally, it is reasonable to expect that there can be meaningful interactions among the survived
factors from the three layers (i.e. individual teacher, PD activities, and school organisation). Thus,
future researchers can consider exploring how these interactions between the influencing factors
can moderate each other’s influences on teachers’ perceptions on their PD experiences.
