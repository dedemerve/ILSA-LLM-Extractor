What are the Most Important Factors Influencing Science

and Finish PISA Data

Abstract
Enhancing science achievement is the fundamental goal of science education.
Although past studies have identified different factors influencing science
achievement, there is still a lack of research to reveal the similarities and differences
in the most significant factors influencing students from different cultures. This study
adopted a machine learning approach (i.e., random forest regression) to analyse
data from Singapore and Finland in PISA 2015 and identified ten top important
factors influencing Singaporean and Finnish student science performance. The
main findings indicate that (i) learning time, justification in epistemological beliefs,
enjoyment, self-efficacy, and Information and Communications Technology (ICT)
use or availability are the top factors of science achievement of both Singaporean
and Finnish students; (ii) while three additional individual factors (i.e., expected
occupational status, test anxiety and student value cooperation) are among the top
factors influencing Finnish students’ achievement, three family factors (i.e., family
wealth, highest parental occupational status, and home educational resources) are
found as top factors influencing Singaporean students’ achievement; at (iii) at the
school level, teacher fairness is the strongest factor influencing of Finnish students’
achievement while the disciplinary climate is the counterpart for Singaporean
students. These shared top factors provide a foundation for collaboration between
educators and policymakers across cultures, fostering the development of global
best practices in science education. Its findings on the context-specific top factors
also highlight the need for tailored educational strategies that cater for the crosscultural difference in science education.

Introduction
Science education is a comprehensive and complex process and student science
performance can be influenced by a huge number of different factors in the process of learning and teaching occurring inside and beyond the classroom. The
student learning process is characterized by mutually influential relationships
between individuals and their contexts, emphasizing how children’s learning is
shaped by the interactions among environmental factors, relationships, and learning opportunities, both inside and outside the classroom (Darling-Hammond
et al., 2020). Considerable efforts have been exerted on uncovering the determinants of student science performance (Karadag, 2017). Existing studies have
summarized the factors interpreting learning science into three categories-the
individuals, familial context, and educational setting (Xie et al., 2015). However,
previous studies often focus on a limited set of factors in their analyses (e.g.,
Areepattamannil, 2012; Areepattamannil & Kaur, 2013; Lam & Lau, 2014; Lau
& Ho, 2022; She et al., 2019) to the constraints of classical statistical methods,
specifically Hierarchical Linear Modeling (HLM) (Wang et al., 2023). This has
resulted in an incomplete understanding of the major factors that contribute to
academic success in science. To fill this gap, this study employed the machine
learning method (which can avoid the multicollinearity which emerges when
including a large number of highly correlated variables in the traditional statistical model) to utilize PISA 2015 data to holistically examine the major factors
influencing school students’ science performance. Choosing PISA data as the target of analysis is because it is one of the most comprehensive global educational
assessments and offers us a series of fruitful variables at different levels related to
student success.
Science education is intertwined with culture, which acts as a structure and
mechanism to guide research and policies aimed at tackling the complexities of
science education (Madkins & McKinney de Royston, 2019; Parsons & Carlone,
2013). The science culture, school science culture, science classroom culture, and
the various individual cultures within the science education process among the
many ways in which culture has been considered in the science education literature (Parsons, and Carlone, 2013). Comparative studies across diverse cultures
are needed to identify commonalities and disparities in factors affecting students’
science performance. Such empirical evidence will yield valuable insights to
enhance science learning theories and offer more specific guidance for science
teachers and policymakers. In this study, we focused on Singapore and Finland,
representing East Asian and Scandinavian cultures, respectively. Both Finland
and Singapore exhibit exceptional results in the PISA, positioning them among
the highest performers in the PISA science rankings (i.e., Singapore as the 1st
and Finland as the 5th) (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2016). In addition, they are both small-sized and developed
economies. Comparing these two comparable countries from two different cultures can help to reveal the similarities and differences in the most significant factors influencing student science performance from different cultures. Moreover,

In this study, three main research questions were addressed: (1) What are the key

Literature Review
Theoretical Frameworks for Categorizing the Factors Influencing Student
Achievement
Efforts have been long to put to seek to improve students’ science performance in
science education. To identify the productive factors which are powerfully linked
consistently with student outcomes, Xie et al. (2015) systematically synthesized
research on STEM education and then framed the determinants of achievement in
STEM education into three types of factors, including individual-level factors, family influences, and contextual factors, from psychological and sociological perspectives (Fig. 1). Subsequent studies in science education based on Xie et al. (2015)
have supported those factors are potent, consistent, and widely generalizable across
cultures (e.g., Ghana, Ansong et al., 2020; Germany, Gutfleisch & Kogan, 2022;
Hong Kong, Tan, 2024; Israel, Diamond, 2020; Vietnam, Ho et al., 2020). In this
study, we categorized different types of factors in influencing student science performance into individual, family-related, and social-contextual factors. Considering the

Individual factors include student characteristics and their motivation towards
science. Student characteristics such as gender and ability are related to students’ science
performance. For example, prior evidence has shown that male students outperformed
female students in science achievement (Bell, 2001; Ganley et al., 2014; Kang &
Keinonen, 2018; Tan, 2024). In science education, attitudes can involve subdomains
such as enjoyment of science, interest in science, valuing of science, or the motivation to
learn science (Darling-Hammond et al., 2020; Grabau et al., 2024; Kang, 2023; Smith,
et al., 2022). Previous evidence has shown that students with a high level of motivation
and positive dispositional attitudes are more likely to perform better in science (e.g.,
Ma, 2023). Other motivational factors such as test anxiety (Putwain et al., 2010),
team enjoyment (Govorova et al., 2020), the value of cooperative learning (Mueller &
Fleming, 2001), and epistemic beliefs (Guo et al., 2022; Karakolidis et al., 2019; She
et al., 2019) are also significantly associated with science performance among primary
and secondary school students. In addition, there have been a number of studies that
investigated the impact of ICT use and ICT-related motivations on student science
performance (e.g., Areepattamannil & Kaur, 2013; Hu et al., 2018; Petko et al., 2017).
Family-related factors also play a critical role in influencing student science
performance. Socioeconomic status (SES), an index of an individual’s overall
social status or prestige in social class, is always strongly associated with students’
science performance, which has been well-documented in several synthesised
reviews in science education (e.g., Marks & O’Connell, 2021; Xie et al., 2015).
Other family-related factors such as parental educational level (e.g., Huang & Liang,
2016), immigration status (e.g., Areepattamannil, 2012; Kang & Keinonen, 2018),
occupational status (e.g., Gutfleisch & Kogan, 2022), and family resources (e.g.,
Areepattamannil & Kaur, 2013) are found to be associated with student science
performance in previous studies based on analysis of PISA data.
Social-contextual factors in determining student science performance have largely
focused on two overlapping and nested contexts: class and school (Xie et al., 2015).
The burgeoning literature pertaining to teaching practices in science classes highlights
that the instructions applied by science teachers can promote student science learning.
Several different forms of teaching practices have been mainly discussed in science
education, which are inquiry-based learning (Cobern et al., 2014; Fang, 2021; Lau &
Ho, 2022; She et al., 2019), direct teaching (Cairns & Areepattamannil, 2022; Cobern
et al., 2014; Tan, 2024), perceived feedback (Lau & Ho, 2022; Tan, 2024), adaptive
instruction (Lau & Ho, 2022), and teacher support (Ansong et al., 2020; Huang &
Liang, 2016). Besides the instructional process adopted in science class, classroom
management practices are essential for creating an effective learning environment that
maximizes student learning time and minimizes disruptions (Baumert et al., 2010).
The Context of Finland and Singapore
Finland is renowned for its superior education system. The Finnish education system
is structured to provide equitable access to education, thereby promoting educational

opportunities for all individuals (Halinen, 2018). The schools in Finland were highly
praised by OECD for advocating for serving all students effectively, irrespective
of their family background, socioeconomic status, or academic abilities (Morgan,
2014). Finland has implemented a decentralized educational policy involving two
levels of curricula, namely the national core curriculum and the local or municipal
school-level curriculum (Lavonen & Juuti, 2016). The national core curriculum
serves as a guideline for teaching and encompasses general aims, objectives and
core contents of different school subjects, while the local curriculum takes into
account the local context and needs of schools and municipalities (Soini et al.,
2022). Therefore, Finnish teachers enjoy a high level of autonomy to organize their
classes, and select appropriate content and teaching materials (Lavonen, 2021). As a
part of the Finnish Core Curriculum for Basic Education (Finnish National Board of
Education, 2014), the lower-secondary science curriculum is composed of separate
biology, physics, and chemistry curricula, which are taught by a secondary teacher
with expertise in two subjects, such as mathematics and physics, mathematics
and chemistry, biology and geography, or biology and chemistry (Lavonen, 2021;
Lavonen & Juuti, 2016). The Finnish lower-secondary level science curriculum
places significant emphasis on the application of scientific knowledge in diverse
contexts (Soini et al., 2022). The Finnish core curriculum is designed to foster and
reproduce constructive scientific learning activities that equip students with the
capacity to engage and persevere when faced with similar experiences. In addition,
the provision of enhanced learning opportunities to students during pivotal moments
also serves to enhance their predisposition towards seeking analogous scientific
learning experiences (Schneider et al., 2016).
Similar to Finland, Singapore is also widely recognized for the remarkable performance of its students in international assessments such as PISA and TIMSS. Education has played a crucial role in Singapore’s economic prosperity since it is a young
and small nation with limited resources (Tan et al., 2016; Yeo & Tan, 2021). The
introduction of the twenty-first-century competencies framework by the Ministry of
Education (Ministry of Education Singapore, 2014) aimed to outline the direction
of education for the future and the essential characteristics necessary for individuals
to make contributions to a constantly evolving world. Within this curriculum framework, science education serves as a foundation for students to acquire not only basic
scientific knowledge but also to foster a spirit of inquisitiveness, integrity, perseverance, and concern for the environment and others through an inquiry-based approach
(Yeo & Tan, 2021). The Singaporean science curriculum is built upon the “science
as an inquiry” framework, which incorporates the domains of “Knowledge, Understanding and Application, Skills and Processes, and Ethics and Attitudes” (Ministry
of Education Singapore, 2012, p. 2; Tan et al., 2016). Although both teachers and
students are involved in the inquiry process, classroom discussions tend to be teacherdominated (Yeasmin & Uusiautti, 2018), with students acting as inquirers who use
appropriate questioning, experimentation, data analysis, and communication to solve
problems and defend their findings (Tan et al., 2016).
In conclusion, Finland and Singapore both demonstrate exceptional educational systems, yet they exhibit distinct approaches reflective of their unique contexts and priorities. Finland emphasizes equitable access and teacher autonomy,

with a decentralized curriculum that incorporates national guidelines and local
adaptations. The Finnish science curriculum prioritizes the application of scientific knowledge and fostering constructive scientific learning experiences. In

curriculum centers on the “science as an inquiry” framework, aiming to instill

highlight the ways in which cultural, economic, and policy contexts shape educational philosophies and practices in each country.

Methods
Samples
The present study used data from the PISA 2015 dataset, which focused on
science as a major assessment area. PISA 2015 focused on assessing the
science-related performance of 15-year-olds and collecting their demographic
information, family information, and school characteristics (OECD, 2017). The
sample from Singapore consisted of 6,115 students, and those from Finland
included 5,882 students. Among them, 3,142 (51.4%) students were boys and
2,973 (48.6%) students were girls in Singapore, while 3,019 (51.3%) students
were boys, and 2,863 (48.7%) students were girls in Finland.

Variables
Dependent variable In PISA 2015, ten plausible values (P.V.s) were estimated
for each student’s science performance, which refers to “the ability to engage
with science-related issues, and with the ideas of science, as a reflective citizen”
(OECD, 2016, p. 22). The PISA science assessment highlights the importance of
applying scientific knowledge in real life for 15-year-old students. The P.V.s were
standardized with a mean of 500 points and a standard deviation of 100. We used
all 10 P.V.s for science as the dependent variable in our analysis.
Independent variable The independent variables used in this study were chosen
from the PISA 2015 student questionnaire. PISA derived a range of sciencerelated variables using the item response theory (IRT) scaling method. Weighted
likelihood estimates (WLEs) for these derived variables were used as individual
participant scores and transformed into scales with a mean of zero and a standard
deviation of one, with equal weighting for each country/economies (OECD,
2017). A total of 39 variables, including contextual factors, family influences,
and individual-level factors, were selected. In addition, all the scales showed
acceptable reliabilities (see OECD, 2017). The description of all of the constructs
is shown in Table S1.

Data Analysis
In recent years, Machine Learning (ML) methods have attracted increasing interest
in education research, which have been widely used to improve evidence-based decision-making in commerce, medical science, science, agriculture, biology, and engineering (Luan & Tsai, 2021). Notably, these methods, particularly Random Forest
Regression, have been increasingly applied in STEM education to identify key variables of student performance in mathematics and science using international largescale datasets (e.g., Chen et al., 2021; Hong et al., 2022; Shin & Shim, 2021; Tan
et al., 2024). ML is a subset of artificial intelligence ‘that focuses on developing automated computer systems that predict outputs through algorithms and mathematics
(Chang et al., 2020, p. 2). Compared with classical statistics, machine learning finds
generalizable predictive patterns from a sample (Bzdok et al., 2018). The purpose of
ML is to obtain the best performance and improve prediction accuracy on the test set.
ML has lots of advantages over classical statistics. For example, ML makes minimal
assumptions about the data generation system; that is, they can be valid even if data
are collected without a carefully controlled experimental design and in the presence
of complex nonlinear interactions (Jacobucci & Grimm, 2020). In addition, when
many variables are feasibly correlated, ML outperforms better than classical statistics, such as logistic regression, in terms of success and prediction rates (Yi & Na,
2020). K-fold cross-validation in ML can help against overfitting during the mode
estimation (Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017).
Random Forest (RF) regression, one of the widely used machine learning
approaches, is employed in this study to determine the importance of a bunch of variables in the model influencing the dependent variable while preventing overfitting
and ensuring accuracy (Biau & Scornet, 2016; Breiman, 2001). To solve the overfitting problem, the random forest algorithm is introduced, which produces more generalized and flexible predictions by incorporating randomness into the construction
of decision trees (Breiman, 2001). It extracts information about the contribution of
each variable and provides variable-importance plots, showing the variables that
contributed the most to the outcome variable.
Several indices are important to evaluate the accuracy of the RF model. The rootmean-square error (RMSE) and explanation of variance ­(R2) showed the degree of
model fit and model accuracy (Chicco et al., 2021). Variables of high importance are
drivers of the outcome, and their values have a significant impact on the outcome
values (Segal, 2004). The most robust and informative measure of importance
given for each variable in the RF model is the percentage increase in mean square
error (%IncMSE), which is based on how much the accuracy decreases when the
variable is excluded. %IncMSE is calculated by shuffling the values of the out-ofbag samples. Higher values of %IncMSE mean that the variable is more important.
Since not all variables included in the full RF models could contribute to the model
accuracy, ten-fold cross-validation with five repeats was applied to reveal the most
important branch of variables of science performance. The cross-validation error
curve will stabilize when the most relevant variables are included (Grömping, 2009).
All data analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 and the R program. Missing data were handled through Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) multiple

imputations. Five imputation datasets were created by using SPSS 25.0. Then we
used the “randomForest” package (Liaw & Wiener, 2002) in R to build a random
forest model with 500 decision trees and 39 feature variables by using those five
imputation datasets, respectively. At each node, 13 variables (one-third of the feature variables) are randomly chosen to determine the best split to generate the decision trees (Breiman, 2001). The ten-fold cross-validation with five repeats was performed by using the rfcv() function in the randomForest R package. The results of
RF models from five imputation datasets were similar, indicating that our data analysis results are robust.

Results
Descriptive and Correlational Analyses
The descriptive statistics of the variables used in this study and bivariate correlations
between variables and science performance were shown in Tables 1 and S1,
respectively. The average science score of Singaporean students (M = 546.679) is
slightly higher than that of Finnish students (M = 531.572).
In terms of the bivariate correlations, we found that several factors had
moderate (r > 0.3, Ratner, 2009) correlations with students’ science performance.
In Singapore, six factors consisting of enjoyment of science (r = 0.348), science
self-efficacy (r = 0.313), highest parental occupational status (r = 0.352), home
educational resources (r = 0.320), family wealth (r = 0.318), and justification
in epistemological beliefs (r = 0.327) have moderate correlations with science
performance. In addition, six factors consisting of enjoyment of science (r = 0.345),
science self-efficacy (r = 0.306), students’ expected occupational status (r = 0.389),
highest parental occupational status (r = 0.304), and both development (r = 0.312)
and justification (r = 0.418) in epistemological beliefs were moderately correlated
with Finnish students’ science performance.

factors that influence Singaporean and Finnish students’ science performance. The

Discussion and Conclusion
This study aims to shed light on the factors that contribute to science achievement by
comparing Singapore and Finland, two high-performing countries, on various international assessments. To achieve this, a machine learning approach is employed to
provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relative importance of different

3346

Z. H. Wan et al.

factors. By comparing these two countries, we identified some similarities and differences in the factors that influence excellent science achievement in the East and West.
The following section will discuss these similarities and differences in more detail.
Similarity in the Top Factors Influencing Science Achievement of Singaporean
and Finnish Secondary Students
The role of motivational factors, such as self-efficacy and enjoyment, in student
science performance has been extensively investigated. This study, similar to
existing evidence from meta-analyses (e.g., Mao et al., 2021) and empirical studies
based on PISA or TIMSS data (e.g., Cohen & Chang, 2020; Lam & Lau, 2014),
found that science self-efficacy and enjoyment were strong factors of science
performance for both Singaporean and Finnish students. This finding suggests that
motivational factors play a significant role in student science learning across Eastern
Asian and Western cultures.
In addition, this study found that science learning time was important
determinant of science performance for Singaporean and Finnish students. Adequate
learning time ensures that students have the opportunity to engage in science
practical experiences, which are essential for understanding scientific concepts and
knowledge (She et al., 2019). Specifically, as for the most important factors among
all factors we examined in this study, student learning time is the most important
factor for science performance in Singapore. Our descriptive analysis revealed
that students in Singapore devote a significantly longer amount of time to learning
science each week, with a considerable degree of variation (Mean = 300.91 min/
week, S.D. = 173.02). The disparity in time allocated to science learning among
students in Singapore is noteworthy and likely contributes substantially to the
variation observed in students’ science performance. Consequently, the duration of
learning time plays a crucial role in influencing Singaporean student performance in
science.
The important role of epistemological beliefs in students’science learning has
received little attention in previous West–East comparative studies (e.g., Chai et al.,
2021; Lau & Ho, 2022). Unlike these studies, the present study found that justification
in epistemological beliefs was an important determinant of science performance for
both Singaporean and Finnish students. Especially for Finnish students, this factor
is the most influential factor of their science performance. Justification of epistemic
beliefs plays a key role in the development of students’thinking, reasoning, and
science performance (Greene et al., 2018). Students who hold stronger justifications
of knowledge are more likely to view knowledge as tentative and justified through
reasoning and firsthand experience (Bråten et al., 2013). Through questioning and
reasoning, individuals develop the ability to analyze information, identify logical
fallacies, and make informed judgments (Halpern & Dunn, 2022). Furthermore, the
belief that scientific knowledge is justified by empirical evidence obtained through
experimentation encourages students to put more effort into science experiments
and contributes to their scientific performance (Guo et al., 2022). Students with
strong justification beliefs tend to adopt deep learning strategies, which are

What are the Most Important Factors Influencing Science…

3347

characterized by a focus on understanding and integrating information rather than
surface-level memorization (Chai et al., 2021). As a result, they are better equipped
to comprehend complex scientific concepts and apply them effectively in different
contexts. Thus, efforts to improve students’justification of epistemological beliefs
should be encouraged and emphasized in science education for both Western and
Eastern students.
Although there are many existing studies on the effects of ICT-related factors on
student science performance in cross-cultural comparative studies (e.g., Hu et al.,
2018; Petko et al., 2017), most of them did not examine the relative importance of
different ICT-related factors on student science performance. However, the current
study found that ICT-related factors at both the individual and school context
levels have important effects on student science performance in both Singapore and
Finland. As individuals, students’positive social interactions increase their intrinsic
motivation to challenge themselves through the use of self-directed technology,
which is conducive to better academic performance (Goldhammer et al., 2017). In
addition, ICT tools such as simulations, animations, and virtual reality environments
help visualize abstract scientific concepts and phenomena. These visual aids
enhance students’ conceptual understanding by providing dynamic representations
and interactive experiences (Smetana & Bell, 2012). For example, the use of ICT for
schoolwork is noteworthy to help students complete their assignments efficiently and
effectively (Courtney et al., 2022). In terms of ICT use in school context, it enables
collaboration between students and science teachers across time boundaries. Online
communication tools, collaborative platforms, and social media networks facilitate
the exchange of ideas, discussion of scientific concepts, and work on collaborative
projects, thus improving their science performance (Giannakos, 2020).
Difference in the Top Factors Influencing Science Achievement Between
Singaporean and Finnish Secondary Students
Our results showed that none of the top ten important factors influencing Finnish
students’ science achievement could be categorized as family related factors, while
three of the top ten important factors, including family wealth, highest parental occupational status, and home educational resources, were found to be family-related factors of Singapore students’ science achievement. Such a difference indicates that the
family seems to be remarkable for the science learning of students in Singapore.
This phenomenon may be attributed to the variation in educational investment from
families toward students in East Asian societies. Influenced by Confucian culture,
parents in East Asia, such as China, South Korea, and Singapore, prioritize their
children’s education more than their counterparts in the West (Zhang, 2021a). Parents in these societies feel a strong sense of duty and responsibility to provide the
best possible educational opportunities for their children (Lau & Ho, 2022; Yamamoto & Brinton, 2010). However, when compared to Finland, Singapore exhibits
a notable disparity in economic income among families from diverse backgrounds,
leading to differing levels of educational investment (Ustun, 2022). Students from
advantaged families typically benefit from more high-quality educational resources

3348

Z. H. Wan et al.

provided by their parents, whereas those from disadvantaged families often lack
such resources (Zhang, 2021b). This indicates that student learning outcomes in Singapore are vulnerable to influence by a combination of factors originating from their
home.
The current study found that teacher fairness was an important determinant of
performance in science in Finland. However, this factor was not so relatively important for Singaporean students. This disparity may be explained by that students from
diverse culture may interpret and respond differently to the same teacher behavior
(Jiang et al., 2021). Western societies place significant importance on nurturing
individual thinking and interests while recognizing and appreciating individual differences. In such environments, teachers actively support student autonomy, freedom, and the ability to make choices (Hofstede et al., 2010). Teachers should regard
each student as a unique individual and maintaining friendly communication with
students, and students are valued for their autonomy and independence in learning
(Jiang et al., 2021). Western students are more likely to be concerned about the fairness of teachers’ behavior and interference with their freedom, independence and
autonomy in learning (Chan & Rao, 2010). If they perceive their teachers to be
unfair or more inferential, their learning efficacy will be reduced, resulting in poorer
learning outcomes.
Notably, we also found that for Singaporean students, the disciplinary climate
in science class was strongly associated with science performance. This finding is
consistent with previous studies that have focused on comparisons between East
Asian and Western students (e.g., Guo et al., 2018; Tan, 2024). This phenomenon
may be attributed to the large class sizes prevalent in East Asia. For instance, while
the average class size for lower secondary education in Finland was 17 students
(The Finnish Education Evaluation Centre (FINEEC), 2021), the mean class size
for secondary schools in Singapore was 36 (Ministry of Education Singapore,
2022). A large class size can present numerous disciplinary challenges during
teaching sessions. Teachers may find themselves frequently diverting their attention
to manage such large class, leading to inefficiencies in teaching and difficulties
in organizing orderly hands-on activities in science classes (Doğan et al., 2023).
Consequently, these challenges can have an adverse impact on student learning
performance in Singapore.
In addition, test anxiety was found to be a strong factor for Finnish students, but
not for Singaporean students. This may be due to the different examination culture
between West and East Asia. East Asia has a longstanding tradition of examinations spanning nearly two thousand years. This tradition has shaped education in
East Asian countries like Singapore and China, where centralized, high-stakes testing determines students’academic pathways amidst intense academic pressure (Kell
et al., 2014). Consequently, Singaporean students, for example, may be more accustomed to the rigors of examinations. In contrast, Western countries such as Finland
tend to rely less on standardized testing, instead placing greater emphasis on formative assessment and individualized learning (Simola, 2005). Consequently, Finnish
students may experience more test anxiety due to the infrequent use of high-stakes
assessments. Therefore, when faced with large-scale standardized assessments like
PISA, students in Finland might be more susceptible to the influence of test anxiety.

Limitations and Future Directions
There are still some limitations that should be addressed. First, this study investigated only two countries—Singapore and Finland—both of which were top performers in science literacy according to the PISA 2015 results. While these countries are notable for their high rankings, the limited scope of this study restricts the
ability to generalize its findings to other countries or cultural contexts. The educational systems and student populations in Singapore and Finland may be unique
in ways that do not reflect broader global trends, meaning that the implications of
these findings may not apply universally. Future research should aim to include a
more diverse sample of countries to explore whether similar variables of student
science performance exist in other cultural or socio-economic contexts. Second,
the study employs a correlational design, which allows for the identification of
relationships between variables but does not establish causality. Although machine
learning methods were utilized to identify significant variables of science performance, these methods cannot infer causal relationships between variables and outcomes. Third, the data used in this study are not truly longitudinal, which makes
it difficult to assess the directionality or the reciprocal nature of the relationships
between variables and student performance.

Implications of the Findings
Based on the above discussion, motivational factors were found to be the
commonalities for both Finnish and Singaporean, and some differences embedded
in social and culture background were also found. These comparisons between
the East Asian and Western cultures discussed above offers valuable insights into
the factors influencing science achievement, shedding light on potential avenues
for improving science teaching and learning.
Undoubtedly, sufficient learning time in science is necessary for students to
gain understanding of science content and knowledge. Our findings align with
previous research, which indicates that East Asian students generally dedicate
more time to academic pursuits compared to their Western counterparts (Zhang
et al., 2021). While this greater time investment is a significant factor, it’s essential to consider the quality of learning time alongside quantity. It’s important to
recognize that students in some East Asian countries already spend a considerable
amount of time on science, both in and out of school. Simply increasing learning
time could potentially overwhelm students and limit their opportunities for meaningful science learning. Research emphasizes the importance of high-quality
learning experiences, such as hands-on experiments, interactive simulations, and
real-world applications, in fostering student interest and comprehension (Hofstein
& Lunetta, 2004). Therefore, instead of solely focusing on increasing learning
time, it’s crucial to prioritize strategies that enhance the quality and effectiveness
of science learning experiences for all students, including those in East Asia.

This study highlights the significant role of appropriate use of ICT both
in and out of school in science learning in both Singapore and Finland. In the
digital age, ICT plays a pivotal role in transforming educational practices,
offering innovative approaches to teaching and learning science for both Eastern
and Western students. ICT provides access to interactive multimedia resources
that engage students in active learning processes. By utilizing digital tools
and resources, educators can create dynamic learning experiences, facilitate
collaborative inquiry, and provide students with access to a wealth of scientific
information and simulations. For instance, integrating multimedia presentations,
virtual simulations, and online laboratories can enhance student engagement and
deepen their understanding of scientific concepts (Maharaj-Sharma et al., 2017).
In addition, with the rapid advancement of Artificial Intelligence (AI) technology,
it is crucial to investigate how AI can be effectively integrated into science
classrooms to further enhance students’ learning experience.
Furthermore, this study underscores the critical role of familial factors play
in shaping educational outcomes, particularly in the context of science achievement among students from different backgrounds. The stark contrast between the
influence of family-related factors in various Eastern cultures and the relatively
lesser impact of such factors in Western contexts highlights how cultural values
and economic disparities within families can significantly affect students’learning
environments and resources. In many East Asian societies, influenced by Confucious, educational attainment is often viewed as a reflection of familial duty, where
parents prioritize investing time and resources in their children’s education (Lau
& Ho 2022). This cultural perspective can lead to significant inequalities in educational opportunities based on socioeconomic status, as families with greater wealth
can provide enhanced educational resources, thus widening the achievement gap
between students from different backgrounds (Ustun, 2022). Addressing these inequalities and ensuring equitable access to quality education for all students, regardless of their socioeconomic background, is a critical challenge in such societies.
The study found that teacher fairness and test anxiety significantly impacted
the academic performance of Finnish students, while the disciplinary climate was
strongly associated with the science performance of Singaporean students. These
findings suggest that students from different cultural backgrounds perceive and
respond to teaching practices differently. In many Western societies, where individual autonomy and student-centered learning are emphasized, perceptions of
fairness in teacher behaviour significantly influence student motivation and performance (Hofstede et al., 2010). In contrast, in Eastern cultures, where discipline and respect for authority are highly valued, students may respond differently
to classroom management strategies, reflecting broader societal values (Doğan
et al., 2023). These findings underscore the importance of cultural sensitivity and
awareness in designing and implementing effective science education programs.
Educators teaching in East Asian contexts should prioritize establishing a wellorganized and supportive learning environment while fostering student engagement
and collaboration. In Western contexts, cultivating a sense of fairness and respect
in teacher-student interactions is crucial for creating an inclusive and supportive
learning environment.

Moreover, addressing test anxiety is critical, particularly in Western educational
settings. Teachers should apply reasonable strategies to help students alleviate this
anxiety, such as teaching effective test-taking techniques, including time management, question interpretation, and problem-solving strategies. Providing opportunities for practice and feedback can also help students build confidence in their testtaking abilities, thereby enhancing their overall academic performance. Additionally,
educational systems should adapt their assessment practices to align with cultural
expectations and student experiences. While students in Western contexts may
benefit from reduced test anxiety through formative assessments, those in Eastern
cultures might thrive under a system that acknowledges their familiarity with highstakes testing (Simola, 2005). Policymakers should consider implementing diverse
assessment methods, such as project-based assessments that encourage collaboration and creativity, which can cater to both Eastern and Western students. Moreover,
creating environments that reduce the pressure associated with standardized tests
can help mitigate anxiety. For example, schools could introduce regular, low-stakes
assessments that provide students with opportunities to demonstrate their understanding without the high stakes typically associated with traditional examinations.
The implications of these findings extend to global science education,
emphasizing the necessity for culturally responsive teaching practices that recognize
and accommodate these differences. Eastern cultures often emphasize collectivism,
respect for authority, and the importance of family involvement in education, while
Western cultures tend to value individualism, personal autonomy, and self-directed
learning (Hofstede et al., 2010). Educators worldwide should be aware of how these
cultural contexts influence student perceptions, motivation, and achievement. To
promote equity and access, particularly for disadvantaged students, strategies such
as incorporating culturally relevant pedagogy, providing professional development
focused on cultural competence for teachers, and fostering community partnerships
can be critical in creating inclusive learning environments. For instance, schools can
implement workshops for educators on the importance of cultural sensitivity and the
diverse backgrounds of their students, thereby enhancing their ability to engage with
all learners effectively.
Overall, the insights from this study advocate for a nuanced understanding of how
cultural factors influence educational outcomes, encouraging the global education
community to develop strategies that not only address shared challenges but also
respect and incorporate the unique cultural dimensions that shape students’ learning experiences. By fostering such an understanding, educators can better support
diverse learners in achieving success in science education and beyond. Concrete
initiatives, such as establishing international teacher exchange programs to share
best practices, developing online platforms for cross-cultural collaboration, and
promoting research on culturally responsive teaching, can further enhance global
educational practices and outcomes. Additionally, schools can create mentorship
programs that connect students with educators from diverse backgrounds, fostering an environment of support and understanding that is sensitive to cultural differences. By implementing these strategies, the global education community can work
towards creating a more inclusive and effective educational landscape that benefits
all students.
