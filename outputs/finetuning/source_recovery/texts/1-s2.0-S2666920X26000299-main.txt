A B S T R A C T
Collaborative Problem Solving (CPS) competence is widely recognized as a critical skill for the 21st century, 
especially in professions that emphasize communication and teamwork. CPS is a complex process involving 
both cognitive and social components, and numerous theoretical frameworks have been proposed for its as­
sessment. Currently, CPS competence is typically measured by analyzing process data from simulated tasks. To 
facilitate a more structured analysis of the CPS process, it is necessary to code behaviors into specific CPS skills. 
Traditionally, this coding has been conducted manually using predefined coding schemes. However, manual cod­
ing is both time-consuming and labor-intensive, making it impractical for large-scale datasets and unsuitable 
for real-time applications. To overcome these limitations, researchers have developed various automated coding 
methods. Nonetheless, most existing methods treat each behavior as independent, overlooking the natural de­
pendencies among behaviors in the CPS process. To address this shortcoming, we propose a novel framework 
called Context-Aware Prompting for CPS (CAP4CPS), which enables automated CPS skill coding by explicitly 
modeling contextual dependencies. Our approach first employs a context extraction module to retrieve dialogue 
histories that reflect the cognitive and social aspects of CPS. Next, a context-infused prompting module generates 
context-rich prompts to guide a pre-trained language model in producing representations of cognitive and social 
abilities. Finally, an ability fusion module integrates these representations into a unified CPS competency rep­
resentation, which is then used to predict the corresponding CPS skills. Extensive experiments on two CPS task 
datasets demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of the CAP4CPS.
1 . Introduction
Collaborative Problem Solving (CPS) refers to the process by which 
two or more individuals engage in mutual communication to develop 
a shared solution to a problem (OECD, 2017). Recognized as one of 
the key competencies of the 21st century (Sun et al., 2020), CPS is 
increasingly essential across diverse sectors of modern society. For in­
stance, in software development, effective collaboration is required 
among front-end engineers, back-end engineers, and product managers. 
In academia, interdisciplinary research often demands cooperation 
among scholars with varied expertise. Following natural disasters such

Effective assessment of Collaborative Problem Solving (CPS) first 
requires the collection of relevant process data. CPS is inherently an in­
teractive, collaborative, and dual-faceted process that involves not only 
evaluating how individuals engage in logical reasoning to solve prob­
lems but also examining how they coordinate social interactions and 
exchange information through interpersonal communication (OECD, 
2017). These complex and dynamic mechanisms pose significant chal­
lenges for the accurate and reliable measurement of CPS skills. In 
real-world contexts, this assessment becomes even more difficult due 
to the complexity of human-environment collaborative interactions. In 
recent years, with the rapid advancement of information technology, 
numerous computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) systems 
(Andrews-Todd et al., 2019; Aslan et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2016) based 
on the evidence-centered design (ECD) framework (Mislevy et al., 2003) 
have been developed. These systems typically present participants with 
collaborative tasks to be completed via computer-mediated interaction. 
Throughout this process, participants’ actions (e.g., mouse clicks or 
keystrokes) and communications (e.g., messages sent through a chat in­
terface) are logged by the system. These behavioral data can then be 
analyzed to examine CPS processes. However, because the logged data 
capture only observable task-related behaviors, they do not directly re­
veal the underlying CPS skills. Therefore, before conducting in-depth 
analysis, it is necessary to first code the behavioral data into specific 
CPS skills.
The coding of behavioral data in CPS processes should be guided 
by established CPS frameworks. These frameworks define the construct 
of CPS, delineate its components, and offer a structured representa­
tion of the competency. By translating the abstract concept of CPS 
into observable and measurable dimensions, they enable researchers 
to systematically analyze participants’ interactions and evaluate their 
collaborative problem-solving abilities. Among these, the Assessment 
and Teaching of 21st Century Skills (ATC21S) framework and the 
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) framework are 
two of the most widely adopted models (Hesse et al., 2015; OECD, 2017).
Existing CPS coding methods can be broadly classified into manual 
and automated approaches. The traditional method relies on human 
coders to perform annotations (Andrews-Todd and Forsyth, 2020). 
Manual coding is typically guided by coding schemes (Flor and Andrews-
Todd, 2022), which are developed based on theoretical frameworks to 
map specific behaviors to corresponding CPS skills. Since CPS skills are 
manifested through participants’ collaborative interactions, coders often 
consider the surrounding contextual information during the annotation 
process. Usually, two or more coders are involved, and a calibration 
phase is conducted to establish high inter-rater reliability and ensure 
annotation quality. While manual coding can yield high-quality data, it 
presents several significant limitations. First, it requires extensive train­
ing, and coders must carefully examine each data point, making the 
process highly time-consuming and labor-intensive. As a result, man­
ual coding is impractical for large-scale datasets (Hao et al., 2019). 
Furthermore, because manual coding is conducted post hoc, it does not 
support real-time analysis or feedback.
The increasing demand for large-scale data processing and real-time 
coding has driven growing research interest in the automated coding 
of CPS skills. Previous studies have proposed various automated cod­
ing methods using machine learning (Flor et al., 2016; Stewart et al., 
2019), deep learning (Stewart et al., 2021), and pre-trained language 
models (Pugh et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2024). However, most of these ap­
proaches treat each behavior as an isolated instance, without accounting 
for interdependencies between behaviors. In practice, CPS is a highly 
context-dependent process, and the same behavior may correspond to 
different CPS skills depending on the context in which it occurs. For 
example, under the PISA framework, the message “What should we do?” 
may reflect different CPS skills based on the conversational context: if 
it appears during a discussion of task goals, it is coded as “Discovering 
the type of collaborative interaction to solve the problem, along with 
goals”; if it appears during a discussion of action steps, it is coded as 
“Communicating with team members about the actions to be/being per­
formed.” In manual coding, coders consider contextual information to 
accurately identify the CPS skill represented by each behavior. Although 
some studies (Hao et al., 2017; Pugh et al., 2021) have attempted to 
incorporate behavioral dependencies into their models, the methods re­
main overly simplistic and fail to fully capture the interactive nature of 
CPS processes. Therefore, further research is needed to develop meth­
ods that effectively model contextual dependencies in automated CPS 
coding.
To address the issues outlined above, this paper proposes a novel 
method called Context-Aware Prompting for CPS (CAP4CPS), which cap­
tures dependencies among behaviors in the CPS process to enable more 
accurate automated coding. Fig. 1 presents a schematic overview of 
the CAP4CPS. Specifically, given a chat sequence from a CPS task, we 
first employ a Context Extraction (CE) Module to independently extract 
dialogue histories associated with the cognitive and social processes. 
We then introduce a Context-Infused Prompting (CIP) Module that con­
structs prompts incorporating the extracted dialogue histories. These 
prompts allow the pre-trained language model to better interpret the 
contextual situations in which the chat messages occur and to generate 
embeddings representing cognitive and social abilities. Subsequently, an

2 . Literature review
In this section, we first introduce the concept of CPS frameworks and 
describe in detail the two frameworks adopted in this study. Next, we 
review prior research on the automated coding of CPS activities. Finally, 
we provide a brief overview of the prompt learning paradigm.
2.1 . CPS framework
To systematically analyze individuals’ behaviors and processes in col­
laborative problem-solving tasks and to assess their CPS competence, 
researchers have developed various theoretical frameworks (Andrews-
Todd and Kerr, 2019; Hesse et al., 2015; OECD, 2017). These frame­
works typically encompass two key dimensions: a social dimension, 
which pertains to collaboration, and a cognitive dimension, which re­
lates to problem solving (Graesser et al., 2018). In this study, the two 
constructed datasets are annotated based on two frameworks: the PISA 
framework (OECD, 2017) and the CPS ontology framework proposed by 
Andrews-Todd and Kerr (2019), both of which are described in detail 
below.
The PISA framework (OECD, 2017) categorizes CPS skills into two 
dimensions—cognitive and social—and provides a detailed classification 
system. The cognitive dimension comprises four subskills: exploring 
and understanding, representing and formulating, planning and execut­
ing, and monitoring and reflecting. The social dimension includes three 
subskills: establishing and maintaining shared understanding, taking ap­
propriate actions to solve the problem, and establishing and maintaining 
team organization. These two dimensions are combined to form a ma­
trix of 12 subskills in total, as shown in Table 1. In this study, the 
PISA framework is applied to annotate the Mondrian Geometric Fantasy 
dataset.
Andrews-Todd and Kerr (2019) proposed an ontology framework for 
CPS research. Similar to the PISA framework, it includes both cognitive 
and social dimensions but provides a more fine-grained categorization 
of subskills. The cognitive dimension comprises five subskills: explor­
ing and understanding (CEU), representing and formulating (CRF), 
planning (CP), executing (CE), and monitoring (CM). The social dimen­
sion includes four subskills: maintaining communication (SMC), sharing 
information (SSI), establishing shared understanding (SESU), and ne­
gotiating (SN). Among these, the subskills executing and monitoring 
appear in both actions and chat messages; therefore, they are further 
divided into executing actions (CE), executing chats (CEC), monitoring 
actions (CM), and monitoring chats (CMC), resulting in a total of 11 
subskills in the ontology. This framework offers a theoretically grounded 
structure for mapping specific task behaviors to standardized subskill di­
mensions. In this study, the ontology framework is employed to annotate 
the Three-Resistor dataset.
2.2 . Automated coding of CPS activities
In recent years, advancements in artificial intelligence have led to 
the development of various methods for the automated coding of CPS 
behaviors. These methods can be broadly categorized into context-free 
and context-aware approaches.
Context-free methods treat each behavior in the CPS process as in­
dependent from others. Most existing studies fall into this category. 
Early research primarily focused on applying traditional machine learn­
ing techniques to CPS coding, where feature engineering was a central 
challenge. For instance, Flor et al. (2016) employed a Naive Bayes 
classifier with N-gram features and found that using only unigrams 
produced the best results, while the inclusion of bigrams and trigrams 
reduced performance. Stewart et al. (2019) used Random Forest clas­
sifiers in combination with N-gram and Linguistic Inquiry and Word 
Count (LIWC) features. Although there was no significant difference in 
classification accuracy between the two feature sets, LIWC-based cod­
ing aligned more closely with human annotations. They also noted that 
N-gram features pose a risk of overfitting to specific datasets, limiting 
their generalizability. With the emergence of word embedding models, 
new approaches have been proposed that utilize semantic representa­
tions to improve CPS coding. Stewart et al. (2021) used Global Vectors 
for Word Representation (GloVe) embeddings in conjunction with Long 
Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models to capture both short- and long-
range word dependencies. Flor and Andrews-Todd (2022) applied a 
K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier to predict CPS skills based on se­
mantic similarity, with each utterance represented by the average of its 
fastText word embeddings. More recently, the rise of pre-trained lan­
guage models has spurred further advances in CPS coding. Pugh et al. 
(2022) compared fine-tuned BERT models with Random Forest classi­
fiers and found that both fine-tuned BERT and Random Forest with LIWC 
features achieved comparable accuracy and generalizability. In contrast, 
Random Forest models using N-gram features exhibited a tendency to

Unlike context-free methods, context-aware approaches consider the 
dependencies between behaviors in the CPS process. Flor et al. (2016) 
noted that the CPS label of a sentence in a dialogue may depend on 
the label of the preceding sentence and experimented with a Hidden 
Markov Model (HMM) to capture this. Although the overall performance 
of the HMM was inferior to that of the Naive Bayes classifier, it out­
performed Naive Bayes on a specific CPS category—students expressing 
agreement with teammates—demonstrating that selectively leveraging 
historical information can improve automated coding. Hao et al. (2017) 
employed a linear-chain Conditional Random Field (CRF) to model se­
quential dependencies in CPS dialogues. The CRF’s feature functions 
include transition features, which capture the probabilities of transitions 
between all label pairs, thus modeling connections between utterances. 
Their method ultimately outperformed non-sequential models. Pugh 
et al. (2021) proposed BERT-seq, a method that incorporates both pre­
ceding and subsequent utterances when fine-tuning BERT for CPS skill 
recognition. Specifically, they fine-tuned two separate BERT models to 
capture forward and backward dependencies, respectively, and obtained 
the final prediction probability by summing the outputs of the two 
models.
Given the inherent contextual dependencies in the CPS process, in­
corporating context into automated coding is essential. While some 
studies have made preliminary efforts to integrate context, their models 
primarily address shallow sequential dependencies and remain rel­
atively simplistic. Significant opportunities remain to develop more 
advanced models that better capture and leverage contextual informa­
tion.
2.3 . Prompt learning
The advent of pre-trained language models (PLMs) has revolution­
ized the research paradigm in natural language processing (NLP) (Han 
et al., 2021). Traditional non-neural methods heavily relied on manually 
engineered features (Och et al., 2004). Subsequently, neural network-
based approaches gained popularity due to their ability to automatically 
learn task-specific features from data (Chung et al., 2014; Hochreiter 
and Schmidhuber, 1997; Kim, 2014); however, they still required large 
amounts of labeled task-specific data. The emergence of PLMs (Devlin 
et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019; Radford et al., 2018) transformed this land­
scape by learning general-purpose language representations through 
pretraining on large-scale unlabeled corpora, followed by fine-tuning on 
specific tasks, demonstrating strong performance across diverse down­
stream applications. BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from 
Transformers) exemplifies this paradigm shift (Devlin et al., 2019). It 
employs a multi-layer bidirectional Transformer encoder architecture 
and utilizes masked language modeling and next sentence prediction 
as pretraining objectives. With minimal task-specific parameters and 
fine-tuning, BERT has achieved remarkable results across numerous 
NLP tasks. Despite their success, fine-tuning PLMs still requires task-
specific datasets, which are often costly and challenging to obtain in 
large quantities.
In recent years, prompt engineering has gradually emerged as a 
prominent paradigm in natural language processing (NLP) (Liu et al., 
2023). Unlike traditional fine-tuning, prompt-based methods guide pre-
trained language models (PLMs) to perform specific tasks by incorpo­
rating textual prompts that describe the task. Prompt learning typically 
involves three steps: prompt construction, answer retrieval, and answer 
mapping. For example, in sentiment classification of the sentence “I 
watched a wonderful movie,” a prompt can be created by appending a 
task description such as: “I watched a wonderful movie. I felt so _,” where 
the underscore (“_”) indicates the token the PLM is expected to predict. 
This prompt is then input into the PLM, which searches for the most 
appropriate answer within its vocabulary or a predefined subset. Finally, 
the predicted answer is mapped to the corresponding label. With care­
fully designed prompts, PLMs can effectively address tasks in few-shot 
(Cui et al., 2022) or even zero-shot (Zhou et al., 2022) scenarios.
An essential aspect of prompt learning is the construction of effective 
prompts. Methods for prompt construction can be categorized into man­
ually designed prompts (Schick and Schütze, 2021a) and automatically 
generated prompts (Lester et al., 2021). Manually designed prompts are 
created based on task characteristics and relevant domain knowledge. 
In contrast, automatically generated prompts aim to identify suitable 
prompts for a given task without human intervention. The search space 
for such prompts may consist of either discrete strings or continuous 
embedding spaces. Training strategies for prompt learning are classified 
according to whether the parameters of the prompt and the pre-trained 
language model (PLM) are updated during training. The main strategies 
include tuning-free prompting (Brown et al., 2020), fixed-PLM prompt 
tuning (Han et al., 2021; Li and Liang, 2021), fixed-prompt PLM tuning 
(Schick and Schütze, 2021a,b), and prompt+PLM tuning (Ben-David 
et al., 2022).
Previous experimental results (Zhu et al., 2024) demonstrate that 
carefully designed manual prompts improve performance. Therefore, 
this paper proposes context-integrated manual prompts tailored to the 
cognitive and social abilities involved in the CPS process. Regarding 
training strategies, fixed-prompt language model tuning is currently the 
most widely adopted approach for prompt learning in textual tasks. Our 
proposed model, CAP4CPS, follows this approach.
3 . Dataset
In this paper, we evaluate the performance of CAP4CPS on two 
datasets to assess its adaptability across different CPS tasks. Therefore, 
this section introduces two datasets collected from the CPS activities 
of the Three-Resistor and Mondrian Geometric Fantasy tasks. We pro­
vide detailed descriptions of these tasks and outline the data collection 
and annotation procedures. Finally, we present statistical analyses and 
visualizations for both datasets.
3.1 . Dataset 1: three-resistor
Task Description. The Three-Resistor task (Horwitz et al., 2017) is a 
team-based collaboration requiring three participants to work together 
using physics knowledge. Each participant is responsible for adjusting 
one resistor. The objective is to ensure that each resistor in the series 
circuit attains a specific voltage. Because the voltages across the resis­
tors are interdependent, successful task completion demands effective 
communication and cooperation to ensure all resistors simultaneously 
reach their target voltages.
In this task, each participant independently operates a computer 
to simulate an electronic circuit. The task interface, shown in Fig. 2, 
displays the known conditions and target at the top of the screen. A 
multimeter for measuring the voltage across the resistor is located at 
the center. The complete circuit structure appears at the bottom, with 
a zoom button to view teammates’ circuit boards. On the right side, a 
text chat box facilitates communication among the three participants. A 
calculator is positioned in the upper right corner to assist with calcula­
tions, while the answer submission button is located in the lower right 
corner.
Four progressively challenging objectives were carefully designed, 
and participants engaged with them through a level-based format. 
Teams that advanced through more levels and reached higher task levels 
within the allotted time were considered to have demonstrated superior 
performance. Table 2 details the specific settings for the four difficulty 
levels. Specifically, in Level 1, participants were provided with the ex­
ternal voltage and resistance values, and all teammates shared the same 
target voltage. Level 2 increased in difficulty by assigning different tar­
get voltages to each member. In Level 3, the challenge intensified as

Dataset Description. The study involved 378 participants, all of 
whom were students enrolled in electronics, engineering, or physics 
courses at universities and community colleges across the United States.
Prior to the formal experiment, participants independently com­
pleted a questionnaire collecting personal information, including back­
ground, race, gender, age, education level, native language, and pref­
erence for individual or team work. During the experiment, sessions 
were conducted in a computer lab where each participant worked inde­
pendently at a separate computer. Participants were randomly assigned 
to teams of three, forming a total of 126 teams. The seating arrange­
ment ensured that team members were seated apart. Throughout the 
task, students remained unaware of their teammates’ identities and 
communicated using virtual usernames (e.g., Lion, Tiger, Bear).
During the task, the system recorded experimental information and 
students’ behavioral data as logs. The experimental information included 
participant IDs, team names, task details, and other relevant metadata. 
Behavioral data were categorized into two types: participants’ manip­
ulations, such as adjusting resistance or using the calculator, and chat 
messages from the communication box, including examples like “Did 
you adjust it?” or “What are your current voltage and resistance read­
ings?”. A total of 50,817 data entries were collected, comprising 34,867 
manipulation entries and 15,950 chat entries.
The behavioral data collected from participants were coded ac­
cording to the CPS ontology framework proposed by Andrews-Todd 
and Kerr (2019). Three trained coders performed the coding follow­
ing a predefined scheme. To ensure inter-rater reliability, 20% of the 
dataset was randomly selected and independently coded by all three 
coders. The overall Cohen’s Kappa coefficient was 0.937, with a value of 
0.871 specifically for chat entries, indicating a high level of agreement. 
Subsequently, the remaining data were distributed among the coders for 
independent annotation.
The final dataset also underwent several preprocessing steps. First, 
anonymization was performed by removing all personally identifiable 
information, retaining only group identifiers, usernames, and behavioral 
data; the usernames used are virtual. The anonymization process is irre­
versible and does not allow re-identification. In addition, after removing 
blank chat entries and invalid labels, the final dataset comprised 34,851 
manipulation entries and 15,895 chat entries. All behavioral data were 
encoded into 11 CPS subskills based on the ontology framework. Table 3 
presents examples of the manual coding results.
Manipulation data could be directly mapped to CPS subskills, as these 
actions are discrete and predefined. Specifically, manipulations such as 
viewing the board, opening zoom, and submitting results were catego­
rized as monitoring (CM); using a calculator was classified as executing 
(CE); and changing the resistor value was coded based on timing—before 
planning, it was categorized as exploring and understanding (CEU), and 
after planning, as executing (CE). Thus, manipulation data could be 
readily identified and accurately mapped to CPS subskills.
Fig. 3(a) (left panel) presents the distribution of CPS subskills in 
chat data, encompassing eight distinct subskills. Among these, sharing 
information (SSI) has the highest proportion, followed by establishing 
shared understanding (SESU); together, they account for nearly 60% 
of all instances. Representing and formulating (CRF) appears the least 
frequently, comprising only 2.2%. In terms of broader dimensions, the 
social dimension constitutes 75.1% of the data, significantly exceeding 
the cognitive dimension. Overall, the distribution reveals notable im­
balances both across subskills and between the two dimensions, which 
presents challenges for automated coding.

3.2 . Dataset 2: mondrian geometric fantasy
Task Description. The Mondrian Geometric Fantasy task (Xu et al., 
2024) is framed within a fictional narrative: two paintings by the 
renowned artist Mondrian, housed in a museum collection, have been 
shattered by a thief. At the request of the museum director, Afu, three 
students each recover a portion of the fragmented artwork. Together, 
they must collaborate to restore the paintings to their original form.
The Mondrian Geometric Fantasy task is a collaborative activity 
completed by three participants, who must translate and rotate frag­
ments to reconstruct the missing areas of a canvas within a specified 
time limit. The task consists of two levels, with the second level in­
cluding more fragments than the first. Participants can only advance 
to the second level after successfully submitting the first-level result 
and receiving confirmation. Teams that assemble more fragments re­

Dataset Description. The data were collected from 840 students in 
Grades 10 and 11 at a high school in China. The task was administered 
in the school’s computer lab, with each participant using a computer 
independently. Participation was anonymous, and participants were 
unaware of each other’s real identities.
The overall experimental procedure consisted of six stages: logging 
into the system interface, automated team assignment, clicking “Start,” 
watching a tutorial animation, completing the online CPS task, and fill­
ing out a post-task questionnaire. Teams were formed randomly, with 
three students per team. Before beginning the task, participants watched 
a tutorial animation lasting approximately 90 s, which explained the task 
rules and operational instructions. The task was limited to 40 minutes, 
while the post-task questionnaire had no time constraint. Throughout 
the task, all manipulation and chat data were automatically recorded by 
the system.
A total of 280 teams participated in the experiment. Due to is­
sues such as unexpected disconnections and missing data, 51 teams 
were excluded as invalid samples. Ultimately, 229 valid team sam­
ples remained, comprising a total of 245,034 data entries—216,547 
manipulation entries and 28,487 chat entries.
The coding was conducted by two coders following the guidelines 
established under the PISA 2015 framework. All manipulation entries 
were coded as enacting plans (C2), while the chat entries contained no 
instances of C2 skills. Chat entries that could not be matched to any 
competencies defined in the PISA framework (OECD, 2017) were labeled 
as unknown (U), indicating an unspecified competency. Initially, the two 
coders collaboratively coded four randomly selected teams through face-
to-face discussions to align their understanding of the coding criteria. 
They then independently coded another five randomly selected teams. 
The resulting Cohen’s Kappa coefficient was 0.827, indicating a high 
level of inter-rater agreement. The remaining data were subsequently 
divided between the two coders for independent coding.
The final dataset likewise underwent the same anonymization proce­
dure as the Three Resistor dataset. In addition, the final dataset included 
only chat entries from the formal task phase, with blank chat entries 
removed. This resulted in 216,547 manipulation entries and 26,715 
chat entries. All chat entries were coded into 12 CPS subskills. Table 4 
presents examples of the manual coding results.
All manipulation entries were categorized under Enacting Plans (C2).

5 . Method
This section presents the technical details of Context-Aware 
Prompting for CPS (CAP4CPS) used to identify CPS skills. As illus­
trated in Fig. 5, CAP4CPS comprises three main components: the Context 
Extraction (CE) Module, the Context-Infused Prompting (CIP) Module, 
and the Ability Fusion (AF) Module. Specifically, CE extracts context in­
formation for the cognitive and social dimensions, respectively. Next, 
CIP generates representations that capture dependencies between these 
contexts by prompting pre-trained language models. Finally, AF inte­
grates competencies from both dimensions, and a classifier predicts the 
CPS skill associated with each message.
5.1 . Context extraction module
Before constructing the prompts, we propose the Context Extraction 
(CE) module to separately extract contextual information from the cog­
nitive and social dimensions, facilitating the identification of CPS skills 
reflected in messages. Given that in real-time communication environ­
ments participants’ CPS skills depend on prior dialogue, we use only 
historical dialogue as context.
Contextual information plays a critical role in encoding CPS skills 
during the CPS process. The same message may correspond to differ­
ent CPS skills depending on its context. For instance, in the Mondrian 
Geometric Fantasy task, a participant’s message, “What should we do?”, 
should be encoded as “Discovering the type of collaborative interac­
tion to solve the problem, along with goals (A2)” if it occurs during 
the goal-discussion phase. However, if the message arises during the 
discussion of how to move specific fragments, it should be encoded 
as “Communicating with team members about the actions to be/being 
performed (C1).” Thus, selecting appropriate contextual information to 
clarify the CPS phase in which a message occurs is essential.
Understanding situations related to the cognitive and social dimen­
sions requires distinct types of contextual information. Collaborative 
problem solving is a complex process involving both the cognitive di­
mension of individual problem solving and the social dimension of 
collaborative efforts. In the cognitive dimension, problem solving com­
petency is defined as “an individual’s capacity to engage in cognitive 
processing to understand and resolve problem situations where a method 
of solution is not immediately obvious.” Therefore, understanding the 
cognitive context relies on an individual’s personal historical dialogue. 
In contrast, in the social dimension, collaborative problem solving com­
petency is defined as “the capacity of an individual to effectively engage 
in a process whereby two or more agents attempt to solve a problem by 
sharing the understanding and effort required to come to a solution, 
and pooling their knowledge, skills, and effort to reach that solution.” 
Consequently, understanding the social context requires interaction 
history with others.

6 . Experiments
In this section, we conduct comprehensive experiments on the Three-
Resistor and Mondrian Geometric Fantasy datasets to evaluate the 
effectiveness of the CAP4CPS model in comparison with baseline models. 
Our objective is to address the following research questions:
• RQ1: How does CAP4CPS perform in predicting CPS skills com­
pared to baseline models?
• RQ2: What is the impact of each module within CAP4CPS on 
overall model performance?
• RQ3: How do the cognitive context window 𝑤𝑐 and the social 
context window 𝑤𝑠 influence the model’s performance?
6.1 . Experimental setup
Pre-trained Language Model. We use BERT as the pre-trained 
language model in all our experiments. Specifically, we employ the
bert-base-cased model for the Three-Resistor dataset and the bert-
base-chinese model for the Mondrian Geometric Fantasy dataset. The 
choice of pre-trained models is guided by the primary language of each 
dataset to ensure optimal semantic representation.
CAP4CPS Setting. We employ grid search to find the best hyperpa­
rameters using the validation set. Specifically, we adjust the cognitive 
context window size (𝑤𝑐) and the social context window size (𝑤𝑠) within 
the range {1, 2, 3}, the number of attention heads (ℎ) within the range 
{1, 2, 4, 8}. Finally, we set the cognitive context window size (𝑤𝑐) to 2, 
the social context window size (𝑤𝑠) to 1, and the number of attention 
heads (ℎ) to 2. There are also several other parameters. The embed­
ding dimension (𝑑) for both cognitive and social abilities is set to 768. 
The maximum token length (𝑙) of the final prompt is set to 140 for 
the Three-Resistor dataset and 112 for the Mondrian Geometric Fantasy 
dataset, based on the distribution of message word counts shown in
Fig. 3.
Training Details. For dataset preparation, we first apply stratified 
sampling to split the data into training, validation, and test sets with a 
ratio of 7:1.5:1.5, ensuring that the data distribution is consistent with 
the original dataset. During training, we use the AdamW optimizer with 
a learning rate of 2 × 10−5 and weight decay of 1 × 10−2. The size of 
mini-batch is set to 32. A dropout rate of 0.1 is applied to prevent over­
fitting. In addition, we adopt an early stopping strategy, where training 
is terminated if the validation loss does not decrease for two consecutive 
epochs.
6.2 . Baseline
To demonstrate the effectiveness of the CAP4CPS model, we com­
pare it with baseline models used in previous CPS coding studies, as 
well as other commonly employed text classification models. Based on 
whether these models utilize context during automated coding, they are 
categorized as follows:

Context-free model
• Naive Bayes (Flor et al., 2016), Random Forest (Stewart et al., 
2019), KNN (Flor and Andrews-Todd, 2022): For these models, we 
use TF-IDF features. When constructing TF-IDF representations, the 
vocabulary is limited to the top 10,000 most frequent n-grams, and 
unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams are extracted. In addition, L2 nor­
malization is applied to each document vector and IDF smoothing 
is enabled. Furthermore, since TF-IDF has weaker representa­
tional capacity than BERT mean-pooled sentence embeddings, we 
additionally evaluate Random Forest and KNN using BERT mean-
pooled sentence embeddings as sentence-level features, in order to 
more fairly compare the impact of model architecture on coding 
performance. Because Naive Bayes does not support features with 
negative values, we do not incorporate BERT mean-pooled embed­
dings for this model. These models use feature dimensions equal 
to the TF-IDF vocabulary size when TF-IDF features are adopted, 
and a feature dimension of 768 when mean-pooled BERT sentence 
embeddings are used as features.
• CNN (Kim, 2014), GRU (Chung et al., 2014), LSTM (Stewart et al., 
2021): These approaches leverage deep learning techniques for fea­
ture extraction, followed by a linear layer for classification. For 
these models, we construct a vocabulary consisting of all words 
with a frequency greater than 2. Since the architectural differences 
of these models are reflected in the construction of sentence-level 
embeddings, we do not additionally include experiments using 
BERT mean-pooled sentence embeddings. The embedding dimen­
sion of all these models is set to 300. The hidden state dimension 
of both the LSTM and GRU is 256. The CNN employs convolu­
tional filters with kernel sizes of 3, 4, and 5, each with 256 output 
channels.
• BERT-finetune (Pugh et al., 2022), BERT-prompt (Zhu et al., 
2024): Both methods are based on pre-trained language mod­
els. BERT-finetune performs classification by fine-tuning BERT 
with an additional linear layer, while BERT-prompt employs a 
prompt-based approach that enables BERT to generate an output 
subsequently mapped to the appropriate classification label. The 
hidden state dimension of all these models is set to BERT’s default 
size of 768.
Context-aware model
• CPS-Rater (Hao et al., 2017): CPS-Rater utilizes a linear-chain 
Conditional Random Field (CRF) to explicitly model transition 
probabilities between adjacent labels, thereby enhancing classi­
fication performance. In the feature construction stage, we build 
vocabularies of unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams as state fea­

of these results, we derived several key findings:
1. The proposed CAP4CPS model outperforms all baselines on 
both datasets. Compared to the second-best model, CAP4CPS 
improves Accuracy by 1.00%, W-F1 by 0.81%, and Kappa 
by 1.20% on the Three-Resistor dataset (compared to BERT-
finetune), and Accuracy by 0.69%, W-F1 by 0.63%, and Kappa 
by 0.95% on the Mondrian Geometric Fantasy dataset (compared 
to BERT-seq). These results indicate that CAP4CPS is more effec­
tive for CPS automated coding by modeling multi-dimensional
context.
2. Methods based on pre-trained language models demonstrate sub­
stantially superior automated coding performance compared with 
other approaches. Most traditional machine learning methods 
that rely on N-gram representations for sentence features per­
form relatively poorly: the Kappa values of Naive Bayes, KNN, and 
CPS-Rater are all below 0.7, and only Random Forest achieves a 
Kappa of 0.7 on the Mondrian Geometric Fantasy dataset. Deep 
learning methods automatically learn sentence representations 
from data and thus exhibit stronger representational capacity 
than direct N-gram features. The CNN, GRU, and LSTM models all 
achieve Kappa values above 0.7 on Mondrian Geometric Fantasy; 
however, their Kappa values on Three-Resistor remain below 
0.7. In contrast, methods based on pre-trained language mod­
els, which are trained on large-scale corpora, produce the most 
semantically rich sentence representations. Consequently, BERT-
finetune, BERT-prompt, BERT-seq, and CAP4CPS all achieve 
Kappa values above 0.7 on both datasets.
3. The performance gains of CAP4CPS are not only attributable to 
the rich semantic representations obtained from BERT pretrain­
ing, but also to the design of the model architecture. To examine 
whether the improvement of CAP4CPS stems solely from BERT’s 
semantic representations or also from the architectural design, 
we further included Random Forest, KNN, and CPS-Rater mod­
els that use BERT mean-pooled sentence embeddings as features. 
For KNN and CPS-Rater, the semantic representations provided 
by BERT substantially improve coding performance; in partic­
ular, CPS-Rater achieves a Kappa improvement of 66.3% on 
the Mondrian Geometric Fantasy dataset. However, we observe 
that Random Forest performance actually degrades when using 
BERT mean-pooled sentence embeddings. This may be because 
Random Forest is not well suited to dense features with high 
inter-dimensional correlations. When comparing these models 
using BERT mean-pooled sentence embeddings with methods that 
directly fine-tune BERT or apply prompt-based BERT, we find that 
the former still perform markedly worse than the latter. These 
findings indicate that although BERT pretraining enables the 
learning of rich semantic representations, designing an appropri­
ate model architecture to effectively exploit such representations 
is also crucial for improving automated coding performance.
4. Context-aware models achieve relatively strong performance. 
Among them, CPS-Rater using BERT mean-pooled sentence em­
beddings outperforms all other machine learning models on the 
Three-Resistor dataset. On Mondrian Geometric Fantasy, its per­
formance is slightly worse than that of KNN, but still superior to 
the other machine learning models. BERT-seq ranks second on 
Mondrian Geometric Fantasy and third on Three-Resistor. One 
possible reason why BERT-seq does not consistently outperform 
BERT-finetune is that contextual information based on fixed in­
fusion may sometimes introduce noise. In contrast, CAP4CPS 
models cognitive and social contextual information separately 
through prompt construction and incorporates an attention-based 
fusion mechanism, enabling a more flexible and effective under­
standing of contextual relationships.
Overall, this evidence indicates that CAP4CPS achieves superior per­
formance on CPS automated coding tasks not only due to the rich 
semantic representations provided by pre-trained language models, but 
also owing to its well-designed model architecture. By constructing 
prompts to model multidimensional contextual information and fusing 
them through an attention-based mechanism, CAP4CPS is able to more 
effectively capture contextual relationships.

6.4.3 . Sensitivity of parameters (RQ3)
In this section, we analyze the sensitivity of the CAP4CPS model to 
the parameters 𝑤𝑐 and 𝑤𝑠, which define the window sizes for cognitive 
and social context, respectively. A key contributor to the strong per­
formance of the proposed CAP4CPS model is its effective utilization of 
contextual information, with 𝑤𝑐 and 𝑤𝑠 serving as crucial parameters 
in constructing this context. Specifically, 𝑤𝑐 governs the modeling of 
an individual’s problem solving process, while 𝑤𝑠 governs the modeling 
of interactions with others. For the experimental setup, both 𝑤𝑐 and 𝑤𝑠
are searched over the range {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5}, resulting in 36 combinations. 
Each combination is evaluated over 10 runs, and the results are averaged 
based on the Kappa score. The results are presented in Fig. 8.
Based on the results, we observe the following: (1) General trend: 
Larger values of both 𝑤𝑐 and 𝑤𝑠 generally lead to better performance. 
When these values are small, the model relies more heavily on the 
current message for CPS skill recognition. In contrast, larger values en­
able the model to incorporate more contextual information, thereby 
enhancing its understanding of the dialogue environment in which the 
current message occurs. (2) Saturation effect: When both 𝑤𝑐 and 𝑤𝑠 are 
large, their specific values have a relatively minor impact on perfor­
mance. In CPS dialogues, participants typically respond to recently sent 
messages; thus, beyond a certain window size, additional context does 
not yield further improvements.
These findings highlight the importance of selecting appropriate val­
ues for 𝑤𝑐 and 𝑤𝑠 to balance cognitive and social contexts, which is 
critical for CAP4CPS’s performance in automated coding. Nevertheless, 
once these values exceed a certain threshold, the model’s performance 
becomes relatively insensitive to further changes, demonstrating robust­
ness to parameter selection.
6.4.4 . Coding results analysis
To more thoroughly evaluate the automated coding performance 
of our proposed CAP4CPS model, we present its performance on the 
classification of individual CPS skills and analyze the associated mis­
classifications. Fig. 9 shows the normalized confusion matrices of the 
labels predicted by CAP4CPS on the two datasets. In each matrix, the 
entry at row 𝑖 and column 𝑗 represents the proportion of samples with 
true label 𝑖 that were predicted as label 𝑗. The diagonal elements indicate 
the recall for each CPS skill.
On the Three-Resistor dataset, the recall rates for five skills—SSI, 
SESU, SMC, SN, and CEC—are relatively high, while those for CP, CMC, 
and CRF are comparatively low. We further analyze the three skills 
with lower predictive performance. For the CP skill, 22% of the samples 
are misclassified as CEC. CP involves discussions about plans for solv­
ing tasks, whereas CEC refers to discussions following task execution. 
Although both pertain to task-related actions, CP typically precedes the 
action, while CEC follows it. One example of a misclassified CP utter­
ance is “take it at 330 again for a sec,” which expresses a plan for the 
next step. However, the model may have interpreted the word “again” as 
an indication that the action had already occurred. For CMC and CRF,

On the Mondrian Geometric Fantasy dataset, the recall rates of the 
four skills D1, A3, B3, and D3 are relatively low. Notably, these four skills 
are exactly the four least frequent ones in the dataset, which indicates 
that CAP4CPS still has limitations in recognizing low-frequency skills. 
In addition, CAP4CPS tends to misclassify low-frequency skills as other 
skills that are semantically similar. Specifically, 38% of D1 instances 
are misclassified as D2. Both D1 and D2 are related to monitoring and 
reflection, but D1 focuses on team consensus, whereas D2 emphasizes 
the completion status of specific tasks. An example of such a misclas­
sification is “that’s wrong, you’re not allowed to cross the white line.” 
The model may struggle with this kind of indirect expression, where the 
speaker corrects an understanding of overall rules by evaluating a spe­
cific behavior. The recognition performance of A3 is particularly poor: 
33% of A3 instances are misclassified as B3, and another 22% as C1. 
A3 refers to explicitly specifying each person’s concrete responsibilities 
in problem solving; therefore, it typically involves both role assignment 
and concrete task execution. If the model attends to only one of these 
aspects, misclassification is likely to occur. For example, “I’ll take over 
at the end.” is misclassified as B3 because the model focuses only on 
role assignment and fails to capture that “at the end” refers to a specific 
stage of task execution. Similarly, “I am responsible for the lower-left 
corner” is misclassified as C1 because the model focuses on the specific 
task region while ignoring the aspect of responsibility assignment. For 
B3, 26% of instances are misclassified as C1. B3 is intended to describe 
high-level role allocation. A misclassified example is “you do it, I’ll tell 
you”. Although no concrete actions are specified, the model may focus 
on the words “do” and “tell” and thus interpret the utterance as refer­
ring to specific actions. For D3, 21% of instances are misclassified as 
C1. D3 is supposed to capture monitoring of responsibility fulfillment. 
Since responsibility fulfillment often involves concrete actions, it is eas­
ily mistaken for C1. An example is “make sure you understand it before 
you talk”, which should be interpreted as reminding someone to be ac­
countable for their speech, whereas the model may focus only on the 
speaking action itself. Finally, 26% of D3 instances are misclassified as 
U. These cases mainly involve expressions that are overly implicit. For 
example, “isn’t there another person?” appears irrelevant on the surface, 
but its underlying meaning is that one teammate has neither acted nor 
spoken for a long time and thus has failed to fulfill their responsibilities.
Overall, CAP4CPS faces several major challenges in automated cod­
ing. First, it often relies heavily on lexical features, which limits its 
ability to distinguish between skills with similar meanings that are ex­
pressed through subtle linguistic differences. Second, when the skill 
reflected in a sentence requires integrating information across the entire 
sentence, and different parts of the sentence may correspond to different 
skills, CAP4CPS tends to focus on local segments rather than the global 
context. Third, when expressions are highly implicit and their under­
lying meanings deviate from the literal meanings associated with the 
sentence labels, CAP4CPS may fail to capture the intended semantics. 
Finally, data imbalance also poses a significant challenge for CAP4CPS.
7 . Discussion
With the growing importance of CPS skills across various domains, 
the assessment and development of these abilities have garnered in­
creasing attention. Computer-simulated CPS tasks offer rich data for 
evaluating such skills, and a critical step in analyzing these data is en­
coding them into distinct CPS skill categories. Given the time-consuming 
and labor-intensive nature of traditional manual coding methods, nu­
merous automated coding approaches have been proposed. However, 
many existing methods fail to account for the interdependencies be­
tween actions in the CPS process. To address this limitation, we propose 
a novel method—Context-Aware Prompting for CPS (CAP4CPS)—which 
effectively captures these dependencies to enhance the accuracy of 
automated coding. In this section, we summarize our key findings, iden­
tify limitations in current research, and outline directions for future
work.
7.1 . Main findings
The comparative experiments between CAP4CPS and other baseline 
models demonstrate CAP4CPS’s superior performance in CPS automated 
coding tasks. We evaluated CAP4CPS and the baseline models on two 
datasets: Three-Resistor and Mondrian Geometric Fantasy. CAP4CPS 
achieved the highest accuracy, weighted F1 score, and Cohen’s kappa 
across all models, indicating its effectiveness for CPS automated coding. 
Furthermore, models based on pre-trained language models consis­
tently outperformed traditional machine learning and deep learning 
methods on both datasets. This suggests that pre-trained language mod­
els not only improve performance but also offer better adaptability 
across different tasks. Notably, the strong performance of both BERT-seq 
and CAP4CPS underscores the value of integrating pre-trained models

with contextual modeling. Finally, a comparison between Prompt-BERT 
and CAP4CPS—both of which utilize prompts—reveals that incorpo­
rating contextual information into prompts is a promising strategy for 
enhancing automated coding performance.
The ablation study reveals that incorporating multi-dimensional 
contextual information and modeling the dependencies between the 
cognitive and social dimensions both enhance automated coding per­
formance. First, removing contextual information from both dimensions 
in CAP4CPS led to a significant decrease in performance, indicating the 
critical role of context in CPS processes. Without context, the model 
must rely solely on isolated individual behaviors, lacking awareness 
of the surrounding situation. Second, replacing the multi-head atten­
tion mechanism with simple concatenation caused a slight performance 
decline, suggesting that multi-head attention is more effective in captur­
ing complex and hierarchical relationships between cognitive and social 
dimensions.
By analyzing the effect of context length in the cognitive and so­
cial dimensions on the CAP4CPS model’s performance, we found that 
CAP4CPS is highly robust to variations in context length. First, we ob­
served that, overall, longer contexts lead to better performance than very 
short ones, as extended context helps the model better understand the 
CPS process. Second, the impact of context length differs between the 
cognitive and social dimensions. A shorter context in the cognitive di­
mension has a more detrimental effect on CAP4CPS’s performance. This 
is because, in conversations, speakers typically respond only to the most 
recent utterances from others, whereas their own utterances often form 
a coherent narrative across multiple consecutive statements. Finally, we 
found that as long as the context is not extremely short, the specific 
length has minimal influence on CAP4CPS’s performance.
7.2 . Limitations and future work
Like many other models, CAP4CPS has several limitations that 
warrant further investigation. First, this study employs BERT as the 
pre-trained language model and adopts a fixed-prompt PLM tuning 
approach. In recent years, large language models (LLMs) have demon­
strated impressive performance and strong zero-shot capabilities. We 
aim to explore the potential of using LLMs for automated coding in a 
zero-shot setting. Second, the proposed model is trained on a specific 
task dataset, achieving satisfactory performance only on that task, which 
limits its generalizability. In future work, we plan to develop models ca­
pable of performing automated coding across multiple CPS tasks. Third, 
our current model uses a fixed-window approach for context modeling. 
Although it exhibits strong robustness to variations in window size, op­
timal performance still depends on manually selecting an appropriate 
window length. Therefore, we intend to explore adaptive context se­
lection methods that allow the model to automatically determine the 
optimal context length. Lastly, CAP4CPS exhibits relatively low recog­
nition accuracy for low-frequency samples when dealing with extremely 
imbalanced data distributions. In future work, we plan to design dedi­
cated algorithms to improve the recognition accuracy of low-frequency 
samples while maintaining overall performance as much as possible.
8 . Conclusion
In this study, we proposed Context-Aware Prompting for CPS 
(CAP4CPS), a novel framework designed to improve the automated 
coding of CPS skills by explicitly modeling contextual dependencies 
within the CPS process. By extracting dialogue histories along both 
cognitive and social dimensions and incorporating them into prompts 
for a pre-trained language model, CAP4CPS facilitates a more accu­
rate understanding of the situational context behind each behavior. The 
model further integrates these dimensions into a unified representation 
of CPS competency for skill prediction. Extensive experiments on two 
CPS datasets validate the effectiveness and robustness of CAP4CPS.
