Using data from PISA 2022, we distinguished latent profiles of math motivation in Finland, Germany, Japan,
Korea, and the U.S. based on students’ self-efficacy, intrinsic and attainment task values, and anxiety in math. We
then examined the covariates and outcomes associated with each identified profile across countries to highlight
both the universal and culturally sensitive factors that are conducive to students’ math motivation. Four math
motivation profiles—highly motivated, mixed, slightly unmotivated, and unmotivated and indifferent—emerged
consistently across all five countries, whereas one more profile with a distinct characteristic was additionally
detected in Japan and Finland. The relative desirability of the profiles was corroborated by students’ engagement
in math class, time spent on math homework, STEM-related career intention, and math achievement. The slightly
unmotivated and mixed profiles demonstrated some notable country-specific differences in their relationships with
these outcomes. Despite several between-country differences, boys were more likely to belong to either the highly
motivated or the unmotivated and indifferent profile compared to girls. Across all countries, students’ growth
mindset and teachers’ general support were consistently associated with students’ membership in more desirable
profiles. In contrast, the association between teachers’ instructional styles and profile membership varied across
countries. Our findings emphasize the interactive function of expectancy and value in shaping math motivation
profiles, while also highlighting the universal benefit of endorsing a growth mindset toward math abilities. By
contrast, the effectiveness of instructional methods appears context-dependent, suggesting math teachers adapt
their pedagogical approaches to the cultural settings in which they teach.

1.1. Key motivation constructs in math: self-efficacy, anxiety, intrinsic
value, and attainment value

1.2. Interplay of motivation constructs and a person-centered approach

Situated expectancy-value theory (SEVT; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020)
stands as one of the most prominent frameworks in contemporary
achievement motivation research. Within SEVT, students’ academic
motivation centers primarily on two key beliefs, expectancy of success
and subjective task value. These two beliefs are positively intercorrelated and serve as direct precursors of achievement-related outcomes
(Wigfield & Eccles, 2000).
Eccles and Wigfield defined expectancy of success as the belief in
successfully performing a future task, which is conceptually similar to
self-efficacy belief or one’s subjective convictions of their capabilities to
perform well in specific domains or tasks (Bandura, 1977; see Wigfield &
Eccles, 2000, for comparison between expectancy for success and selfefficacy). Students who expect to succeed in tasks not only achieve
better but also persist longer, participate more actively in the tasks, and
demonstrate less anxiety (Bong, 2001; Bong et al., 2012; Song & Chung,
2020; Watt et al., 2012; Zhang & Jiang, 2025).
Along with expectancy for success, task value also plays a determining role in students’ choice behavior and performance in math
(Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). Subjective task value comprises four subcomponents: intrinsic value (i.e., enjoyment derived from engaging in a
task), attainment value (i.e., perceived importance of doing well on a
task for one’s self-concept), utility value (i.e., perceived usefulness of a
task), and cost (i.e., perceived negative consequences of engaging in a
task). Intrinsic value is commonly viewed as similar to interest (Eccles &
Wigfield, 2020) and predicts deeper engagement in the task and stronger
career intentions in the domain (Lauermann et al., 2017; Zhang & Jiang,
2025). Attainment value is another key predictor of adolescents’ mathrelated career intentions. Students who view math as important to their
identity are more likely to pursue a math-related career, even when they
lack interest in the subject (Watt et al., 2012). As such, different task
values often interactively manifest in certain outcomes.
While intrinsic, attainment, and utility values all have a positive
valence, cost, a fourth task value component in SEVT, carries a negative
valence, undermining the net value of a task (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020;
Jiang et al., 2018). One key aspect of cost is emotional cost, which refers
to the negative feelings or fear of failure in academic tasks (Wigfield &
Eccles, 2000). Emotional cost is conceptually distinct from anxiety, as it
encompasses a broader range of negative emotions, including confusion,
boredom, anger, frustration, and anxiety (see Beymer & Schmidt, 2023).
Anxiety, the fear and apprehension associated with tasks (Ashcraft,
2002), typically arises from prolonged failure or aversive learning experiences, whereas emotional cost reflects a more context-dependent
evaluation of task demands and outcomes. Nevertheless, the two constructs display strong positive intercorrelations and similar predictive
utility (Song et al., 2023). Earlier conceptualizations of cost also
included math anxiety as one of the core facets of perceived cost (e.g.,
Eccles, 2005; Eccles et al., 1983). Furthermore, anxiety is commonly
experienced in math contexts (Ashcraft, 2002) and undermines academic success (Jiang et al., 2018); it is negatively correlated with selfefficacy and shows inconsistent relationships with value (Chen, 2024;
Guo et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2018). Given the empirical overlap between emotional cost and anxiety (Song et al., 2023), as well as the
measures available in the PISA 2022 dataset, we included math anxiety
as a proxy for emotional cost along with other expectancy-value constructs in the present study.
Another central assumption of SEVT is that key motivational constructs are highly context-sensitive and tend to decline across school
years (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Adolescence, in particular, represents a
critical period for motivation development, as students typically

For the past several decades, SEVT literature has emphasized
examining the interplay of motivation constructs. Accumulated findings
have demonstrated that, when expectancy of success is low, high task
values can sometimes reduce adaptive learning outcomes such as
achievement (Trautwein et al., 2012) or contribute to negative outcomes
like heightened anxiety and reliance on superficial study strategies (Lee
et al., 2014; Song & Chung, 2020). Given the potential for complex interactions among motivational beliefs within each learner, investigations into possible combinations of expectancy-value beliefs in
the adolescent population carry a particular significance.
1.2.1. Person-centered approaches to student motivation
A commonly adopted method to fulfill such a goal is the personcentered approach, specifically the latent profile analysis (LPA). Many
LPA studies have been conducted within the SEVT framework by
treating either self-efficacy, values, and certain types of costs (e.g., Jiang
& Zhang, 2023; Lee, 2023; Conley, 2012, also included achievement
goals) or self-concept, values, and/or costs (e.g., Dietrich & Lazarides,
2019; Lazarides et al., 2019; Raufelder et al., 2022; Widlund et al., 2024)
as indicators of adolescents’ motivation profiles. Self-efficacy, anxiety,
importance, and enjoyment (e.g., Wang et al., 2018) have also been used
to form math motivation profiles. A majority of these studies have
detected profiles with low/moderate/high self-efficacy and values,
coupled with high/moderate/low cost or anxiety, respectively.
However, research showing interaction effects between expectancy
and value constructs challenges the representativeness of these simple
profile patterns. For example, Widlund et al. (2024) reported that 25%
of Finnish ninth graders displayed a profile with high cost, low selfconcept and interest, and moderate utility and attainment values. Dietrich and Lazarides (2019) discovered that 4.5% of German ninth and
tenth graders espoused low self-concept and low intrinsic and utility
values, but their attainment value was only moderate than low, and their
cost was also only moderate than high. Lee (2023) found that 18.3% of
Korean middle and high school students can be characterized by high
attainment and utility values accompanied by high cost and low intrinsic
value. Findings thus point to the inherently complex nature of students’
motivation profiles and the utility of LPA in uncovering them.
1.2.2. Need for cross-cultural person-centered studies
While a substantial amount of LPA research has been conducted
around the world, evidence is lacking for the cross-national generalizability of observed math motivation profiles among adolescent learners.
To maximize both the theoretical and practical implications of several
unique expectancy-value profiles (Dietrich & Lazarides, 2019; Lee,
2023; Widlund et al., 2024), it is essential to examine their cross-cultural
generalizability along with general and country-specific antecedents of
identified profiles. Consistent with the SEVT’s emphasis on cultural
milieu as a primary foundation of motivation (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020),
attention can be directed toward person-centered and structure-oriented
cross-cultural findings beyond variable-centered and level-oriented results (e.g., Li et al., 2024). The extant literature has yet to provide evidence of similarities and differences in adolescent math expectancyvalue profiles across different countries.
Few LPA investigations disclosed country-specific profiles (e.g.,
across three countries in Fan et al., 2019), region-specific profiles (e.g.,
in Western and Eastern countries in Li et al., 2024), or the general
variance of latent profiles across multiple countries (e.g., 79 countries in
Lee & Stankov, 2023). Among these studies, only Li et al. is based on

1.3. Academic outcomes of math motivation
The desirability of latent profiles can be determined based on how
the profiles explain important math outcomes. The adaptive motivation
profile with higher expectancy and value and/or low cost, for example,
is often accompanied by greater engagement (Jiang & Zhang, 2023;
Kang et al., 2024), more effort (Lazarides et al., 2019), stronger aspiration toward STEM career and math-related educational paths (Fong
et al., 2021; Widlund et al., 2024), as well as better test performance in
math (Jiang & Zhang, 2023; Raufelder et al., 2022). Through their relationships with various outcomes, groups identified by LPA allow easy
diagnosis of their relative strengths and weaknesses.
Profiles can consist of both positive and negative indicators. Relying
on a single outcome when assessing the desirability of each profile may
thus prove misleading. A seemingly beneficial profile may predict
greater engagement without necessarily predicting higher achievement
(Jiang & Zhang, 2023), or it may result in less engagement than another
profile defined by high attainment value and moderate intrinsic and
utility values (Kang et al., 2024). Accurate evaluation of identified
profiles hence requires multiple outcomes. In the present study, we
included learners’ math achievement, engagement, and orientations
toward STEM careers as outcomes against which to assess the comparative merit of latent profiles.

2. Present study
Our primary aim was to identify math motivation profiles among
adolescents and investigate their associations with potential covariates
and outcomes. The other purpose was to examine country-level differences in latent profiles and their relationships with other variables, to
test the generalizability of our findings. The following research questions guided our study.
2.1. Research questions
First, what are the typical math motivation profiles among 15-yearolds? We sought to uncover the representative math motivation profiles
among the sample, based on their self-efficacy, anxiety, intrinsic value
(i.e., interest), and attainment value (i.e., perceived importance). We
expected that at least two subgroups would likely emerge—highly
motivated (high self-efficacy/value and low anxiety) and unmotivated
(low self-efficacy/value and high anxiety; Hypothesis 1a). In addition,
we anticipated subgroups characterized by more complex patterns of
motivational beliefs, such as high self-efficacy/value accompanied by
high or moderate anxiety (e.g., Dietrich & Lazarides, 2019; Lee, 2023;
Widlund et al., 2024; Hypothesis 1b).
Second, do the profiles differ in their association with academic
outcomes in math? We examined whether the emerged profiles differ in
key outcomes. Profiles defined by positive motivation, such as high selfefficacy, high task values, and low anxiety, were hypothesized to be
associated with better engagement in both math classrooms and
homework completion, stronger intention to pursue STEM careers, and
higher math achievement (Hypothesis 2a). Specifically, students with
profiles characterized by high task value and low perceived cost were
expected to demonstrate more active engagement and stronger STEM
career intentions, whereas those with high expectancy were anticipated
to attain higher achievement, reflecting the stronger association between expectancy and achievement, as well as between task values and
choice (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). For profiles with negative motivation
(Hypothesis 2b), we expected the opposite pattern, such that students
with profiles characterized by high perceived cost would exhibit
avoidance-oriented behaviors, including lower engagement (Eccles &
Wigfield, 2020). We did not formulate specific hypotheses for other
potential profiles, except that, due to their mixed nature, they were
expected to be more beneficial than maladaptive profiles but less so than
adaptive ones.
Third, what are the personal and contextual factors that explain the
likelihood of each profile membership? We hypothesized that girls
(Hypothesis 3a) and students with a fixed mindset (Hypothesis 3b)
would be less likely to belong to an adaptive profile, depicted by relatively high self-efficacy and values, accompanied by low anxiety. Given
the significance of teachers for adolescent learners, we hypothesized
that students who perceived their teachers to provide general academic
support (Hypothesis 3c) as well as specific types of instructional support

1.4. Personal and contextual precursors of math motivation
Both personal and contextual factors play a critical role in shaping
individuals’ achievement motivation (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Personal characteristics that guide the subjective interpretation of experiences can influence one’s expectations of success and perceptions of task
value. Socializers like teachers also play a critical role in adolescents’
development of their abilities and task values. Accordingly, we included
students’ gender and fixed mindset as well as teacher-related variables
as antecedents of students’ math motivation profiles and examined potential between-country differences in their roles.
Literature is replete with evidence suggesting a gender gap in math
motivation. The gender gap typically manifests in girls’ underconfidence, low perceived value, and higher anxiety in math (Else-Quest
et al., 2010; Hill et al., 2016; Keller et al., 2022). Evidence from LPA
studies indicates that girls are less likely than boys to belong to more
adaptive profiles in math and STEM (e.g., Fong et al., 2021; Lazarides
et al., 2022). Moreover, findings from Finland (only in Grade 7; Raufelder et al., 2022) and Korea (Lee, 2023) suggest that girls may be more
likely to belong to a profile characterized by both high cost and high
value in math, highlighting the need for further investigation.
Mindset, or the belief in the malleability of intelligence (Dweck,
1986), is another significant personal factor that may explain profile
membership. Mindset governs how individuals interpret setbacks and
failures, influencing other motivational beliefs. Empirical findings show
that a growth mindset predicts higher self-efficacy, higher value, and
lower anxiety (Degol et al., 2018; Gonzalez-DeHass et al., 2024; Huang
et al., 2019). The role of mindset in math motivation profiles has rarely
been investigated (cf. Kang et al., 2024), and we wanted to ascertain its
explanatory utility for students’ membership in math motivation
profiles.
Contextual factors such as teachers’ behaviors and instructional
practices are also assumed to play a pivotal role in determining students’
profile membership. Teachers can support students’ math motivation by
academically facilitating students’ understanding (Johnson et al., 1983)
and emphasizing conceptual learning, such as mathematical reasoning
and the practicality of math content (Desimone et al., 2005), over mere
skill acquisition. From the PISA 2022 dataset, we included available

3. Method
3.1. Data and participants
We used the student data from PISA 2022 (OECD, 2023, 2024), a
triennial worldwide project aimed at assessing 15-year-old adolescents’
ability to employ knowledge and skills learned at school to solve real-life
problems. Participants were recruited through a stratified two-stage
sampling design. In total, 10,239 students from 242 schools in Finland
(48.8% girls), 6,116 students from 257 schools in Germany (48.9%
girls), 5,760 students from 182 schools in Japan (50.4% girls), 6,454
students from 186 schools in Korea (48.5% girls), and 4,552 students
from 154 schools in the U.S. (49.1% girls) were included in the present
study.
Depending on the starting age of compulsory education, 15-year-old
students in PISA 2022 were enrolled in grade levels ranging from Grade
7 to Grade 12. Most students, however, were in Grades 9 or 10, which
generally correspond to the final years of compulsory education in most
participating countries, including the five selected in the present study.
Further information about the data and participants can be found in the
technical report (OECD, 2024). The present study was exempted from
deliberation by the institutional review board (IRB) of the authors’
university (Exemption No. KUIRB-2025–0081-01) because it used the
preexisting dataset.

2.2. Countries included in the present study
Finland, Germany, Japan, Korea, and the U.S. were selected from the
PISA 2022 dataset for the following reasons. First, we aimed to include
Eastern and Western countries, which a majority of cross-cultural
studies compare (e.g., Li et al., 2024). Second, we focused on countries where a decent amount of evidence from motivation research exists, despite the scarcity of cross-cultural LPA research (cf. Fan et al.,
2019; Li et al., 2024). Third, we limited the selection to five OECD
members for a parsimonious yet comprehensible comparison. We
acknowledge that countries not analyzed in the present study may yield
different patterns due to cultural or system-specific reasons. For readers
who wish to explore this possibility, the Mplus syntax for the analytical
steps is provided in Supplementary Material A.
Among Western countries, we included one from North America and
two from Europe—Germany, Finland, and the U.S.—where a substantial
body of variable- and person-centered evidence on student motivation
and outcomes has accumulated (e.g., Dietrich & Lazarides, 2019; Fong
et al., 2021; Widlund et al., 2024). For Eastern countries, we selected
Korea and Japan due to existing scholarly interest in their unique
motivational patterns, such as lower confidence and higher anxiety
compared to Western counterparts despite higher achievement (Lee,
2009). These well-investigated countries were chosen to serve as anchors for generalizing our findings.
Although examining the role of educational systems was not the aim
of the current study, it is important to note that the five countries differ
in their tracking systems, instructional practices, and evaluation norms.
For example, tracking may have shaped the constellation of motivation
indexes, as the differentiation between vocational and academic tracks
could amplify variance in motivation (e.g., perceived importance)
among 15-year-old students. Compared to Finland, Germany, Japan,
and Korea, where between-school tracking is customary, tracking in the
U.S. is less prevalent and more strongly associated with socioeconomic
or regional differences (LeTendre et al., 2003). Even among countries
that employ tracking, the timing of its implementation varies considerably. In Germany, tracking typically begins at the end of primary
school, much earlier than in Korea and Japan, where it starts in middle
school, of in Finland, where it begins in junior high/lower secondary
school.
The centralization of curricula may also have influenced the role of
each covariate. Korea and Japan implement a centralized national curriculum, which renders their school systems relatively uniform. By
contrast, Finland, Germany, and the U.S. adopt decentralized or
partially centralized approaches (e.g., state-level curricula), which could
diversify the effects of instructional differences. Furthermore, standardized testing and normative grading play a more prominent role in
East Asian contexts compared to the other three countries (e.g., Shin
et al., 2018), potentially shaping the function of anxiety in those cultures. Such divergence may have fostered country-specific patterns,
though these will not be addressed in detail in this article.

3.2. Measures
We utilized the ready-made index variables provided by PISA 2022
whenever available. Because of the within-construct matrix sampling
design, in which only a subset of items was administered to each
participant, responses were scaled into the weighted likelihood estimates (WLE). These WLE scores were used for all variables except in the
case of single-item measures (see Supplementary Material B). Although
formal tests of measurement invariance across countries were not
possible due to the questionnaire design, the PISA 2022 technical report
indicated that over 90% of mathematics-related items demonstrated
invariance across countries (OECD, 2024). For further details, see the
technical report. Below, we outline the role of each variable along with a
sample item.
As math motivation indicators, we included self-efficacy (e.g., “How
confident in math tasks: Solving an equation like 3x + 5 = 17”), anxiety
(e.g., “I feel anxious about failing in mathematics.”), intrinsic value
(“Mathematics is one of my favourite subjects.”), and attainment value
(“I want to do well in my mathematics class.”). As math-related outcomes, math class engagement (e.g., This school year, how often: I
actively participated in group discussions during mathematics class.”),
time spent on math homework (“In a typical school week, approximately
how much time do you spend on homework in the mathematics?”),
STEM career intention (“What kind of job do you expect to have when
you are about 30 years old?;” responses recoded into STEM [1] and nonSTEM [0] occupations), and achievement on a math literacy test were
examined. Gender, fixed mindset (“Some people are just not good at
mathematics, no matter how hard they study.”), teacher support (e.g.,
“The teacher helps students with their learning.”), and teacher’s practice
fostering reasoning (e.g., “The teacher taught us to memorize rules and
apply them to solve mathematics problems.”) and encouraging thinking
(e.g., “The teacher showed us how mathematics can be useful in our
everyday lives.”) were treated as covariates of latent profiles. All items
specifically refer to mathematics.
3.3. Data analysis
Latent profile analysis (LPA) was performed using Mplus Version
8.10 to identify math motivation latent profiles in each country.
Maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors (MLR) and
full information maximum likelihood estimation (FIML) were applied.

Between 7.3% and 11.8% of the data was missing for the variables
analyzed in the present study, and the missingness was not completely at
random (MCAR), as indicated by Little’s MCAR test p < .001. The FIML
method addresses such missingness efficiently within the estimated
model and yields robust and unbiased estimates, comparable to other
accurate approaches for large-scale data with a modest proportion of
non-MCAR missingness (Gomer & Yuan, 2023; Köhler et al., 2017). No
auxiliary variables were included, given the complexity of the analyses.
Since students were recruited through multistage sampling, we
included the final student weight (W_FSTUWT) provided by OECD in all
analyses. It assigns the appropriate weight for each student to account
for the varying possibilities of being selected as a sample. By adjusting
sampling variance, the statistical estimates for the sample are more
likely to be generalized to the population. Furthermore, given the large
sample size, the statistical significance of all tests was conservatively
judged at p < .01 to ensure the robustness of the findings.
Standardizing the profile indicators may obscure the original measurement scale, thereby limiting our ability to interpret betweencountry differences in absolute terms. Nevertheless, we standardized
the variables to facilitate the identification of distinct motivational
profiles within each country. Before fitting the latent-profile models, we
centered the WLE scores of self-efficacy and anxiety around the country
mean and standardized the scores of intrinsic and attainment values into
z-scores. Accordingly, a score of zero indicated the country average.
To determine the optimal number of latent profiles, models with
varying numbers of profiles were sequentially fitted and compared using
information criteria, entropy, and the Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood
Ratio Test (LMR). We first compared models with varying numbers of
classes using fit statistics. Next, we evaluated entropy values, which
represent the accuracy with which individuals are classified into their
most likely class. We then examined the LMR statistics to assess whether
a more complex model (k profiles) provided a better fit than a more
parsimonious model (k-1 profiles). Finally, only theoretically meaningful profiles were retained. The characteristics of the latent profiles were
further explored using a manual three-step approach (Asparouhov &
Muthén, 2014).
The estimated means of the outcome variables across latent profiles
were compared using the Wald test. For this analysis, multiple imputations, rather than FIML, were applied to achievement data provided as
10 plausible values (PVs). Because each student completed only a subset
of the math literacy test to reduce respondent burden, test scores were
imputed based on other available information, including responses from
other cognitive domains and background data. When multiple imputations are applied, Mplus does not provide alternative results for all
possible contrasts of profiles. Therefore, covariate effects on student
membership in a given latent profile were assessed by averaging the
parameters obtained from the 10 manual three-step analyses, each
incorporating one of the 10 plausible values.

4.2. Identification of math motivation profiles
As can be seen in Table 1, the optimal number of profiles differed
across countries, suggesting configural variance. The four-profile solution displayed the best fit for Germany, Korea, and the U.S. The LMR
tests revealed that the four-profile solutions are statistically superior to
the three-profile solutions at p < .01. In Finland and Japan, in comparison, the five-profile solution indicated a statistically superior
depiction of the data (ps < .001) compared to the four-profile solution,
as judged by the LMR tests and information criteria. More importantly,
each profile in every country was theoretically meaningful. Although
the six-profile solution in Finland demonstrated a better fit to the data
than the five-profile solution, we considered it problematic because one
of the profiles included too few cases (0.4%).
Supporting our decision on the number of unique profiles, the
pattern of four profiles that emerged from the set of math expectancyvalue constructs is highly similar between countries with slight deviations (see Fig. 1; numerical information of each profile is in Supplementary Material C). Although the profiles may not be strictly invariant
across countries, the similarity in their patterns justified the application
of common labels. One profile was identified as the unmotivated and
indifferent subgroup, who lacked confidence in math, did not find math
interesting, and strongly believed that math is unimportant. The
smallest proportion of students belonged to this profile in all countries
except Finland, ranging from 5.9% in the U.S. to 11.2% in Finland. In
Korea, anxiety levels were slightly below the national average, unlike in
the other countries where they were at or slightly above the average; this
pattern suggests a state of complete amotivation. Similar, though
slightly different, profiles emerged in Finland and Japan. In Finland, an
additional unmotivated profile, comprising only 3% of the sample, was
identified. This profile exhibited even lower confidence than the unmotivated and indifferent subgroup and reported high levels of math
anxiety, leading us to label it the unmotivated and anxious subgroup. In
Japan, another unmotivated profile was detected, representing 16.7% of
the sample. While this subgroup also perceived math to be unimportant,
the belief was notably weaker than in the unmotivated and indifferent
subgroup; we thus labelled it simply as the unmotivated. Collectively,
these three profiles—unmotivated and indifferent, unmotivated and
anxious, and unmotivated—were referred to as “unmotivated” profiles
throughout the results and discussion sections.
The slightly unmotivated subgroup was the next common profile
across all countries. This subgroup was characterized by indicator scores
near the national average. While the indicator scores varied across
countries, falling either below or above the national average, the
perceived importance of math was consistently lower than the national
average in all countries. Aside from this, these students appear to exhibit
neither notable strengths nor weaknesses in their math motivation. For
students in Finland, the importance score was close to the country’s
mean, though still slightly below it. This profile was the most prevalent
in Finland (35.9%), Germany (36.2%), and the U.S. (37.0%), and the
second most prevalent in Korea (29.9%).
Whereas the preceding four profiles share below-average math
importance, the remaining two profiles are characterized by aboveaverage perceived importance of math. We named the next profile the
mixed profile because students who belonged to this subgroup showed
slightly low confidence and limited interest, yet recognized its importance and experienced some anxiety toward the subject. Although the
mixed profile had similar self-efficacy scores to the slightly unmotivated
profile in Germany, Korea, and the U.S., it was associated with higher
levels of math anxiety. The mixed profile is also the only profile where

4.3. Outcome differences across latent profiles
We examined the relative desirability of the latent profiles by
comparing their associations with the outcome variables. As Table 2
shows, the profiles differed significantly from each other in math
classroom engagement, time spent on math homework, STEM-related
career aspiration, and math achievement, with some variations across
countries. In all five countries, students with the highly motivated profile
engaged most actively in math classes, expressed the strongest intention
to pursue STEM-related careers, and achieved the highest scores in
math; those with the unmotivated profiles, in contrast, demonstrated the
lowest means in the same three outcomes. The two unmotivated profiles
in Finland and Japan functioned similarly. In Japan, the unmotivated and
indifferent profile engaged less actively in math class and homework than
the unmotivated profile, Bonferroni-corrected p < .0003. These results
support the characterization of the highly motivated and unmotivated
profiles as reflecting adaptive and maladaptive patterns, respectively.

5.1. Consistent patterns of adolescents’ math motivation profiles across
countries
We identified four common math motivation profiles across Finland,
Germany, Japan, Korea, and the U.S. Although not supported by formal
tests, these profiles displayed largely consistent patterns and had similar
proportions of students in each profile across countries, with Finland
and Japan being notable exceptions. In line with prior research (Jiang &
Zhang, 2023; Lee, 2023; Widlund et al., 2024) and our expectations, the
highly motivated profile was characterized by a positive pattern of
motivational beliefs, defined by high self-efficacy, high intrinsic and
attainment values, and low anxiety across all five countries. In comparison, the unmotivated and indifferent profile was characterized by a
generally negative pattern with few exceptions—specifically, low confidence and intrinsic value, markedly low attainment value, and moderate to low anxiety. Hypothesis 1a thus received strong support for the
positive profile, while support for the negative profile was weaker due to

5.1.2. Unique composition of the “mixed” profile among Finnish students
In Finland, the mixed profile emerged with a slightly different
motivational pattern. While math self-efficacy and anxiety stayed at
levels similar to those in other countries, those of the two value constructs were noticeably lower. Compared to students in other countries
with the same profile, the Finnish mixed group showed a greater lack of
interest in learning math and deemed doing well in math considerably
less important. Reflecting these motivational characteristics, Finnish
students with the mixed profile exhibited significantly lower math
achievement compared to those with the slightly unmotivated profile.
The Finnish mixed profile observed in the present study echoes the
results of Widlund et al. (2024), who found that roughly 22% of Finnish
ninth graders belonged to an indifferent group with below-average selfconcept and task values, especially intrinsic value, along with average
costs. Taken together with the present findings, the evidence indicates
that interest, or intrinsic value, may play a relatively stronger role in
Finnish adolescents’ math motivation than in other countries. Consistent
with this conjecture, the unmotivated and indifferent profile in Finland
was also defined by considerably lower math interest compared to the
same profile in the remaining four countries. As will be discussed in the
next section, Finnish students’ math motivation also depended more
heavily on their math teachers’ encouraging mathematical thinking in
everyday contexts rather than fostering mathematical reasoning in
formal instructional settings. Whether this difference accounts for the
unique composition of math motivation profiles among Finnish adolescents remains to be explored.

5.1.1. Cross-national variabilities in the profile-outcome linkages
While the four profiles—highly motivated, mixed, slightly unmotivated,
and unmotivated and indifferent—emerged consistently across countries,
their associations with math outcomes varied between countries. Crossnational differences were especially prominent concerning the mixed
profile. In Finland, Germany, and Korea, the mixed profile appeared
relatively adaptive despite the high levels of anxiety and the low levels
of intrinsic value. For adolescent learners in these three countries, the
recognition of math as an important subject seems to have mitigated the
potentially negative impact of low interest and high anxiety. Consequently, they maintained relatively active engagement in math, as reflected in their significantly higher class participation and significantly
longer time spent on math homework compared to students in various
unmotivated profiles. Similar findings have been reported for Chinese
(Jiang & Zhang, 2023) and a different group of Korean adolescents (Lee,
2023).
In contrast, U.S. students with the mixed motivational profile reported significantly less engagement in math classes and significantly
less time spent on math homework than those with the slightly unmotivated profile. These students thus appear to be hindered by high anxiety
and low interest in math, despite recognizing its importance. This suggests that alleviating math anxiety and preventing the development of
the mixed profile may be especially pressing in the U.S. At the same time,
the observed cross-national differences in homework engagement may
also be explained by differences in educational systems (e.g., Dettmers
et al., 2009). In the U.S., secondary school students can select mathematics courses with varying academic demands (e.g., Prealgebra,

In Finland and Japan, varying forms of unmotivated profiles emerged—the unmotivated and anxious profile in Finland and the unmotivated
profile in Japan. One might question the necessity of identifying these
two profiles, given their resemblance to the unmotivated and indifferent

supported not only by statistical indices but also by the substantial
sample size (i.e., as many as 274 students categorized into the unmotivated and anxious profile, comprising 3% of the Finnish sample) and the

In support of our decision, the unmotivated profile identified in Japan
demonstrated significantly more adaptive functioning compared to the
unmotivated and indifferent profile, especially with respect to engagement. Covariates further demonstrated distinct utility in explaining
membership in the two groups. Likewise, in Finland, an unmotivated and
anxious group emerged. Unexpectedly, however, this group did not
differ statistically from the unmotivated and indifferent group, despite its
exceptionally low self-efficacy and remarkably high anxiety. This suggests that high anxiety did not necessarily exacerbate maladaptive behaviors beyond the effects of low intrinsic and attainment values. As
speculated earlier, this may reflect a potential motivating function of
anxiety among Finnish students.

devoted as much time to homework as those in the highly motivated
profile, whereas students in the mixed profile devoted even more time.

provides a useful lens for interpreting our findings: students in the unmotivated and anxious profile demonstrated high attainment value (likely
linked to perfectionistic striving) alongside high anxiety (possibly
reflecting perfectionistic concerns), with the mixed profile showing a
similar though weaker pattern. For these students, anxiety may have
acted as a motivating force.

instructional practices linking math to everyday life predicted students’

perception that teachers fostered mathematical reasoning during formal
math problem-solving demonstrated significant classifying power in
Germany, Japan, Korea, and the U.S., but not in Finland.
We speculate that these cross-national differences may reflect variations in instructional and assessment norms. In Korea and Japan, the
exam-driven nature of the mathematics curriculum compels teachers to
emphasize procedural knowledge to prepare students for standardized
assessments (Leung, 2006; Wu & Zhang, 2006). Within such contexts,
students may perceive instruction that promotes the application of
mathematics to everyday problems as less beneficial than approaches
that foster mathematical reasoning directly tied to problem solving.
Also, in Germany, where mathematics education traditionally emphasizes proving mathematical theories, classroom practices often involve
the discussion of mathematical concepts and methods. In contrast, the
Finnish education system prioritizes flexibility, creativity, and relatively
limited standardization and testing (Sahlberg, 2007). Consequently,
instructional strategies that integrate mathematics into real-life contexts
are consistent with Finland’s broader educational philosophy and
appear more effective in supporting students’ math motivation.
Such differences are also evident in PISA 2022. According to the
country-specific descriptive statistics reported in Supplementary Material C, students in Germany and Japan were more likely to report that
their teachers emphasized mathematical reasoning in formal instructional contexts than the application of mathematics to everyday situations. In Korea and the U.S., both practices were observed to similar
degrees, though fostering reasoning was slightly more prevalent. The
opposite was true in Finland. Therefore, the more salient a particular
instructional practice was to students, the more strongly it functioned as
a classifier of the profiles. Our findings further suggest that teachers may
need to tailor their instructional strategies to their specific educational
contexts to motivate students more effectively in math. That said, and as
discussed in the following section, we caution that it is equally possible
that students in more adaptive profiles perceive greater support from
their teachers and are thus more responsive to instruction.

5.2. Personal characteristics associated with profile membership
Across countries, being a boy meant a greater likelihood of being
classified into the highly motivated profile than the mixed (except in the
U.S.) or the slightly unmotivated (except in Korea) profile. This is
consistent with the previous findings that boys are usually more confident and interested in math (Else-Quest et al., 2010; Keller et al., 2022)
and also with our Hypothesis 3a. Unexpectedly, boys were also more
likely than girls to belong to the unmotivated and indifferent profile than
the slightly unmotivated (except in Finland) or the mixed (except in Japan
and the U.S.) profile. Raufelder et al. (2022) found that Finnish seventhgrade boys were more than twice as likely to belong to either the “disengaged relaxed” or the “positively engaged” profile than to the
“struggling ambitious” profile, the distinguishing feature of the latter
being high perceived cost. The researchers suggested that this may
indicate that boys are generally more relaxed than girls during school
transitions. This explanation is consistent with our observation that boys
were disproportionately represented in the unmotivated and indifferent
profile compared to other unmotivated profiles with higher anxiety,
including the mixed profile, the unmotivated profile in Japan, and the
unmotivated and anxious profile in Finland. These findings collectively
suggest that boys may be overrepresented at both ends of the motivational spectrum, thereby challenging the assumption that only girls are
at a disadvantage. Accordingly, motivational support strategies should
be tailored to address the needs of all genders.
Across all five countries, mindset emerged as another strong covariate of profile membership, consistent with Hypothesis 3b. Endorsing a
growth mindset or, conversely, rejecting a fixed mindset, seemed almost
essential for students to be positively motivated in math. In nearly all
cases, students who held a fixed mindset were significantly less likely to
belong to the highly motivated profile than to any other profile. Because
the highly motivated group achieved the most desirable outcomes, educators may wish to foster students’ belief in the malleability of math
ability. These findings also highlight the potential benefits of interventions that simultaneously address growth mindset and
expectancy-value constructs, rather than addressing each in isolation (e.
g., Gaspard et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2021).

single-item measurement of intrinsic value and attainment value falls
short of capturing every aspect of the constructs. For example, the item
“I want to do well in my mathematics class” may not sufficiently capture
how math is essential to one’s identity (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). It may
also tap utility value, as the usefulness of math may have facilitated the

our study does represent the critical aspect of attainment value, as the

limitation, as the two constructs may not necessarily share the same
antecedents or consequences. Math anxiety is typically conceptualized
as a stable emotional trait, whereas cost represents a more contextdependent evaluation of task demands and outcomes. Accordingly,
strategies to mitigate anxiety may differ from those that reduce
perceived emotional cost. In addition, despite the considerable expertise
of the PISA research team in administering large-scale cross-national
surveys, the limited number of items employed constrained our ability to
establish the psychometric validity of the constructs. Replication of the
present findings with culturally validated measures is therefore
warranted.

believe including all countries in the analysis would have provided a
better perspective, especially since this is one of the first attempts of its
kind. That is why we share Mplus syntax in Supplementary Material A, so
interested readers can obtain results for any country of interest in the
PISA dataset. We are also aware that our interpretations may lack

school-, region-, and country-specific education systems and practices,
we thought it would be better to leave further interpretations and applications of our findings to educators within each context.

5.5. Educational implications

Funding

Several practical implications can nonetheless be derived from the
present study. Given the emergence of four distinct motivational profiles
across countries, teachers and schools should move away from the onesize-fits-all approach to motivating students to learn math. Teachers
should not assume that students with any single desirable motivational
characteristic, such as the perceived importance of math, will automatically adopt an adaptive approach to math. Other motivational dimensions, such as math anxiety, may hinder their engagement.
Moreover, our findings suggest that math confidence may be the most
crucial element for teachers to work on, as it sets apart the highly motivated, most adaptive profile from the remaining profiles.
Teachers should also consider employing differentiated instructional
strategies that take into account the educational culture and norms of
their country. As previously discussed, the effectiveness of any specific
instructional method, such as promoting problem-solving strategies or
encouraging the application of math outside of school, may depend on
how well it aligns with existing practices and educational objectives in
each country. Educators are cautioned against uncritically adopting
math instructional strategies validated elsewhere without adaptation, as
a culturally sensitive approach is recommended.

6. Conclusions
Analysis of PISA 2022 data revealed four distinct math motivation
profiles—highly motivated, mixed, slightly unmotivated, and unmotivated
and indifferent—consistently present across Finland, Germany, Japan,
Korea, and the U.S., with further variations detected in Finland and
Japan. Overall, the relative desirability of the profiles and the factors
contributing to profile membership were also similar across countries,
with math self-efficacy, mindset, and teachers’ general academic support emerging as key factors in students’ membership in adaptive math
motivation profiles. Our findings once again highlight the significance of
expectancy-value constructs and point to the value of both globally
applicable and country-specific approaches to fostering student motivation in mathematics.
