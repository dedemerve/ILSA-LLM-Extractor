Keywords:

Large-scale assessment
Multilevel modeling

Despite a substantial body of research examining the relationships between ICT-related factors
and student academic performance, there exists a notable gap in the literature regarding how
students’ ICT use is associated with their complex problem-solving competency. This study aims
to address this gap by exploring the connections between complex problem-solving competency
and various ICT-related factors both in the home and school contexts. To unravel these relationships, this study employed a three-level multilevel modeling (MLM) analysis to analyze a
large-scale dataset gathered from 33 countries and economies participating in the Program for
International Student Assessment (PISA) in 2012. The final sample for data analysis included
194,701 14-year-old students from 900 schools. The results of the MLM analysis demonstrated
that students who exhibited a greater openness for problem-solving were more likely to be
complex problem-solving competent. However, the ICT availability at school and home and the
ICT use intensity at school were negatively related to students’ complex problem-solving performance. Overall, in comparison with the factors within the student/home context, school ICTrelated factors are less strongly associated with student complex problem-solving competency.
Possible explanations for and implications of the findings were discussed.

students’ CPS competency is especially needed for their future career success (OECD, 2014). In this regard, extensive international
assessment and evaluation practices have deemed problem-solving a core competency of student development. For instance, the

Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), an international study assessing students’ academic achievement in reading,
science, and mathematics, has evaluated student problem-solving since 2003. To address the multidimensional nature of
problem-solving, information and communication technology (ICT) has been increasingly used to support the assessment of
problem-solving in the 21st century (Redecker & Johannessen, 2013), which goes beyond traditional assessment paradigms (Hu et al.,
2017). Specifically, as problem-solving involves multiple skill and knowledge components, international educational evaluation
programs mostly assess students’ problem-solving skills through computer-based assessments, which are advantageous in simulating
real-life scenarios where students solve complex tasks. Nowadays, since students need to rely on ICT to solve complex real-life
problems, how ICT-related factors are associated with student CPS competency needs to be examined with more empirical evidence.
Nevertheless, existing research on student problem-solving primarily focused on probing problem-solving processes through analytics of data from various sources (e.g., process data, Gao et al., 2022; qualitative content analysis, Kwon et al., 2021; eye-tracking
data; Hu et al., 2017) to profile and understand students’ problem-solving levels and strategies. Another strand of research on
problem-solving focused on the effects of pedagogical interventions or programs on students’ problem-solving-related outcomes (e.g.,
Battistich et al., 1989; Song, 2018). In contrast, despite many studies probing the relationship between ICT and academic performance
(Odell et al., 2020), relatively less research has examined the associations between contextual factors, especially ICT-related factors,
with student CPS competency. In addition, existing studies have identified mixed results for the relationships between ICT-related
factors and educational outcomes, depending on the outcome subject and the context where the data was collected (Odell et al.,
2020). Moreover, most of the previous ICT-related findings were based on region-level or small-scale analyses with limited generalizability to other contexts. Therefore, given a lack of empirical studies and the controversial effects of ICT on other educational
outcomes, this study aims to scrutinize the relationship between ICT and students’ CPS competency with the analytical approach of
multilevel linear modeling analyzing a large-scale dataset involving 33 countries and economies.
1.1. Complex problem solving
CPS is distinguished from static problem-solving by its dynamic, partially intransparent, non-routine, and interactive characteristics (Frensch & Funke, 1995). As modern life becomes increasingly complex, CPS has emerged as a crucial competency for the 21st
century, recognized in frameworks like the PISA 2012 Problem Solving Framework (OECD, 2013a). The PISA 2012 framework defined
problem-solving competency as “an individual’s capacity to engage in cognitive processing to understand and resolve problem situations where a method of solution is not immediately obvious. It includes the willingness to engage with such situations in order to
achieve one’s potential as a constructive and reflective citizen.” (OECD, 2013a, p. 122). This definition highlights that the cognitive
processes and skills involved in problem-solving (e.g., memorization, comprehension, application, analysis, evaluation, and creativity)
are domain-general and can be used across a variety of contexts. Moreover, students’ affective and psychosocial components (e.g.,
motivation and attitudes), as well as their interconnections with other cognitive processes and skills, are also involved in
problem-solving (Hu et al., 2017; OECD, 2014).
A group of researchers has primarily conceptualized CPS as a purely cognitive ability, emphasizing psychometric improvements in
CPS measurement tools (Funke, 2014; Funke et al., 2017; Schoppek & Fischer, 2015). For example, distinguishing CPS as a
two-dimensional construct, knowledge acquisition and application has demonstrated better predictive power than reasoning in predicting academic performance (Greiff et al., 2013; Wüstenberg et al., 2012). Debate persists on the relationship between CPS and
intelligence, with studies showing varying correlations from weak to strong (Stadler et al., 2015). In response to this debate, Stadler
et al. (2019) proposed a four-dimensional CPS model, comprising connectivity detection, dynamics detection, connectivity handling,
and dynamics handling, finding that the two dynamic dimensions account for variability in correlations with intelligence.
The traditional “classical CPS” model (e.g., “Lohhausen”), which includes over 2000 variables, has been largely replaced nowadays
by “multiple or minimal complex systems” (MCS) that focus on simpler dynamic situations (Greiff, Stadler et al., 2015). Some scholars
argue that an excessive focus on psychometric rigor neglects other essential dimensions of CPS (Dörner & Funke, 2017; Gyaurov et al.,
2022). For example, Beckmann and Goode (2017) proposed a tripartite complexity framework to conceptualize the complexity
dimension of CPS, considering information cues from Person (determined by individuals’ subjective reflection of complexity related to
their ability and motivation), Task (determined by system characteristics), and Situation (determined by the circumstances in which
the given task is to be performed). By distinguishing complexity from different sources, this model may help to facilitate an integrative
view of CPS research findings over several decades (Beckmann & Goode, 2017). In addition, Dörner and Funke (2017) emphasized
CPS’s real-world applicability, defining it as “a collection of self-regulated psychological processes and activities necessary in dynamic
environments to achieve ill-defined goals that cannot be reached by routine actions.” This definition highlights CPS as a self-regulated
process incorporating cognitive, emotional, and motivational aspects, especially under high-stakes conditions.
Moreover, CPS definitions incorporate non-cognitive factors, acknowledging that complex problems cannot be addressed by
cognitive skills alone. Fischer and Neubert (2015) describe problem-solving competency as “a bundle of skills, knowledge, and abilities
required to deal effectively with complex, dynamic, non-routine situations across various domains.” Their definition integrates
cognitive elements (e.g., analytical skills) and non-cognitive factors (e.g., motivation, affect). Similarly, Bubnic et al. (2024)
conceptualized CPS as a combination of cognitive, metacognitive (e.g., goal setting, self-regulation), and affective (e.g., attitudes,
beliefs) components, reflecting the growing consensus that successful CPS requires a holistic approach.
In summary, CPS competency has evolved from a primarily cognitive construct to a complex, multi-dimensional skill set,
encompassing cognitive, emotional, and motivational aspects essential for dynamic, real-world problem-solving. Unlike being treated
as a process targeted for achieving other educational goals in the past (S. Greiff et al., 2013; Mayer, 1990), it has been regarded as an
expected educational outcome nowadays (OECD, 2013a; van Merrienboer, 2013). In particular, the PISA 2012 framework situated the

with diverse educational outcomes (Herde et al., 2016).
1.2. ICT in education
Despite significant interest in ICT research across various fields over the past decade, there remains a lack of consensus in the
literature on how to conceptualize and measure ICT constructs (He et al., 2021). Major definitions of ICT in education still largely align
with Blurton’s (1999) foundational description: “ICT is a diverse set of technological tools and resources used to communicate, and to create,
disseminate, store, and manage information.” For example, Zhang et al. (2008) defined ICT as “technologies used by people and organizations for their information processing and communication purposes” (p. 628). Aucoin (2011)) defined ICT as “technological tools
used to manipulate and communicate information, such as recording media (e.g., CDs/DVDs), broadcasting systems (e.g., radio, television),
computing hardware and software (e.g., World Wide Web, Web 2.0, email), and mobile network devices (e.g., cell phones, smartphones).”
Kaware & Sain (2015)) similarly describe ICT as “a combination of technological tools and resources that are used to manipulate and
communicate information.” Over time, ICT definitions in education have expanded to incorporate diverse tools, including
general-purpose technologies (Cruz-Jesus et al., 2016) and web-based systems like Web 2.0 applications for knowledge-sharing (the
communication tools and web-based system especially the Web 2.0 to support teachers in teaching process for acquiring, sharing, and creating
knowledge,” Razak et al., 2019).
These definitions generally position ICT as an umbrella term encompassing various general-purpose technologies, often focusing
more on technology itself than on its broader impact on information access and communication. Tamilselvan and Sivakumar (2019),
however, argue that the significance of ICT lies not in the technology itself but in its potential to provide greater access to information
and communication, particularly for underserved populations. They define ICT as “the use of information in order to meet human needs or
purposes, including the use of contemporary devices such as the Internet” (Tamilselvan & Sivakumar 2019). Similarly, Hatlevik and Hatlevik (2018), within the OECD (2014) framework, propose that ICT in education should be viewed as a transversal skill embedded in
various curriculum subjects.
2. Theoretical background
In this study, our focus on the associations of ICT availability and ICT use with student CPS competency draws on DIGCOMP, a
framework of ICT literacy for developing and understanding digital competence in Europe (Ferrari, 2013). The DIGCOMP framework,
a five-level conceptualization of ICT literacy, was originally developed based on a comprehensive review of 15 frameworks. The first
level of DIGCOMP indicates the five major competence areas: Information, Communication, Content Creation, Safety, and ProblemSolving. The review by Siddiq et al. (2016) revised the framework by including a new competence area of Technical Operational Skills.
Specific to the area of problem-solving in the revised DIGCOMP framework, it consists of the following four competencies: solving
problems with use of digital technology, collaborative problem solving, innovating and creatively using technology, and identifying digital
competence gaps (Siddiq et al., 2016). As the most transversal area of the DIGCOMP framework, problem-solving is intertwined with all
other competence areas (Ferrari, 2013), implying a significant interplay between ICT and problem-solving. To separate the measurement of problem-solving from existing ICT literacy assessments, the revised DIGCOMP framework by Siddiq et al. (2016) adopted
the definition of problem-solving proposed by the PISA 2012 framework. Therefore, our study adopted the PISA 2012 definition, given
its interconnections to other ICT competencies in previous conceptual frameworks.
3. Literature review
3.1. ICT-related factors
Since problem-solving is conceptualized as a key competence area of ICT literacy and problem-solving tasks are often situated in
computer-based contexts, student problem-solving competency cannot be examined separately from ICT-related factors (Kuo &
Hwang, 2015; Lai & Hwang, 2014). International large-scale assessment exercises, such as PISA, conceptualize ICT use as a multidimensional construct, typically categorized into ICT availability at home and school, ICT use for school-related tasks, and ICT
entertainment use (OECD, 2014, 2018). While links between ICT factors and general educational outcomes have been extensively
reported in the literature (OECD, 2014; Bulut & Cutumisu, 2018; Erdogdu & Erdogdu, 2015; Li & Petersen, 2022), fewer studies have
explicitly examined how ICT-related factors shape student problem-solving competencies (e.g., Kuo & Hwang, 2015; Lai & Hwang,
2014; Liem et al., 2014; Odell et al., 2020; Siddiq & Scherer, 2019).
For instance, Liem et al. (2014) showed that ICT use in arts-related learning activities positively impacted problem-solving skills
among 15-year-old students in PISA 2003. Similarly, Kuo and Hwang (2015) also reported that ICT use significantly enhanced students’ intention to engage in online learning, and subsequently improving their problem-solving skills. Despite these findings, research
remains limited and inconclusive regarding how different ICT-related factors contribute to student problem-solving competency,

3.1.1. ICT availability at home and school
ICT availability refers to the presence of digital tools and resources accessible to students at home or in school settings. ICT
availability is frequently assessed through international large-scale studies such as PISA. Over the past four PISA cycles (2012–2022),
the conceptualization and measurement of ICT availability at home have remained largely consistent with slight changes. From PISA
2012 to PISA 2018, ICT availability at home was measured by listing the digital devices students had access to. However, PISA 2022
broadened this scope to include devices available outside school settings, emphasizing how frequently students use them.
For ICT availability in schools, PISA 2012 focused on the basic presence of ICT devices (e.g., computers, projectors). By 2015 and
2018, the focus expanded to include internet connectivity and interactive devices or systems, such as data projectors, interactive
whiteboards, and storage space for school-related data. In PISA 2022, the emphasis shifted from simple availability to the frequency of
use, including more modern technologies like smartphones. Additionally, PISA 2022 introduced the assessment of educational software, games, apps, and learning management systems or school-based learning platforms.
Although studies have reported a positive correlation between ICT availability, especially effortless access to computers and the
internet at home, and academic performance (Bulut & Cutumisu, 2018; Erdogdu & Erdogdu, 2015; Mediavilla & Escardibul, 2015),
findings remain mixed across different contexts in the literature. For example, Luu and Freeman (2011) found no significant association between ICT access and science literacy in Canadian and Australian households, while Hu et al. (2018) reported a negative
correlation between ICT availability at home and student science achievement.
Studies analyzing the PISA data have consistently shown a positive correlation between ICT availability at school and student
achievement (Mediavilla & Escardibul, 2015). In contrast, the positive relationship between ICT availability at home and academic
achievement did not always hold in the literature (e.g., Hu et al., 2018). These inconsistencies indicate that psychological and
behavioral factors, such as students’ autonomy in ICT use and engagement in self-directed learning, may mediate how ICT availability
shapes learning outcomes (Li & Petersen, 2022). While substantial research has explored the role of ICT availability in academic
performance (e.g., Arpacı et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2018; Kolikant, 2012; Li & Petersen, 2022), few studies have investigated its connections with problem-solving competency. Since problem-solving often requires students to effectively use available digital tools in
complex learning environments, understanding this relationship is crucial for improving student CPS competency through ICT-based
intervention.
3.1.2. ICT use for school-related tasks
A key indicator assessing students’ ICT use behaviors in international assessment programs is their ICT use at home for schoolrelated tasks, such as communicating with teachers via emails, digitally submitting homework and school assignments, and accessing learning materials on the school’s website (OECD, 2014, 2018). In terms of changes to the definition and measurement of ICT use
for school-related tasks, PISA 2012 emphasized the use of computers for various educational purposes. PISA 2015 expanded this focus
to include “digital devices” for similar activities. PISA 2018 further broadened the scope by incorporating the use of learning apps and
educational websites. By PISA 2022, the focus had shifted towards a wider range of educational activities, emphasizing the purpose of
ICT use in schools, its application across different subjects, and its role in providing learning support.
Research on ICT use at home for school-related tasks has presented inconsistent findings (Bulut & Cutumisu, 2018; Hu et al., 2018;
Kolikant, 2012). For example, Sit et al. (2017) found that ICT use variables, especially ICT use at home for school-related tasks, were
positively associated with Macau students’ digital problem-solving skills. However, analyzing the PISA 2015 data, Hu et al. (2018)
identified negative associations between students’ ICT use for school-related tasks beyond the school context and academic performance. Like home ICT use for school-related tasks, the role of use of ICT at school was primarily discussed for student academic success
with mixed findings (Kuo & Hwang, 2015; Li & Petersen, 2022; Luu & Freeman, 2011; Woessmann & Fuchs, 2004). For example,
analyzing the PISA 2015 Finland data, Saarinen et al. (2021) identified a negative association between frequent use of ICT at school
and students’ cognitive learning outcomes. Their findings show that mere proficiency with digital devices is inadequate for mastering
the necessary skills for appropriate ICT use, which is essential for improved learning outcomes. However, more empirical evidence is
needed to clarify further how school ICT use shapes students’ development of problem-solving skills (Wilson et al., 2008).
3.1.3. ICT entertainment use
ICT entertainment use refers to student activities relevant to ICT in their leisure time, such as gaming, using social media and
browsing internet content for entertainment (OECD, 2014, 2018). The conceptualization and measurement of ICT entertainment use
have evolved over time in PISA. PISA 2012 focused on the frequency with which students used computers for leisure activities. In PISA
2015, the focus shifted to digital devices, retaining most of the 2012 activities while expanding the list to include new activities such as
playing online games via social networks and downloading apps on mobile devices. PISA 2018 maintained consistency with the
approach used in PISA 2015. By PISA 2022, the emphasis shifted to the duration of time students spent on these activities, with an
additional focus on differentiating between entertainment use during weekdays and weekends rather than exclusively outside school
settings.
The impact of ICT entertainment use on learning outcomes is found to be highly context-dependent (Hatos, 2020). A few studies
have found that ICT entertainment use is negatively associated with academic achievement (Bulut & Cutumisu, 2018; Hatos, 2020; Hu
et al., 2018). For example, Alderete and Formichella (2016) found that higher engagement with ICT for entertainment was linked to
lower academic performance among Spanish students. Similarly, Bulut and Cutumisu (2018) found that excessive ICT entertainment
use negatively affected mathematics performance among Finnish students. However, some studies suggest that moderate ICT

entertainment use may benefit student academic outcomes. For example, in Italy and Turkey, students who engaged in ICT entertainment use more often at home performed better in mathematics (Bulut & Cutumisu, 2018). These findings echo the “Goldilocks
Hypothesis,” which suggests that moderate technology use may enhance cognitive development, whereas excessive use can be
detrimental (Przybylski & Weinstein, 2017).
Despite a large body of research on ICT entertainment use and academic achievement, its relationship with problem-solving
competency remains underexplored. Given that student interactions with digital contexts and online activities may foster their
cognitive development, more research is needed to investigate how ICT entertainment use contributes to CPS in educational contexts.
In addition to ICT-related factors, our study has examined two additional relevant student-level factors: openness for problemsolving and sense of belonging to school. This inquiry addresses the affective and psychosocial components of problem-solving
competency. Specifically, students’ willingness to engage in problem-solving tasks is reflected by the extent to which they are open
to problem-solving, and their sense of belonging to school is indicative of their relatedness to the school learning context where
problem-solving is primarily shaped. Previous studies have indicated that students’ openness for problem-solving is strongly associated
with their problem-solving performance. Moreover, as student learning mainly takes place in schools, students’ sense of belonging to
school was found to be strongly linked to various educational, developmental and psychological outcomes (OECD, 2017). Therefore, in
addition to the targeted ICT-related factors, this study includes openness for problem-solving and a sense of belonging to school as two
affective and psychosocial covariates. Moreover, informed by previous studies (e.g., Luu & Freeman, 2011; Senkbeil, 2022; Siddiq &
Scherer, 2019), our study included two widely examined demographic indicators, gender and economic, social, and cultural status
(ESCS), as covariates to account for students’ variance in CPS competency. As evidenced by extensive empirical findings, gender and
ESCS were found to significantly impact a wide range of educational outcomes (Bradley & Corwyn, 2002; Liu et al., 2020; Siddiq &
Scherer, 2019). The following sections provide a brief review of the associations between the above factors and CPS.
3.2. Openness for problem-solving
Openness for problem-solving is an important motivational factor that positively correlates with students’ problem-solving performance (OECD, 2014; Scherer & Gustafsson, 2015). Openness for problem-solving was conceptualized as the extent to which students are willing to engage in problem-solving based on their attitudes and personality (Schulze et al., 2005).
Openness for problem-solving is positively associated with problem-solving competency across different educational contexts
(Beaty et al., 2014; Lin et al., 2012; Scherer & Gustafsson, 2015). One explanation for this relationship is that open-minded individuals
tend to engage in divergent thinking (Lin et al., 2012), which fosters their ability to explore potential solutions to a problem. In
addition, students with higher openness are more likely to show greater behavioral and cognitive flexibility (Moutafi et al., 2006),
facilitating adaptive learning strategies. These findings suggest that educational interventions targeting openness may promote students’ ability to navigate CPS tasks in technology-rich environments. Given its significance, openness for problem-solving should be
considered a critical predictor of students’ CPS abilities.
3.3. Sense of belonging to school
Sense of belonging to school is defined as “the extent to which they feel accepted, respected, included and supported by others in
the school social environment” (Goodenow & Grady, 1993, pp. 60–61), a relationally derived psychosocial construct used to characterize the “sense of fit” or “feelings of acceptance” that a person experiences in their community (Bouchard & Berg, 2017).
A steady decline was observed in students’ sense of belonging to school (OECD, 2017, 2022) over the recent PISA cycles. Previous
studies have reported its potential effects on student learning and psychological outcomes. Students with a strong sense of belonging
tend to demonstrate higher levels of academic motivation, engagement, and resilience (Allen et al., 2021; Anderman, 2002). In
contrast, low belonging was found to be associated with increased dropout rates, social isolation, and psychological distress (Tseng
et al., 2022). The positive effects of sense of belonging to school on students’ psychological outcomes may in turn promote their
cognitive and academic outcomes (Goodenow & Grady, 1993; Kiang et al., 2020). Moreover, students’ sense of belonging to school can
be shaped by a variety of contextual factors such as school climate, teacher-student relationships, and family SES levels (Allen et al.,
2021, 2022).
However, despite much empirical evidence supporting the positive role of the sense of belonging to school in shaping students’
academic and psychological outcomes, little is known about its role in influencing students’ problem-solving competency. Given the
growing importance of social-emotional factors in education, it is crucial to examine how a sense of belonging shapes students’ CPS
performance.
3.4. Gender and socioeconomic status
Although many studies have shown that girls outperformed boys in problem-solving, studies within different contexts reported
mixed problem-solving gender gaps (e.g., Dindar, 2018; Hämäläinen et al., 2019; Li & Liu, 2017; Lin et al., 2012; Zhu, 2007). The
gender disparities are usually observed in high school and college settings and vary depending on the tasks of problem-solving assessments, which might be accounted for by a variety of biological, psychological, and environmental factors.
The relationship between students’ family socioeconomic status (SES) and a variety of educational outcomes has been extensively
documented in the literature. In contrast to the effects of SES on student academic achievement (Liu et al., 2020) and well-being
(Bradley & Corwyn, 2002), few studies have investigated the role of SES in shaping student problem-solving competency.

competency can be established based on previous findings on relevant concepts such as ICT literacy.
3.5. The current study
Our review has identified the following research gaps in the literature on ICT and CPS. First, despite a large body of research
focusing on the associations between ICT factors and student academic outcomes, little is known about the relationships between ICT
factors and student non-academic outcomes, such as CPS competency. Second, the literature reported mixed findings on the links of
ICT factors to academic outcomes across various contexts. Despite potential overlaps between CPS and academic outcomes in reading,
science, and mathematics, student CPS is not domain-specific and involves other ICT-related competencies required by the digital age.
This implies that the findings for academic outcomes may not directly apply to CPS. Therefore, further empirical investigations are
needed to examine the specific effects of ICT factors on CPS. Third, most of the previous quantitative studies on ICT and educational
outcomes are region- or nation-level analyses with statistical approaches such as multiple regression analysis and MLM. A comprehensive investigation across multiple countries and regions with a large-scale dataset is needed to improve the generalizability of the
findings on the ICT effects. Therefore, the primary purpose of the current study is to probe the relationships between ICT-related
factors and student CPS competency. To maximize the generalizability of our findings, this study analyzed a large-scale dataset
involving 33 countries and economies participating in the PISA 2012 study.
Despite more recent PISA cycles, this study strategically focuses on PISA 2012 due to the following considerations. First, PISA 2012
is the only wave that directly and comprehensively assessed student CPS skills, making it the most relevant and the largest dataset for
CPS studies. Later cycles, such as PISA 2015, have focused on other higher-level skills such as collaborative problem-solving and
creative thinking, which are less directly applicable to our research objectives. Second, while ICT-related indicators evolved in PISA
2015 and 2018, the key constructs of PISA 2012 such as ICT availability and ICT use for school-related tasks remained largely
comparable across cycles. Finally, empirical precedents on the relationships between ICT and problem-solving competencies (e.g.,
Claro et al., 2015; Siddiq & Scherer, 2019) have indicated the suitability of PISA 2012 data for addressing research questions in our
study.
PISA 2012 adopted a multi-stage sampling approach for data collection. As students in the same school/country might be more
alike than students in different schools/countries, the PISA data has a nested structure. Therefore, this study adopted a comprehensive,
rigorous statistical approach, MLM, to identify the directions and strengths of the links of ICT factors to CPS competency. Compared
with linear regression, MLM can account for the potential effects of schools and countries on CPS performance (i.e., group-level
variance) and improve the accuracy of parameter estimates. However, it should be noted that despite addressing the nested nature
of the data with MLM, our study focuses on the effects of ICT factors on CPS at the student level. Due to its scope, the study does not
address the effects of school- and country-level ICT factors. Specific research questions addressed in the current study are as follows.
• After accounting for the effects of student demographic variables (i.e., gender and SES), do student/home factors, including ICTrelated factors (i.e., ICT availability at home, ICT use at home for school-related tasks, and ICT entertainment use) and openness for
problem-solving, predict CPS competency across 33 countries and economies?
• After accounting for the effects of student demographic variables (i.e., gender and SES), do student/school factors, including ICTrelated factors (i.e., ICT availability at school and use of ICT at school) and sense of belonging to school, predict CPS competency
across 33 countries and economies?
4. Methods
4.1. Data
We analyzed the data from PISA (https://www.oecd.org/pisa) 2012 to address our research questions in this study. PISA is a
popular international age-based assessment that evaluates how well 15-year-old students who are enrolled in grade 7 or higher
perform in reading, mathematics, and science. In most participating countries and economies, these students are nearing the end of
compulsory education (OECD, 2013b). Due to the fact that the ICT familiarity questionnaire in PISA 2012 was optional for participating countries and economies, the final sample for data analysis consisted of 194,701 students from 900 schools (male = 98,005,
female = 96,696).
4.2. Measures
Outcome measure. The outcome measure was student CPS competency. PISA 2012 employed a total of 16 units with about 40
computer-based assessment items to evaluate CPS competency (OECD, 2013a). The plausible value methodology (Mislevy, 1991) was
used to derive the final CPS achievement scores accounting for student context information, which decreased measurement errors. For
6

Thinking Skills and Creativity 57 (2025) 101805

F. Chen et al.

each student, five plausible values of CPS scores were generated in PISA 2012 data. According to the OECD’s recommendations about
analyzing PISA data, this study incorporated the five plausible values of CPS scores by employing the TYPE=IMPUTATION function in
Mplus. Specifically, all five plausible values were used in the estimation process rather than averaging them or conducting separate
estimations. Multiple imputations were performed across the five datasets, estimated model parameters separately for each plausible
value, and then combined the results to account for both within- and between-imputation variances according to Rubin’s (1987) rules.
This approach resulted in more reliable statistical inferences since standard errors can be appropriately adjusted.
Covariate measures. Gender (0 = female, 1 = male) and students’ ESCS (OECD, 2013b) levels were included in the MLM analysis
as two covariates. The original assessment item for gender was included as the gender variable in the analyses. In PISA 2012, ESCS was
derived based on parents’ educational background, their highest occupation, and home possessions such as the number of books in the
home. Therefore, we adopted the ESCS index, a derived measure, for MLM analyses.
Predictor measures. Predictors included the following measures: openness for problem solving (OPENPS, five items; e.g., “I can
handle a lot of information”; five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 [Very much like me] to 5 [Not at all like me]); ICT use at home for
school-related tasks (HOMSCH, seven items; e.g., “Doing homework on the computer”; five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 [Never or
hardly ever] to 5 [Every day]); ICT entertainment use (ENTUSE, 11 items; e.g., “Playing one-player games”; five-point Likert scale
ranging from 1 [Never or hardly ever] to 5 [Every day]); ICT availability at home (ICTHOME, 11 items; e.g., “Desktop computer”;
three-point Likert scale ranging from 1 [Yes, and I use it”] to 3 [No]); ICT availability at school (ICTSCH, seven items; e.g., “Desktop
computer”; three-point Likert scale ranging from 1 [Yes, and I use it”] to 3 [No]); use of ICT at school (USESCH, nine items; e.g., “Using
email at school”; five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 [Never or hardly ever] to 5 [Every day]); and sense of belonging to school
(BELONG, nine items; e.g., “I feel like an outsider (or left out of things) at school”; four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 [strongly
agree] to 4 [strongly disagree]). Our study adopted the derived scale indices of each predictor provided by PISA 2012 (OECD, 2013b)
for data analysis. Specifically, PISA 2012 constructed the scale indices using a one-parameter item response model to scale multiple
items of each measure with a weighted likelihood estimate (WLE; Warm, 1989). Readers can refer to the PISA 2012 technical report
(OECD, 2013b) for details on how each measure was derived and scale reliabilities for each measure.
4.3. Analytic strategy
Data on the ICT-related measures were mainly collected through the ICT familiarity questionnaire in PISA 2012, which was
optional for participating countries and economies. This led to students’ missing responses on ICT-related measures such as ICT use at
home for school-related tasks and ICT entertainment use for 32 countries and economies. As a result, the final sample for data analysis
includes students from 33 participating countries and economies (see Appendix). Regression-based multiple imputations were applied
to handle missing values for the MLM analysis. Specifically, multiple imputation predicts missing values based on a linear regression
model with values of the other variables in the dataset, which generates several complete versions of the dataset where missing values
are replaced with plausible values. The plausible values are randomly chosen from posterior distributions for each missing value based
on the model. It should be noted that the percentages of missing values for most predictors in our analysis ranged from 30 % to 40 %.
According to previous guidelines, listwise or pairwise deletion can be acceptable when missing data account for a small percentage (e.
g., < 5 %), but imputation is needed to improve statistical power when missing data account for a large percentage (Tabachnick &
Fidell, 2019). However, imputation is likely to introduce bias when missing values account for a substantial proportion (e.g., > 40 %)
and deletion may be preferable (Little & Rubin, 2019). Therefore, in this study, we conducted multiple imputation instead of deletion
to maintain the power of the analysis and minimize the bias.
Due to the hierarchical structure of PISA data, we adopted a three-level multilevel modelling (MLM) approach (Raudenbush &
Bryk, 2002) to minimize the type 1 errors in our analysis. The study employed a three-level MLM, treating countries as random entities
rather than fixed, due to the following considerations. First, treating countries as random allows findings to be generalized beyond the
33 countries studied, compared with limited generalizability of conclusions with a fixed-effects model. Second, a random-effects model
makes use of all available data to estimate between-country variation, leading to more efficient and stable parameter estimates. In
contrast, a fixed-effects model requires estimating country-specific effects separately, reducing statistical power. Third, PISA data are
naturally nested—students are within schools and schools are within countries. Failing to address the three-level nature of the data
may underestimate standard errors and lead to potential biases in hypothesis testing. Finally, a three-level model allows the partitioning of variance into student, school, and country levels. This can help in identifying how much variance in CPS is attributed to each
level.
Moreover, model comparisons were conducted to identify the unique contribution of predictors from different contexts. Since PISA
adopted a two-stage stratified sampling procedure, students might be sampled with unequal probabilities, resulting in unequal
representativeness of the student population. To make sure the data of each student contributes proportionally to the MLM analysis
based on their probabilities of being sampled, we used normalized student weights to account for the sampling design of the
assessment. Based on the weights, the variable scores of each student will be adjusted according to how representative they are of the
entire population, reducing the biases of model estimates (OECD, 2013b).
Null model. We first built an intercept-only model without any predictors to partition the total variance of CPS competency scores
into variance components at the student, school and country levels. The MLM analysis requires that a significant proportion of the total
variance of CPS scores can be accounted for by schools or countries.
Model 1. In Model 1, gender and ESCS were modelled at the student level as two covariates. In addition, according to previous
MLM studies (e.g., Cairns & Areepattamannil, 2019; Ho, 2010; Perry & McConney, 2013), school- and country-level socioeconomic
status was found to be associated with student achievement, which was often incorporated as school- and country-level covariates. As
7

Thinking Skills and Creativity 57 (2025) 101805

F. Chen et al.

such, our study included school- and country-mean ESCS as the contextual covariates at the school and country levels. However, it
should be noted that our study focuses on the effects of the targeted factors on CPS at the student level and, therefore, no school- and
country-level targeted factors were examined.
Model 2. In Model 2, in addition to gender and ESCS, four student/home predictors — openness for problem-solving, ICT use at
home for school-related tasks, ICT entertainment use, and ICT availability at home — were included as additional predictors. That is, at
the student level, there are six random-slope predictors, whose effects vary among schools. Compared with the previous model, Model
2 can be used to examine the unique contribution of the four student/home predictors to student CPS competency.
Full model. In the full model, in addition to all predictors included in previous models, three school-related predictors — ICT
availability at school, use of ICT at school, and sense of belonging to school — were additionally included. Compared with Model 2, the
full model can be used to examine the unique contribution of the three school-related predictors to student CPS competency.
As suggested by previous studies (e.g., Enders & Tofighi, 2007; Wu & Wooldridge, 2005), since our study mainly focused on the
relationships between variables at the student level, all student-level predictor variables were centred on the group means, and schooland country-level predictor variables were centred on the grand mean prior to the MLM analysis. The MLM analysis was conducted
with maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors in Mplus version 7.4 (Muthén & Muthén, 2012).
5. Results
5.1. Descriptive results
Students’ CPS competency was significantly correlated with all other measures (see Table 1). Particularly, it was positively
correlated with students’ ESCS and openness for problem-solving levels but negatively correlated with ICT availability at school and
use of ICT at school.
5.2. MLM results
According to Table 2, for the null model, the grand mean of CPS competency scores was γ0.00 = 498.40, and the CPS competency
scores significantly varied across students (σ2 = 8565.92, p < .001), schools (τπ000 = 5031.31, p < .001), and countries and economies
(τβ000 = 5076.33, p < .001). Moreover, 46 %, 27 %, and 27 % of the variance in the CPS competency scores can be explained by the
student, school, and country levels, respectively, supporting a good fit of MLM to the data.
After the inclusion of gender and ESCS, Model 1 accounted for 11 %, 92 %, and 79 % of the CPS competency score variance at the
student, school, and country levels, respectively. This implies that the school- and country-level CPS competency variances were
mostly contributed by student differences in gender and ESCS. In addition, gender and ESCS positively predicted CPS competency (ps <
0.001). In other words, male students and students with high ESCS levels showed higher CPS competency achievement.
Model 2, with the inclusion of the four student/home predictors, accounted for 16 %, 92 %, and 82 % of the CPS competency score
variance at the student, school, and country levels, respectively. Compared with Model 1, Model 2 additionally accounted for 5 % and 4
% variance at the student and country levels, respectively. Similar to Model 1, Model 2 showed that student gender and ESCS positively
predicted complex student problem-solving competency (ps < 0.001). Among the four student/home predictors, student openness for
problem-solving positively predicted CPS competency (p < .001), while ICT availability at home negatively predicted CPS competency
(p < .001). The effects of the other two measures were not significant.
The full model includes all predictors, which accounted for 18 %, 92 %, and 81 % of the CPS competency score variance at the
student, school, and country levels, respectively. In comparison with Model 2, the full model additionally explained 2 % variance at the
student level, but <2 % variance at the country level. Moreover, the full model suggested that openness for problem-solving positively
predicted (p < .001), and ICT availability at home and school, and use of ICT at school negatively (ps < 0.001) predicted student CPS
competency. In addition, in the full model, school-mean ESCS was found to positively predict student CPS competency (p < .001),
indicating that students from high-ESCS schools generally showed high CPS competency levels.
Regarding the differences in the links of ICT factors to CPS between countries, according to the variances of regression coefficients,
the MLM analysis showed that the effects of gender (σ2 = 42.92, SE = 21.50, p < .05), ESCS (σ 2 = 55.47, SE = 27.85, p < .05), openness
for problem-solving (σ 2 = 30.13, SE = 14.34, p < .05), ICT entertainment use (σ2 = 28.70, SE = 9.13, p < .01), ICT availability at home
(σ2 = 13.58, SE = 4.92, p < .01), and use of ICT at school (σ2 = 24.78, SE = 8.94, p < .01) on CPS are significantly different between
countries. However, no significant differences in the effects are observed between countries for sense of belonging to school (σ 2 = 2.79,
SE = 4.58, p = .54), ICT use at home for school-related tasks (σ2 = 40.05, SE = 22.07, p = .07) and ICT availability at school (σ2 = 12.36,
SE = 8.39, p = .14).

between ICT and educational outcomes identified in the literature across different contexts (Odell et al., 2020), accounting for multiple

and CPS. It is worth noting that CPS is rarely addressed as a primary assessment domain in large-scale assessments. The PISA 2012 data
offers a valuable opportunity to explore CPS within a comprehensive contextual framework. Accordingly, our study analyzed the
relationships between ICT and CPS using data from PISA 2012. Although this assessment was implemented a decade ago, our review of
the definitions, conceptualizations, and operationalizations of ICT, its related factors, and CPS suggests that these constructs have
remained relatively stable over time. As such, the findings of our study remain relevant and provide valuable insights with implications
for educational practices nowadays.
Consistent with previous findings (e.g., Claro et al., 2015; Hämäläinen et al., 2019; Lin et al., 2012; Siddiq & Scherer, 2019), our
study found that male and high-ESCS students were more likely to perform better in CPS. In terms of gender, male students’ advantages
in CPS may be due to their higher proficiency in using problem-solving strategies, according to previous log data analyses (Wüstenberg
et al., 2014; Wittmann & Hattrup, 2004). With respect to ESCS, our findings echo the consensus in the literature on the positive link
between socioeconomic status and educational outcomes (Liu et al., 2020). Although problem-solving competency is domain-general,
students’ successes in problem-solving may be associated with various cognitive, affective, and psychosocial attributes (OECD, 2013a).
These components involved in CPS are also integral to student success in other academic domains, which may be largely shaped by
one’s socioeconomic status. Low-ESCS students’ worse performance in CPS may be attributed to multiple factors. For instance, they
may suffer from inadequate nutrition and health care and a negative academic home environment without adequate financial and
psychological support from their guardians (Thomson, 2018). In general, our global analysis highlights gender and socioeconomic
differences in CPS.
Moreover, our study found that students’ openness for problem-solving was positively associated with CPS competency. This
finding is consistent with previous studies that supported the beneficial role of openness for problem-solving in promoting CPS performance (e.g., Pejic & Molcer, 2018). Students with stronger openness for problem-solving tend to be more motivated, persistent,
adaptable, and confident when facing challenging problems. As an attitude toward problem-solving, it was found that students’
openness for problem-solving was also positively correlated with academic achievement across different subjects, such as reading,
mathematics and science (Cutumisu & Bulut, 2017; Sebastian & Huang, 2016; Yalcin, 2017). This implies that student CPS competency
might contribute to their academic success. For example, student CPS can more strongly predict academic achievement than fluid
intelligence, which can be considered a cross-curricular skill regardless of educational context (Greiff & Neubert, 2014). In addition,
despite the nonsignificant link, the positive role of home ICT use for school-related tasks aligns with the previous finding that the use of
ICT for productivity is beneficial for student academic success (e.g., Luu & Freeman, 2011). A possible explanation for this finding is
that students’ home ICT use for school-related tasks may equip them with meaningful mastery experiences, fostering the development
of their ICT self-efficacy (Rohatgi et al., 2016). ICT entertainment use, however, did not predict student CPS competency, consistent
with previous research across other subjects (e.g., Luu & Freeman, 2011). Nonetheless, it is important to acknowledge that ICT
10

F. Chen et al.

entertainment use can also be beneficial, as it has been shown to strongly predict students’ ICT self-efficacy (Rohatgi et al., 2016).
Responsible use of ICT for recreational purposes may strengthen students’ ICT efficacy, ultimately promoting their academic success.
Moreover, our findings indicate that ICT availability at home and school and use of ICT at school were negatively associated with
CPS competency after controlling for the effects of other contextual factors. This corroborates previous research that reported negative
associations between ICT availability and use and student performance in science, reading, and mathematics (Bulut & Cutumisu, 2018;
Hu et al., 2018). These negative associations imply that merely providing and using ICT resources in home and school contexts does not
guarantee success in CPS. Rather, the appropriate and guided use of ICT for pedagogical purposes at home and school is crucial.
Excessive exposure to ICT might cause potential detrimental effects on academic performance, such as fostering a gaming addiction
(Grüsser et al., 2006). However, previous studies have shown that ICT availability at home indirectly contributes to student academic
performance through improving students’ autonomy and interest in ICT use. This implies that access to ICT resources at home and
school, as well as the use of ICT at school, can shape students’ dispositions toward ICT, which are strongly associated with their academic outcomes. It should also be noted that the adopted analytic approach, MLM, assumes linear relationships between ICT-related
factors and CPS. The negative associations observed might hint at potential nonlinear relationships between access to and use of ICT at
home and school and CPS competency. For example, moderate ICT resources and use, when increased, might be positively related to
students’ CPS performance. However, excessive ICT use in environments with sufficient ICT resources could lead to negative effects on
educational outcomes. In essence, regulated access to and use of ICT in home and school settings are essential to ensure their positive
roles in shaping CPS competency.
In contrast to the established consensus (e.g., Rodríguez et al., 2020; Thien et al., 2015), our study did not identify a significant
association between students’ sense of belonging to school and CPS competency. Schools serve as the primary arena for students to
acquire subject-specific knowledge, such as science and mathematics. Therefore, a positive school climate is pivotal for students’
academic success and overall well-being. However, the development of CPS skills, which are often domain-general, transcends the
confines of school contexts, where a positive school climate might play a less determining role. In addition, our analysis revealed that,
except for gender and ESCS, most factors within the student/home context were positively associated with CPS, while most factors
within the school context negatively predicted CPS. From a model comparison perspective, after controlling for the effects of gender
and ESCS, the four student/home factors and the three school factors accounted for an additional five and two percent of the variance
in CPS scores at the student level, respectively. These findings collectively suggest that, in comparison with student/home ICT-related
factors, school ICT-related factors are less strongly associated with student CPS competency.
Our findings hold substantial research and pedagogical implications for researchers and educational practitioners who aim to
harness the potential of ICT to promote students’ CPS competency. First, the MLM analysis highlights that both schools and countries
account for a large proportion of the variance in students’ CPS scores. This implies that students’ performance in CPS varies significantly between schools and countries. Therefore, findings regarding the impacts of ICT-related factors on CPS within a specific context
may have limited generalizability. Our study, analyzing a global dataset from 33 countries and economies, offers a comprehensive
examination of these effects, contributing to more generalizable insights into the links of ICT-related factors to CPS. Additionally, due
to the significant school- and country-level variance, educational practitioners should account for the school- and country-level disparities when making educational policies targeted at fostering students’ CPS competency. Second, our findings corroborate that CPS
should be regarded as a type of educational outcome, sharing similar antecedents and consequences with other subject domains,
including reading, science, and mathematics. Particularly, as our study identified both gender and socioeconomic status gaps in CPS,
guardians and teachers should attend to providing more support for female and socioeconomically disadvantaged students in
developing their CPS skills. Third, our study underscores the strong positive relationship between openness for problem-solving and
CPS competency. In practice, to foster students’ CPS skills, educators may consider designing and implementing targeted interventions
aimed at fostering positive problem-solving attitudes. For example, school-based social and emotional learning interventions have
proven effective in improving students’ positive learning and developmental outcomes (Taylor et al., 2017). Finally, according to the
comparison of effect sizes between home and school factors (5 % vs. 2 % at the student level), our study identified that school
ICT-related factors exert a weaker effect on students’ CPS competency when compared to student/home factors. Particularly, our
analyses highlighted the positive role of home ICT use for school-related tasks in fostering CPS competency. This suggests that environments beyond schools, particularly home, are advantageous in providing students with an autonomous and relaxing context
where they can access and use technologies. However, as suggested by our findings, students’ ICT use for leisure at home was not
associated with CPS performance. Therefore, we argue that guardians should strive to promote students’ meaningful and guided ICT
utilization for pedagogical purposes while discouraging their excessive exposure to ICT, which could potentially lead to negative
consequences. Future studies are encouraged to probe the specific types of afterschool activities that contribute most significantly to
the development of student CPS.
7. Conclusion
Access to and use of ICT are critical to promoting students’ educational outcomes, particularly in the digital age. However, although
the effects of ICT-related factors on subject domains have been widely examined in the literature, it is imperative to scrutinize the roles
of ICT-related factors in influencing students’ CPS competency. Our study embarked on this endeavor by identifying the associations
between ICT-related predictors and CPS competency among students from 33 countries and economies. Employing an advanced
statistical approach, MLM, our research brought to light that after controlling for the effects of gender and ESCS, students’ openness for
problem-solving was positively related to CPS, while the ICT availability at home/school and use of ICT at school, showed a negative
association with CPS. Our findings underscored that compared with ICT-related factors within the school context, factors within the

student/home context played more influential roles in fostering students’ CPS competency. This study represents an initial exploration
into the associations between ICT-related factors and CPS with a representative dataset of multiple countries and regions.
Limitations
Notwithstanding its valuable insights and pedagogical implications, our study is not without limitations. First, despite the inclusion
of a large-scale dataset comprising 33 countries and economies, the predominant representation of OECD member countries in our
sample may potentially result in an underrepresentation of under-developed countries and regions. However, it should be noted that
the effects of ICT on CPS might differ between developed, developing, and under-developed countries (Skryabin et al., 2015). Second,
due to its scope, this study did not examine the mechanism of ICT-related factors shaping CPS. Future studies are encouraged to clarify
the associations between ICT and CPS through experimental designs or accounting for more contextual factors. Third, due to its scope,
this study did not address the effects of school- and country-level ICT factors on CPS. A more complex analysis with MLM, including
school- and country-level ICT factors, can be conducted to investigate cross-level interactions. Fourth, the ICT-related measures in PISA
2012 might not fully reflect students’ ICT-related perceptions and dispositions nowadays. For example, the ICT items of PISA 2012 may
not fully capture aspects of ICT competence or usage, such as digital literacy or AI literacy (Long & Magerko, 2020), that could be
associated with CPS competency. This might restrict the interpretability and generalizability of our findings in the context of certain
ICT dimensions. Fifth, our study focuses on higher-level associations rather than detailed mechanisms of ICT effects on CPS. Future
studies are encouraged to examine specific process-level data or conduct more complex analyses, such as mediation and moderation
analyses, that could shed light on the mechanisms underlying CPS performance. Sixth, prior research has suggested that the relationship may follow an inverse U-shaped pattern (e.g., Gorjón & Osés, 2023), where moderate ICT use enhances problem-solving
abilities, but excessive use could be detrimental. Although our study focuses on linear modeling, exploring non-linear trends in ICT
use and CPS outcomes can be an important direction for future research. Finally, although our study comprehensively examined the
associations between ICT and CPS from the student perspective, it did not account for teachers’ and parents’ perspectives. Considering
the importance of teachers’ and parents’ ICT attitudes and practices in influencing students’ academic success (Bingimlas, 2009; Sun
et al., 2021), more investigations are needed to examine the roles of ICT-related factors in shaping educational outcomes from
teachers’ and parents’ perspectives.
