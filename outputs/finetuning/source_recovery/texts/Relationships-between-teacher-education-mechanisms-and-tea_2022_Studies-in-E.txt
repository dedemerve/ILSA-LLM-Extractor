This study (N = 1175) examined relationships between mechanisms of teacher education and lower-secondary
teacher practices, self-efficacy, and job satisfaction in the U.S. Through secondary analysis of Teaching and
Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018 data, we investigated relations between these important constructs
and various formal and informal pre-service and in-service teacher learning activities (e.g., types of professional
development, induction, mentoring, teacher cooperation). Multi-level regression analyses adjusted for the TALIS
sampling design—and controlling for an array of teacher and school characteristics—revealed evidence for the
potential value of a variety of teacher-education mechanisms. For instance, we found evidence for the potential
benefit of teacher cooperation, particular pre-service teacher education foci, and specific in-service teacher
education activities. Limitations and future directions are discussed.

A significant portion of the variance in K-12 students’ academic
outcomes can be ascribed to teachers—teachers’ characteristics, behaviors, and beliefs, etc. (Hanushek et al., 2005; Nye et al., 2004). Decades of teacher-effectiveness research has also helped identify
particular teacher characteristics such as verbal ability and content
knowledge as ones which promote student outcomes (e.g., Darling-Hammond & Youngs, 2002). Less is known about how exactly to equip
teachers with the qualities most associated with classroom performance,
as well as retention in the field. However, we do know that some
mechanisms of teacher education (e.g., professional development) can
effect change in teacher and student outcomes, at least under the right
conditions (Blank & de las Alas, 2009; Desimone, 2009; Garet et al.,
2001). There is also strong evidence of variability in teacher effectiveness as a function of pre-service teacher preparation program (Boyd
et al., 2009).
Extant evidence about the efficacy of specific teacher education
mechanisms (e.g., pre-service teacher education models, different professional development activities) has been derived from diverse studies
with different populations/samples, in different contexts, and with
different outcome measures. This complicates direct comparison and
synthesis of, and the derivation of conclusions from, findings in this
body of literature about the impact of various mechanisms of teacher

education. The present study sought to address this by broadly exploring
relationships between teacher education mechanisms and three important teacher-level outcomes in the context of a single large, nationallyrepresentative dataset, the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018 (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2019a, 2019b).
The present study aimed to address open questions about teacher
education mechanisms and the relative impact of different forms thereof
(Boyd et al., 2009; Levine, 2006; National Research Council, 2010;
Wilson et al., 2001). In particular, we examined relationships between
an array of teacher education and development mechanisms and three
salient teacher-level constructs associated with performance and/or
retention: teacher practices, teacher self-efficacy, and teacher job
satisfaction. The independent variables represented a diverse set of
teacher education mechanisms, at different career stages, and inclusive
of both formal and informal teacher-learning activities. In doing so, we
also describe the distribution of these teacher-education mechanisms in
the U.S. as estimated by TALIS for lower-secondary (e.g., grades 7–9)
core content teachers.

empirical linkages between particular teacher-education mechanisms
and teacher outcomes with newer and different data sources and

1. Theoretical framework

1.2.1. Pre-service teacher education
Most U.S. teachers begin their training in an initial, pre-service
teacher education program associated with an institution of higher education. Traditional pre-service preparation programs are typically
time-limited experiences (2–4 years) that are generally structured as a
series of courses, and one or more applied, clinical experiences (Ainley &
Carstens, 2018). Theoretically, pre-service teacher education programs
effect changes in teachers’ knowledge, skills, beliefs; program graduates
then apply those characteristics to their practice in K-12 settings; and
finally, these practices affect K-12 student learning or other student
outcomes (Diez, 2010).
Although rigorous evidence of pre-service teacher education is
somewhat limited, we do know that there is variability in teacher
effectiveness as a function of which teacher preparation program one
attends (Boyd et al., 2009), and that many teachers remain unprepared
for their practice, even after their pre-service education (Levine, 2006).
There is also some evidence for the impact of particular pre-service
program design elements (Wayne & Youngs, 2003, Wilson et al.,
2001). Others have found associations between particular pre-service
content emphases such as classroom management, content standards,
parent-teacher relationships, and teacher job satisfaction (Kelly et al.,
2019).
Additionally, there is some evidence for the efficacy of particular preservice teacher education mechanisms. For example, Boyd et al. (2009)
found that active, practice-focused learning opportunities during
pre-service education were predictive of student achievement in math
during the first year of teaching. Research also supports the value of field
or clinical experiences, specifically student teaching (National Research
Council, 2010; Ronfeldt & Reininger, 2012). Further, studies have
linked measures of candidate instructional quality to the quality of the
in-service teachers (i.e., cooperating teacher) who supervises teacher
candidates’ clinical or field experience/s (Ronfeldt et al., 2013).

1.1. Outcome variables
Our study considered relationships between teacher education
mechanisms and three teacher-level outcomes (practices, self-efficacy,
job satisfaction), all of which have been linked to teacher performance
and/or retention. Given the complexity of teaching, teacher practices
are inherently multi-dimensional (Fauth et al., 2014). However, some
enacted teacher practices have been linked to student achievement
outcomes. For example, prior researchers have highlighted relationships
between student outcomes and instructional practices that promote
clarity of instruction and cognitive activation (Hattie, 2009; Rjosk et al.,
2014). The present study’s measure of teacher practices emphasized
these two dimensions along with classroom management practices and
was based on teacher reports.
Additionally, a component of Bandura’s (1997) social cognitive
theory, teacher self-efficacy is a teacher’s “judgement of his or her capabilities to bring about desired outcomes of student engagement and
learning” (Tschannen-Moran & Hoy, 2001, p. 783). Teacher self-efficacy
can theoretically facilitate behavior, and it has been shown to be positively related to teacher practices (Guskey, 1988; Holzberger et al.,
2014). Formal and informal teacher education activities can avail
so-called “mastery experiences” and “vicarious experiences,” two sources of self-efficacy within a domain (Bandura, 1997; Klassen & Chiu,
2010).
Teacher job satisfaction has been defined as, “the sense of fulfilment
and gratification that teachers experience through their work as a
teacher” (Ainley & Carstens, 2018, p. 43). Teacher job satisfaction is an
important construct because it relates to a host of negative teacher
outcomes including teacher burnout and attrition (Ingersoll, 2001). As
such, our analyses related to teacher job satisfaction shed light more
directly on problems of teacher attrition than they do problems of
teacher effectiveness.
Although we conduct distinct analyses by the outcome, there is some
evidence for relations amongst these three constructs. For example,
prior work has identified a positive relationship between job satisfaction
and self-efficacy (Ford et al., 2018; Organisation for Economic
Co-operation and Development, 2014) and still others have linked job
satisfaction to teacher practices (Renzulli et al., 2011). Self-efficacy
theory and prior research further substantiate a relationship between
teacher self-efficacy and teacher practices (Bandura, 1997; Holzberger
et al., 2014).

1.2.2. In-service teacher education
Given the problems noted above—and the complex, dynamic nature
of educational policy and practice at all levels—it is reasonable to assume pre-service education alone is inadequate. Instead, in-service
teacher education—also known as professional development—is
needed as well to sharpen the capacity of the teaching workforce (Allen,
1940; Nir & Bogler, 2008). Scholars have articulated similar theories of
action for ongoing professional learning as they have for pre-service
education; in-service teacher education changes teachers’ characteristics (e.g., knowledge, skills, beliefs), which then drives changes in their
practice, which in turn bolsters student learning (Desimone, 2009).
While in-service teacher education can have a robust impact on teacher
characteristics, evidence of its impact on instructional practice and
student achievement is generally more elusive (Blank & de las Alas,
2009; Garet et al., 2001; Hattie, 2009).

Like teaching itself, learning to teach is extraordinarily complex.
Accordingly, the process of teacher learning and development itself has
been theorized as a progression from novice to expert (Ingersoll &
Collins, 2018). During this progression, teachers encounter a variety of
mechanisms that may promote their expertize (National Research
Council, 2010; Veenman, 1984). Broadly, there are two institutionalized

coherence; and the collective participation of educators from the same
professional context (Garet et al., 2001). Another hallmark of effective
in-service teacher education is that it emphasizes content knowledge
(Blank & de las Alas, 2009), which may be especially important for
secondary educators, the object of this study.
Prior research has also shed light on the relative effects of different
forms of in-service teacher education activities. For instance, evidence
for stand-alone professional development events such as workshops or
seminars is limited or mixed (e.g., Yang, 2019; Liu & Liao, 2019).
However, more promising evidence exists for other forms of professional
development such as coaching, training in non-school organizations,
training at other schools or school visits, and as discussed in later sections, teacher collaboration and mentoring (Han et al., 2018; Liu & Liao,
2019; Yang, 2019).
Teacher Collaboration and Cooperation. Another salient form of
in-service professional development is teacher collaboration and cooperation. Through informal collegial interactions, teachers can learn from
one another’s experiences vicariously, share expertize or resources, and
receive feedback related to the difficult work of teaching and learning
(Kools & Stoll, 2016). Though collaborative processes are not without
their challenges (Little, 1990), these interactions have been linked to
improvements in student achievement (Goddard et al., 2007); teacher
job satisfaction (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (2009); Stearns et al., 2015; Torres, 2019); teacher self-efficacy
(Ainley & Carstens, 2018; Organisation for Economic Co-operation
and Development (2009)); and teacher practices (Han et al., 2018).
Using TALIS 2013 data, for example, Han et al. (2018) found a positive
association between teacher collaborative activities and classroom
practices related to technology.
Induction. Teacher induction is another general mechanism by
which to promote teacher quality and retention. Induction may be
viewed as a specific form of in-service teacher education that is intended
specifically for our newest teachers1 (Ingersoll & Strong, 2011; Veenman, 1984). As noted by Ingersoll and Collins (2018), induction is
intended to “aid new practitioners in adjusting to the environment, to
familiarize them with the concrete realities of their jobs, to socialize
them to professional norms, and also to provide a second opportunity to
filter out those with substandard levels of skill and knowledge” (p. 203).
Induction programs are fairly common, though they vary considerably
in their form and duration, and they are not always available for new
teachers (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development,
2009).
Extant research suggests that teacher induction can be effective. This
body of literature has linked participation in teacher induction to variety
of outcomes including teacher retention, teacher self-efficacy, practices,
and even student achievement (Helms-Lorenz et al., 2016; Rockoff,
2008; Smith & Ingersoll, 2004).
There is also evidence for the efficacy of specific induction activities,
including efficacy in relation to the outcomes we consider here (e.g.,
self-efficacy and job satisfaction; Liu & Liao, 2019; Kelly et al., 2019). In
particular, prior work indicates that the following may be effective
forms of teacher induction: mentoring and coaching (Ingersoll, 2012;
Helms-Lorenz et al., 2016; Kelly et al., 2019); common planning/collaboration time (Ingersoll, 2012); teaching observations (Helms-Lorenz
et al., 2016; Kelly et al., 2019); orientations; and reduced workload
(Kelly et al., 2019). Further, research indicates that comprehensive induction programs which provide multiple supports, and induction programs that are dosed more highly, may be best (Ingersoll, 2012).
Mentoring. Another specific mechanism of in-service teacher

education is mentoring. Accordingly, prior research has demonstrated
lower attrition among those teachers who participated in a school
mentoring program (Borman & Dowling, 2008). Other research using
TALIS 2013 also found a positive relationship between participation in
mentoring and teacher self-efficacy (Liu & Liao, 2019). Teacher service
as a mentor has also been shown to be associated favorably with the
mentor’s self-efficacy and instructional practices (Riggs & Sandlin, 2002,
April). Separate from induction, we also assess relationships between
teacher practice, job satisfaction, and teacher self-efficacy and both
receipt of mentoring and service as a mentor.
Feedback. Lastly, in-service teacher receipt of feedback from various
stakeholders (e.g., principals, external evaluators, other teachers) may
influence teacher practices, self-efficacy, and job satisfaction. The TALIS
2013 report indicated that the provision of meaningful, practicallyuseful feedback to teachers was associated with higher job satisfaction
(OECD, 2014). Also using TALIS 2013 data, Ford et al. (2018) found a
relationship between teacher job satisfaction and teacher perceptions of
the quality of the feedback they received; Ford et al. also linked teacher
job satisfaction to the receipt of feedback from other teachers, rather
than principals or others. Our study re-examined this latter relationship
between the source of professional feedback and an expanded set of
outcome variables.
1.3. Control variables
Our study included a number of teacher-level control variables to
mitigate internal validity threats. All control variables had been shown
to be related, at minimum, to our dependent variables and/or relevant
correlates. We controlled for teachers’ highest degree, which has been
linked to student achievement (Clotfelter et al., 2007; Liu & Liao, 2019);
and teacher full-time status, which has been shown to be related to
teacher job satisfaction in TALIS 2013 (Ford et al., 2018). We also
controlled for teacher age, which may be associated with teacher practices (Goodson et al., 2006); as well as teacher experience, which has
been linked to various teacher outcomes including self-efficacy and job
satisfaction (Klassen & Chiu, 2010; Yang, 2019). Finally, we include
teacher gender as a control variable, as this has been shown to be related
to teacher self-efficacy in TALIS 2013 (Yang, 2019).
2. Method
This associational study secondarily analyzed data from the OECD’s
2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS). TALIS 2018
was a large-scale international comparative study that collected data
concerning lower-secondary teachers’ socio-demographic and professional characteristics, professional roles and responsibilities, and professional practices in 48 countries (Organisation for Economic
Co-operation and Development, 2019a,2019b). TALIS also provides a
rich set of information about teacher participation in various formal and
informal mechanisms of teacher education and development, including
initial preparation, induction, and professional development.
2.1. Participants
TALIS 2018 featured a two-stage complex sampling design. The
design first selected a stratified random sample of 200 eligible schools,
followed by a simple random sample of 20 teachers within each sampled
school. The school-level sampling employed a probability proportional
to size approach wherein larger schools had a higher probability of being
selected (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development,
2019b).
For our study, the full U.S. TALIS 2018 dataset was filtered to include
only full-time teachers (i.e., more than 90 % of full-time hours at current
school; variable TT3G10A), teachers of core content areas (i.e., reading,
writing, and literature, mathematics, science, and social studies;
TT3G37), teachers who only teach in one school (TT3G12), and non-

2.2. Variables and measures
Our analysis primarily relied on data collected via the TALIS 2018
Teacher Questionnaire, which was self-administered by Northern
Hemisphere participants between March and May 2018. Our study
relied on a mix of individual survey item responses, scales constructed
by TALIS 2018, and scales constructed by our team from sets of item
responses. Scales constructed by TALIS 2018 underwent classical procedures vis-à-vis the reliability and validity of their scores before construction (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development,
2019b). All variables and measures are discussed in the sections that
follow, organized by their role in the analysis.

2.2.3. Control variables
Individual-level control variables included: gender (TT3G01), age
(TCHAGEGR), teaching experience (TT3G11B), employment status
(TT3G09), and educational attainment (TT3G03). The categorical control variables were dummy coded prior analysis.
The final models also included eight continuous school-level control
variables. In addition to examining main effects of each of these eight
school-level variables on the three teacher outcome measures, we also
tested whether these variables moderated the relationship between
teacher cooperation and teacher outcomes (i.e., cross-level interactions), which was observed for all three outcome variables.

2.2.1. Dependent variables
There were three dependent variables: teacher practices (T3TPRA),
teacher self-efficacy (T3SELF), and teacher job satisfaction (T3JOBSA).
The teacher practices measure (T3TPRA) was also a scale supplied by
TALIS, which was based on 12 items pertaining to implementation of
specific practices, and for this scale, TALIS reported an internal consistency estimate of.90 among teachers in the U.S. (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2019b). The teacher self-efficacy
measure (T3SELF) was a scale supplied by TALIS, which was based on 12
items pertaining to teacher self-efficacy related to classroom management, instruction, and student engagement and for this scale, TALIS
reported an estimate of score internal consistency of.91 among teachers
in the U.S. (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development,
2019b). Also supplied by TALIS, the teacher job satisfaction measure
(T3JOBSA) was based on 13 items pertaining to teacher satisfaction with
their work environment, profession, and autonomy. TALIS did not report
an estimate of scale score internal consistency among teachers in the U.
S. due to a negative item variance for one item (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2019b).

2.3. Analytic approach
The complex sampling design of TALIS 2018 presents several analytic challenges. Particularly, secondary analyses of TALIS 2018 data
need to account for unequal selection probabilities and the nesting of
individuals (teachers) within clusters (schools). Thusly, variables
defining a sampling weight (TCHWGT) and cluster membership
(IDSCHOOL) for each teacher were used during two analytic steps: 1)
multiple imputation of missing data, and 2) estimation of regression
models.
All analyses were conducted in R. After filtering to the analytic
dataset and normalizing the sampling weights, we then investigated the
scope and nature of the missing data. Missing data ranged from 0.00 %
to 9.62 % across the full set of analysis variables. Little’s test for Missing
Completely at Random with the analytic dataset was not statistically
significant, χ 2 (2316) = 1969.32, p = 1.00. We then multiply imputed
five datasets via R’s mice package (Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn,
2010). The sampling weights and cluster membership variables were
also included in the imputation model.
We used multi-level modeling to answer our research questions,
relying primarily on the R packages lme4 (Bates et al., 2015) and
lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). While unconditional models estimated for teacher practices (0.16) and teacher job satisfaction (0.16)
revealed non-trivial intra-class correlations, this was not the case for
teacher self-efficacy (0.01). However, it is still appropriate to use
multi-level modeling (Maas & Hox, 2005; Musca et al., 2011) and we did
so for all outcomes.
Given the large number of variables and potential parameters that
may be estimated, we relied on an iterative and selective model building
approach. All multi-level analyses were weighed with TCHWGT
(normalized) and results were pooled across multiply imputed datasets.

2.2.2. Independent variables
Our primary independent variables represented teacher participation in various mechanisms of teacher learning and development. The
content of initial teacher education was operationalized with a set of 10
indicator variables (TT3G06A-J). Teacher participation in a formal or
informal induction program was operationalized with four indicator
variables (TT3G19A1, TT3G19A2, TT3G19B1, TT3G19B2). Respectively, these items represented: teacher participation in a formal induction program at one’s first employer, teacher participation in a
formal induction program at one’s current school, teacher participation
in an informal induction program at one’s first employer, and teacher
participation in an informal induction program at one’s current school.
The type of professional development activity was operationalized with
a set of nine indicator variables (TT3G22A-I). Participation in mentoring
was operationalized with a set of two indicator variables (TT3G21A and
TT3G21B). Teachers were given the opportunity to indicate participation as both a mentor and a mentee. The response format for all these
variables was dichotomous (Yes/No), and the items were re-coded such
that 1 =Yes and 0 =No.

TALIS, which was based on eight items pertaining to exchange, coordination, and professional collaboration among teachers. For this

3. Results

The distribution of pre-service teacher education content foci indicates that most content foci were common or very common. Preservice teacher education less often emphasized use of Information
and Communications Technology (ICT) for teaching (65 %) and teaching in a multicultural or multilingual setting (71 %). Teachers reported
induction about half the time. The most common in-service professional
development activity was in-person courses and seminars. However, a
variety of other in-service professional development activities occurred
with some regularity (e.g., about half participated in online courses or
seminars, education conferences, peer and or self-observation and
coaching, and a network of teachers). The least common was participation in a formal qualification or certification program (e.g., National
Board Certification in the U.S.).

3.2. Multi-level model results
In our initial multi-level models, estimated with fixed effects only for
all level-1 control and teacher education variables, 15 of the 29 initial
teacher education variables were statistically significant for at least one
outcome. These included teacher cooperation, six pre-service teacher
education content foci, formal induction program at one’s first
employer, four professional development activities, receiving mentoring
and serving as a mentor, and receipt of feedback from both external
individuals or bodies and principal or member/s of the management
team. While subsequent models retained these level-1 variables and 11
level-1 control variables, non-significant level-1 teacher education variables were dropped from subsequent analyses to reduce model
complexity. We then iteratively added level-2 control variables, and for
teacher practices and teacher job satisfaction, a random effect of teacher
cooperation as well as cross-level interaction between teacher cooperation and the level-2 variables.
Table 2 presents pooled regression coefficients and standard errors
for each of the three final multi-level regression analyses. We present our
results here by dependent variable, then report results related to control
variables, and consider results altogether in Discussion.

teaching practices. Participation in peer and/or self-observation and
coaching was also associated with better teaching practices. Receipt of
feedback from either external individuals or bodies or the principal or
other member(s) of the school improvement team was associated with
statistically more favorable teacher practices as well. Having participated in an induction program at one’s first school was, however,
associated with less optimal teaching practices. As it relates to the crosslevel interactions, only one was statistically significant: the interaction
between barriers to professional development and teacher cooperation.
The more barriers to professional development, the weaker the relationship between teacher cooperation and teacher practices. However,
note that when adding the cooperation random effect and its level-2
predictors, the cooperation fixed effect was only marginally significant
(p = .06).

3.2.4. Control variables
Most level-1 control variables were also statistically significantly
related to at least one of the outcome variables. For teacher practices, we
observed more favorable practices for those aged 30–39 and 60 and
above (compared to those age 40–49) and female. For teacher selfefficacy, we found higher self-efficacy among those with a master’s
degree (compared to a bachelor’s degree or lower) and those with more
years of teaching experience. Individuals aged 50–59 reported lower
teacher self-efficacy. For teacher job satisfaction, we found higher
satisfaction among those with a fixed-term contract for a period of more
than 1 school year (compared to those with a permanent employment
status).
As for level-2 control variables, school-level need for professional
development in subject matter and pedagogy was associated with statistically lower teaching practices. For teacher self-efficacy, higher
stakeholder involvement and school delinquency and violence were
associated with lower self-efficacy. Finally, for teacher job satisfaction,
higher professional development barriers and more lack of special needs
personnel were both associated with lower job satisfaction.

we respond to calls for more research on teacher education, including
the relative value of various teacher education structures, processes, and
contents (Boyd et al., 2009; National Research Council, 2010; Wilson
et al., 2001). Two other affordances of the present study are its 1)
exploration of potential moderating variables and 2) use of different
data and methods to provide new evidence related to specific teacher
education mechanisms.
We found evidence for the potential value of a variety of teacherlearning mechanisms, including mechanisms at both pre-service and
in-service stages. Generally speaking, observed “effects” of particular
teacher-education mechanisms were limited to a particular outcome
variable, though three were associated with two outcomes: pre-service
content focus on teaching in a multicultural or multilingual setting;
peer and/or self-observation and coaching; and feedback from school
principal or member(s) of school management team.
We found a small, positive relationship between teacher cooperation
and both teacher self-efficacy and job satisfaction, but not teacher
practices. Our findings comport with the results of prior research linking
collaborative teacher interactions to teacher job satisfaction (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2009; Stearns et al.,
2015; Torres, 2019) and teacher self-efficacy (Ainley & Carstens, 2018;
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2009).
Though cooperation was only marginally related to teacher practices,
prior research does evidence this relationship as well (Han et al., 2018).
We found limited evidence for cross-level interactions between select
school-level factors and teacher cooperation, that is, no contextual influences on the “effects” of teacher cooperation. The general absence of
main or interactive effects of level-2 variables may be partly attributable
to small cluster sizes in many schools, or the specific array of level-2
variables considered. We did observe that the “effect” of teacher cooperation on teaching practices was smaller when there were more barriers
to professional development in a school. Whereas prior research has
identified the relative frequencies of different professional-development
barriers or differences in the distribution of such barriers across school
or teacher populations (Fang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020), our study
suggests that these barriers might bear on the efficacy of particular
forms of teacher learning.
In the final statistical model the net teacher cooperation effect was
nevertheless statistically zero for teacher practices—in the context of the
large suite of control variables. Considering the results of earlier
research connecting informal teacher interactions and various teacher
outcomes (e.g., Ainley & Carstens, 2018; Han et al., 2018), this may be
somewhat unexpected. However, this non-significant effect is an
average effect and as discussed in the prior paragraph the effect of
teacher cooperation may be positive in some contexts. This discordance
with prior research may otherwise be due to instrumentation or analytic
aspects of our study design.
In relation to pre-service teacher education content foci, we found
evidence for the potential positive value of emphasizing teaching in a
multicultural or multilingual setting, student behavior and classroom
management, teaching cross-curricular skills, and monitoring students’
development and learning. Our finding of an association between a preservice emphasis on teaching in multicultural/multilingual settings and
teaching practice is consistent with prior evidence that pre-service
teachers’ multicultural awareness is related to their teaching skills as
operationalized by performance assessments (Cherng & Davis, 2019).
The observed positive relationship between a pre-service emphasis on
student behavior and classroom management and teaching practices is also
consistent with prior experimental research on the effects of pre-service
classroom management training (Klopfer et al., 2019).
Previous research using the TALIS dataset had found that teachers’
prior pre-service opportunities to learn about teaching cross-curricular
skills were related to their later job satisfaction and teaching practices
(Blömeke et al., 2021), though our study links these prior opportunities
to teacher self-efficacy as well. Extant meta-analytic research had also
well linked training in curriculum-based measurement and data-based

decision making to teachers’ knowledge, skills, and self-efficacy (Gesel
et al., 2021), whereas our study finds an association between a
pre-service emphasis on monitoring students’ development and learning
and job satisfaction.
Despite these more intuitive findings, we also found that an emphasis
on teaching in a multicultural or multilingual setting was associated
with lower job satisfaction, and pre-service emphasis on use of ICT for
teaching was associated with worse teaching practices. As it relates to
the former, the negative “effect” of preparation for teaching in a multicultural or multilingual setting may be due to confounding; preparation for
diverse contexts may be associated with later service in diverse,
potentially more challenging, contexts in which teachers are less satisfied. A plausible substantive explanation for a negative “effect” of preservice emphasis on use of ICT for teaching is more elusive. Methodological artifacts (e.g., confounding by unmeasured variables) may also
be possible explanations for either finding.
Given consensus that teachers rely on and need various domains of
knowledge (e.g., content knowledge, pedagogical knowledge, pedagogical content knowledge; National Research Council, 2010; Wilson
et al., 2001), it was surprising that we find null effects of such
pre-service content emphases. This discrepancy could be due to the fact
that earlier findings pertained to science and mathematics, whereas our
study includes all core subject areas. Alternatively, other pre-service
content foci (e.g., content knowledge) may not have reached significance due to limited variation, and we should not conclude that these
foci do not matter based on these results.
We also observed a number of relationships between particular
professional development activities—specifically online courses or
seminars; peer and/or self-observation and coaching; participation in a
network of teachers; and education conferences—and the considered
outcomes. By and large, this cluster of findings bolsters prior evidence
about professional development strategies such as online courses (e.g.,
Watson, 2006); observations and coaching (Fabiano et al., 2018;
Mousavi, 2014; Reddy et al., 2021); and informal teacher networks
(Edinger & Edinger, 2018; Toropova et al., 2021). Much less is known
about the effect of teacher participation in conferences as a form of
professional development, though available observational and descriptive studies suggest that they might advance self-efficacy or other
teacher outcomes (Carpenter and MacFarlane, 2018; Gümüş and
Bellibaş, 2021).
With the exception of online courses or seminars, the professional
development activities we observed as related to teacher self-efficacy do
tend to be of a more active nature, one hallmark of effective professional
development generally (Desimone, 2009). Our findings imply that a
variety of teacher professional development activities may serve to build
teacher capacity. However, we did not find evidence for more traditional
forms of professional development (e.g., face-to-face workshops). As for
null relationships between many professional development activities
and these outcomes, a number of explanations are possible. They simply
may not work. Or, the typical design of such activities may be of
insufficient quality to appreciably change teachers or their instruction.
Null findings may also reflect limitations or our data source or methodology, as well.
Next, having an assigned mentor was associated with lower selfefficacy and serving as a mentor was associated with higher selfefficacy. The negative “effect” of receiving mentoring is neither intuitive nor in step with theory and prior research on mentoring (Feng et al.,
2019; Hobson et al., 2009). A superficial interpretation of this finding
would be that a mentor causes one to have less self-efficacy, but the
effect could actually operate in the other direction: teachers who lack
self-efficacy may be more likely to be assigned a mentor. Indeed, a prior
cross-sectional observational study using TALIS 2013 (rather than 2018)
data in Australia (Perera et al., 2019) observed a similar negative effect
of mentoring on self-efficacy and characterized it as an instance of
reverse causality. By this same logic, mentoring another teacher may not
build self-efficacy but instead the most self-efficacious teachers may

We also found that teacher practices are more favorable for those
receiving more instructional feedback from the principal or other
member(s) of the school management team, as well as feedback from
external individuals or bodies. Feedback from the school principal or
members of the school management team was also associated with
teacher job satisfaction. These findings cohere well with prior evidence
on the value of providing specific feedback to teachers (Smith et al.,
2020), and the value of feedback from administrators in particular
(Mireles-Rios & Becchio, 2018). That feedback from the school principal
or members of the school management team was statistically significant
for two of three outcome variables implies an especially important role
of feedback from a principal or other school administrators.
The latter finding coheres with research showing that principals
matter to teachers; prior research shows that teacher relationships with
the principal are associated with enhanced job satisfaction (Price, 2012;
Stearns et al., 2015). While another study using TALIS data (Ford et al.,
2018) found that feedback from other teachers mattered, there were key
differences between our studies in that the variables were slightly
different (e.g., Ford emphasized quality rather than quantity of such
feedback and principal feedback was modeled at the school rather than
the individual level).
Several other researchers have deployed TALIS to address similar
research questions, and it is especially important for us to examine our
findings in relation to the findings of those studies. There were potentially important differences between our studies and those of other researchers. The first is that most of those studies (except Katsantonis,
2020) used TALIS 2013 data, and we used TALIS 2018 data. The specific
information collected during these cycles were not identical, so in some
instances our data were distinct. Second, there were methodological
differences in the use of TALIS variables and statistical modeling (e.g.,
construction of composite variables, inclusion of individual indicators,
incorporation of school-level variables). Generally, we also used individual indicator variables to preserve nuance rather than constructing
composite variables. Despite these differences, we note that we did
control for a number of the same individual-level covariates. Third, it
also appears that prior studies used the full TALIS teacher sample,
whereas our study was restricted to only regular, core-content area
teachers. Other observed differences between the findings of our study
and those of prior studies may reflect methodological choices, such as
the choice of outcome measures (Hill et al., 2011), focal population (e.g.,
lower-secondary educators) or modeling choices (e.g., inclusion of
covariates).

4.1. Limitations
This study has a number of important limitations. Whilst we relied on
a large, nationally-representative dataset, there were limitations of the
data source we selected. For example, the job satisfaction measure may
be problematic in the U.S. teacher population given estimation challenges reported by its authors, and measurement error in the other
measures is also a possibility. As well, a reliable performance-based
measure of teacher practices may have been preferable to selfreported evidence (Little et al., 2009). Descriptive statistics also reveal
restriction of range for some independent variables, a likely reflection of
the distribution of participation in select teacher-education activities in
the U.S. We also do not connect teacher education mechanisms to student
outcomes or other important teacher outcomes such as teacher
well-being or stress. In addition, our findings may only apply to
lower-secondary, non-SPED educators.
Although we capitalized on a rich set of primary variables and
covariates and addressed missing data issues, there are also limitations
associated with our observational design and analytic choices. We report
here only evidence relationships rather than causal relationships, due to
potential extraneous variables. Observed relationships might plausibly
reflect unmeasured teacher or school-level characteristics and the
