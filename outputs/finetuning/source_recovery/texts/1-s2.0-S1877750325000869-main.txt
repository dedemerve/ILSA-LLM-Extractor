A B S T R A C T
We analyze the topic structure of 10,299 publications from the International Conference on Computational 
Science (ICCS) between 2001 and 2024 as well as the Journal of Computational Science (JoCS) between 2010 
and 2023, using natural language processing techniques and network analysis. The computational science 
classification corpus was created into 15 main disciplines and 256 sub-disciplines sourced from Wikipedia. 
Among the 15 main disciplines, machine learning became the most popular topic after 2019, surpassing 
parallel & distributed computing, which peaked in the early 2010s. ICCS and JoCS show differences in research 
popularity in both first and second-level disciplines. Algorithm theory, Mathematical modeling, and network 
science are the most dominant topics in both ICCS and JoCS. Different disciplines present different trends in 
ICCS and JoCS. In the past 24 years, machine learning related topics have gained the most attention in both 
ICCS and JoCS. We also examined and compared the correlation between the trends in ICCS and Google search 
Trends. The collaboration of disciplinary networks of second-level disciplines exhibits a scale-free characteristic, 
and the network structures have undergone significant evolution over 24 years. Moreover, different disciplinary 
communities exhibit different "introverted" and "extroverted" community characteristics within the network. 
Additionally, we examined the life span of thematic workshops and the evolution of authors’ collaborations 
inside and after ICCS.
1. Introduction
The framework of modern science is intricate. The total number 
of academic publications reached 3 million in 2020, and it contin-
ues its exponential growth [1,2]. As previous studies have indicated, 
the number of newly published papers doubles every 15 years [2]. 
However, among tens of thousands of scholarly publications, the emer-
gence of new ideas only follows a linear trend [3]. Therefore, utilizing 
quantitative methods to measure scientific development trends across 
disciplines is crucial. In contrast to the past, modern science divi-
sion presents the character of diversification, multidisciplinary coop-
eration, and interdisciplinary collaboration. The quantitative analyses 
offer advantages in studying the complex relationships among massive 
academic publications.

Scientometrics serves as an important approach to studying the 
science of science by providing empirical evidence through multiple 
quantitative research methods to uncover trends in scientific devel-
opment. Current scientometrics measures the impact of science and 
technology on society critically based on citation counts, keywords, 
and paper counts [10,11]. The traditional keywords-based analysis 
lacks quantitative measures in computational linguistics, which makes 
it harder to provide more detailed evidence in depicting the trends, 
guiding science policy, and the exploration of multi-disciplinary col-
laboration. The significance of this research is thereby clear: applying 
quantitative measures to understand the development laws of com-
putational science; providing evidence for science policy to allocate 
resources effectively to promote its advancement; and analyzing and 
predicting the trend of computational science development.
In the field of computer and computational science, the continuous 
growth and digitization of academic publications provide vast amounts 
of data for scientometric research. The International Conference on 
Computation Science (ICCS)1 and the Journal on Computational Sci-
ence (JoCS)2 offer an extensive corpus for scientometric studies in 
the field of computational science. ICCS and JoCS serve as two pres-
tigious academic platforms for researchers, scientists, and engineers 
to explore computational topics spanning mathematics and computer 
science. Computational science is inherently interdisciplinary, offering 
advanced computational techniques to address problems, identify new 
issues, and shape future directions in physics, biology, chemistry, social 
sciences, and other fields. Additionally, computational science inves-
tigates how to enhance computing power and develop higher-quality 
algorithms based on the nature of computation.
In this study, we employ natural language processing techniques 
and complex networks to model and analyze the content of academic 
publications in ICCS and JoCS. Based on the premise that a document 
consists of various topics corresponding to specific disciplines, each 
topic is characterized by distinct professional terminologies. We utilize 
topic modeling techniques and similarity comparison to discover, ana-
lyze, and quantify these topics. With a standardized corpus categorized 
by discipline, we calculate a popularity score for each discipline, which 
we apply to investigate emerging and declining research disciplines 
based on their popularity and apply time-series analysis to examine and 
predict future trends. Furthermore, we conduct an annual analysis to 
explore the changes in the distribution of research popularity and the 
evolution of the interdisciplinary network over 24 years for ICCS and 
14 years for JoCS. We conduct this research to answer the following 
questions: How does the popularity trend in computational science 
change? How can we model and investigate the interdisciplinary nature

2. Related work
2.1. Topic modeling methods
Among the most commonly used methods for topic modeling, La-
tent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) and Non-Negative Matrix Factorization 
(NMF) are widely employed to decompose documents into specific top-
ics with distinct keywords. Initially applied to machine learning by Blei 
et al. [12], LDA is widely used as an unsupervised generative probabilis-
tic model for topic modeling that explicitly represents a document by 
collecting discrete tokens. LDA breaks documents into multiple topics, 
where words are generated with maximum likelihood to represent each 
topic [12–14]. NMF is also regarded as a common matrix decomposi-
tion method in multiple application fields [15]. In natural language 
processing applications, Greene et al. proposed NMF as a method 
based on matrix factorization to model topics in documents [16]. 
However, previous studies have highlighted certain drawbacks of NMF. 
Wang et al. demonstrated that NMF-based topic modeling requires 
high computational complexity and computing power for optimization 
purposes [17]. Pan et al. also indicated that the non-uniqueness of NMF 
can result in multiple different factorizations for a given input, leading 
to unstable topic and keyword combinations [18]. The ambiguity and 
context-dependent keywords within each topic make interpretation 
more challenging for highly professional topics for both topic modeling 
methods [19]. Additionally, feature selection and non-uniqueness of 
tokens can cause variations in results, resulting in mislabeling and 
blurred boundaries between topics.
2.2. TF-IDF & cosine similarity
The cosine similarity of term frequency-inversed document fre-
quency (TF-IDF) vectors has been employed as a method to measure 
text similarity, in line with the distributional hypothesis in natural lan-
guage processing, which posits that similar words tend to co-occur [20]. 
Gunawan et al. demonstrated the applicability of cosine similarity for 
categorizing papers based on text keywords, proposing a methodol-
ogy for classifying research disciplines by analyzing a series of key-
words [21]. It implies that cosine similarity could be utilized when 
labeling technical terminologies or texts with a standardized corpus 
with a unique document-term matrix.
Wang et al. identified limitations in the traditional ‘‘Bag of Words’’ 
(BoW) representation and introduced an approach using Wikipedia to 
compute content-based similarity between documents [22]. This tech-
nique enables the classification of papers on unknown topics through 
high-similarity references. Furthermore, Wang et al.’s findings revealed 
that the directed acyclic graph Wikipedia links forms containing mul-
tiple classification schemes within each entry [22]. Under this context, 
the standardized classification with hierarchical tree structures could 
be considered to avoid the drawback of unstable decomposition raised 
by classical topic modeling. Classifying unknown publications by a 
stable and unique combination of similarity classification labels could 
mitigate the instability and explainability of unsupervised learning. 
Nastase et al. meanwhile, proposed creating a large-scale multilingual 
concept network from Wikipedia’s knowledge base [23]. It implies that 
the methodology of applying concepts in science could be used to create 
an interdisciplinary network.

2.3. Previous research on ICCS
Among previous topic modeling of ICCS, Abuhay et al. employed 
the NMF-based topic modeling method and categorized topics into 
13 high-level topics [13,14]. The authors highlighted that modeling, 
HPC, and e-science were among the most prevalent subjects before 
2017. Additionally, Abuhay et al. also proposed comprehensive analy-
ses for both static and dynamic networks of topics in ICCS topics [14]. 
However, the drawback identified in previous research pointed out 
that NMF and LDA-based topic modeling rely on probabilities. It leads 
to unstable and non-unique topics and keyword decomposition. At 
the same time, manually labeled keyword shows potential ambiguity, 
context-dependency, and subjectivity issues [19]. Context-dependency 
results in keywords presenting completely different meanings within 
varying contexts, thus necessitating researchers with a high level of 
comprehensive knowledge and time cost in labeling.
Luo et al. on the other hand, introduced a method of utilizing 
the TF-IDF matrix to classify papers by comparing cosine similarity 
between paper vectors and classification corpus vectors [24]. The 
study revealed that parallel and distributed computing reached its peak 
in 2012, while machine learning and AI-related topics have become 
increasingly popular since 2019. Furthermore, the authors analyzed 
the collaborations across higher-level disciplines. However, the authors 
also emphasized that a more precise and comprehensive classification 
library of disciplines with a broader publication corpus can yield results 
that are closer to the actual situation [14,24].
On the other side, the ICCS preface also reported that the thematic 
tracks and workshops have changed a lot over time [25–28]. As an 
important component of a conference, the workshop of the conference 
has a crucial impact on the topic structure. The life span of work-
shops and thematic tracks would influence the topical structure of the 
conference. In the two previous studies on ICCS, the authors did not 
consider the topic changes caused by the change of the conference 
board’s adjustment in workshops based on the conference’s long-term 
and short-term development strategic planning [14,24].
2.4. Computational science classification
In the field of computer science classification, the Association for 
Computing Machinery (ACM) proposes two main categories [29,30]. 
The ACM computer science curricula classify computer science into 17 
bodies of knowledge and numerous sub-disciplines [30]. Computational 
science is not regarded as a separate category compared with previous 
editions. Due to its nature as an educational guide for computer science, 
excessively detailed classification results in an imbalance of topics.
In contrast, the ACM Computing Classification System divides com-
puter science into 14 disciplines [29]. The advantages of this system are 
its inclusion of computer hardware and applied computing categories, 
as well as providing a more balanced and reasonable classification 
of lower-level disciplines that align closely with real research fields. 
However, algorithms and computing methods remain over-detailed 
in classification, while other disciplines are vaguely classified. Nei-
ther classification category adequately covers interdisciplinary appli-
cations and interdisciplinary modeling extensively enough. Britannica 
and Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy defined and briefly describe 
the classifications of scientific modeling [31,32]. The Oak Ridge Na-
tional Laboratory divides computational science applications into eight 
fields based on their scenarios [33].
2.5. Network analysis
Barabasi & Albert first proposed the concept of the scale-free net-
work [34]. They pointed out that new vertices attached are more 
likely to connect with already well-connected nodes. The authors also 
pointed out a characteristic of scale-free networks in the research: 
the self-organization, and a few nodes have more connections than 
other nodes, the degree distribution follows a power-law distribution. 
Broido et al. rephrased the definition of scale-free networks into four 
levels from weakest to strongest [35]. The author pointed out that 
only a few networks could be recognized as strong scale-free networks. 
Karunakaran et al. analyzed the information spreading in a complex 
network for developing more efficient methods from the perspective of 
graph theory [36].
Watts & Strogatz introduced the concept of local clustering coef-
ficient in measuring the ‘‘small world’’ networks [37]. The clustering 
coefficient describes the degree nodes in a network tend to cluster 
together by calculating the number of actual connections and the 
number of potential connections between neighbors. Based on previ-
ous research, Kemper introduced the global clustering coefficient to 
evaluate the whole network [38]. Li et al. performed calculations on 
some large-scale networks in reality and made comparisons on the scale 
of clustering coefficient [39]. A high clustering coefficient indicates 
a stronger community structure in the network, as well as densely 
connected sub-graphs or groups, are formed between nodes.
The properties of the edges are also worthy of attention. Kang et al. 
applied a method of changing intra-community and inter-community 
edges to improve community detection [40]. Malekzadeh & Long 
used these concepts to measure community similarity in complex 
networks [41]. It implies that these two parameters show different 
properties of communities. Since interdisciplinary networks are real-
world networks with manually classified disciplines, Luo et al. intro-
duced these two concepts into pre-determined communities to explore 
the characteristics of the discipline [24]. Okamura divided all academic 
publications into eight disciplines and studied the communication be-
tween them [42]. It provided evidence that the degree of affinity varied 
between different pairs of disciplines. At the same time, Okamura 
pointed out that the Landscape is highly dependent on the classification 
scheme of the research fields.
2.6. Collaboration network evolution & sustainability science
In addition to theoretical research on network science, there are 
many studies on network science in scientific collaboration, researcher 
collaboration networks, and sustainability science. As Barabasi points 
out, the evolution of the social network of scientific collaborations 
follows some specific rules [43]. The authors point out an important 
conclusion: the author collaboration network is scale-free, and the 
network evolution follows the preferential attachment rule. Based on 
the truth that the internal linking process is dominant, existing authors 
prefer to work with authors they already ‘‘know’’ rather than new 
authors.
There is also some other significant collaborative network-related 
research in the scientific community. Based on the rule that if two scien-
tists are considered connected if they have co-authored the same paper, 
Newman created explicit networks of the science community [44]. 
The author found that authors wrote 4 papers in a five-year period, 
and each paper had 3 authors on average. The author network fits a 
power-law form with an exponential cutoff and which also satisfies 
the definition of a ‘‘small worlds’’ network. Bella et al. also found that 
the co-authorship network has the properties of a scale-free network 
when studying the Italian Institute of Technology author collaboration 
network [45]. This evidence also suggests that the networks formed 
by collaboration between researchers have the properties of scale-free 
networks.
At the same time, the scientific community’s attention to sustain-
ability science and the interdisciplinary application of science has 
also further promoted the research of scientometrics. Hajkowicz et al. 
conducted a quantitative analysis of the application of AI in various 
fields with over 130 million documents in 333 research fields. The 
authors point out the rapid growth of AI applications across different 
fields [46]. Kalhor et al. also investigated the interconnections within 
computer science fields and subfields to evaluate the interdisciplinarity 
and multidisciplinary. The result indicates that computing method-
ologies and privacy are the two most interdisciplinary topics [47]. 
Other researchers have also studied the interdisciplinary applications 
of computer science. All studies point to the highly interdisciplinary 
nature of computer science [47–49].

2.7. Important external events
Just as Wang illustrated, ‘‘The progress of science is not a heroic 
view of history, but the accumulation of results of countless
researchers’’ [2]. Past research on the science history and science policy 
pointed out that the development process of science is affected by a se-
ries of factors such as social events, policies, and external conditions [2,
50,51]. Computational science has both theoretical and applied aspects, 
which makes this field more influential on external changes, especially 
engineering advancement and policy, than academic journals with 
more theoretical attributes. In 2007, Nvidia published the Compute 
Unified Device Architecture (CUDA),3 which allows the software to 
utilize graphics processing units (GPUs) to accelerate general-purpose 
processing. The ‘‘Titan’’, created by the Oak Ridge National Laboratory, 
achieved the title of first GPGPU supercomputer and topped the top500 
in 2012, which pushed computational science to a new era [52]. 
Vaswani et al. first introduced the Transformer architecture for deep 
learning with a brand new attention mechanism, which reformed the 
shape of deep learning [53]. The National Strategic Overview For 
Quantum Information Science, published by the National Science & 
Technology Council of the United States of America and the Na-
tional Quantum Initiative Act in 2018, had a profound impact on the 
development of quantum communications and computing [54,55].
3. Data collection, preprocessing & modeling methods
In this study, the data is divided into two corpora: the ICCS corpus 
and the classification corpus. We collected all the papers published 
in the ICCS proceedings by Springer Lecture Notes in Computer Sci-
ence (LNCS) (2001–2009 & 2018–2024), as well as Elsevier Procedia 
Computer Science (2010–2017). For the JoCS, we directly obtained 
the journal papers from the ScienceDirect Journal of Computational 
Science, all issues (2010–2023). The links to published volumes can 
be found in the conference webpage4 and the official journal website.5
The ICCS corpus encompasses 7985 papers over the twenty-four 
years, an average of 333 papers each year, with an average length of 
eleven pages each. The JoCS corpus contains a total of 2314 papers 
with a time span of fourteen years, averaging around 178 papers per 
year and ten pages each (see Table 1). We processed both corpora as 
follows: eliminating corrupted files due to coding issues, and excluding 
editorial information, updates regarding journal editorial boards, and 
workshop information. After this process was completed, there remain 
7861 papers in the ICCS corpus and 1817 papers in the JoCS corpus 
(see Table 1).
As for the creation of the text classification library, we mainly refer 
to four sources: the 2023 computer science curricula, the Comput-
ing Classification System provided by the Association for Computing 
Machinery (ACM), the outline of computer science by Curlie, and 
the Wikipedia category entry ‘‘Computer Science’’. We also consulted 
the ACM 2013 computer science curricula, the Oak Ridge National

We then apply the following pre-processes to both text contents:
(1) We removed the HTML tags and the unrelated content from 
the classification corpus from the standardized classification corpus 
for each second-level discipline and made matching annotations for 
first-level and second-level subjects
(2) In the ICCS and JoCS corpora, all documents are standardized 
into text format. Only the main content of the papers (from abstract to 
conclusion & discussion) is retained. Before the textual preprocessing, 
we apply the position-of-speech (POS) in Spacy6 to chunk and extract 
the professional terminology as well as the abbreviations for further 
analysis.
(3) Then, English stop words, punctuation marks, figures, and num-
bers that do not contain topical information are eliminated. Newline 
characters, stop characters, and other text symbols that may alter 
token information are also deleted or replaced. Additionally, interfering 
elements such as place names, personal names, organizations, etc., 
which could disrupt the classification process, are extracted to create 
the author networks and excluded from the corpora.
(4) We utilize lemmatisation to standardize the various forms of 
words used by authors for grammatical purposes (such as tense, sin-
gular, and plural) while preserving the inherent information contained 
within each word.

4. The trends of ICCS & JoCS topics
4.1. The trends in ICCS
Initially, we present the trends in ICCS. By calculating the co-
sine similarity between tf-idf vectors from the ICCS corpus and the 
standardized classification corpus, we obtained the rank of the topic 
similarity score for each paper. First, we conducted five preliminary 
experiments with different numbers of topics, 𝑁= 10, 20, 30, 40, 
and 50, respectively. The exclusive classification between disciplines 
prevents the shortcomings of numerous topics with high similarity. On 
the other hand, many chosen disciplines will lead to issues of irrelevant 
topic inclusion. Based on the experiment results, we decided to adopt 
𝑁= 20 as it represents a significant number of strongly relevant 
disciplines and set the rest of the similarity score to 0. The results 
revealed a high degree of linear correlation with the research from 
2017 between the two methods (see Table 3) [13,14].
We then calculate the popularity score of topics for each year by 
aggregating the similarity scores across all first-level disciplines and 
standardizing them to average popularity scores per 100 papers each 
year for comparability. After smoothing the data to observe the long-

5. The dynamic analysis of networks
We then proceed to investigate the interdisciplinary relationship 
within computational science. To construct the interdisciplinary net-
work of ICCS, we utilize methodologies akin to previous studies of 
co-occurrence: if two disciplines are covered by the same paper, it 
signifies there is a correlation between them [13,24]. To standardize 
the weight, we normalize the weights of all other topics based on 
the weight of the topic with the highest relevance. Subsequently, we 
assign weights to edges by aggregating the similarity scores for each 
pair of disciplines. Simultaneously, we conduct several experiments by 
pruning edges and selecting the weight threshold at 0.5, where all 
edges with a normalized weight of less than 0.5 will be pruned, to 
construct the twenty-four undirected interdisciplinary networks over 
twenty-four years. In these networks, each node represents the specific 
second-level discipline, edges signify co-existence among disciplines in 
the group of academic papers, and edge weights indicate the strength 
of the co-occurrence [24].
After analyzing the structure of the networks of collaborations in 
ICCS over 10 years, we observed that the distribution of the degree 𝑘 of 
the nodes exhibits the characteristics of a ‘‘strong scale-free network’’, 
according to the classification [35] for scale-free networks. To do so, we 
examine the distribution of the degree of the networks and compare the 
goodness-of-fit of the power-law distribution (𝐹(𝑘) = 𝐶𝑘−𝛼) with the 
lognormal, the exponential, and the truncated power-law distributions. 
The result shows no significant preference for the lognormal and expo-
nential distributions over the power-law distribution (see Table C.1). 
Comparing the power-law and the truncated power-law distributions, 
we found that more than 50% of the graphs have a 𝑝-value 𝑝>
0.1, which means that the power-law distribution cannot be rejected 
compared to the truncated power-law for these networks. At the same 
time, the power-law region contains more than 20% of the nodes (the 
number of nodes in the tail is larger than 50 nodes), and more than 
50% of the exponents verify that 3 > 𝛼> 2 (see Table 6). For example, 
Fig. 9 shows the degree distribution for 2024, and the fit of the upper 
tail of the complementary cumulative distribution, appearing to be a 
straight line on a log–log scale.
The average clustering coefficient and the total number of edges 
over the past five years retained approximately 0.5 and 3000 (see Table 
6). It implies that there are obvious local cluster structures in ICCS topic 
networks. The topics in the conference formed some local community 
structures, which were more efficient for interactions between different 
disciplines. Since most of the exponent 𝛼 falls between two and three, 
it indicates a strong, heavy-tailed nature of the disciplinary network 
(see Fig. 9 bottom). Most of the disciplines in the network have very 
little collaboration with other fields, while only a few disciplines have 
a large chance to be highly active in the whole network. It further 
implies that discoveries, concepts, and methodologies in the topics that 
have higher degrees will spread faster than other fields in practical 
interdisciplinary networks. New methods and discoveries in unpopular 
topics spread slowly and less comprehensively. From the perspective of 
the researchers, they prefer to do research on popular fields or apply 
hot topics in their own field in the whole research community.
The structure of the network is also undergoing significant changes. 
Throughout the timeline, there have been several switches in the hubs 
and essential edges within the network. Fig. 10 shows standardized 
average intra-community and inter-community edges of each first-level 
discipline each year, by regarding the manually divided first-level 
disciplines as network communities. Each community presents differ-
ent ‘‘Introverted’’ and ‘‘extroverted’’ characteristics (See Table 7 & 
Fig. 11). The introverted property with a more significant number of 
intra-community edges exhibited by modeling & simulation, computer 
network & security, and mathematics of computing indicates their 
likelihood to form a stable internal research community (See cluster 
4 in Fig. 11). Conversely, the extroverted communities with a more 
significant number of inter-community edges observed in disciplines 
such as parallel & distributed computing and information systems 
& data science suggest their preference for external interaction (See 
cluster 2 in Fig. 11).
At the same time, we observe that algorithms & data structures, as 
well as machine learning, show both introverted and extroverted char-
acteristics, which implies the truth that both the theoretical algorithms 
and machine learning methods are progressing, and their exploration 
of broader application in other fields are advancing over the past few 
years (See cluster 1 & 3 in Fig. 11). This observation aligns with the 
practical situation where introverted disciplines are more theoretical 
research or highly professional fields tend to form communities with 
barriers and interacting with other communities through a few interme-
diaries [24]. On the other hand, extroverted disciplines mostly operate 
in the general application layer of computational science. They need to 
collaborate with other research topics to maximize their value. From 
the perspective of the workflow of knowledge, the process from theory 
to industry requires the application of existing theories in applied 
science. Therefore, a hub is required to serve as an intermediary to 
connect both sides of a topic.

The third period, spanning from 2015 to 2018, is referred to as 
the era of big data. Although ICCS is not a data science conference, 
the impact of big data on ICCS is obvious. The predominant keywords 
and terminologies during this period include big data, data analysis, 
data mining, statistical analysis, etc. Throughout this period, there 
was a noticeable surge in research interest in information systems and 
databases. Various sectors, including society, academia, and industry, 
were all significantly influenced by technologies such as big data and 
data mining [56]. Moreover, the advancement of big data has also 
played a pivotal role in fostering the progress of other disciplines. As 
defined by IBM, ‘‘machine learning focuses on utilizing data and algo-
rithms to enable AI to mimic human learning processes while gradually 
enhancing its accuracy’’ [57]. It demonstrated that data serves as an 
indispensable component for machine learning. In conjunction with 
previous findings, the interaction between data science and machine 
learning algorithms has stimulated advancements in machine learning 
techniques.
Lastly, the period between 2018 and 2024 is characterized as the 
era of machine learning. The predominant keywords and terminologies 
during this period include machine learning, deep learning, natural lan-
guage processing, neural networks, etc. During this time, we witnessed 
a substantial surge in academic publications related to machine learn-
ing across both theoretical research and practical applications. Just as 
the transformer architecture boosted deep learning after 2017 [53], 
machine learning and AI have already emerged as the leading research 
focus within computational science. Furthermore, machine learning has 
profoundly influenced various disciplines by establishing a framework 
for contemporary computational science research through collabora-
tions with algorithms, mathematical foundations, and modeling tech-
niques. At the same time, we noticed the activation of the modeling 
community and its rapid increase in interaction with the machine 
learning community. Combining with the result that the widely applied 
machine learning in modeling in ICCS, we have enough evidence 
to believe that the increase in popularity in machine learning re-
search has led to an increasing research interest in modeling and 
simulation-related disciplines.
We then discuss the linear correlation between Google Trends and 
ICCS topic trends. Table 9 presents the Pearson correlation test of 
some representatives between the two trends. From the Pearson linear 
correlation comparison test, we found that different disciplines show 
different degrees of collinearity. Although most trends present a high 
degree of linearity, it is worth mentioning that parallel computing, 
algorithms, numerical analysis, ABM, and mathematical modeling show 
no linear correlation with public search preferences. These disciplines 
show one common characteristic: They are all disciplines with strong 
theoretical attributes. The public’s insensitivity to theory-driven re-
search stems from the fact that non-scientific researchers find it hard to 
directly understand highly specialized theoretical research. Therefore, 
public search preference is not sensitive to these changes in academia, 
resulting in poor linear correlation. In contrast, application fields with 
strong social attributes, such as infectious disease models, climate 
models, and network security, etc. are more sensitive. It makes them 
more likely to show higher similar trends compared with the public 
interest. In the same way, disciplines with a strong industrial nature, 
such as GPGPU, operating systems, and blockchain, also present a very 
strong linear correlation. It indicates that the concerns of academia, 
industries, and society tend to show similar interest in these topics with 
their advancements.

7.2. Author network
Within the study, we were trying to identify ICCS’s role as a living 
community and a forum for building new collaborations. We used Ope-

in ICCS proceedings (16 178 authors, 321 848 papers).
For identification of emergent collaboration events, we performed 
a search of author pairs, where authors both have publications at some 
ICCS separately (without prior joint publications, so we consider that 
they met at ICCS). We then selected pairs that published joint papers 
after the meeting in any source (including ICCS). The total number of 
pairs in the selected set was 3623 pairs of authors.

later) is lower, partly because there was not enough time yet to publish 
joint results. The appearance of the first joint publication is an average 
delay of 4.8 years. It is worth noticing that the number of thematic 
workshops and author pairs that appeared in ICCS both peaked in 2007 
and 2015. Combined with previous results, they present a similar trend 
of increase and decrease. It becomes strong evidence that the thematic

8. Discussion and conclusions
Within the time span of 24 years, ICCS has witnessed four main 
periods: the era of network and security, parallel and distributed com-
puting, big data, and finally, machine learning. These four disciplines 
have successively gained the most popularity in 2006, 2012, 2016, 
and 2024 (see Fig. 1). Among them, Modeling & simulation, Algorithm 
& data structures, mathematics on computing, and applied computing 
have consistently remained highly popular throughout these 24 years

with a combined proportion exceeding 30% of total popularity in both 
ICCS and JoCS (see Figs. 2 & 6). As for ICCS 2024 specifically, the ma-
chine learning-related topic emerges as the most popular topic within 
ICCS. While in JoCS, except for machine learning, other disciplines gen-
erally follow different trends (see Fig. 5). Mathematics foundation and 
modeling & simulation gained a different level of research popularity. 
Despite the rapid growth of machine learning related topics, Modeling 
& Simulation still maintains its dominance in JoCS (see Fig. 6).
From a second-level disciplinary perspective over the past two 
decades, the top three researched areas are algorithm theory, mathe-
matical modeling, and network science for both ICCS and JoCS (see 
Tables 4 & 5). In contrast, in terms of current research popularity 
in 2024, the top three ranked topics are machine learning theory, 
deep learning, and mathematical modeling in ICCS (see Table 4). The 
top three ranked second-level disciplines in JoCS are network science, 
machine learning theory, and algorithm theory (see Table 5).
From the perspective of magnitude change, machine learning the-
ory, deep learning, and supervised learning stand out as three dis-
ciplines that have experienced substantial growth in popularity (see 
Fig. 3), while parallel computing, distributed computing, and operating 
systems have seen the most decline over the past twenty-four years (see 
Fig. 4) in ICCS. While in JoCS, the top three emerging second-level 
disciplines over the past 14 years are the same as those of ICCS (Deep 
learning, supervised learning, and machine learning theory). However, 
the top three declining disciplines consist of parallel computing, dis-
tributed computing, and computational sociology in JoCS (see Figs. 7 
& 8).
The topology of the interdisciplinary network shows an average 
clustering coefficient of 0.5, with a community structure characterized 
by relatively tight connections within each community (see Table 
6). We also found strong evidence to identify the interdisciplinary 
networks in computational science as a scale-free network. A small 
number of nodes possess high-weighted degrees, while most points 
share fewer edges. Some disciplines serve as hubs in the network and 
act as intermediaries for other fields. The framework formed by core 
disciplines plays a crucial role in maintaining stability. Specifically, 
modeling and simulation, algorithms, and mathematics served as three 
core areas within these networks (see Fig. 12). Since the core topics of 
the interdisciplinary network in computational science are not affected, 
the network structure will not change significantly. This result further 
implies the interdisciplinary network exhibits fault tolerance: changes 
to nodes with lower weighted degrees will not lead to collapse so long 
as important disciplines remain unaffected.
Different communities exhibit varying levels of introversion and 
extroversion within their respective communities (see Fig. 11 & Table 
7). Programming languages and compilers, computer network & secu-
rity are considered strong introverted disciplines, whereas algorithms, 
parallel & distributed computing, and information systems play a more 
extroverted role. Mathematics in computing, along with machine learn-
ing, possesses both introverted and extroverted characteristics. From 
the practical situation, disciplines that are highly professional or have 
difficulty directly collaborating with other fields in introverted com-
munities tend to show certain barriers to collaborating with other 
communities. On the other hand, disciplines related to computational 
methods & algorithms, practical applicability, and data science tend 
to be more extroverted, where they are more likely to co-exist with 
other theories or methods. The machine learning community not only 
presents strong internal collaboration, but is also active with many 
other communities (see Table 7 & Fig. 11). Collaboration in machine 
learning has experienced a remarkable increase both internally and 
externally in the late 2010s. It implies that both inner theory and 
external applications are progressing in machine learning during this 
period.

simultaneously with the number of workshops (2007, 2015), and they 
are highly similar in time series. The number of pairs decreased in 
the past five years, and the average first joint publication delay is 
4.8 years. The average number of papers per pair is approximately 6.83. 
The connectivity of the author pairs presents two patterns. First, the 
highly connected community tends to attract members from the same 
institution and new connections to the conference. Second, the large 
connected component covers most of the researchers: 65.6% of the total 
authors (1687 authors) belong to this large community.
On the other hand, there are still certain areas for future research 
or potential drawbacks in this field that warrant further exploration. 
Despite the esteemed reputation of ICCS and JoCS as conferences 
and journals in computational science, their coverage remains limited. 
This limitation may introduce bias into our assessment of the overall 
trend. Given the vast and intricate disciplinary network of computer 
science, it is imperative to incorporate a broader range of journals and 
conference papers as part of our corpus for studying the comprehensive 
development of computer science.

Considering the classification corpus, although we referenced three 
classification libraries, there are still certain research topics that have

unbalanced in specific fields. This limitation may lead to a biased 
weighting in the classification process. Additionally, the results are 
influenced by the Wikipedia editor’s language habits and background in 
the field. It caused the limitation that the classification corpus created 
by Wikipedia is not completely balanced. Future research could explore 
applying a broader academic publication corpus with an advanced pre-
trained LLM with the ability to reason and inference to prompt or 
fine-tune a more accurate, comprehensive classifier.
Modern science is characterized by its complexity. Computational 
science research is influenced by various external factors such as social 
events, policies, and technological advancements in other fields. To 
provide a more holistic understanding of scientific development, future 
researchers could consider incorporating a broader model encompass-
ing additional fields and considering external influences to depict a 
more comprehensive picture.
