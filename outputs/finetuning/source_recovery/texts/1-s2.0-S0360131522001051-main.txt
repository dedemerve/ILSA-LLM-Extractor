The authors recruited 267 Taiwanese elementary school students to play digital games in order to
investigate the influence of prior game play experience on problem-solving performance, with
specific focuses on self-regulated learning, problem-solving processes, problem contexts, and
problem characteristics. Data were acquired using a self-regulated learning scale, a game experience questionnaire, a problem-solving process evaluation instrument based on the 2012 Program for International Student Assessment, a computer-based survey focused on seven problemsolving scenarios, and four digital games. Our results indicate a positive and significant effect on
problem-solving performance among study participants with seven or more years of game play
experience, and positive feelings toward game learning as reflected in negatively skewed selfregulated learning scores. We also found that the “representation and formulation” stage
served as a significant performance predictor, the “planning and execution” and “monitoring and
reflection” stages were significant predictors of dynamic/technical problems, and the “exploring
and understanding” stage was a predictor for static/non-technical problems. Our data indicate
significant differences in problem-solving cognitive processes across ranges of contexts and
characteristics. We believe our findings will be useful for researchers studying the potential use of
digital game frameworks to measure specific learning mechanisms.

1. Introduction
Digital literacy—the ability of individuals to use software tools and perform simple information retrieval tasks—is now considered
essential at all levels of society. The concept has grown to the extent that digital technology is now perceived as an indispensable aspect
of daily life (Buckingham, 2008). Requirements in terms of basic hardware and software knowledge or prior experience are
increasingly pervasive in daily work, leisure, and communication tasks (Hepp, Hinostroza, Laval, & Rehbein, 2004). Greiff, Wüstenberg, Holt, Goldhammer and Funke (2013) describe such activities and contexts as involving dynamically changing environments,
with multiple interconnected influences demanding active interaction with technical systems. Acquiring information, clarifying
problem representation, and planning and executing goal-oriented actions increasingly demand such forms of interaction, therefore
digital literacy now requires a combination of learning, analysis, and practical hands-on skills (Tsatsou, 2018).

Since 2003, the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) has collected information on problem-solving and digital
literacy trends in education. PISA represents a commitment by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)
to monitor the outcomes of international education systems in terms of student achievement. One of its primary activities is measuring
scholastic performance among 15-year-old students, a procedure first performed in 2000 and repeated every three years since. PISA
covers reading, mathematics, and science, with one topic emphasized over the others during each cycle.
A problem-solving subject was added in 2012, followed by collaborative problem-solving in 2015 and global competence in 2018;
creative thinking will be the focus in 2022. Problem-solving skills—defined as the ability to use cognitive processes to solve crossdisciplinary problems in which a solution path is not obvious—was added as a new assessment element in 2003 (OECD, 2003). The
PISA emphasis on problem-solving as an independent subject area reflects a belief that it is now foundational for learning, societal
participation, and personal success. In response to this understanding and the expanding digitized lifestyles of citizens in all parts of the
world, the OECD investigated student familiarity with information and communication technologies, and used its findings when
adding computer-based assessments to its problem-solving test section in 2012. In addition to focusing on daily life problems and
interdisciplinary cognitive skills, the 2012 version addressed the use of technical tools in problem-solving—PISA’s first attempt at
measuring digital literacy (Stacey, 2015; Thomson, Hillman, & De Bortoli, 2013). The PISA2015 focus on problem-solving performance differed from that in PISA 2012 in that it paid greater attention to collaborative skills associated with taking appropriate action,
team organization, and maintaining a shared understanding in situations where two or more agents need to work together to solve a
problem (OECD, 2017). The PISA 2012 computer-based problem-solving assessment effort shared similarities with digital game play;
in contrast, collaborative problem-solving assessment in PISA 2015 emphasized the idea of working cooperatively with others. We
therefore chose PISA 2012 as the primary reference when designing our assessment tool, which is more suitable for our main study
focus: digital literacy and its connections with technical systems in digital games.
Digital games require players to navigate across complex systems, collaborate with others, and employ digital media for problemsolving task completion (Squire, 2002). Usually played separately from decontextualized classroom activities, well-designed digital
games have the potential to prioritize knowledge construction within learning environments, thus supporting critical thinking practice
and problem-solving skill development (Checa-Romero, 2016). As an interactive medium, digital games occupy a central position in
digital literacy studies, and a large number of researchers have analyzed the potential for games to support learning operations (Squire,
2008). Thus, game characteristics are now perceived as core factors in shaping processes and outcomes associated with
learning-by-gaming (Hung, Yang, Hwang, Chu, & Wang, 2018). Example topics include the role of information feedback systems in
learning quality and effectiveness (Wu & Wang, 2008), and how to inject repetition into practice and tests (Coyne, 2003). Some researchers are focusing on specific effects of digital games on learning—for example, whether action video games help learners develop
skills in extracting environmental patterns or regularities (Bavelier, Green, Pouget, & Schrater, 2012). Regardless of the research focus,
improvement in problem-solving skills is the most-identified player benefit from digital game play (Hsiao, 2007). Accordingly, researchers now consider game play as an activity for improving learner capabilities to overcome challenges, which in turn supports their
efforts to address real-life social and personal problems (Kailani, Newton, & Pedersen, 2019).
The PISA 2012 assessment tool approaches problem-solving processes as consisting of four stages:
1. Exploring and understanding: building mental representations from information gathered via observation, interaction, finding obstacles, and identifying relevant concepts.
2. Representation and formulation: identifying relevant problem factors and cross-relationships for purposes of constructing a coherent
mental representation.
3. Planning and execution: clarifying goals, devising strategies, and executing plans to achieve a goal state.
4. Monitoring and reflection: checking progress at each stage, critically evaluating assumptions from different perspectives, and
identifying needs for additional information.
Note that these same stages are central to problem-solving success in digital games (Kiili, 2007; Sun, Wang, & Chan, 2011).
According to descriptions of problem-solving cognitive processes given in PISA 2012, these stages are not necessarily sequential,
nor are all four required in all situations (OECD, 2010). In our previous research we observed that problem-solving processes in
different game genres tend to correspond to specific PISA test attributes such as system analysis, decision-making, and debugging—in
other words, since individual game genres emphasize specific problem-solving stages, it is not unusual to find players with similar
levels of game play experience performing at different levels across the four stages. We believe it is important to identify the effects of
individual stages when evaluating the overall influence of game experience on problem-solving performance, but few efforts have been
made to examine problem-solving processes empirically. We therefore used the PISA 2012 framework to gather empirical data
regarding the relationship between problem-solving processes and learning performance.
The OECD (2010) also notes that problem contexts and characteristics have important consequences for identifying solutions
because of individual differences in the understanding of problem contexts and problem-solving skill requirements. PISA 2012
categorized problems as technical and non-technical based on device requirements, as well as the need to understand device functionality. Problems can also be divided into static and interactive categories, the first referring to problems in which all necessary
problem-solving information is disclosed at the outset, and the second referring to problems in which a certain degree of relevant
information is missing, thus affecting the ways that problem solvers perform operations, interact with scenarios, and acquire necessary
knowledge for problem-solving. In this study we emphasize the kinds of dynamic problem-solving assessment skills now considered
standard and required for daily life activities (Csapó & Molnár, 2017; Funke & Greiff, 2017).
Whereas PISA is a formal measure of digital literacy skills, learning during digital game play is considered informal—that is,

without fixed learning goals or methods, and with learning processes determined to a great degree by the self-regulated learning
capabilities of individuals. Similar to many daily life tasks involving digital technology, game-related information and operations are
presented non-linearly, thus requiring constant adjustments in response to evolving problem-solving processes. When learning new
technologies or systems, users must analyze their situations and establish goals before making decisions and executing actions. Selfregulated learning—considered an important factor in digital technology usage (Azevedo, Winters, & Moos, 2004)—helps learners
absorb content, think about learning process efficiency, and increase participation. Self-regulated learning also affects the flow experiences of users during game play (Chen & Sun, 2016). Digital games entail challenges, flexible solutions, clear rules, immediate
feedback, and a sense of satisfaction when problems are solved—conditions that use intrinsic motivation plus self-regulated learning to
generate senses of active participation and exploration in support of fun. Pintrich (2000) defines self-regulated learning as a
goal-oriented process involving a mix of intention, motivation, and strategy selection, with learners serving as active rather than
reactive participants in their respective learning processes. Almost all digital games present players with goals requiring active pursuit
and one or more self-regulated learning processes. Successful goal achievement requires competency in using digital tools embedded in
technical systems, which in turn requires a degree of digital literacy that undergoes further development during game performance.
To clarify the relationship between game experience and digital literacy, we used the PISA 2012 computer-based assessment
process to measure the impacts of self-regulated learning on problem-solving performance. Our specific motivations are to test the
premise that digital game play supports the cultivation of self-regulated learning skills, and to determine whether self-regulated
learning skills established while playing digital games can be transferred to other aspects of learning, thus enhancing potential success when dealing with certain kinds of off-screen life activities. According to PISA 2012, the key assessment elements of problem
context, problem characteristics, and problem-solving processes provide broad problem-solving domain coverage. We therefore used
the PISA 2012 framework when designing our approach to evaluating problem-solving performance at various stages of digital
gameplay and examining relationships between individual in-game processes and problem-solving performance. As part of our
assessment approach we attempted to identify technical/non-technical and dynamic/static problem characteristics.
This study was guided by two research questions and one hypothesis:
RQ1. What is the relationship between self-regulated learning and problem-solving performance among students who play digital
games?
RQ2. What relationships exist between problem-solving performance and four in-game problem-solving stages across different
contexts and characteristics?
H1. Students with more digital game play experience perform better at problem-solving tasks than students with less digital game
play experience.
2. Literature review
2.1. Digital game play, self-regulated learning, and problem-solving
Digital game play has been described as providing positive support for knowledge construction. Game elements identified as
possibly promoting active learning include goal-setting, challenges, feedback, adaptability, performance, competence, and problemsolving scenarios (Prensky, 2001)—the same elements that trigger a sense of pleasure during game play (Kerr, Kücklich, & Brereton,
2006). In addition to giving players tools, mechanisms, and environments for completing tasks creatively (Genvo, 2008; Järvinen,
2008), the interactive characteristics of digital games likely enhance learner motivation and participation (Anastasiadis, Lampropoulos, & Siakas, 2018; Wichadee & Pattanapichet, 2018). Positive gaming experiences and a sense of fun can motivate learners to
become more active explorers, especially in terms of accepting and experimenting with new technical tools (Kiili, 2005).
Self-regulated learning skills are important digital game play-associated products exerting significant impacts on knowledge
construction (Nietfeld, 2018), with games presenting dynamic and diverse tasks at different levels of difficulty that require a mix of
strategic thinking, action control, and motivation (both intrinsic and extrinsic) to achieve high-level problem-solving and learning
experiences (Murad, 2017; Olson, 2010; Rieber, Smith, & Noah, 1998). According to Sternberg’s (1996) definition of problem-solving
as the integrated performance of various abilities and high-level mental activities, all problem solvers utilize preexisting knowledge
and skills to match the requirements of new situations, to analyze initial states and existing narratives, and to consider processes for
goal achievement (Krulik & Rudnick, 1987; Mayer, 1992). Problem solutions require accurate player assessments of game content and
processes in order to identify and adjust relevant strategies believed to assist in and encourage the development of general
problem-solving skills associated with task performance (Lazakidou & Retalis, 2010; Perels, Gürtler, & Schmitz, 2005). Good
problem-solving performance has been linked with self-regulated learning characteristics such as motivation, strategy selection, and
action control. Motivated individuals consider and formulate strategies, control and make adjustments to their actions during plan
implementation, and narrow gaps between current conditions and stated goals (Cherng & Lin, 2001; Zimmerman, 1998).
According to Gabbiadini and Greitemeyer (2017), a correlation exists between strategy games and self-regulated learning in terms
of five planning skills: gathering information, evaluating options, adjusting to evolving situations, analyzing actions, and visualizing
future possibilities (Hamlen, 2018). Adachi and Willoughby (2013) emphasize other features of strategy games, including encouraging
players to take their time in order to explore various possibilities and considering alternative approaches to problem-solving. In
contrast, fast-paced digital games such as racing and fighting games are less likely to enhance problem-solving skills because they
encourage immediate reactions without stopping and considering long-term strategy development. Further, highly interactive digital
games can promote motivation and engagement leading to persistence in finding problem solutions, thereby impacting

problem-solving skills (Kailani et al., 2019).
We attempted to integrate these features into the present study, which assumes that digital games with specific features are capable
of increasing learning motivation, and that users are capable of developing self-regulated learning skills during game play. Prior
findings suggest that knowledge and skills acquired while playing digital games can be transferred in limited ways to real-life scenarios, with games such as The Sims and Age of Empires giving students opportunities to interact with complex systems while reducing
extraneous trivialities (Squire, 2003). However, learning effectiveness tends to be situation-specific (Masson, Bub, & Lalonde, 2011),
and skill-transfer levels are often inferior to motivation enhancement (Yang, 2012). Researchers acknowledge a need to carefully
choose their games of focus and to consider the feelings of study participants regarding initiative and sense of voluntariness. Since
research contexts differ greatly from regular game play situations, there is potential for artificial playing and learning environments to
interfere with the identification of causal relationships (Coulson & Ferguson, 2015). It is rare to find individuals playing games for the
specific purpose of practicing problem-solving skills—most want to be entertained, raising questions about how to best use digital
games to achieve learning objectives (Von Ahn & Dabbish, 2008). We therefore made a special effort to retain a sense of voluntary
participation and autonomy when searching for links between degree of digital game experience and problem-solving performance. In
this regard, autonomy refers to a sense of willingness or volition when performing a task (Ryan, Rigby, & Przybylski, 2006). As Deci,
Eghrari, Patrick, and Leone (1994) note, providing more choices and relaxing instructions and rules can improve player-learner
perceptions of autonomy—an important consideration frequently overlooked in study designs.
2.2. Digital games and the PISA framework
Computer-based assessment instruments and digital games consist of dynamic exploration scenarios while providing continual
feedback from user interventions (Klieme, 2004). We employed the computer-based PISA 2012 assessment instrument to determine
whether digital game similarities and characteristics are useful for developing problem-solving skills that can be transferred to
non-game playing tasks. Similar to digital game players, PISA 2012 test takers had to collect, analyze, and organize relevant information in order to verify causal relationships among problem components, and then formulate response strategies (Ebner & Holzinger,
2007; Gee, 2006).
Problem-solving generally follows a specific order, with each stage exerting separate effects on performance (Dewey, 1910; Hayes,
1989; Polya, 1945). The PISA 2012 framework consisted of four stages associated with the problem-solving process: exploring and
understanding, representation and formulation, planning and execution, and monitoring and reflection (OECD, 2010). In addition to
overall problem-solving results, PISA 2012 also measured test-taker performance at each stage. The assessment instrument was
designed so that its problem scenarios required a combination of strategic and planned exploration (Greiff, Wüstenberg, & Avvisatib,
2015).
PISA 2012 considered problem characteristics and contexts as important problem-solving elements, especially dynamic/static
characteristics and technical/non-technical contexts (OECD, 2010). According to Klieme (2004), problem-solving assessment
frameworks should incorporate dynamic tasks requiring active searching and the continuous processing of information and feedback in
authentic settings. Required competencies and skills change according to problem characteristics. Specifically, changes in
self-regulated learning skills and adaptability are required when relevant information is only indirectly available via feedback (Wirth
& Klieme, 2003). In terms of problem contexts, the creators of PISA 2012 identified authentic technology settings of interest to students. Context plays an important role in several domains, especially when predicting changes, explaining unanticipated events, and
focusing attention (Brézillon, 1999). The introduction of contextual elements supports computer-based assessments for purposes of
representing and simulating real-world scenarios involving devices such as mobile phones or ticket machines that require sequences of
interactions to use properly (Greiff, Holt, & Funke, 2013). In contrast, digital games present players with multiple dynamic and
technical problems in which player familiarity with context and practical play procedures might influence their ability to solve
problems.
The literature on connections between game experience and problem-solving performance contains mixed findings. Prior studies
have found that problem-solving performance is affected by factors cultivated during game play, including creativity, adaptability,
cognitive function, insights, and ability to reflect on process (Barr, 2017; Blanco-Herrera, Gentile, & Rokkum, 2019; Blumberg,
Rosenthal, & Randall, 2008; Choi et al., 2020; Gee, 2003; Hamlen, 2017). However, Dindar (2018) argues that the transfer of digital
game skills to real-life problem-solving is not as obvious as previous researchers have claimed. According to Kailani et al.’s (2019)
survey of game-based learning and problem-solving skill studies published between 2009 and 2016, most describe positive correlations
between problem-solving skills and game-based learning, but at different levels of intensity. We therefore examined the possibility that
digital game-playing experience contributes to skill development without directly influencing problem-solving success. To identify
links between overall performance and the four problem-solving stages described above, we used the PISA 2012 framework to monitor
problem-solving results while examining performance at each stage.
3. Method
3.1. Participants
A total of 290 sixth-grade students (ages 11 and 12) were recruited from nine classes at a school in Taoyuan County, Taiwan, where
one of the authors is a teacher. After removing unusable surveys due to incomplete data, absences, computer crashes, or project
withdrawal, the final sample consisted of 267 students.

3.2. Instruments
Five data collection and assessment tools were used, including a self-regulated learning scale, a game experience questionnaire,
four digital games, a problem-solving process evaluation instrument, and a computer-based survey.
3.2.1. Self-regulated learning scale
We used a modified version of Cherng and Lin’s (2001) Self-regulated Learning Scale for High School Students. The original version
contained motivation, strategy, and action control subscales consisting of 42, 54 and 42 questions, respectively. Responses were
recorded along a 6-point Likert scale: 1, totally disagree; 2, strongly disagree; 3, disagree; 4, agree; 5, strongly agree; 6, totally agree.
For the motivation section (based on an expectancy-value model) those factors were task values, self-efficacy, expectancy for success,
examination anxiety, internal goal orientation, positive affect, and negative affect. For the action control section (which combined
personality traits and behavioral aspects) they included hesitation, volatility, preoccupation, effort and persistence, affective control,
cognitive control, environmental control, time management, and “other” control. For the strategy section (based on metacognition and
information processing theory) they were rehearsal strategies, elaboration strategies, organizational strategies, planning, monitoring,
modification, and evaluation. Cronbach’s alpha values for the motivation, action control, and strategy subscales were 0.89, 0.90 and
0.96, respectively. Test-retest coefficients at five-week intervals were 0.77 for the motivation subscale, 0.77 for the action control
subscale, and 0.75 for the strategy subscale. Test results indicated acceptable coefficients for internal consistency, reliability, and
stability.
In our modified version, questions deemed inapplicable to our research (e.g., items designed to measure examination anxiety) were
deleted, and the wording of other items were modified to match the ages and reading levels of elementary school students. A total of
234 students took a pre-test consisting of 30 motivation, 42 strategy, and 30 action control items. Item and factor analyses for
measuring validity and reliability were performed. For our item analysis, the highest and lowest 27% of pre-test scores were selected
for detailed examination, including t-tests for identifying extreme outliers. Significant differences were found in 30 motivation (t > 3,
p < .01) and 42 strategy subscale items (t > 3, p < .01). For the action control subscale two statistically non-significant items were
deleted (t < 3, p > .01), leaving 28 items (t > 3, p < .01). Corrected item-total correlations ranged from 0.53 to 0.86 in the motivation
subscale, 0.42 to 0.76 in the strategy subscale, and 0.39 to 0.70 in the action control subscale. All corrected total item correlations were
identified as positive and higher than the suggested cut-off value of 0.30.
A Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test was used to determine suitability for factor analyses. KMO values were 0.97 for the motivation
subscale, 0.94 for the strategy subscale, and 0.92 for the action control subscale—all exceeding 0.9, indicating suitability for factor
analyses. A principal component analysis was performed using a Varimax rotation. Results from the first factor analysis for the
motivation subscale showed that one of the factors contained only 2 items; this factor was removed. Results from the second factor
analysis indicated a factor loading range from 0.42 to 0.82, with 74.2% of the variance explained by all four factors. For the strategy
subscale, results from the first factor analysis identified 2 items with no discriminant validity. After these items were deleted, a second
factor analysis revealed a factor loading range from 0.44 to 0.81, with 64.3% of the variance explained by the six factors. The factor
loading for the action control subscale ranged from 0.49 to 0.86, with 63% of the variance explained by the five factors. Overall factor
loading for each item (ranging between 0.42 and 0.86) exceed the suggested minimum of 0.3, indicating factor validity.
The final subscales consisted of 28 motivation, 40 strategy, and 28 action control questions. The highest possible score was 576: 168
for the motivation subscale, 240 for the strategy subscale, and 168 for the action control subscale. Higher scores indicate better selfregulated learning skills. Measurement factors included self-efficacy, positive affect, task values, and expectancy for success in the
motivation subscale; rehearsal strategies, organizational strategies, elaboration strategies, planning, monitoring, and evaluation in the
strategy subscale; and persistence, time management, cognitive control, affective control, and “other” control in the action control
subscale. Cronbach’s alpha values for the motivation, strategy, and action control subscales were 0.97, 0.97 and 0.92, respectively,
indicating high internal consistency.
3.2.2. Game experience questionnaire
A researcher-designed game experience questionnaire was used to collect data on amounts and types of game-playing experience
among the study participants. Survey items addressed game play locations, devices, motivations, frequency per weekday and frequency per weekend. Some items addressed student strategies for overcoming within-game barriers (practice, search walkthroughs,
asking friends for help), others elicited information on the attitudes of the participants’ parents toward digital games and the ways they
managed their children’s playing time.
3.2.3. Digital games
For three of the four games used in this research—Snail Bob, Factory Balls and Jelly Alien—players focused on arranging action
orders or using tools in response to various scenarios. To retain a sense of autonomy and voluntary participation, study participants
were also given the option of selecting one of their own favorite games and completing the self-regulated learning scale at home.
Another game, Cataroundasia, was used to measure problem-solving process performance. The game objective in Cataroundasia is to
feed a cat by removing certain objects and manipulating others, while collecting stars during the process. Game scenarios consist of
regularly changing environments and barriers such as springs, conveyors, and power grids, thus requiring active interaction and game
operations to clarify information and to devise and execute strategies—similar to the problem-solving process in the PISA framework.
The authors selected these particular games because they contain features considered influential in the establishment of self-regulated
learning and problem-solving skills (Adachi & Willoughby, 2013; Gabbiadini & Greitemeyer, 2017; Kailani et al., 2019). Primary

3.2.4. Problem-solving process assessment
For this task we used the PISA 2012 framework to create 21 items for evaluating participant performance while playing Cataroundasia. Assessments consisted of the four previously-described stages (exploring and understanding, representation and formulation, planning and execution, and monitoring and reflection), each worth 10 points (total possible score of 40). Higher scores indicated
better problem-solving process performance. The exploring and understanding stage consisted of 10 true-or-false items designed to
measure player observation skills and game understanding; the goal was to determine the abilities of players to build mental representations of problem information presentation. The representation and formulation stage consisted of 3 open-ended questions
addressing game problem spaces, including problem-solving process solutions and component paths; here the objective was to examine
participant ability to create coherent mental representations of problem situations. The planning and execution stage consisted of two
open-ended questions asking for descriptions of the means and steps taken to complete two missions based on their Cataroundasia game
play experience. The purpose here was to identify goal-setting, strategy-devising, and actions taken to solve problems. The monitoring
and reflection stage consisted of 2 open-ended and 4 short-answer questions (e.g., “Which object must not be clicked in order to pass
the mission?“) designed to identify conditions for task completion. The goal was to determine participant critical thinking skills for
solution identification from different perspectives. Example items are presented in Fig. 2.
3.2.5. Computer-based survey
We used a computer-based survey consisting of seven problem-solving scenarios: a ticket vending machine, an MP3 player, route
planning, environmental control, a birthday party, a robot vacuum cleaner, and online booking. Six of the seven were taken from PISA
2012 and one was designed by the authors. The highest possible score was 71, based on the same scoring procedure as used for PISA
2012; higher scores indicate better computer based problem-solving performance. Each scenario combined different combinations of
technical/non-technical contexts and dynamic/static characteristics. For instance, the route planning scenario was both non-technical
and static—it could be solved without interacting with a technical system because its problem state did not change. Computer-based
assessment examples are presented as Fig. 3. Since our participants were between 11 and 12 years of age and PISA 2012 was aimed at
15-year-olds, we made several minor age-appropriate adjustments.1
3.3. Procedure
Questionnaires and surveys were distributed and completed over a five-week period. During week 1 students were asked to
complete the game experience questionnaire, listen to descriptions of the Snail Bob, Factory Balls and Jelly Alien games, and choose one
of them or their personal favorite for responding to the motivation subscale. During week 2 they were asked to use their experiences
playing one of these games or another game of their choice when responding to the action control subscale items; they were asked to do
the same for the strategy subscale in week 3. During week 4 they were asked to play Cataroundasia for 20 min before responding to
items designed to assess their problem-solving processes. During week 5 they were given 70 min to complete the seven-section
computer-based assessment instrument.
4. Results
All results reported in this section are based on data derived from SPSS 18.0 statistical analyses, including correlation coefficients,
regressions, and variances.

claimed to have zero digital game playing experience. Experience levels were 21 (7.9%) with less than 2 years, 67 (25.1%) between 3
and 4 years, 43 (16.1%) between 5 and 6 years, and 103 (38.6%) with 7 years or more (meaning as early as first grade). For the
computer-based assessment results, higher scores (71 maximum) indicate better problem-solving performance. As shown in Table 1,
the average computer-based problem-solving performance score for experienced players was 62.6 (N = 234, SD = 16.18), compared to
52.8 for students with no digital gaming experience (N = 33, SD = 17.94). Regarding the computer based problem-solving performance scores of experienced and completely inexperienced players, Levene’s test results indicate that an assumption of variance
homogeneity was met (F = 0.374, p > .05). Combined, these results support the hypothesis that students with more digital gameplaying experience perform better at problem-solving tasks than students with less or no experience.

4.2. Self-regulated learning and problem-solving performance
The highest possible self-regulated learning scale score was 576 (168 each for the motivation and action control subscales and 240

5. Discussion
5.1. Game play experience and problem-solving performance
Our results offer additional evidence that experienced elementary school-aged digital game players tend to perform better on
problem-solving tasks compared to new or inexperienced players, but the largest positive effect we observed was based on a minimum
of seven years of game play experience. Extensive experience gives players greater familiarity with multiple game genres, which allows
them to quickly move among game scenarios and to refine a higher number of playing skills. As Bransford, Brown, and Cocking (1999)
note, the ability to work within diverse contexts is an important factor in abstract knowledge construction and successful skill transfers.
While individual games may contain diverse arrays of elements and contextual backgrounds, each one focuses on a single context,
therefore learners are likely to benefit from playing games across multiple genres to increase the potential for developing
problem-solving skills that may be transferable to non-game scenarios.
5.2. Self-regulated learning and problem-solving performance
Study participants with digital game-playing experience ranging from “some” to “extended” had higher self-regulated learning
scores, but with weak negative correlations with computer-based problem-solving performance, likely due to the high level of
skewness observed in the self-regulated learning measure. The observed motivation, action control, and strategy subscale distributions
were all negatively skewed, indicating positive feelings toward learning among the study participants in terms of attitude, self-efficacy,
self-expectation, and sense of value. There are at least two possible explanations. First, our research design was purposefully aimed at
giving participants feelings of autonomy and voluntariness when working with learning missions—the fact that they were allowed to
complete the self-report questionnaires based on playing their chosen or favorite games likely produced gaps between personal
learning experiences and learning effect. Second, despite evidence showing that digital games support learning (Thomas & Brown,
2009), and despite the large number of entertainment-oriented commercial games based on real-world scientific principles and with
opportunities to apply them to problem-solving tasks (Mohanty & Cantu, 2011), there are limitations regarding the numbers, genres,
and details of concepts that can be effectively taught through digital game play, with each game, topic, and learning environment
requiring careful inspection to confirm accuracy and completeness (Van Eck, 2006). In circumstances where there are no restrictions
on game selection, the characteristics, contexts, levels of difficulty and knowledge coverages of individual games are likely to differ
greatly. As Dindar (2018) notes, genre and content must be carefully controlled to ensure that games given to students actually address
chosen learning objectives. We allowed study participants to choose their favorite games to retain as much autonomy and motivation
as possible, but also offered three vetted games to ensure a degree of control over game content and type. Future researchers may be
interested in tracking the games that students choose or restricting certain genres (e.g., games of chance or gambling) to control the
potential for digressive gameplay.
5.3. Problem-solving performance and problem-solving process
Our data suggest that the four problem-solving stages were all significant predictors of problem-solving performance, with high-tolow predictive power identified as representation and formulation followed by monitoring and reflection, planning and execution, and
exploring and understanding. The representation and formulation stage entails means, procedures, and steps associated with problem
attributes and characteristics that allow for problem coding and action—in other words, the knowledge structure organization process
(Newell & Simon, 1972). Our results also indicate that the monitoring and reflection stage had higher predictive power for
problem-solving performance than the exploration and understanding or planning and execution stages, a finding that agrees with
Zimmerman’s (1998) identification of self-reflection as the most important stage in self-regulated learning. We gathered game process
data for each stage and found that 29.6% of problem-solving performance was explained by the game process variable. Similar to
Greiff, Kretzschmar, Müller, Spinath, and Martin (2014), we observed a significant relationship between ICT (information and
communication technology) literacy and complex problem solving. Digital games do not entail all aspects of ICT literacy, but successful
digital gameplay may indicate some level of proficiency in specific ICT skills.
We considered four computer-based assessment characteristics (dynamic, static, technical, non-technical) when examining
problem-solving process performance involving different problem attributes. Once again our results identified the representation and
formulation stage as having the greatest predictive value for problem-solving performance. The exploring and understanding stage had
a strong predictive value for the static and non-technical problem-solving characteristics only, perhaps because those characteristics
emphasize observation skills with few (if any) requirements for interacting with technical systems for purposes of information clarification. The planning and execution and monitoring and reflection stages had predictive value for dynamic and technical characteristics only, likely because complete comprehension of dynamic/technical problems requires progressive interaction with
technological systems for purposes of monitoring real-time feedback, evaluating decisions, and proposing new strategies, among other
tasks. According to our findings, the representation and formulation stage was the strongest predictor of problem-solving success.
However, in light of the increasing numbers of dynamic and technical problems that individuals must deal with on a daily basis, the
planning/execution and monitoring/reflection stages are increasingly indispensable.
Here we will again emphasize that our process performance data were acquired during gameplay, increasing the potential that it
reflects at least some aspects of ICT literacy. According to Greiff et al. (2014), the effect of ICT literacy declines as the complexity of
human-computer interaction decreases. Note our findings that 20.2% of the variance in static problem-solving performance and 25.8%

of the variance in dynamic problem-solving performance were explained by problem-solving processes, leading us to speculate on the
effects of ICT literacy. Regarding problem contexts, Blanco-Herrera, Gentile, and Rokkum (2019) argues that they serve as constraints
on problem-solving even without explicit intervention, and that human-machine problem performance is heavily dependent on user
acceptance of computer systems. The large majority of study participants were experienced digital game players, indicating familiarity
with technical contexts that may have affected problem-solving performance.
Although the literature contains numerous studies on the relationship between gaming experience and problem-solving performance, very few emphasize links between game play experience and individual problem-solving stages, or the effects of problemsolving processes on problem-solving performance. As Greiff, Wüstenberg, and Avvisati (2015) have observed, most studies
measuring cognitive ability focus on results only, without considering data associated with cognitive processing. Different stages of
identical problem-solving processes are theoretically relevant, but collinearity issues arise when examining correlations between
performance and individual stages. However, the capacities and psychological processes required for individual problem-solving
stages differ. For example, the representation and formulation stage entails information reduction, information generation, and
model building, while the planning and execution stage involves predictions and decision-making (Funke & Greiff, 2017). When
working with problems, individuals use different approaches that transcend the boundaries of linear, trial-and-error, or step-by-step
models. Due to this problem-solving flexibility, successful problem-solving processes are neither certain nor sequential. This is an
important distinction because of differences in process performance during various problem-solving stages—when experienced
game-playing students exhibit the same results, it does not necessarily mean that their problem-solving process skills are identical.
Further research is required to precisely identify learning characteristics so that game parameters can be adjusted to match individual
user strengths and weaknesses.
6. Conclusion and study limitations
To clarify the relationship between digital game experience and problem-solving performance, we examined aspects of selfregulated learning in terms of motivation, action control, and strategies by observing game play in a group of sixth grade elementary school students, with data collection based on the four-stage framework of PISA 2012: exploration and understanding, representation and formulation, planning and execution, and monitoring and reflection. Our results indicate that study participants with
more digital game-playing experience performed better at computer-based problem-solving tasks—in other words, enhanced digital
literacy associated with digital game play exerted a positive effect on problem-solving. In contrast, our data on the relationship between problem-solving stage and problem attribute performance indicate that the cognitive processes involved in solving dynamic/
technical problems differ from those associated with static/non-technical problems. Since digital gameplay involves solving dynamic
problems in technical contexts, we believe the interactive and immediate feedback characteristics of digital games helps players
develop and improve technology application skills, perhaps by encouraging them to use trial-and-error and other exploration processes
to form problem-solving strategies.
There are several possible directions for future researchers to choose from. One is to study the details of how various problemsolving stages are affected by level of gameplay experience, especially in terms of factors such as game genre. For example, researchers may be interested in determining whether problem-solving skills cultivated while playing FPS games differ from those
developed while playing simulation games. Another direction is to expand on our effort to ensure that the students in our study felt a
sense of autonomy and voluntariness, and take a closer look at this and other factors that affect self-regulation and positive learning
perspectives. Third, the term “problem-solving” is very broad; researchers may be interested in identifying specific attributes in terms
of problem structure, definition, and personal or societal meaning, with individual attributes possibly exerting different effects on
problem-solving performance. Fourth, additional factors such as prior academic achievement and existing problem-solving capabilities
should be considered when examining the relationship between gaming experience and problem-solving performance.
A significant study limitation is associated with our effort to instill a sense of autonomy and voluntariness in the study participants:
a lack of uniformity in terms of game genre, tasks, and difficulty level, likely resulting in uneven player success. Additionally, the
problem-solving process assessment measure used in this study was based on specific games and items created by the authors, raising
questions about validity and the limited generalizability of our findings. Further, the generally positive reactions we identified in the
self-regulated learning data may be a result of the nature of game play or the ease of playing basic and not very demanding games. We
also acknowledge that the self-reporting aspect of the self-regulated learning scale poses questions regarding descriptive accuracy.
Future researchers will likely be interested in using detailed qualitative methods to supplement quantitative data.
