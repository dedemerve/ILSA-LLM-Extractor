Encouraging the integration of ICT in educational settings remains a significant challenge globally. However,
successful enactment is still limited, and more understanding is essential. The International Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS) 2018 was conducted to address this challenge systematically. This study focused
on exploring the factors influencing teachers’ utilization of technology, employing the Republic of Korea data
from ICILS 2018as a pilot study. Grounded on the Cognitive Behavioral Theory (CBT) and Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), a three-layer model – comprising external factors, internal factors, and action layers – was
formulated to predict teachers’ technology usage and verified by using constructs from ICILS 2018. The external
factors layer encompassed professional development, school ICT resources, and teachers’ collaboration, while the
internal factors layer included teachers’ experience, ICT self-efficacy, emphasis on ICT and coding tasks in
classrooms, and perceptions regarding ICT usage. Within the action layer were constructs measuring the use of
ICT for teaching practices, digital learning tools, and general utility software. Partial Least Squares Structural
Equation Modeling was used to evaluate the model. The findings strengthen both foundational theories. Specifically, the findings highlighted significant total effects from constructs in the external and internal factors
layers on constructs in the action layer. In other words, the three-layer model can successfully explain the
influential factors impacting teachers’ usage of ICT, including specific causes and effects information. Beyond
offering empirical evidence for theoretical frameworks, this study identified specific factors influencing technology usage among teachers and outlined potential pathways for changes in teachers’ practices affecting
technology utilization. Further research is essential to delve into causal relationships and validate the model via
empirical evidence.

• Some extended models added more factors to TAM to better

• The International Computer and Information Literacy Study

collected ICT-related information from stakeholders including
teachers, principals, and ICT coordinators.

• This study introduced more factors to TAM based on the data

one aspect of the action layer.

1. Introduction
Technology plays a pivotal role in every academic discipline, with
education being no exception. Additionally, information and communication technology (ICT) skills are considered indispensable for the
younger generations (Voogt, Knezek, Cox, Knezek, & Brummelhuis,
2011; Voogt & Roblin, 2012; Weber & Greiff, 2023). Given that a significant portion of students can readily access computers and the
internet either at home or in school (OECD, 2020a; 2020b), ICT tools
and competence to use emerge as promising tools for enriching students’
learning experiences. As per a comprehensive review, technology in
education has yielded positive results across various fields and subjects
(Bacca-Acosta, Tejada, Fabregat, Kinshuk, & Guevara, 2022; Belland,
Walker, Kim, & Lefler, 2017; Doo, Bonk, & Heo, 2020; Peng, Yuan, Sun,
Jiang, & Wang, 2022).
Among these advantages of technology integration, however, successful implementation hinges on several critical factors. Regarding
teachers, numerous variables influence the integration of technology
into classrooms, including teachers’ age, teaching subjects, years of
experience, and class size (Ifinedo, Rikala, & Hämäläinen, 2020). One of
the main problems of effective technology integration is the lack of
teachers’ competencies (Atabek, 2020; Ramorola, 2013; Vatanartiran &
Karadeniz, 2015) and their perceptions and confidence (Demissie,
Labiso, & Thuo, 2022; Ozden, Yang, Wen, & Shinas, 2024). Understanding factors influencing teachers’ ICT usage is crucial, especially for
preparing future teachers.
Many studies tried to explain factors that influence teachers’ technology usage, especially using the technology acceptance model (Yuen
& Ma, 2008; Zarafshani, Solaymani, D’Itri, Helms, & Sanjabi, 2020).
However, most studies strictly follow the theoretical and mainly
contribute to the theoretical aspect (Chun & Yunus, 2023; Du & Liang,
2024). Although some of them proposed some new factors, those factors
were treated independently (Teo, Sang, Mei, & Hoi, 2018). Moreover,
literature usually collected data from a small sample size which is not
suitable for generalization (Huang, Teo, & Zhao, 2023; Sun & Luo,
2024). The large-scale survey could provide insightful data for deeper
understanding and allow for the proposal of more complex models.
The Republic of Korea (Korea), renowned for its rapid technological
adoption and innovative educational strategies, stands as an exemplary
focal point for this investigation. Korea has emerged as a technological
powerhouse, boasting extensive digital infrastructure and a culture
deeply intertwined with technological progress (Campbell, 2012; Dayton, 2020). Moreover, Korea’s distinct educational landscape, characterized by government initiatives emphasizing technology in education,
presents a compelling case for an in-depth analysis of teachers’ technological practices (Grzybowski, 2013). The successful results of Korea
are undeniable with evidence from international surveys such as the
Program for International Student Assessment (PISA) or Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMMS). Deeper investigations in the Korean context could shed light on how to enhance
teachers’ use of ICT.
In conclusion, understanding factors influencing teachers’ technology usage is crucial for supporting a technologically integrated learning
environment. However, the available literature mainly considered factors independently without considering teachers’ changing processes.
The objective of this study is to investigate the teachers’ factor structure

2. Theoretical framework
2.1. Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and Unified Theory of
Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT)
The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) is a validated survey to
assess the level of users’ acceptance of a new technology developed by
Davis (1989). In this survey, Perceived Usefulness (PU) and Perceived
Ease of Use are two factors affecting current and future technology usage
(Davis, 1989). Although this survey was originally designed for business
use, many studies have extended this work in terms of fields and factors
to assess (Abdullah & Ward, 2016; Davis, Bagozzi, & Warshaw, 1989;
Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003). For example, a review by
Abdullah and Ward (2016) studied the extension of TAM in different
settings and found that Self-Efficacy, Perceived Enjoyment, Prior Experience, Computer Anxiety, and Subjective Norm are commonly used as
external factors with the ability to predict PU and PEU. Many variations
of this model with more factors were also studied (Feng et al., 2021; Teo,
2009; Zarafshani et al., 2020) including the extended theory such as the
Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT)
(Venkatesh et al., 2003). In UTAUT, behavioral intention to use and
usage behavior is proposed to depend on more factors such as social
influence and facilitating conditions (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Although
many studies investigated technology usage via either TAM or UTAUT or
any extended model, they mostly proposed factors as separate and independent without considering actual behavioral changes. For instance,
although the work by Zarafshani et al. (2020), which was based on TAM,
defined extrinsic and intrinsic constructs, all factors were on the same
layer in their final model. To change human behavior, however, factors
are intertwined and influence each other in different levels and orders.
2.2. Cognitive Behavioral Theory (CBT)
Cognitive Behavioral Theory (CBT), also known as Cognitive
Behavioral Therapy, posits a dynamic interconnection among thoughts,
emotions, and behaviors (Beck, 2020; Center for Substance Abuse;
Padesky & Mooney, 1990; Center for Substance Abuse Treatment,
1999). Originally rooted in psychological therapy, CBT demonstrates
versatility by extending its application into diverse fields, such as education (Mennuti, Freeman, & Christner, 2006; Toker & Avcı, 2015).
Within the framework of this theory, both the external environment and
internal thought processes of educators significantly influence their
behaviors and actions (Early & Grady, 2017). The theory acknowledges
the uniqueness of each individual when dealing with external factors or
the environment. Social Cognitive Theory, for instance, underscores that
individuals may respond differently to the same external environment
based on their distinct internal characteristics (Stajkovic & Luthans,
1998). Consequently, beyond solely examining teachers’ observable
behaviors, there is merit in exploring the potential for internal transformations among teachers. Combining this flow of change with
extensively verified TAM and UTAUT could provide insightful understanding by recognizing that both external influences and internal shifts
contribute to teachers’ practices.

2.3. The International Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS)

models. Some ICILS constructs are aligned with factors in these theories
which make it feasible to propose hypotheses. Thus, combining these
frameworks is valuable for theory extension and some correlations between constructs are predictable.

The International Computer and Information Literacy Study,
commonly referred to as ICILS, represents a pivotal global initiative
aimed at understanding and evaluating the digital competencies of
students across the world (Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Friedman, & Duckworth, 2020b). In an age where information and technology are ubiquitous, ICILS serves as a comprehensive yardstick for assessing the
critical digital and information literacy skills of Grade 8 students with 12
countries participating in 2018 (Fraillon et al., 2020b). This international assessment, conducted by the International Association for the
Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA), provides valuable insights into the readiness of students to navigate the digital landscape,
critically analyze information, and harness technology for learning and
problem-solving. Besides students, ICILS also collects data from teachers, principals, and ICT coordinators to evaluate stakeholders involved
in ICT integration (Fraillon et al., 2020b).
The conceptual framework of ICILS 2018is based on literature stating
that students’ learning depends on a multilevel structure (Gerick,
Eickelmann, & Bos, 2017; Schulz, Ainley, Fraillon, Losito, & Agrusti,
2016). Thus, ICILS proposed a four-level framework: the wider community, schools and classrooms, home environment, and the individual
(Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Duckworth, & Friedman, 2019). Teachers, as
individuals akin to their students, can also have the potential to undergo
development in ICT usage. within this multilevel framework. Environmental or external factors experienced by teachers influence their
mindset – internal factors – thereby affecting their actions in using ICT.
By shedding light on the state of information and computer literacy,
ICILS contributes essential data for a deeper understanding of technology integration in education. The full list of ICILS questions and variable
names can be found in the user guide (Mikheeva & Meyer, 2020)
whereas scale construction can be found in the technical report
(Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Friedman, & Duckworth, 2020a).
Since the items and constructs from ICILS 2018assessed teachers’
technology-related backgrounds, they are ideal data sources for verifying models related to technology usage, especially extended TAM

2.4. The present study
Based on CBT, this study aims to investigate the impact of the
environment on teachers’ actions in ICT usage, with a focus on how their
unique internal characteristics play a pivotal role in shaping these interactions. Moreover, TAM and UTAUT provide insight into some correlations between constructs in the model. The items and scales from
ICILS 2018were employed to verify the model, systematically organized
into cohesive layers, as illustrated in Fig. 1.
2.5. Correlations among variables
With TAM and UTAUT as a core framework, some proposed constructs could be categorized to match TAM’s and UTAUT’s variables.
The same variable names as in ICILS 2018were used in this study
(Mikheeva & Meyer, 2020). The dependent variable in this study is action layer which is comparable to actual use (AU) in the TAM. ICTPRAC,
USETOOL, and USEUTIL belong to this layer. On the external factors
layer, COLICT and RESRC belong to facilitating conditions (FC). The
rationale for this grouping is that COLICT and RESRC are facilitating
factors from colleagues and school, respectively. Regarding the internal
factors, ICTEMP, CODEMP, and VWPOS are clustered together as PU
since emphasizing and holding a positive perspective reflects teachers’
perceived usefulness in those respects. Some constructs can be mapped
to UTAUT directly including ICTEFF to self-efficacy (SE).

3. Materials and methods

2.5.2. Internal factors layer

3.1. Participants and data collection

2.5.2.1. ICTEMP, CODEMP, and VWPOS as perceived usefulness (PU).
PU has been confirmed by extensive studies to be a very strong predictor
of attitude, behavioral intention to use, and actual usage of technology
(Chocarro, Cortiñas, & Marcos-Matás, 2023; Fearnley & Amora, 2020;
Papakostas, Troussas, Krouska, & Sgouropoulou, 2023; Sukendro et al.,
2020; Vanduhe, Nat, & Hasan, 2020). Thus, ICTEMP, CODEMP, and
VWPOS, which are comparable to PU in different aspects, should also
affect AU.

The teachers’ data from ICILS 2018is used in this study. The data
were collected by using an online 30-min teacher questionnaire (Fraillon
et al., 2020a). The sampling method, data collection, and data coding
followed the procedure and standard by IEA (Fraillon et al., 2020a). To
eliminate unnecessary factors, Korea is used as a case study. Since the
number of missing data is very small for each variable (maximum is
3.6%), mean imputation was used to replace the missing data. The total
number of cases is 2127 (N = 2127). The average age of this group is
44.0 (SD = 10.1) and 66.6% of them are female. The percentage of
participants with the main subject domain in language and arts, mathematics and sciences, human sciences, and others are 28.4%, 23.9%,
15.8%, and 31.9%, respectively.

Beginning with questions from the ICILS 2018database, four questions (Q4, Q8, Q10, and Q16) were excluded from this study. Q4 (“In the
current school year, at how many schools do you teach [target grade]
students?”) was excluded because 94.1% of participants teach only at
one school. Q8 (“Which of the following best describes the subject for
this reference class”) was excluded because the teaching subject is not
considered in this study. Q10 (“How often do students in your reference
class use ICT for the following activities?”) was excluded because it is
about students’ activities, not teachers. Finally, Q16 (“Did your [initial

because the duration is varied and the effect from initial teacher education is uncontrollable. On the other hand, items from Q6, which were

All scales in ICILS, both original and modified scales, were separated
into three layers based on the teachers’ perspective including the
external factor layer, internal factor layer, and action layer. The external
factor layer is composed of three constructs: PD, T_RESRC, and T_COLICT. The constructs in this layer relate to external support provided for
teachers including professional development (PD) (How often have you
participated in any of the following professional learning activities in the
past two years? A course or webinar on integrating ICT into teaching and
learning), school ICT resources (RESRC) (To what extent do you agree or
disagree with the following statements about using ICT in teaching at
your school? My school has sufficient ICT equipment (e.g., computers)),
and teacher ICT collaboration (COLICT) (To what extent do you agree or
disagree with the following statements about your use of ICT in teaching
and learning at your school? I collaborate with colleagues to develop
ICT-based lessons). The next layer, the internal factor layer, is about
teachers’ attitudes and perspectives toward ICT. It is composed of 6
constructs including teachers’ prior experience (EXP) (How often do you
use ICT in these settings? At school when teaching), teachers’ ICT selfefficacy (ICTEFF) (How well can you do these tasks using ICT? Using a
spreadsheet program (e.g., [Microsoft Excel®]) for keeping records or
analyzing data), teacher emphasis on ICT capabilities in class (ICTEMP)
(In your teaching the reference class in this school year, how much
emphasis have you given to developing the following ICT-based capabilities in your students? To access information efficiently), teacher
emphasis on coding tasks in class (CODEMP) (In your teaching of the
reference class this school year, how much emphasis have you given to
teaching the following skills? To display information in different ways),
positive views and negative views on using ICT in teaching and learning
(VWPOS and VWNEG) (To what extent do you agree or disagree with the
following practices and principles in relation to the use of ICT in
teaching and learning? Helps students develop greater interest in
learning and Distracts students from learning). The final layer, the action layer, is associated with teachers’ technology usage in three aspects
including the use of ICT for teaching practices in class (ICTPRAC) (How
often do you use ICT in the following practices when teaching your
reference class? The assessment of students’ learning through tests), the
use of digital learning tools (USETOOL) (How often did you use the
following tools in your teaching of the reference class this school year?
Digital learning games), and the use of general utility software (USEUTIL) (How often did you use the following tools in your teaching of the
reference class this school year? Word-processor software (e.g.,
[Microsoft Word®])). The complete model used in this study is shown in
Fig. 1.

3.3. Data analysis
Since the secondary data was used in this study, PLS-SEM is an
appropriate analysis technique (Hair, Risher, Sarstedt, & Ringle, 2019)
and SmartPLS4 was used throughout this study (Ringle, Wende, &
Becker, 2022). 10,000 bootstrap was used to generate a significant level.
According to Hair et al. (2019), the measurement model of reflective
constructs was evaluated by indicator loadings, internal consistency
reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. After that,
collinearity, R2 value, and Q2 predict value were used to evaluate the
structural model (Hair et al., 2019). From the preliminary analysis, some
items must be removed to satisfy the measurement model criteria. The
removed items are 7A, 7B, and 7D from ICTEFF, 12I from USETOOL, and
18D from VWNEG.

The analysis results for measurement model evaluation are shown in

Cronbach’s alpha (α), composite reliability (ρc), and reliability coefficient (ρA). The range of those parameters is between 0.778 and 0.941

discrimination validity as shown in Tables 7 and 8. The former requires

root of AVE of each construct whereas the values of the latter one should
be less than 0.85 (Hair, Hult, Ringle, Sarstedt, et al., 2021).

4.3. Assessment of the structural model

For the total effects, 5 paths from 27 paths are not significant as

The collinearity level of the structural model was evaluated by the
variance inflation factor (VIF). The acceptable range of this value is less
than 5 (Hair et al., 2019) and the maximum value in this study is 4.522.
The values for R2, R2 adjusted, their t and p values, and Q2predict are
shown in Table 9. The values of R2 of 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 represent
weak, moderate, and substantial explanatory power, respectively, while
the value larger than 0.90 implies overfit (Hair et al., 2019). Similarly,
the value of Q2predict, according to Hair et al. (2019), larger than zero is
meaningful with 0, 0.25, and 0.50 referring to small, medium, and large
predictive accuracy of the model, respectively. The values of R2 and R2
adjusted are significant at a 95% confidence level for all constructs
except VWNEG. The result of Q2predict is similar where VWNEG’s value is
very close to zero.

5.3. Effects of indirect paths
Since the 95% confidence intervals of PD - > VWNEG - > USEUTIL
and RESRC - > ICTEMP - > USEUTIL do not include zero, as mentioned
earlier, these two paths are treated as significant.
5.3.1. VWNEG as a mediator construct
Most of the paths passing through VWNEG are not significant. As
previously discussed, there is lack of understanding on the effects of
negative perceptions on technology usage. A plausible explanation is
that while other constructs tend to provide positive effects on technology usage, VWNEG tends to have the opposite effects. Consequently, the
total effects are not significant.

5.2.2. Direct effect from ICTEFF to USETOOL
Similar to EXP, the path from ICTEFF to USETOOL is the only nonsignificant path from ICTEFF to AU while the others have positive effects and agree with the literature (Al-Adwan et al., 2024; Li, Worch,
Zhou, & Aguiton, 2015; Teo, 2009). It should be noted that this finding
aligns with some literature (Paetsch et al., 2023). A similar explanation
as EXP is plausible. Most items in the ICTEFF construct also focus on
basic ICT skills which can imply that teachers with high self-efficacy in
general ICT skills might not use tools mentioned in the USETOOL
construct.

5.3.2. RESRC as a starting construct
Half of the paths, nine out of eighteen, originating from RESRC are
not significant. Like VWNEG, all paths through ICTEFF are not significant. Although this finding might contradict with some literature (Fu,
2013), it is reasonable in the context of this study because schools’ ICT
resources, without other interventions, should not impact teachers’ attributes, either experience or self-efficacy. Teachers must use those resources by any means such as professional development or collaboration
to change their attributes and competence.

5.2.3. Direct effects from VWNEG to AU
Many studies extensively confirm that positive attitude positively
influences technology usage (Koutromanos, Mikropoulos, Mavridis, &
Christogiannis, 2023; Wang & Zhao, 2023). On the contrary, negative
attitude is expected to have the opposite result. Findings from VWNEG
are unexpected because it does not affect ICTPRAC, have a positive effect on USETOOL, and have a negative effect on USEUTIL. Literature in a
similar context showed that disadvantages are supposed to negatively
affect the intention to use technology (Cenfetelli & Schwarz, 2011).
Moreover, it should be noted that the comparison of the results for this
construct is limited due to the lack of focus on negative attitude. A
possible explanation for ICTPRAC is that although teachers are concerned about the drawbacks of using ICT, ICT has been emphasized by
many stakeholders and society. Most teachers are required to implement
ICT in their classrooms. More systematic investigations into inhibitors of
technology usage are essential.

5.3.3. USETOOL as a final construct
The final four non-significant paths share the same final construct,
USETOOL, with a similar mediator which is either EXP or ICTEFF. These
mediators are long-term and require persistence (Marra, Rodgers, Shen,
& Bogue, 2009). Among the three AU constructs, moreover, USETOOL is
the most technical one. Combining these two effects results in
non-significant indirect paths.

5.5. Theoretical contributions and practical implications
This study’s findings lend empirical support to two theoretical
frameworks: BCT and TAM. Within the context of CBT, the indirect
pathways underscore the intricate interplay between external factors,
internal factors, and teacher behaviors. Similarly, our model delves into
the sub-constructs of TAM and reveals that their relationships are not as
direct as previously thought. For example, while COLICT and RESRC
typically converge to form FC—a key predictor of AU—our research
indicates that only COLICT significantly influences AU.
Despite the widespread application of TAM and UTAUT in technology acceptance studies, most have treated their constructs independently. Our study’s insights highlight the need for recognizing the
interconnectedness of these constructs and understanding the hierarchy
of influence each factor holds. Furthermore, it underscores the criticality
of providing multi-tiered support to teachers to optimize their technology utilization.

6.1. Limitations and future research
This study used the data from ICILS 2018which is limited to twelve
countries. The data from Korea was used as a pilot study and studies with
different contexts could give more insight into this domain. The existing
theories, such as TAM and UTAUT, can explain parts of this model but
investigation of new constructs proposed in this model is necessary.
More studies might be needed to support the findings of this study,
especially the theoretical perspectives behind these constructs and their
relationship. Moreover, it should be noted that the causal relationships
are not considered thoroughly in this study. More studies on the causal
relationships of these paths should be investigated.
