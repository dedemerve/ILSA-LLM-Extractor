A B S T R A C T
Collaboration has long been recognized as an efficacious pedagogical strategy. With the visual 
pedagogical scaffold, for instance, mind maps, learners can externalize their comprehension and 
engage in meaning negotiation. However, given the limitations posed by the conventional 
collaborative environment such as the difficulty for groups to achieve deep discussions and 
generate group intelligence, the generative artificial intelligence (GAI) tool was introduced. The 
efficacy of the GAI tool in boosting task performance is still contested and its influence on 
collaborative knowledge construction remains unclear. Therefore, this study was conducted in an 
elective course at a university. A total of 30 student teachers were the participants, including 10 
groups of 3 students. In the subsequent quasi-experimental study, 5 experimental groups 
employed the GAI tool and mind mapping based collaborative environment (GMMCE) and 5 
control groups employed the conventional mind mapping based collaborative environment 
(MMCE). The results showed that the experimental groups outperformed the control groups on 
both collaborative tasks in the course. According to Ordered Network Analysis (ONA), the 
experimental groups developed a collaborative knowledge construction process in cognitive 
dimension, i.e., progressive interaction from individual to peer to group. On the contrary, the 
control groups were mainly reflected in the transition between learners’ individual expression 
and peer interaction since they were more centered on the regulative dimension. We also sup­
plemented students’ perception of using the GAI tool from the interview data. Finally, we 
highlighted the implications for pedagogy and described the research limitations as well as future 
directions.

rapid development of technology in the age of artificial intelligence has led to an accelerated rate of knowledge updating and the need

for integration of interdisciplinary knowledge (e.g., Cress & Kimmerle, 2023). CKC is embodied in the process of learners interacting 
with classmates, teachers, and industry experts, which essentially facilitates the joint exploration of problems (e.g., Lin et al., 2013). 
Therefore, it requires group members to share information and resources, continuously improve ideas through peer interaction, and 
develop collective knowledge at the group level (e.g., Ouyang et al., 2023). However, recent research and educational practices have 
confirmed the difficulties and challenges associated with this process. It was found that collaborating as a team may reduce the 
quantity and quality of learning outcomes compared to working individually (e.g., Fischer et al., 2002; Paulus et al., 2010). For 
example, students might hesitate to share their ideas due to concerns about peer judgment. When group members are required to take 
turns presenting their thoughts, they might forget their ideas or choose not to voice them (e.g., Sun et al., 2022). Furthermore, this can 
impose a significant cognitive load, as individuals must simultaneously generate their own ideas and concentrate on the perspectives of 
others (e.g., Chen et al., 2019; Paulus et al., 2010). In these cases, the interaction and communication among learners remain confined 
to elementary stages, demonstrating the difficulty of reaching high levels of CKC.
To facilitate the expression of learners’ individual perspectives and to minimize the cognitive load generated by the complex 
interweaving of perspectives within a group, it is necessary to incorporate pedagogical scaffolding as part of the instructional strategy 
(e.g., Chen & Hong, 2016; Fischer et al., 2002). Stone (1993) noted that the support given by the teacher during scaffolding depended 
greatly on the characteristics of the context. Learners can utilize scaffolding to reduce their cognitive load in complex tasks, thereby 
accomplishing tasks which they would not otherwise be able to do. Davis and Miyake (2004) described classroom scaffolding as a kind 
of help. For example, visualization tools allow learners to construct representations in the form of mind maps, diagrams, and texts (e.g., 
Stone, 1993; Van De Pol et al., 2010). Specifically, mind maps help group members better understand each other’s perspectives and 
ideas by clearly demonstrating the relationships between information through hierarchical structures and connecting lines (e.g., 
Cendros Araujo and Gadanidis, 2020). When properly designed and embedded with appropriate pedagogy, mind maps can improve 
learning outcomes (e.g., Cendros Araujo and Gadanidis, 2020; Malycha & Maier, 2017; Suharto et al., 2023). Previous research has 
validated the effectiveness of using mind maps in math (e.g., Araujo & Gadanidis, 2020; Cendros Araujo and Gadanidis, 2020), lan­
guage learning (e.g., Marriott & Torres, 2014), science teaching (e.g., Sun et al., 2022), and informal learning environments (e.g., Chen 
et al., 2019; Malycha & Maier, 2017). Meanwhile, students also maintained a positive view of mind mapping since they believed that 
the scaffolding helped them to save time and actively read the literary text (Suharto et al., 2023). In conventional mind mapping-based 
collaborative environments, the ideas generated by team members remain homogeneous and lack novelty. This phenomenon is 
particularly evident when creating shared mind maps, despite they can effectively facilitate information sharing and improve 
communication efficiency (Chang et al., 2018). Specifically, when group members edited and constructed mind maps together, they 
tended to imitate each other’s ideas and expressions (Suharto et al., 2023). This tendency would undoubtedly limit the individual’s 
self-reflection and creativity. As a result, the final output of group members tended to be similar and lacked diversity and novelty. 
Therefore, there is an urgent need to address this issue of how to break the convergence and promote diversified thinking among 
members in conventional mind mapping-based collaborative environments. On the other hand, mind maps are known for being highly 
concise, a feature that makes them effective in summarizing and presenting the ideas of individuals (e.g., Marriott & Torres, 2014). 
However, in the context of group collaboration it may prevent groups with differences in a priori knowledge levels from engaging in 
deep conversations (Fadillah, 2019). In particular, when group members utilized mind maps to organize their respective viewpoints 
during the consultation process, high-level students tended to be able to distill the key information more quickly and present it in a 
concise manner. By contrast, low-level students may find it difficult to formulate a clear viewpoint in a short period of time due to 
insufficient knowledge base. Furthermore, they were gradually placed in a passive position or even marginalized. Therefore, during the 
process of utilizing mind mapping as a highly effective tool to facilitate collaboration, we need to explore how to take into account the 
needs of students with different prior knowledge levels and ensure that each student can actively participate in the dialogues.
As a pioneering digital resource capable of bringing diversity, Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) shows great potential in 
addressing the challenges inherent in conventional mind mapping collaborative environments. GAI is equipped with an accessible 
interface facilitating human-like interactions and generating original text based on user prompts (e.g., Rudolph et al., 2023). Firstly, as 
an aggregator of wisdom, it provides group members with unprecedented perspectives and insights from its extensive knowledge base. 
By interacting with GAI, group members were able to broaden their sources of inspiration. Many research have demonstrated that this 
interactive process greatly enriched the diversity and novelty of their perspectives (e.g., Anantrasirichai & Bull, 2022; Wieland et al., 
2022). For instance, Wieland et al. (2022) conducted a quasi-experimental study that asked participants to brainstorm with either a 
chatbot or a human as a partner. It was found that chatbot was more capable of allowing participants to generate more diverse ideas. 
Secondly, GAI shows great potential for balancing differences in ability among group members. In other words, it can serve as an 
effective bridge to narrow the knowledge gap among group members to a certain extent. Through precisely matching the needs of 
different learners, GAI provides customized learning resources and more specific practical solutions. Such process enables even 
learners with lower knowledge level to find suitable entry points and actively participate in group discussions and practices. For 
example, Habib et al. (2024) investigated the impact of GAI, namely ChatGPT-3 on the creativity of college students. The study found 
that the use of ChatGPT-3 promoted thinking fluency. Students enjoyed the brainstorming and idea generation, as GAI dynamically 
optimized its output based on user feedback and needs, enabling it to better meet the personalized needs of users. Meanwhile, they also 
noted the potential for GAI to negatively impact creative confidence as well.
To conclude, GAI holds the promise of solving many problems that currently exist in conventional mind mapping, such as 
convergence of viewpoints, low engagement, etc. Despite the increasing research on GAI tools applied to education in recent years, 
there is a dearth of practical explorations within the field of collaborative learning regarding the integration of GAI tools with 
collaborative environments supported by pedagogical scaffolds. Given that groups often exhibit varied learning behaviors contingent 
upon the support of diverse technological resources and instructional scaffolds, a meticulous analysis and comparison of these

2. Literature review
2.1. Collaborative knowledge construction
To comprehend the process of knowledge construction that learners accomplish through online interactions, researchers have 
proposed a series of influential theories (e.g., Mayordomo & Onrubia, 2015). For example, in collaborative learning theory, CKC was 
considered to be an advanced stage of the collective cognitive process, i.e. intellectual convergence (e.g., Fischer & Mandl, 2005). 
Collaborative learning theory identified three stages that learners are required to go through as generating ideas, organizing ideas, and 
intellectual convergence. In addition, Mayordomo and Onrubia (2015) characterized the process of CKC in small groups as a four-stage 
process, i.e., “initiation”, “exploration”, “negotiation”, and “co-construction”. CKC at the elementary stage primarily involves learners 
engaging in the output of ideas and further developing a lower degree of interrelationship between peers. Getting to the advanced stage 
implies that learners are able to negotiate and reconstruct new meanings from each other’s contributions (e.g., Araujo & Gadanidis, 
2020). In other words, learners’ expression of their perspectives is a necessary condition for collaborative knowledge construction, as 
the exchange of different viewpoints is the starting point for negotiation (e.g., Malmberg et al., 2022; Ouyang & Chang, 2018). 
Therefore, surpassing the mere division of tasks among team members, it emphasizes learners’ sharing of ideas and information and 
continuous improvement through peer interaction, culminating in the development and construction of knowledge at the group level 
(e.g., Vygotsky, 1978). As a result, researchers have explored at the individual level how learners’ self-learning behaviors are related to 
normative learning stages at the group level. For example, Malmberg et al. (2022) focused on the individual learning process as part of 
collaborative learning. They investigated how learners’ individual monitoring evolved through phases of cyclic regulation in a 
collaborative learning context using temporal and sequential analysis. Lund (2019) proposed a multi-theoretical, interdisciplinary 
model that addressed how individuals and groups work together to construct knowledge in group interactions. As a key unit of 
analysis, the peer level is frequently used to reveal how groups realize the process of knowledge construction through peer-to-peer 
interactions and how they regulate and facilitate each other’s learning process. For example, Zhang et al. (2017) revealed a di­
versity in teachers’ behavioral patterns regarding social knowledge construction across various phases of activities. Notably, no sta­
tistically significant discrepancy was observed in the distribution of these behaviors, indicating that both actively engaged and less 
interactive teachers exhibited a range of strategies in facilitating knowledge construction. In contrast, Ouyang and Chang (2018) found 
that students who frequently interacted with their peers tended to make more cognitive contributions to the process of knowledge 
inquiry and construction. Prior researches have often delved into collaborative knowledge construction (CKC) with a tendency to focus 
on either singular or dual levels. However, the evolution of CKC across individual, peer, and group domains is not a linear progression 
but rather a complex interplay of interconnected dynamics (e.g., Lund, 2019; Ouyang et al., 2023). Therefore, it becomes imperative to 
examine the intricate features from multiple levels to gain a nuanced comprehension of the mechanisms underpinning knowledge 
construction within collaborative group settings.
2.2. Mind mapping in collaborative learning
Reiser (2004) argued that scaffolding worked by reducing the complexity of the task. For example, tasks are organized in such a 
way that teachers direct students to focus on appropriate task components or guide them in problem solving (e.g., Davis & Miyake, 
2004; Van De Pol et al., 2010). In addition, representations and thinking related to the co-construction of knowledge can be exter­
nalized in technology-rich learning environments by providing visualization tools (e.g., Fischer et al., 2007; Lu et al., 2010). For 
example, mind maps, which use a combination of text, graphics, color, and layout, have become an important tool in collaboration 
learning designed to enhance learners’ knowledge organization and conceptual understanding (e.g., Malycha & Maier, 2017; Sun 
et al., 2022). Research studies have shown that the use of mind maps not only enhances individual learning, but also helps to promote 
peer interaction (e.g., Araujo & Gadanidis, 2020). During collaborative knowledge construction, mind mapping as a common reference 
point can help group members quickly understand and grasp the overall framework and details of existing knowledge. In the process of 
drawing mind maps, group members can add new ideas and perspectives liberally, which promotes knowledge innovation and 
expansion by continuously expanding and deepening the hierarchical structure of mind maps (e.g., Sun et al., 2022). Cendros and 
Gadanidis (2017) compared an online tool for group discussion, i.e., topic forums, with collaborative mind mapping. They argued that 
the hierarchical and structured nature of mind maps allowed for the smooth flow of ideas and a rapid visualization of key concepts in a 
more intuitive way. Furthermore, they incorporated collaborative mind mapping activities as an alternative to threaded forums into an 
elementary mathematics education curriculum (Cendros Araujo and Gadanidis, 2020). In this way, they explored how pre-service 
math teachers engaged in interaction and knowledge construction when participating in CKC. With the aid of mind maps, students 
no longer spent a great deal of effort describing or commenting on each detail of classroom activities, as they did in the threaded 
forums. Instead, they extracted the key topics or main ideas of the discussion and developed them (Cendros Araujo and Gadanidis, 
2020). Overall, mind mapping can facilitate the clear presentation and exchange of ideas among peers through its visual features (e.g., 
Chen et al., 2019; Davis & Miyake, 2004). However, its inherent framework can also constrain students’ creative thinking and lead to 
homogenized productions. This phenomenon may limit team creativity (Chang et al., 2018). In addition, mind maps are characterized

by simplicity and highly summarize the views and ideas of individual students. However, this high level of generalization sometimes

contribute ideas and ask questions. Meanwhile, high-level members tend to dominate the negotiation process, and in some cases even

members differ significantly in their knowledge base and professional backgrounds. Given the above, there is a need to incorporate 
more open and flexible digital resources along with the use of mind maps to promote diversity in the outcomes of the group’s products 
and deeper dialogue among members (Chang et al., 2018).
2.3. Generative artificial intelligence
As a frontier product of technological development, GAI tools such as ChatGPT, Bing Chat, or ERNIE Bot, are gradually showing 
profound influence in multiple fields with their excellent learning and creation capabilities (e.g., Liu, Li, & Dong, 2024). A series of 
studies investigating the application of GAI tools in the field of education have presented complex and multifaceted results (e.g., Chen 
et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2024). It has proven to show promising results in improving the quality of programming education (e.g., Sun 
et al., 2024; Yılmaz & Yılmaz, 2023a). For example, Sun et al. (2024) employed a quasi-experimental design to explore the effects of a 
ChatGPT-based programming model on college students’ programming behavior and academic performance. Upon examination, it 
was detected that the employment of a ChatGPT-based programming model elicited an enhancement in students’ programming 
performance, albeit this improvement failed to attain statistical significance. Also utilizing a quasi-experimental design approach, 
Yılmaz and Yılmaz (2023a) found that generative artificial intelligence was beneficial for improving students’ computational thinking 
skills and programming self-efficacy by comparing pre-test and post-test scale scores. In addition, GAI is also widely used to facilitate 
learners’ language comprehension and writing skills (e.g., Liu, Zhang, & Biebricher, 2024). Liu, Zhang, and Biebricher (2024)
compared students’ cognitive processes of text generation in a GAI tool-assisted environment with a traditional writing environment. 
Furthermore, the study examined the behavioral patterns of creating images. It was found that the GAI tool was able to provide 
suggestions and guidance on textual elements for writing, thus facilitating students’ language development, as well as helping learners 
to generate images as vivid examples. Moreover, the perception of students regarding the use of GAI tools are invaluable (Chiu, 2024). 
By delving into students’ experiences, we can more accurately grasp the actual effects of these tools in facilitating knowledge 
acquisition and enhancing learning efficiency. For example, Chiu (2024) adopted a mixed deductive and inductive thematic analysis 
approach to analyze student questionnaire and interview data. In particular, the study focused on the three major themes of learning 
outcomes, pedagogy, and assessment to determine the changes that may be required in higher education following the implementation 
of GAI. Chan and Hu (2023) revealed that the students surveyed were generally optimistic about the incorporation of GAI in learning, 
with particular emphasis on its advantages in facilitating personalized learning.
While numerous researchers have hailed the potential positive impacts of GAI on task performance, a parallel strand of inquiry has 
surfaced, scrutinizing its accompanying limitations. Specifically, it has been observed to generate academic articles with spurious 
authorship and titles upon being tasked with producing cited articles (e.g., Liu, Zhang, & Biebricher, 2024). Furthermore, ethical 
dilemmas surrounding GAI-produced content have garnered attention (e.g., Sun et al., 2024). Yilmaz and Yilmaz (2023b) employed an 
open-ended question format to gather student feedback on the integration of ChatGPT in programming education. While ChatGPT can 
aid students in debugging their programs and boost their confidence, it may also provoke career-related anxiety due to concerns about 
its potential to replace human roles.
Up to now, research has mainly explored the impact of GAI on learning performance and learning behaviors across subject areas, 
while its application in mind mapping-based collaborative learning environments has not yet been discussed. In mind mapping based 
collaborative environments, several challenges arise, including convergence of viewpoints, low engagement, etc. (e.g., Chang et al., 
2018; Suharto et al., 2023). To address these challenges, the introduction of generative artificial intelligence (GAI) presents a 
promising solution. Although there is a long tradition of using digital technologies and analytic tools to support knowledge con­
struction (e.g., Chen & Hong, 2016), no previous work has integrated GAI tools into this context. In addition, the role of GAI tools in 
facilitating student learning performance is still controversial. Meanwhile, few studies have provided insights into how GAI tools 
specifically contribute to the knowledge construction process in teamwork (e.g., Cress & Kimmerle, 2023; Ouyang et al., 2023). 
Therefore, this study attempts to integrate the GAI tool into a mind mapping-based collaborative learning environment in order to 
explore its impact on task performance and the process of collaborative knowledge construction within groups. In the subsequent 
quasi-experimental study, the experimental groups employed the GAI tool and mind mapping based collaborative environment 
(GMMCE) and the control groups employed the conventional mind mapping based collaborative environment (MMCE). Based on the 
findings of previous studies (e.g., Sun et al., 2024; Chiu, 2024; Liu et al., 2024), we hypothesize that prudently integrating generative 
AI as an innovative digital resource into conventional mind mapping-based collaborative environments can positively impact student 
outcomes. Specifically, it can enhance task performance and promote deeper knowledge construction. The research questions are as 
follows. 
1. What is the impact of integrating the GAI tool into mind mapping-based collaborative environment on students’ task performance?
2. How does the collaborative knowledge construction process differ between GMMCE and MMCE groups at different stages of 
collaborative learning?
3. How meaningfully do student teachers in GMMCE perceive using the generative Al tool?

3. Methodology
3.1. Research context and participants
This study was conducted at a comprehensive university in central China during the autumn semester of 2023 in the course 
"Pedagogical Applications of Information Technology". As an elective course, the course lasted for eight weeks. Each week a 90 min 
session was conducted. Students enrolled in the course were student teachers in their sophomore and junior years who were actively 
seeking ways to integrate information technology into their subject matter. These student teachers have recognized the transformative 
potential of information technology in contemporary education. They actively explored and experimented with digital tools to enrich 
their instructional practices. Each student teacher was encouraged in our course of study to apply these theoretical concepts to their 
respective subject areas and to integrate technology in meaningful ways. Consequently, all student teachers were selected as partic­
ipants in this study, including 13 males and 17 females. They majored in physics, history, mathematics, and many other disciplinary 
backgrounds respectively.
3.2. Experimental procedure
The experimental procedure is shown in Fig. 1. The study began with two questionnaire tests, which were completed by grouping 
the participants on the principle of homogeneity between groups. Moreover, five groups were randomly assigned as the control group 
and the other five as the experimental group. During the eight-week course study, all students worked collaboratively in groups to 
complete two tasks assigned in the course, namely, writing an instructional design (ID) and creating a multimedia courseware design 
(MD). It is worth noting that the MD task was completed on the basis of the ID task. The participants in the experimental group were 
interviewed at the end of the course study. The interviews focused on their experiences with the GAI tool. Finally, the products of all 
groups were evaluated using the Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) gauge (Chen et al., 2021), which is primarily 
designed to assess technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge in educational settings.
3.3. Measuring tools
3.3.1. Two instruments for grouping
To ensure the robustness of the findings, we followed rigorous protocols for filtering and pairing procedures prior to the grouping 
stage. Firstly, a comprehensive assessment of students’ relevant abilities and knowledge was conducted by distributing a test on 
problem solving ability and a questionnaire on subject pedagogy and technological knowledge. The first test instrument, adapted from 
PISA 2005, assesses problem-solving ability. Test items were translated into Chinese and validated through forward and backward 
translation to ensure reliability and validity. The second instrument, a questionnaire, focuses on pedagogical understanding, profi­
ciency in information technology, and their collaborative endeavors, offering a comprehensive evaluation of students’ educational 
competencies. The questionnaire has been used in previous studies (e.g., Zhang et al., 2022, 2023), and demonstrated strong reliability 
with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.868. After that, the research assistant ensured inter-group consistency by precisely dividing the 30 student 
teachers into 10 groups of three students each based on their scores of two questionnaire tests (Refer to Table 1 for details). In addition, 
in order to verify the rationality of the grouping, this study also used the Kruskal-Wallis test to test the significance of the difference 
between the groups in terms of two tests. The results showed that there was no significant difference between the groups in either of 
these areas (Kruskal-Wallis H = 9.000, df = 9, p = .437 for two questionnaire tests). Therefore, five groups were randomly selected as 
control groups (named as groups 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5), and another five as experimental groups (named as groups 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10). The

3.3.2. The coding framework for discourse data
Each group completed the ID task and MD task during the course study by collaboration via a synchronous online discussion 
platform (i.e. Tencent QQ). Therefore, discourse data from the group’s discussion were systematically gathered. Two research assis­
tants conducted the coding process for all discourse data at the sentence level based on a coding framework adapted from Ouyang et al. 
(2023) (see Appendix 1). This coding framework was used to elucidate the relationship between the cognitive, regulative, and 
emotional dimensions of the knowledge construction process at the individual, peer, and group levels (Ouyang et al., 2023). Since the 
collaborative discourse data collected for this study rarely involved emotional expression, we focused primarily on the cognitive and 
regulative dimensions. The cognitive dimension included the individual level (e.g., students sharing their understanding), the peer 
level (e.g., peers negotiating their views), and the group level (e.g., forming a collective consensus). The regulative dimension stems 
from Hadwin et al. (2017, pp. 83–106), including self-regulation, co-regulation and the socially shared regulation. During the coding 
process, each utterance was given a code. The inter-rater reliability coefficient (Cohen’s Kappa) was calculated to be 0.83, indicating 
strong consensus (Fleiss, 1981). It is worth noting that responses provided by the GAI in this study usually appeared as part of the 
student teachers’ mind maps, with individual members negotiating the modules of the mind maps during the group discussion phase. 
Student teachers processed the content of the GAI and posted the content they consider appropriate to the QQ group for members to 
discuss. Therefore, we further provided two examples to illustrate how to assign codes to the content of the GAI and peer contributions 
(see Tables 4 and 5). Finally, a total of 6759 pieces of discourse data were obtained. Within the cognitive dimension, discussions at the 
individual, peer, and group levels were as follows: 1705 utterances (25.2%), 1407 utterances (20.8%), and 846 utterances (12.5%), 
respectively. Within the regulative dimension, there were 820 utterances (12.1%) at the individual level, 353 utterances (5.2%) at the 
peer level, and 1628 utterances (24.0%) at the group level.

3.4. The course study process within MMCE and GMMCE
As shown in Fig. 2, two tasks consist of three stages, namely, the first draft (stage 1.1 and stage 2.1), first round of intra-group 
revision (stage 1.2 and stage 2.2), and second round of intra-group revision (stage 1.3 and stage 2.3). Specifically, this study 
focused only on the two round of revision phase of the group’s products, i.e., stage 1.2 and stage 2.2, stage 1.3 and stage 2.3. During the 
first round of intra-group revision, groups improved the products regarding the first draft through in-depth communication within the 
group. During the second round of intra-group revision, groups firstly evaluated the other groups’ productions and then discussed the 
feedback within the group. To ensure fair evaluation, this study designed a mechanism for separate peer reviews between control and 
experimental groups. Specifically, the student teachers used ZHIXI MINDMAP as an individual to map their mind and clarify their ideas 
for revision before engaging in discussions and interactions within the group (See Fig. 2).
3.4.1. The first round of intra-group revision of ID
The control group utilized the ZHIXI MINDMAP for individual mind mapping. The creation of individual mind mapping helped 
with information visualization, allowed individuals to understand the ideas of team members, enhanced the efficiency of group dis­
cussions, and promoted the effective completion of learning tasks. As shown in Fig. 3, Student 1 suggested that the group’s instruc­
tional design product could be modified in terms of both instructional method and instructional process. The other two students, i.e. 
Student 2 and Student 3, also suggested their own modifications from different aspects of the instructional design product.
While the control group mainly relied on conventional mind mapping, the experimental group additionally adopted a GAI tool - 
ERNIE Bot. Prior to the start of the course tasks, the research assistants provided the experimental groups with video tutorials and 
examples to share on the use of GAI, as shown in Table 2.
In addition, there is a need to learn how to identify potential problems with GAI outputs and select the valuable content. We have 
carefully crafted a series of video resources that vividly demonstrate how to critically embrace the content provided by the GAI. For 
example, possible sources of bias, limitations in data selection, and implicit assumptions in algorithm design. By sharing some ex­
amples of research failures that resulted from relying too heavily on GAI predictions, we have encouraged the group to remain cautious 
when utilizing GAI outputs. Student teachers firstly interacted with ERNIE Bot in depth by asking questions as an individual unit. In 
this process, they made full use of ERNIE Bot’s intelligent analysis capabilities to obtain constructive comments and perspectives and 
completed individual mind maps prior to the group discussion. To ensure their peers gain a comprehensive understanding of the 
proposed modifications, the student teachers included links to the specific interactions with ERNIE Bot alongside each constructive 
suggestion for modification (see blue boxes in Fig. 4). The three student teachers in the Group 7 referred to three suggested modifi­
cations during their interactions with ERNIE Bot in their respective mind maps. The majority of them were about the design details of 
classroom interactions in instruction, such as the simulation game of "Tian Ji Racing", the role-playing session, the card game, or the 
rules of evaluation in the read-aloud part.

3.5. Data collection and analysis
Fig. 11 illustrates the systematic procedure adopted for the data collection and subsequent data analysis. To address research 
question 1, we conducted the Mann-Whitney U test to determine whether a statistically significant difference existed between the 
scores of the experimental and control groups. To address research question 2, we visualized the ordered network model of the 
experimental and control groups using R package, i.e., ona (Tan et al., 2023). Built upon the foundation of the Epistemic Network 
Analysis (ENA) method, ONA quantifies and visualizes the transition frequencies between coded events within the datasets (e.g., Tan 
et al., 2023). The ONA algorithm first uses the coded and segmented data to accumulate connections for each unit of analysis. In the 
application of the ONA algorithm to each unit, a sliding window methodology is employed to discern the connections instantiated by 
the instant data row. Drawing upon a review of prior literature, we have determined an optimal sliding window length of seven for the 
analysis of the current datasets (e.g., Li et al., 2023). Notably, a comprehensive technical elaboration of the ONA methodology 
transcends the boundaries of the present research. The seminal study conducted by Tan et al. (2023) offers a profound expansion on the 
technical principles underlying ONA.
To address research question 3, semi-structured interview data from experimental groups (GMMCE) were collected using an online 
conference system (Tencent Conference system). Before recording interviews, we ensured students knew about and agreed to the 
recording. In addition, we conducted a pre-interview to test the applicability of the protocol. Subsequently, we refined the interview 
process to delve deeply into student teachers’ perceptions and experiences with GAI tools (refer to Appendix 2 for the interview 
protocol). The interview data collection ranged from 40 to 60 min, averaging around 55 min per recording. A rigorous thematic 
analysis approach, as outlined by Braun and Clarke (2006), was employed to scrutinize the interview data. Firstly, two research as­
sistants transcribed the audio data while achieving familiarity of the interview data through checking and rereading. Secondly, 
develop initial codes based on the data. Thirdly, identify themes based on initial codes. The different codes were categorized into 
potential themes at a broader, thematic level and organized all relevant coded data within the identified themes. Fourth, revise and 
refine themes. Fifth, define and elaborate themes. The essence of each theme was further defined considering the themes themselves 
and the relationship of each theme to the others. After developing a complete set of themes, the final analysis and report was 
conducted.
4. Results
4.1. What is the impact of integrating the GAI tool into mind mapping-based collaborative environment on students’ task performance?
We collected the scores of each group’s products under the TPACK gauge. As shown in Table 3, during the ID task stage, the MMCE 
groups’ product scores ranged from a high of 31 to a low of 16 (M = 21.8, SD = 5.7). The GMMCE groups’ product scores ranged from a 
high of 33 to a low of 24 (M = 28.8, SD = 3.8). During the MD task stage, the MMCE groups’ product scores ranged from a high of 32 to 
a low of 19 (M = 24.6, SD = 4.7). The GMMCE groups’ product scores ranged from a high of 33 to a low of 27 (M = 29.4, SD = 2.3). 
Furthermore, we explored the statistical significance of differences in the above scores using the Mann-Whitney U test. The results 
found a significant difference between the MMCE and GMMCE groups in the ID product scores (U = 3.000, z = −1.984, p < .05). 
However, the significant difference between the groups of MMCE and GMMCE was not observed in the scores of the MD (U = 4.000, z 
= −1.776, p = .076).

to current technological limitations, student teachers were more likely to upload screenshots to ask questions about the MD. Finally, 
regarding the interactive effects of ERNIE Bot, Groups 9 and 10 reported significant contributions to ID revision but limited impact on 
MD. While some praised ERNIE Bot’s convenience for information retrieval, others noted challenges in obtaining practical advice and 
accurately describing MD difficulties through textual descriptions. Since ERNIE Bot’s familiarity with the advanced functionalities of 
the Seewo Whiteboard 5 was limited, which hindered its ability to provide practical advice. Consequently, its guidance was more 
general in nature, focusing on technical operations rather than offering constructive advice at a detailed level. Additionally, the 
interactive exchange primarily involved uploading images, which posed challenges in accurately conveying the interactive aspects of 
MD. This method struggled to capture the characteristics of developing MD, making it difficult for student teachers to articulate their 
design challenges through textual descriptions alone.
5. Discussion
In this study, a generative artificial intelligence tool was introduced into mind mapping-based collaborative environment. The 
experimental design and findings of this study demonstrate a new possibility for the application of GAI tools in collaborative learning.
5.1. Differences of task performance between GMMCE and MMCE groups
GMMCE groups outperformed MMCE groups on both collaborative tasks in the course. In particular, the experimental group 
significantly outperformed the control group on ID task. This result is consistent with Liu, Zhang, and Biebricher (2024) that the 
application of GAI tools in the collaborative learning field enhances learners’ achievement in a certain way. Furthermore, in the 
context of text-based content creation, GAI tools prove valuable as a supplement. They help students enhance the quality of their work 
by improving relevance, logic, and educational value through powerful text-processing capabilities and intelligent recommendations 
(Chan & Hu, 2023). However, no statistically significant difference was found between the experimental and control groups in scoring 
group work on multimedia courseware design tasks. This may be due to the nature of the task itself. Creating multimedia courseware 
relies heavily on integrating images, sounds, and videos to boost interactivity and engagement. Current GAI tools lack the capability to 
generate such complex content, limiting their effectiveness in this area. This finding underscores the need to consider task types when 
evaluating digital resources and tools in education, to better identify appropriate applications and optimize tool design (Chang et al., 
2018). Accordingly, Chang et al. (2018) innovatively incorporated mobile online resources on the basis of conventional mind mapping 
functions. This initiative not only broadens the information sources of mind maps, but also greatly facilitates the process of information 
retrieval and data storage for users. In conclusion, GAI tools combined with effective pedagogical scaffolding (e.g., mind mapping) in

5.2. Differences of CKC process from three levels between GMMCE and MMCE groups
The student teachers’ discourse data were analyzed in depth using ONA to explore the groups’ knowledge construction process. The 
results showed that the experimental and control groups demonstrated various characteristics across the different task phases. Spe­
cifically, the experimental group’s (GMMCE condition) preliminary discussion of collaborative tasks focused primarily on cognitive 
dimensions, such as the transition from the individual level to the peer level. They developed a more coherent process of knowledge 
construction in the cognitive dimension by the later stages, i.e., shared understanding and collective wisdom at the group level (e.g., 
Fischer et al., 2002). However, this strength was weaker in the control group (MMCE condition) once they entered the transition from 
the peer level to the group level. Not to mention the fact that on this basis individuals are able to internalize and form their own unique 
and systematic knowledge structures. This may be due to the fact that the experimental group was given the opportunity to develop 
further with the help of the GAI. Although the advice given by the GAI was often rather general, it was to some extent able to promote 
more detailed and in-depth discussion on a certain topic among the group members (Suharto et al., 2023). This phenomenon suggests 
that GAI’s involvement in complex tasks can positively contribute to the dynamics of group discussion and further accelerate the 
formation of collective wisdom (Yilmaz & Yilmaz, 2023b). On the contrary, Yilmaz and Yilmaz (2023b) found that using GAI for 
relatively simple tasks had a negative impact on students’ thinking skills. This occurred because students tended to stop developing 
their thinking skills after obtaining the desired answers from tools like ChatGPT. Referring to the four phases of collaborative 
knowledge construction proposed by Mayordomo and Onrubia (2015), i.e., “initiation”, “exploration”, “negotiation”, and “co-con­
struction”, we found that GAI’s answer was seen as the starting point easing the challenges in the initial stages of project, thereby 
contributing to a steady improvement in the quality of the group’s work. As Ouyang and Chang (2018) posited, the process of indi­
vidual knowledge inquiry, peer interaction, and group construction unfolds in a gradual and developmental manner. To foster greater 
peer interaction, students are encouraged to engage in elaborate construction of their own arguments. On the other hand, with the 
assistance of GAI, the initial task obstacles within the group are alleviated, allowing for more focus and energy to be directed toward 
the construction of collective intelligence (e.g., Chen et al., 2019; Paulus et al., 2010). As Chang et al. (2018) argued, the innovative 
integration of mind mapping with digital resources within a conventional educational framework not only significantly stimulated 
students’ interest, but also effectively facilitated the networked construction of their knowledge systems. Meanwhile, it provided 
strong support for students to deepen their understanding and expand their learning boundaries. We also observed that control groups 
tended to shift to focusing on individual task comprehension, planning, and completion progress after articulating their personal views 
and engaging in peer interactions, which may also account for the inability of group discussions to further develop into collective 
intelligence. Specifically, they were conducted by highly motivated group members who often led the discussions. The main focus is on 
regulating and clarifying the tasks that need to be accomplished within the group and the progress of the various tasks. Low-motivated 
or low-performing participants tend to be marginalized in this process. They were involved in the group discussion mainly through 
monitoring their own task completion. Compared to the control groups, the experimental groups developed more divergent thinking 
with the support of the GAI tool. As a core indicator of the creative potential, divergent thinking plays a crucial role in stimulating 
novel ideas and promoting diversity in problem solving (Runco & Acar, 2012). As a result, the GAI tool expanded students’ divergent 
thinking and significantly increased the heterogeneity and inventiveness of group productions (Habib et al., 2024).
5.3. Perception of using GAI tools in GMMCE groups
Thematic analysis of the interview data revealed four major themes: frequency of interaction with ERNIE Bot, content of interaction 
with ERNIE Bot, approaches of interaction with ERNIE Bot, and effect of interaction with ERNIE Bot. According to content of inter­
action with ERNIE Bot, the majority of student teachers agreed that the multiple examples provided by ERNIE Bot developed new ideas 
and thoughts within the both collaborative task phases. Concordant findings were reported by Habib et al. (2024) and Wieland et al. 
(2022), showing that GAI was expected to be effective in combining with pedagogical methods to promote creative thinking devel­
opment. Habib et al. (2024) revealed that university students increasingly preferred to utilize GAI technology when seeking brain­
storming and creativity stimulation. This technology can quickly and comprehensively provide multiple answers, greatly assisting 
students in the depth and breadth of their elaboration processes. According to effect of interaction with ERNIE Bot, it is a platform that 
allows student teachers to ask questions, explore and learn more about the basic and advanced features of multimedia courseware 
development software. It not only responds to these questions instantly, but also provides personalized guidance and advice based on 
the student teachers’ specific needs. This result is consistent with the findings of previous studies (e.g., Sun et al., 2024; Yılmaz & 
Yılmaz, 2023a). Sun et al. (2024) found that GAI facilitates students to efficiently share their raw code or complete error information, 
which greatly improves the efficiency of students in receiving personalized feedback from multiple sources. Furthermore, Yılmaz and 
Yılmaz (2023a) provided a comprehensive summary of the versatility of GAI in programming education. For example, it not only 
facilitates personalized learning, but also provides clear explanations and programming examples to help students better understand 
programming concepts and techniques. Biggs (1999) suggested that students’ perceptions of the learning environment and instruc­
tional strategies can greatly influence their learning outcomes. Specifically, positive attitudes and tendencies are likely to drive 
learners to explore knowledge in depth and reach higher levels of knowledge construction. On the other hand, negative attitudes tend 
to lead to superficial learning (Mayordomo & Onrubia, 2015). The current study revealed that students showed a more positive 
acceptance of the GAI tool, which explained the previous two questions. In particular, students were generally able to follow the

adaptability. Accordingly, the interview results also explained in part why the experimental group obtained a better task performance

6. Pedagogical implications
Based on the findings of this study, GAI tools have demonstrated significant benefits in enhancing task performance, especially in 
document composition assignments (e.g., Liu, Zhang, & Biebricher, 2024). This implication emphasizes the need to incorporate such 
tools in the field of higher education. In addition, there were challenges in realizing in-depth discussions associated with the themes of 
tasks in the collaborative process. It was reflected in the fact that group members tended to focus excessively on the completion of the 
task without engaging in the construction of collective intelligence during the collaboration process (e.g., Cress & Kimmerle, 2023). 
Confronted with this challenge, GAI presented a potential solution in its own unique way. Specifically, this tool effectively stimulated 
students’ perspectives and ideas through its powerful content generation and intelligent interaction capabilities, which in turn spawns 
richer and deeper discussions within the group (Wieland et al., 2022). Furthermore, it not only had the ability to respond instantly to 
students’ queries, but it was also able to provide guidance and advice based on learners’ individual needs (Yılmaz & Yılmaz, 2023a). 
This feature greatly facilitated the efficiency of students’ information retrieval and collection, shortening the period of knowledge 
acquisition. However, the generalized large language model (LLM) confronts challenges in in-depth pedagogical practice, especially in 
the integration of multidisciplinary knowledge and diversified media resources (e.g., text, images, video), which makes it difficult to 
output constructive and practical pedagogical suggestions. Therefore, teachers and instructional designers should firstly design 
instructional activities by setting open-ended questions, role-playing, and case studies to guide students to have in-depth discussions 
related to the topic of the task. Based on this, teachers and instructional designers may skillfully conceive and design a personalized 
intelligent agent (PIA) application program driven by generative artificial intelligence as the core technology. With the deepening of 
instruction, PIA will automatically adjust instructional strategies and resource allocation by accumulating data on students’ learning 
behaviors, thus promoting the overall development of each student in an optimal way. Given the nature of collaborative learning 
activities, which are highly complex and dynamic, GAI tools must be properly integrated with well-designed collaborative scaffolds. 
Specifically, with abundant case studies and opportunities for hands-on practice, teachers and instructional designers can help students 
gradually acquire the skills to create and apply effective prompts based on learning tasks and their own needs, laying a solid foundation 
for their learning in the age of artificial intelligence (Rudolph et al., 2023).
7. Conclusions, limitations and future studies
This study conducted a quasi-experimental design to explore the impact of GAI on the task performance and collaborative process 
in higher education. The instructor of the course provided student teachers with mind maps to scaffold during various stages of the 
collaborative task. The student teachers first mapped their thinking maps individually, and then engaged in refined discussion and 
negotiation within their groups. Prior research has found that the application of mind maps in collaborative learning processes can 
effectively address difficulties and challenges encountered (e.g., Fischer et al., 2002). Our research further designed a collaborative 
environment combining a GAI tool and mind maps, validating its effectiveness in enhancing group task performance. The control 
group (MMCE condition) primarily relied on conventional information technology tools to complete group tasks, while the experi­
mental group (GMMCE condition) brought in the application of a GAI tool. Moreover, this study collected discourse data from group 
discussions and analyzed the collaborative knowledge construction process across different task stages using ONA. Relying on an 
existing coding framework, we investigated the sequence and frequency of connections between different behaviors from two di­
mensions and three levels. In contrast to the task performance of the group, the analysis of process data revealed transitional patterns 
among student teachers during collaboration, providing an in-depth understanding of group interactions and dynamics. The experi­
mental group (GMMCE condition) focused on discussions in the cognitive dimension throughout the collaborative tasks, and devel­
oped a process of collaborative knowledge construction that primarily reflected a progressive collaboration and interaction from 
individual to peer to group. It emphasized the initiative and independence of individual student teachers, as well as the negotiation and 
interaction among peers and the joint exploration and construction at the group level. Empirical analysis revealed that the integration 
of Generative AI (GAI) in complex task significantly enhanced group dynamics and accelerated the development of collective intel­
ligence. GAI effectively mitigated initial task barriers, enabling group members to concentrate more on the deeper construction of 
collective knowledge. In contrast, discussions in the control group (MMCE condition) focused primarily on moderation, facilitating 
task completion. Some knowledge construction was achieved in the cognitive dimension, as seen in the interplay between individual 
expression and peer interaction. Therefore, the innovative use of mind mapping and digital resources can significantly boost students’ 
exploratory interest and foster a networked construction of knowledge. This enriched learning environment strongly supports deeper 
understanding and broader learning horizons.
The current study encounters several constraints, firstly stemming from the sampling composition, which comprises exclusively of 
student teachers from a central Chinese university. We conducted the research in authentic educational settings, observing and 
recording real-world occurrences. This approach ensures that our research has strong practical implications, but it also suggests that 
the findings may not generalize well to significantly different research contexts. Future research has the potential to apply collabo­
rative environments based on GAI tools to a wider range of educational contexts and participants. Another key limitation of this study 
is that the traditional collaboration tools and GAI tools adopted are rooted in the Chinese language environment. This specific cultural 
context may limit their multilingual applicability and functionality in a global perspective. Therefore, future research can effectively

supplement or replace the traditional collaboration tools and GAI tools to support their educational applications. For example, ERNIE 
Bot corresponds to ChatGPT, and Tencent QQ, as a mainstream instant messaging software, is similar to Facebook and Instagram in 
terms of the functions and usage scenarios provided. In addition, future research should endeavor to implement a more refined coding 
strategy at the data preprocessing stage to clearly differentiate between GAI information and group members’ own ideas. Through this 
differentiated coding, we can not only clearly outline the embedded trajectory of GAI content in group discussions, but also further 
analyze the sequential transition characteristics of group members’ statements immediately following the delivery of GAI information. 
Lastly, we primarily focused on group product scores as an indicator of task performance and collected discourse data from group 
discussions for in-depth analysis. While these data provide valuable insights into the learning process and collaborative outcomes, the 
study has some notable limitations. Specifically, we were unable to collect behavioral data such as click streams, which could offer a 
more intuitive reflection of students’ engagement and interaction during learning. Future research should explore the use of more 
advanced technologies and methods to collect and analyze multi-modal data. For instance, eye-tracking technology and electroen­
cephalography (EEG) devices can be utilized to gather eye-movement and brain activity data from students during collaborative 
learning.
