Reading as a catalyst for creative attitude: evidence from rural
China and the cognitive pathways linking literacy and innovation
Lidong Zhang , Hongyu Guan * , Yijia Li , Wenting Liu , Fang Chang
Center for Experimental Economics in Education, Faculty of Education, Shaanxi Normal University, Xi’an, 710000, China

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Reading ability
Creative attitude
Rural students in China
Rural education
Longitudinal study

This study investigates how reading enhances the creative attitude of rural children, drawing on
longitudinal data (2015–2017) from fourth-grade students in 120 primary schools in Jiangxi
Province, China. Reading achievement shows a significant positive association with creative
attitude score, and this relationship remains robust after accounting for individual, family,
teacher, and school characteristics. Instrumental variable estimates and lagged models further
support the influence of reading on creative attitude. Mechanism analyses indicate that reading
enhances creative attitude by strengthening self-efficacy, encouraging more engaged reading
behaviour, and enhancing comprehension and logical reasoning skills. Heterogeneity analyses
reveal that the positive effects of reading are more pronounced among students who attend
reading courses, benefit from supportive home reading environments, have access to reading
spaces, participate in reading activities, come from better-off households, or achieve higher
reading scores. By providing rare longitudinal evidence from under-resourced rural China, this
study not only confirms that reading enhances creative attitude but also clarifies the channels and
conditions under which this effect is strongest. The findings underscore the role of reading as a
powerful pathway for improving creative attitude among rural children and highlight the
importance of strengthening reading skills in settings with limited educational resources.

1. Introduction
Creativity plays a critical role in children’s holistic development, influencing not only their academic (Akpur, 2020; Gajda et al.,
2017; Nami et al., 2014) and career outcomes (Fernández-Díaz et al., 2021; Fillis & Rentschler, 2010) but also driving social innovation
and cultural advancement (Glăveanu, 2018; Kwan et al., 2018; Moran, 2010). With the global demand for innovative capabilities
continuing to rise, creativity is now widely regarded as one of the core competencies of the 21st century. Scholars and policymakers in
the fields of education, economics, and sociology are increasingly focused on devising strategies to foster creativity during early
childhood, acknowledging its significance for both individual success and broader societal progress (Potters et al., 2023; Robson &
Rowe, 2012; Simonton, 2000; Yildirim & Yilmaz, 2023; Žarnauskaitė, 2023).
In the creativity literature, researchers distinguish among several related but conceptually distinct constructs, including creative
potential, creative performance, creative thinking, and creativity-related attitudes or dispositions (Diržytė et al., 2021; Hennessey &
Mueller, 2020; Nielsen et al., 2008). Numerous studies indicate that the creative attitude constitutes a vital component of creativity,

and the assessment of creative attitudes plays a significant role in predicting creative behaviour (Hoffmann et al., 2025; Ivcevic &
Hoffmann, 2022). This study follows the attitudinal tradition, using the Creativity Attitude Survey (CAS), which captures children’s
orientation toward creativity—such as openness, curiosity, imaginative inclination, and willingness to engage in novel tasks—rather
than creativity performance. Thus, throughout the paper, we refer to our outcome variable as creative attitude, consistent with the
CAS’s original purpose and prior applications in educational settings.
Existing research indicates that creativity and creative attitudes are shaped by a multitude of factors, such as educational resources,
cultural settings, and exposure to cognitive stimuli (Chua et al., 2015; Deng et al., 2016; Shao et al., 2019). In underdeveloped regions,
however, limited educational provisions and insufficient teacher training often lead to learning environments that offer inadequate
support for creative thinking (Fearon et al., 2013; Runco et al., 2017; Torrance, 1968). On one hand, schools in these areas tend to
emphasize exam-oriented instruction (Hernández-Romero & Sun, 2021) at the expense of encouraging divergent thinking and
innovative problem-solving. On the other hand, socio-cultural and familial contexts—often constrained by economic hardships and
insufficient cognitive resources—may further hinder children’s opportunities for diverse and enriching learning experiences (Hu et al.,
2016; Wu et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2021). Consequently, children in these environments face unique challenges that may prove
detrimental to fostering a positive attitude towards creativity, thereby hindering their creative development (Smare & Elfatihi, 2024;
Ucus, 2018; Wolska-Długosz, 2015).
Against this backdrop, a growing body of literature has begun to explore the potential impact of reading on children’s creativity
(Bezerra et al., 2022; Mourgues et al., 2014; Ritchie et al., 2013; Wang, 2012). In many underdeveloped areas, marked by insufficient
educational resources, a shortage of qualified educators, and limited infrastructure, the role of reading as a means of universal
educational access becomes increasingly evident (Davidson & Hobbs, 2013; Wang et al., 2021). As a relatively accessible activity,
reading exposes children to a wide array of narratives, characters, and ideas, stimulating imagination, expanding associative thinking,
and fostering creative cognition (de Vicente-Yagüe Jara et al., 2023; Lysaker et al., 2011). However, the mechanisms through which
reading may influence creative attitudes are still not fully understood. Some scholars argue that reading enriches linguistic input and
emotional engagement (Dickinson et al., 2012), thereby enhancing children’s cognitive flexibility and critical thinking. Others suggest
that exposure to diverse reading materials helps construct broader knowledge networks and cultural perspectives, ultimately
improving problem-solving abilities and fostering innovative expression (Scorer & Vardy, 2025).
Moreover, this study also considers the heterogeneity of these mechanisms across different groups or contexts. Children’s reading
behaviors and paths to developing creative attitudes may vary significantly by age, gender, or family background (Chiu &
McBride-Chang, 2006; Vlachos & Papadimitriou, 2015). For instance, older children are better at thinking deeply in their reading
(Paris & Jacobs, 1984), potentially impacting the degree to which reading fosters their creative attitudes. Children from families with
higher education levels may receive more guided reading support (Myrberg & Rosén, 2009), resulting in different patterns of the
relationship between reading and creative attitudes. Consequently, investigating how reading and creative attitudes interact within
these different subgroups provides valuable insights into “which children benefit most from reading, and under what circumstances,”
offering more targeted guidance for policy interventions and educational practices.
Unlike previous studies that have primarily examined urban or high-resource settings, this study provides rare longitudinal evidence from under-resourced rural China, thereby extending the literature on the reading–creative attitudes nexus to a new population
and context. Drawing on the educational production function framework and theories of creativity (Amabile, 1996), we conceptualize
reading achievement as a key input that enhances creative attitudes through both cognitive and non-cognitive mechanisms. Guided by
these considerations, the present study investigates three interrelated questions: how reading achievement influences children’s
creative attitudes in underdeveloped rural areas; through which mechanisms reading contributes to creative attitude development;
and whether this relationship differs across children with diverse backgrounds, learning environments, and family resources. By
addressing these questions, the study aims to advance theoretical understanding of the reading–creative attitudes nexus and to
generate context-specific insights for educators and policymakers working to foster creative attitudes in resource-limited settings.
2. Methods
2.1. Participants
This study is based on a three-year longitudinal survey conducted between 2015 and 2017, involving principals, teachers, and
fourth-grade students from 120 rural primary schools in Jiangxi Province, China. Jiangxi was selected as the focal province due to its
significant rural population, which accounted for 48% of the total population in 2015—higher than the national average of 44%
(Ganzhou Municipal Bureau of Statistics, National Bureau of Statistics Ganzhou Survey Team, 2016; National Bureau of Statistics,
2016). Given this demographic structure, Jiangxi serves as a representative case for provinces with extensive rural regions in China.
The sampling process was conducted in three stages:
2.1.1. Stage 1: county selection
In the first stage, three counties within Jiangxi Province were identified for inclusion in the study. These counties were classified as
underdeveloped and had been officially designated as national poverty-stricken counties since 2012. In 2015, their per capita GDP was
less than $3000, representing <40% of the national average. However, the per capita income of rural residents in these counties was
approximately equal to the provincial average. Thus, these counties can be considered broadly representative of the 680 officially
designated low-income counties across China.

2.1.2. Stage 2: school selection
In the second stage, a comprehensive list of all rural public primary schools in the three selected counties was compiled with the
assistance of local Education Bureaus. This list comprised 458 schools. A stratified random sampling approach was employed, wherein
schools were selected from each township in proportion to the total number of schools within that township. This process resulted in a
final sample of 120 schools, distributed as follows: 37 schools (30.9%) in County A, 25 schools (20.8%) in County B, and 58 schools
(48.3%) in County C.
2.1.3. Stage 3: class and student selection
In the final stage, sample classes were identified within each selected school. If a school had no more than two fourth-grade classes,
all classes were included in the sample. In cases where there were more than two classes, two were randomly selected for inclusion. The
Chinese language teacher (or, if unavailable, the general language teacher) of the sampled class was designated as the sample teacher.
All students present in the sample class on the day of the survey were included in the study.
Following this sampling protocol, a baseline survey was conducted in May 2015, assessing fourth-grade students in the selected
classes. The survey was repeated in May 2016 and May 2017, using the same methodology and assessment tools to track the students
longitudinally. After accounting for sample attrition and missing data, the final dataset included complete observations for 3424
students across three waves of data collection, yielding a total of 10,272 valid survey responses for analysis. The average age at
baseline was 10.5 years, and approximately 49.6% were boys (details in the results). We obtained written informed consent from all
respondents, granted by one of their parents or legal guardians.
2.2. Instruments
2.2.1. Assessment of students' reading skills
Each student participated in a 30-minute standardized reading test and completed an eight-page questionnaire. To assess students'
reading achievement, we employed test items and questionnaire components from the Progress in International Reading Literacy
Study (PIRLS) 2011 (Mullis et al., 2012). PIRLS, developed by the International Association for the Assessment of Educational
Achievement (IEA), evaluates fourth-grade students' reading proficiency across 45 countries and regions with varying levels of economic development.
For this study, we developed a reading test by referencing the PIRLS item bank while aligning it with China’s primary school
curriculum standards. Instead of directly translating the original PIRLS test, we designed the assessment to ensure content validity and
contextual relevance. A panel of subject-matter experts and experienced primary school teachers was invited to develop the test items,
ensuring that they maintained equivalence with PIRLS in terms of cognitive demand and assessment objectives. The finalized test was
subsequently piloted in multiple rural schools to evaluate its reliability and appropriateness before formal implementation.
Students' reading achievement was analyzed not only in terms of overall performance but also across four key dimensions: retrieval
ability, inferential ability, integration ability and evaluation ability. These dimensions reflect different cognitive processes in reading
comprehension and provide a nuanced understanding of students' reading skills (Roey, 2012).
2.2.2. Creative attitude measurement
Children’s creative attitudes were assessed using the Creativity Attitude Survey (CAS), originally developed by Schaefer and
Bridges (1970) as an attitudinal instrument rather than a performance-based creativity test. Designed for students in Grades 4 - 6, the
CAS consists of 20 dichotomous items (1 = “yes”, 0 = “no”) that tap children’s curiosity, imaginative inclination, and openness to novel
ideas. Some of these items require reverse encoding. Example items include “I like to take my time and think up a number of ideas
before trying to solve a problem” and “I think children can write good poetry.” Higher scores indicate a more positive creative attitude.
Prior studies have shown that the CAS is suitable for use with primary-school students in China (Wang et al., 2022), and recent work
has reaffirmed its conceptual coherence and factorial stability in diverse primary-school populations (Weiss et al., 2024). In this study,
minor linguistic refinements were made to improve clarity for rural students, and a small pilot test was conducted to ensure
comprehensibility. The scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency across survey waves (Cronbach’s α = 0.74 - 0.80), and
exploratory factor analysis supported a unidimensional factor structure, with all item loadings exceeding 0.40, indicating adequate
construct validity. The full instrument is presented in the Supplementary Material.
2.2.3. Student and household characteristics
In addition to the reading test, students completed a structured questionnaire designed to collect information on their personal and
family backgrounds, reading behaviors, and peer interactions. The questionnaire included demographic variables such as gender (male
= 1), age (in years), and boarding status (boarding = 1). To assess students' socioeconomic backgrounds, we collected data on parental
education, recording whether at least one parent had attained junior high school and above (junior high school and above = 1).
Household economic status was measured using a household assets index, constructed based on seven items, each coded as a binary
variable ("yes" = 1, "no" = 0), which was then subjected to dimensionality reduction using principal component analysis to construct
the household asset index. Additionally, we captured classroom resources by recording the presence of a classroom book corner (yes =
1, no = 0), which serves as a proxy for access to reading materials within the learning environment.
To examine students' reading behaviors and peer interactions, we included measures of reading engagement and social reading
habits. Students reported how frequently they discussed books, read with friends, and read books recommended by peers, with responses recorded on a four-point scale (1 = never, 2 = a few times a year, 3 = 1–2 times a month, 4 = at least once a week).

2.2.4. School and teacher characteristics
To capture variations in school and teacher characteristics, additional data were collected through principal and teacher questionnaires. School principals provided information on the availability of a school library (1= yes), which is considered an indicator of
students' access to reading materials and opportunities for book borrowing. The study also examined teacher characteristics by collecting data from Chinese language teachers in each sampled class. Specifically, teachers reported their gender (1= male), educational
attainment (1= college degree or above), teaching experience (1= >10 years), professional title (1= second grade or higher), and class
size.
2.2.5. Creative attitude-related mechanisms
The Creative Attitude-related mechanisms were measured using constructs widely adopted in educational psychology and literacy
research, including self-efficacy, reading behavior, and comprehension and logical abilities.
To measure self-efficacy, we focus on students’ reading confidence, which was assessed using the Students’ Confident in Reading
Scale (SCR). This scale consists of seven items, each rated on a four-point Likert scale (0= Disagree a lot, 1= Disagree a little, 2= Agree
a little, 3= Agree a lot), with reverse coding applied where appropriate. Example items include “I usually do well in reading” and
“Reading is easy for me.” The final score represents the sum of all items, capturing students’ overall confidence in reading. The SCR was
adapted from the PIRLS framework and has been widely applied in studies among Chinese elementary students (Gao et al., 2020; Wang
et al., 2020). The scale demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency in our sample (Cronbach’s α= 0.72–0.76 across three survey
waves), consistent with previous reports (α= 0.69–0.74). In addition, students were asked to self-assess whether they considered
themselves imaginative (1= Yes), which was included as an additional indicator of self-efficacy. The full instrument is provided in the
Supplementary Material.
To assess reading behavior, four indicators were recorded: the frequency of sharing reading experiences with peers, the frequency
of reading with friends, and the frequency of reading books recommended by friends, following (Yi et al., 2018). Each indicator was
measured on a four-point frequency scale (1= Never, 2 = A few times a year, 3= Once or twice a month, 4= At least once a week). In
addition, average daily time spent on video games was included as a negative behavioral indicator (1 = 0–15 min, 2 = 16–30 min, 3 =
31–59 min, 4= >60 min) to capture potential substitution effects of digital entertainment on reading.
Measures of comprehension and logical abilities were developed in line with the educational production function framework
(Hanushek, 1979; Shepherd, 2011), which conceptualizes reading proficiency as a key cognitive input to creative attitude development. Two variables were used to capture these abilities. First, reading speed was derived from students’ self-reported reading time for
a complete book, with the reciprocal of time used as the performance indicator (faster reading reflecting higher comprehension efficiency). Second, standardized mathematics test scores (normalized within the sample) were included as a proxy for logical reasoning,
as mathematical problem solving reflects core cognitive processes closely associated with creative thinking. Together, these measures
provide a comprehensive view of the cognitive pathways through which reading achievement may promote creative attitudes.
2.3. Procedure
The data collection procedure was standardized across the three survey waves. All enumerators participated in a centralized
training workshop that covered test administration procedures, standardized instructions, and ethical guidelines. Trained enumerators
administered all student assessments and questionnaires during regular school hours in classroom settings. Teachers and principals
completed structured questionnaires separately, with enumerators providing assistance when needed. All instruments were piloted in
advance to ensure clarity and reliability, and the same procedures were followed in each year to maintain comparability over time.
During each testing session, all students completed the measurement instruments in a fixed order: (1) the standardized reading
assessment, (2) the student questionnaire, and (3) the Creativity Attitude Survey (CAS). This sequence was identical for every student
and school. The fixed order was chosen for two practical reasons: the reading test was placed first to ensure students’ optimal concentration, while the questionnaire and attitudinal scale were administered afterward to avoid interference with cognitive performance. Because all students followed the same sequence, any potential order effects are held constant across the sample rather than
introducing systematic bias.
2.4. Research design
This study employed a longitudinal panel design using three waves of data collected between 2015 and 2017. The primary
empirical strategy was a two-way fixed effects (FE) model, which controlled for school-level and year-level unobserved heterogeneity.
By including both school and time fixed effects, the model mitigates potential biases arising from unobserved time-invariant characteristics and time-varying shocks. The empirical specification is as follows:

2.5. Data analysis
When examining the impact of reading on creative attitude, two key endogeneity concerns arise. The first is reverse causality,
where creative attitude itself may influence reading habits or reading performance, rather than reading driving creative attitude. For
instance, students with a more positive creative attitude might develop a preference for particular types of reading materials, affecting
both the volume and nature of their reading activities. The second concern is unobserved heterogeneity, where individual creative
attitude and reading performance may be simultaneously influenced by shared but unobservable factors, such as educational environment, family background, and social context. These omitted variables may introduce bias into the estimation.

In addition to the instrumental variable method, we conducted additional robustness tests to further reduce potential bias.
Considering that creative attitude and reading performance may have a bidirectional causal relationship, we introduced lagged
variables into the analysis to clarify the causal direction by comparing current creative attitude with previous reading performance.
Including past reading scores reduces the interference of existing creative attitude levels on the estimation of creative attitude's impact
on reading. Additionally, lagged variables help distinguish between short-term and long-term effects, revealing the dynamic adjustment process of creative attitude. Immediate reading performance may reflect instant learning outcomes, while lagged reading variables capture the cumulative and enduring effects of reading on creative attitude development.
By integrating two-way fixed effects analysis, instrumental variable estimation, and lagged-variable analyses, we developed an
identification strategy that reduces endogeneity bias to some extent and provides plausible and robust evidence on the relationship
between reading and creative attitude, while stopping short of establishing definitive causal proof.

4. Discussion
The importance of nurturing reading skills among individuals stands as a cornerstone for ensuring comprehensive educational
access and plays a pivotal role in promoting creative attitude—an essential trait for fostering innovation and personal development.
However, students in developing regions, particularly those in rural areas, often lack the resources and opportunities needed to
cultivate their creative attitude. In this context, reading may emerge as an effective and critical means to foster creative attitude.
Our study explores the relationship between reading and creative attitude, emphasizing the essential role that reading plays in
stimulating imaginative cognition and problem-solving abilities. Using three-period longitudinal panel data from 3424 rural Chinese
students, we first outlined the basic characteristics of the sample and then examined the impact of standardized reading scores on
creative attitude. We conducted a series of robustness tests and subsequently performed mechanism and heterogeneity analyses.
Our findings indicate that reading achievement exerts a significant positive influence on rural students' creative attitudes. While
prior research has established a positive correlation between reading and creativity, these studies did not specifically examine the
element of creative attitudes (Marcos et al., 2020; Ritchie et al., 2013; Wang, 2012). This study focuses on this aspect, offering supplementary evidence from the perspective of creative attitudes. It provides a fresh viewpoint and empirical data, thereby enriching the
research outcomes in this field. Furthermore, when reading skills were categorized into four distinct dimensions—retrieval ability,
inference ability, integration ability, and evaluation ability—each dimension was found to facilitate creative attitude. Specifically,
retrieval ability enabled students to efficiently access the knowledge they needed from various information sources, which fed their
creative attitude (Miroshnik et al., 2023); inference ability helped students logically analyze problems and develop new ideas and
solutions (Bayat & Çetinkaya, 2020); integration ability allowed students to combine knowledge from different fields to generate novel
concepts and approaches (Neumann et al., 2020); and evaluation ability taught students to critically examine established ideas,
challenge them, and optimize them, which continuously nurtured their creative attitude (Bakı, 2020).
Heterogeneity analysis suggested that the benefits of reading are amplified in supportive environments where reading is prioritized
and facilitated. First, participation in structured reading courses and reading-related activities is critical in developing reading and
critical thinking skills. Through teacher guidance and peer interactions, students not only refine reading strategies but also engage with
texts more deeply (Daniel et al., 2024; Hasnani & Ismail, 2020), which significantly enhances their creative attitudes. This highlights
the importance of structured, collaborative learning environments encouraging diverse perspectives and active intellectual engagement. Second, the family reading environment and personal reading spaces are key contributors to fostering creative attitude. Active
parental involvement in reading, such as through parent-child reading sessions, nurtures curiosity and imagination, creating a positive
atmosphere for creative exploration (Chiu & McBride-Chang, 2010). Additionally, personal reading spaces provide students with the
quiet and independence needed for deep, reflective reading, which improves sustained creative attitude. These factors underscore the
value of both familial and personal spaces for reading as essential in developing creative attitude. Finally, economically privileged
students have more variety reading materials (Zhang, 2025), which enhances cognitive growth. Access expands their intellectual
horizons, fostering creative attitude and critical thinking. Stronger reading abilities help children understand, evaluate, and take
inspiration from literature, which enhances creative attitude and innovation.
This study highlights the significant impact of reading on creative attitude in rural Chinese students, identifying three key
mechanisms: self-efficacy, reading behavior, and comprehension and logical abilities. Self-efficacy, a crucial factor in linking reading
proficiency to creative attitude, grows as students’ reading skills improve. With increased confidence in their ability to tackle reading
tasks, students engage more deeply, which in turn boosts both their cognitive and creative attitude. Research shows that higher selfconfidence is linked to greater involvement in creative problem-solving (Du et al., 2020; Stolz et al., 2022), and our findings confirm
that students with enhanced self-efficacy through reading tend to exhibit more positive attitudes towards creativity. This underlines
the importance of fostering reading skills to support cognitive and creative attitude growth, especially in resource-limited environments. In addition, reading habits contribute significantly to creative attitude development. When students engage in collaborative
reading activities, they exchange diverse ideas with peers, sparking creative thought (Catarino et al., 2019). Furthermore, as students
improve their reading abilities, they prefer immersive reading over passive activities like video games (Robinson & Borzekowski, 2006;
Savić Tot et al., 2023). This shift fosters cognitive growth and creative attitude development, emphasizing the need for educational
settings that promote both independent and collaborative reading. Finally, comprehension and logical abilities are integral to creative
thinking. As reading proficiency increases, students are able to process information more effectively, expanding their knowledge base
and fueling creative thought (Beydogan, 2012; Rezaee & Nourzadeh, 2011). Integrating insights from diverse sources fosters cognitive
flexibility, a key component of creative attitude. Our findings suggest that stronger reading skills enable students to navigate multiple
disciplines more efficiently, enhancing their ability to generate new ideas and solutions. Additionally, advanced reading skills improve
logical reasoning, particularly in subjects like mathematics, facilitating innovative problem-solving (Can, 2020; Vista, 2013).
Despite the valuable insights this study provides, several limitations should be acknowledged to guide the interpretation of the
findings and inform future research. First, the sample is restricted to rural areas in Jiangxi Province, which raises concerns about the
external validity of the results when generalizing to other rural regions of China. Replication in different provinces and contexts would
strengthen the robustness of the conclusions. Second, due to data constraints, we were unable to differentiate between types of reading
materials. Future studies could refine the classification of reading resources to assess whether different genres or formats exert distinct

influences on creative attitude. Moreover, although we employed several strategies to mitigate bias, endogeneity concerns cannot be
fully resolved, and this study does not achieve perfect causal inference. Future research could extend this work by examining nonlinear
relationships in greater depth and by employing randomized controlled trial designs, which would help move beyond the correlational
evidence provided here toward stronger causal claims. In addition, one procedural limitation is that the measurement instruments
were administered in a fixed order. Although this order was consistent for all students, minimizing differential order effects, it remains
possible that the sequence may have influenced absolute levels of self-reported attitudes.
A methodological limitation of the present study concerns the use of the CAS, which assesses creative attitudes rather than creative
performance or divergent thinking ability. Although attitudinal measures are theoretically meaningful and strongly associated with
creativity development—especially in middle childhood—future research would benefit from combining attitudinal assessments with
performance-based tasks such as the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking (TTCT). Nevertheless, attitudinal dispositions such as curiosity, imaginative interest, and openness to novelty are widely recognized as foundational psychological precursors of creative
development, and thus the CAS provides a theoretically grounded and empirically informative indicator in the context of large-scale
school-based studies.
Our study contributes to the existing literature in several important ways. While much research has focused on factors influencing
student creativity, few studies have specifically explored the impact of reading on creative attitude, particularly in rural settings where
opportunities for creative attitude development are limited. This study employs a robust two-way fixed effects model, controlling for
year and school fixed effects, and uses a comprehensive identification strategy to address potential endogeneity concerns, ensuring the
reliability of the results. Additionally, the study offers a detailed examination of the mechanisms through which reading influences
creative attitude, providing a more nuanced understanding of how reading can foster creative attitude development. By systematically
examining both mechanisms and heterogeneity, our study not only confirms the general link between reading and creative attitude but
also identifies the conditions under which this relationship is most pronounced, thus contributing to the broader literature on creative
attitude development.
Creative attitude development in children from resource-constrained regions of developing countries presents a widespread
challenge. Limited access to educational resources, inadequate infrastructure, and a lack of support for innovative teaching methods
hinder the cultivation of imaginative and problem-solving skills, essential for holistic development and future success. Our study in
rural China highlights how reading, particularly in environments with limited resources, can play a crucial role in enhancing creative
attitude. The positive relationship between reading and creative attitude underscores the importance of promoting creative reading
practices, such as reading clubs and workshops, to stimulate students' imagination and innovative thinking.
Given the challenges in regions with limited educational resources, leveraging community and peer networks can be a key strategy
for fostering reading and creative attitude. Policymakers and educators must prioritize investments in expanding access to quality
reading materials, supporting teacher development, and creating environments conducive to creative attitude growth. The insights
from our study offer valuable guidance for similar contexts globally, demonstrating that prioritizing reading can unlock students'
potential and help them become innovative thinkers who contribute meaningfully to their societies.
Beyond its practical significance, this study also makes theoretical contributions to the literature on the reading–creative attitude
nexus. By drawing on longitudinal data from under-resourced rural China, our findings extend existing theories of creative attitude
development to new populations and contexts, demonstrating that the positive effects of reading on creative attitude are not confined
to high-resource educational environments. Moreover, the identification of self-efficacy, reading behavior, and comprehension/logical
skills as key mediating mechanisms provides a more nuanced theoretical framework for understanding how literacy enhances creative
attitude. Future research could further enrich this framework by incorporating socio-emotional factors, examining classroom interaction dynamics, or applying alternative methodological approaches (e.g., experimental or qualitative designs) to triangulate and
deepen our understanding of these processes.
