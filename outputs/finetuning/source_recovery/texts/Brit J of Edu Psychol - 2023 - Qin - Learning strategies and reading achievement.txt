Abstract
Background: Learning is a self-­regulated loop where learning strategies and achievements are interrelated. In reading,
although some studies have explored the relationship between different learning strategies (memorization, elaboration and control) and reading achievement, little is known
about how they interact over time. Even though the longitudinal relationship was revealed in some studies, most of the
evidence was based on the whole population, regardless of
gender differences.
Aims: This study was designed to examine the longitudinal relationship between memorization, elaboration, control
strategies and reading achievement, as well as the gender
difference in the longitudinal relationship.
Sample: The sample consisted of 3878 Chinese students
(2025 boys, 1853 girls) who were surveyed in Grade 4 and
Grade 6.
Methods: A cross-­lagged model was conducted to examine
the longitudinal relationship between memorization, elaboration, control strategies and reading achievement while
controlling for gender, age and parents' educational levels.
Multigroup cross-­lagged models were conducted to examine
gender differences in the longitudinal relationship between
these variables.
Results: Memorization and elaboration strategies were reciprocally related. Both predicted subsequent control strategies,
but not vice versa. Only memorization strategies positively
predicted subsequent reading achievement, while prior reading achievement positively predicted the subsequent three
strategies. The effects of prior reading achievement on subsequent learning strategies were stronger for boys.
Conclusions: Memorization strategies play a prominent
role in promoting deeper strategies and reading achievement in Chinese primary schools, which might relate to

Reading is a basic subject that not only stimulates language and literacy development (Wasik &
Bond, 2001) but also lays the foundation for learning in other domains (Adelson et al., 2015). Studies
have explored the learning process in reading; among these studies, self-­regulated learning (SRL) has received increasing focus (Dent & Koenka, 2016; Lau & Ho, 2016). SRL depicts learning as a self-­d irected
loop in which learning strategies are important cognitive factors (Zimmerman, 2001). In SRL, students
not only use learning strategies to improve their reading achievement but also evaluate and adjust these
strategies in response to feedback; for example, students may increase or reduce the use of one strategy
according to previous achievement (Braund & DeLuca, 2018; Butler & Winne, 1995). Thus, it is speculated that learning strategies and reading achievement are interrelated.
Relying on the framework of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), we focused mainly on the following learning strategies from the low to the high level: memorization, elaboration and control (OECD, 2009). Memorization possesses knowledge through repetitive exposure to
study materials (Weinstein & Mayer, 1986); examples of such strategies include rereading the text, reciting details and rehearsing key points (Weinstein et al., 2010). Elaboration connects what students are
learning with their prior knowledge or experience by modifying materials according to their personal
understanding (Weinstein et al., 2011). Elaboration strategies are applied to tasks such as paraphrasing, creating an analogy or relating the complex structure of texts to existing knowledge (Weinstein
& Mayer, 1986). Control, a much higher-­order strategy, enables students to plan what they need to
learn and to regularly check their mastery of the content during learning (Pintrich & De Groot, 1990;
Schneider, 2008). Self-­questioning is one of the most common control strategies. When reading a text,
students monitor their understanding and search for information to address unsolved problems by self-questioning (Weinstein & Mayer, 1986).
As learners grow older and become more competent, the use of all three learning strategies increases,
along with the improvement in reading achievement (Bouffard, 1998; Pintrich & Zusho, 2002; Waters
& Andreassen, 1983). This synchronized increase leads to speculation about the reciprocal relationship
between memorization, elaboration, control strategies and reading achievement. Although many studies
have explored the relationship between these variables, the findings are mixed (Li & Chun, 2012; Tavsancil et al., 2019), and little is known about how they interact in the long term. Furthermore, gender
difference is a persistent topic in reading (Machin & Pekkarinen, 2008). However, past attempts to
examine the relationship between these variables were based mainly on the whole population instead of
taking gender difference into account. Therefore, to check for possible different reciprocal relationships
between these variables for boys and girls, we probed further into the relationships for different genders.

understanding would make it challenging to convert the knowledge into long-­term memory and
would hamper higher-­order reading (Biggs, 1993). In these cases, memorization is less effective

Students who reported repetitively reciting the text more frequently often scored lower in reading
(Chiu et al., 2007). However, Eastern Asian students might benefit more due to the emphasis on
memorization in reading instruction as well as their different implementations of memorization by
using it with deep understanding, for example, imaging the pictures when reciting the poems instead
of repeating contents mechanically (Biggs, 1998; Kember, 2000). As Li and Chun (2012) suggested,
more use of memorization was related to better reading achievement among 15-­year-­old Hong Kong
students.
Empirical studies found that in most countries, elaboration strategies were positively related to students' concurrent and subsequent reading achievement in both primary and secondary schools (Li &
Chun, 2012; Qi, 2020; Santiago et al., 2008; Strømsø et al., 2003). The effective use of elaboration
involves students in deep cognitive activities (Walker et al., 2006) and foster students creation of meaningful representations of materials, which has to do with the interpretation and efficient retrieval of
the information (Mayer, 1980; Reder, 1980). However, other studies sampling secondary school students across over 30 countries showed the effects of elaboration on reading were nonsignificant (Callan
et al., 2017; Chiu et al., 2007). It was explained that without explicit guidance, elaboration strategies may
not be well applied for knowledge transfer in different situations (Chiu et al., 2007; Garner, 1990).
Since control strategies facilitate students to identify and fill knowledge gaps, studies consistently
suggest the conducive effects of control strategies on reading achievement across 12–­16-­age students
(Callan et al., 2017; Chiu et al., 2007; Chuy & Nitulescu, 2013; Santiago et al., 2008; Strømsø et al., 2003;
Tavsancil et al., 2019). In a meta-­analysis, Sun et al. (2021) reviewed studies from 1999 to 2019 and found
that control strategies positively predicted reading achievement, though larger effect sizes were revealed
in Western countries than in Eastern countries (Lee, 2014).
Other studies, in turn, focused on the strategy profiles of high achievers in reading and found that in
almost all age groups (from primary school to college), students with higher reading achievement used
a wider variety of strategies at a higher frequency (Karomah, 2017; Zare & Othman, 2013). For high-achieving readers, specific strategies—­including memorization (through repetition and notetaking) and
elaboration—­were employed frequently (Karomah, 2017), and more control strategies were also applied
to monitor their learning (Dermitzaki et al., 2008). Longitudinal studies targeted at primary students
also supported the predictive effects of reading achievement on subsequent control strategies (Braund &
DeLuca, 2018; Connor et al., 2015; Roebers & Spiess, 2017). High achievers used strategies more often
not only because they were skilled (i.e., had higher previous knowledge about strategies) but also because
they had received positive feedback from their previous reading achievement (Butler & Winne, 1995).

The relationship between memorization, elaboration and control strategies
The high correlations between memorization, elaboration and control strategies (Lau & Ho, 2016;
Qi, 2020) and the development of these strategies during school years have been revealed by existing
studies (Pressley, 1982). These correlations raised the question of how memorization, elaboration and
control strategies interact over time.
The Model of Domain Learning (MDL; Alexander, 1997, 2004) proposed the premise that lower-level strategies are precursors of higher-­level strategies. Memorization and elaboration are believed to
be the basic and lower-­level strategies, and control strategies are higher-­order. According to the MDL,
memorization and elaboration normally occur earlier and provide foundations for developing control
strategies, which are always latecomers (Alexander, 2004; Baker, 2005). Students may be better at using

Other studies have proposed the opposite hypothesis that prior higher-­level strategies may orient the
subsequent use of basic strategies (Akturk & Sahin, 2011). When learners are equipped with more control strategies, their memorization and elaboration strategies can be better implemented. Noticeably, the
two propositions are not mutually exclusive. The two basic strategies are easy to learn and thus appear
earlier; however, the use of control strategies promotes the effectiveness of using the two strategies. In
this case, considering the above-­d iscussed relationship between strategies and reading achievement, we
speculated that memorization, elaboration, control strategies and reading achievement are reciprocally
related in the long term.

Gender differences in reading
Gender differences in reading are an important issue (OECD, 2014, 2016, 2019; Pekkarinen, 2012).
International surveys revealed that, cross-­nationally, girls outperformed boys in reading (Machin &
Pekkarinen, 2008). Using nationally representative samples from the National Child Development
Study (NCDS) and the British Cohort Study (BCS), two well-­k nown cohort studies, revealed a significant gender gap in favour of girls in reading from preschool to high school (Cavaglia et al., 2020; Machin
& McNally, 2006), though the gender gap narrowed with age (Feingold, 1988).
Boys' backwardness in reading may be related to their lagged developmental trajectories of learning
strategies (Chuy & Nitulescu, 2013). Evidence has suggested an earlier cognitive maturation for girls
(Dwyer, 1973). In the early school years, girls have already shown a more extensive use of learning strategies, while boys eventually catch up with the gender gap (Griva et al., 2012; Kolić-­Vehovec et al., 2010).
In high school, gender differences in learning strategies were negligible (Chuy & Nitulescu, 2013; Kolić-Vehovec et al., 2010). The closing gender gap in reading achievement and strategies may be because of
boys' improvement of metacognitive awareness and SRL skills that strengthen the relationship between
learning strategies and reading achievement (Chuy & Nitulescu, 2013; Winne, 1996), especially in upper
primary school, where intensive developmental changes occur (Kolić-­Vehovec et al., 2010). As Zimmerman (2002) proposed, SRL might be a way to compensate for students' individual differences. As boys
learned to reflect on their learning process through SRL, they consciously used learning strategies more
frequently, which in the long run decreased the achievement gap (Zimmerman, 2002). However, empirical evidence is lacking about whether gender differences exist in the longitudinal relationship between
learning strategies and reading achievement and whether the differences in the relationship contribute
to a narrower gender gap.

The present study
In this study, memorization, elaboration, control strategies and reading achievement were measured
in Grades 4 and 6, two representative phases of the middle and later primary school years in China.
Based on the theoretical framework and empirical evidence, we first investigated the relationship between memorization, elaboration, control strategies and reading achievement. Expanding on previous
research that relied mainly on cross-­sectional data, our goal was to understand the longitudinal relationship between these variables. Second, we considered the gender differences in the longitudinal relationship from Grade 4 to Grade 6. The specific research questions were proposed as follows:
1. What is the longitudinal relationship between memorization, elaboration, control strategies, and
reading achievement from Grades 4 to 6?
2. How are the longitudinal relationships further differentiated by gender?

M E T HOD S
Participants and procedure
Two sampling time points of this longitudinal study were Grade 4 ([T1]) and Grade 6 ([T2]). A total of
3886 students from 99 schools (whole class sampling, one or two classes sampled in each school) in four
Chinese provinces participated in the first measurement. Schools were stratified based on their location
(city, town or rural area). A total of 1163 students withdrew at T2. Among these students, 447 from 13
small-­sized schools were absent at follow-­up due to their being moved to other larger schools and thus
having difficulty tracking them at T2. The remaining students were either absent on testing days due to
illness, unwillingness to continue participating in the study, or because they had transferred to a different school or a different class in the same school.
There were no significant differences in memorization [t(3, 802) = .602, p > .05], elaboration [t(3,
790) = 1.163, p > .05] or control strategies [t(3, 790) = 1.500, p > .05]. Although a significant difference
existed in reading achievement, the effect size was relatively small [t(3, 840) = 3.074, p < .01, d = .10]
(Cohen, 1988). Therefore, the missing data are believed to stem from administrative unavailability
rather than student characteristics. There were eight students whose gender information was missing.
The final analysis sample contained 3878 students (2025 boys, 1853 girls), whose mean age was 10.76
(SD = .89) and 12.43 (SD = 1.16) at two waves.
Informed consent was obtained from the principals, parents and students at participating schools.
They were informed of their voluntary and anonymous participation and their right to withdraw. Questionnaire completion time was averaged 15 min, and reading achievement was assessed within 40 min.

Measures
In the study, the reading achievement test was self-­developed, and scales for learning strategies were
adapted from PISA2009 (OECD, 2009). To ensure the applicability of the instrument, a double translation was used for linguistic equivalence and a pilot study was conducted. Students were asked to indicate how often they use the strategies on a four-­point Likert scale ranging from ‘1’ (almost never) to ‘4’
(almost always).

Reading achievement
Referring to the Reading Curriculum Standards for Compulsory Education (2011 Version; Ministry of Education, MOE, 2011), the reading achievement test contained 33 multiple-­choice items in basic Chinese
language knowledge (e.g., Which sentence uses the wrong conjunction?) and reading comprehension
(e.g., Which sentence best summarizes the main content of the first paragraph?). A correct response to
each item counts for 1 point, and an incorrect response counts for 0 point. The test demonstrated good
internal consistency at two measurements with Cronbach's alpha coefficients of .77 and .83. The Rasch
model was used to link the scores of two measurements to an identical scale by the ‘TAM’ package in
R 4.2.2, and students' reading achievement was estimated by Expected a Posterior Estimation (EAP).
The mean square (MNSQ) statistics for all items at the two time points ranged from .841 to 1.140,
which was acceptable (Bond & Fox, 2007).

Memorization strategies
The memorization strategies consisted of four items (e.g., ‘When I study, I read the text over and over
again’). The coefficient alpha reliability estimates were acceptable at two waves, with values of .70 and .82.

Elaboration strategies
The elaboration strategies consisted of four items (e.g., ‘When I study, I try to understand the material
better by relating it to my own experiences’). The coefficient alpha reliability estimates were acceptable
at two waves, with values of .65 and .81.

Control strategies
The control strategies consisted of five items (e.g., ‘When I study, I check if I understand what I
have read’). The coefficient alpha reliability estimates were acceptable at two waves, with values of
.65 and .83.

Demographic variables
Students' gender, age and parents' educational levels were collected. The index of parents' educational
levels was averaged by the values reported by fathers and mothers (‘1’ = did not complete primary school,
‘2’ = primary school, ‘3’ = lower secondary, ‘4’ = upper secondary, ‘5’ = university or post-­g raduate).

Data analysis
The descriptive statistics and correlations for memorization, elaboration, control strategies and reading
achievement at two waves were estimated using SPSS 25.0. t-­Tests were conducted to compare gender
differences in the mean levels of these variables.
A cross-­lagged model was specified by Mplus 8.3 (Muthén & Muthén, 2017) to examine the longitudinal relationship between memorization, elaboration, control strategies and reading achievement.
In the model, the mean scores of the memorization, elaboration and control strategies were used. The
variables at T1 were correlated, as were the residual errors at T2. The demographic variables were controlled in the model.
A multigroup cross-­lagged model was then conducted to explore whether the longitudinal relationship differed by gender. In the unconstrained model, all parameters were allowed to vary across groups.
To identify gender differences, nested model comparisons were employed. The unconstrained model
was compared with a set of more parsimonious models in which the constraints of equal path coefficients between two groups were imposed one by one. A significant Satorra-­Bentler scaled chi-­square
difference (Δχ 2) between the constrained and unconstrained models indicates a gender difference. In
the final model, paths with gender differences were allowed to vary across the two groups, and others
were constrained to be equal.
All analyses were conducted using the robust maximum likelihood estimator (MLR), which is
robust to nonnormality observations (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). For all the models, the full information maximum likelihood (FIML) was used to handle the missing data; the FIML is flexible to
use all available data to obtain unbiased and efficient estimates (Newman, 2003; Newsom, 2015). For
the hierarchical data structure, the ‘type = complex’ option was set in Mplus. Model fit was assessed
by the chi-­square statistic ( χ 2), the Tucker–­L ewis index (TLI), the comparative fit index (CFI), the
root-­mean-­square error of approximation (RMSEA) and the standardized root-­mean-­square residual (SRMR). For the CFI and TLI, values above .90 are widely considered to denote an acceptable
fit (Bentler & Bonett, 1980). For the RMSEA and SRMR, values less than .05 indicated a good fit
(Schermelleh-­Engel et al., 2003).

Descriptive analysis
The correlations between the scores of the memorization, elaboration, control strategies and reading
achievement, and the means [M] and standard deviations [SD] of these variables were presented in
Table 1. The correlations among the two waves of main variables were positive in the .16–­.81 range.
The M, SD and correlations among the main variables by gender were presented in Table 2. t-­tests
were conducted to compare boys and girls on the use of strategies and reading achievement. The results
showed that compared with boys, girls used more memorization [t(3, 794) = 7.36, p < .001, d = .24], elaboration [t(3, 782) = 3.63, p < .01, d = .12] and control strategies [t(3, 782) = 5.70, p < .001, d = .17] in Grade
4, but there were no gender differences in the use of the three learning strategies in Grade 6. In addition,
girls scored higher on reading achievement at both time points [T1: t(3, 832) = 10.32, p < .001, d = .34;
T2: t(2, 716) = 10.07, p < .001, d = .39].

Longitudinal relationship between learning strategies and reading
achievement
The cross-­lagged model fit the data well: χ2(14) = 102.739; CFI = .992; TLI = .965; RMSEA = .040
(90% CI: .033–­.048); SRMR = .027. As Figure 1 showed, students' prior reading achievement significantly predicted the use of memorization, elaboration and control strategies at a later time (β = .223,
p < .001/β = .187, p < .001/β = .192, p < .001). However, in turn, only prior memorization strategies significantly predicted subsequent reading achievement (β = .138, p < .001).
Regarding relationships within learning strategies, reciprocal relations were revealed between memorization and elaboration strategies (β = .137, p < .001/β = .082, p < .01). Prior memorization and elaboration strategies significantly predicted subsequent control strategies (β = .159, p < .001/β = .100, p < .001),
while prior control strategies failed to predict later memorization or elaboration strategies (β = .004,
p > .05/β = .006, p > .05).

Gender differences in the longitudinal relationship
To determine whether the longitudinal relationship differed by gender, the cross-­lagged model was run
using multisample path analysis. The model fitted the data well: χ2(22) = 110.644; CFI = .992; TLI = .962;
RMSEA = .046 (90% CI: .037–­.054); SRMR = .031.

DIS C US SION
Although extensive studies have been carried out on the relationship between learning strategies and
reading achievement, most of them have used cross-­sectional designs. Even if the longitudinal relationship was discussed, gender differences were neglected. Our study attempts to enrich our understanding of the longitudinal relationship among memorization, elaboration, control strategies and reading
achievement with consideration of gender differences.

Longitudinal relationship between memorization, elaboration, control
strategies and reading achievement
Longitudinal relationship between learning strategies and reading achievement
This study provided insights into how learning strategies interact with reading achievement over a 2-­year
period. Inconsistent with previous findings (Chuy & Nitulescu, 2013; Tavsancil et al., 2019), memorization
was revealed as the only predominant strategy that positively influenced subsequent reading achievement.
This finding may be explained by the different perceptions and implementations of memorization among
students in different cultural contexts. Western students related memorization to ‘pure remembering’, while
Chinese students linked it more with ‘deep processing’ (Biggs, 1998). The complexity of memorization used
by Chinese students when combined with understanding has been illustrated in previous studies (Dahlin
& Watkins, 2000; Kember, 2000, 2016). For example, memorizing Chinese characters effectively might

It seemed that Chinese students distinguished passive memorization from active memorization associated

The predominant role of memorization in Chinese primary students is also related to language characteristics and developmental stages. Chinese is a no-­inflectional language without a normative grammatical system (Ho et al., 2011). Chinese reading comprehension strongly depends on the language context
(Shu et al., 1995). Teachers believe that reciting the poems or passages is a basic way to improving students' language sense, increasing their reading fluency and comprehension in Chinese texts (Cheung &
Ng, 2003; Reutzel & Hollingsworth, 1993). Moreover, memorizing domain-­specific knowledge also helps
in comprehending the literary allusions in Chinese reading (Hu, 2004), especially in primary schools.
Different from previous PISA studies that sampled 15-­year-­old students and used tasks in interpreting
and evaluating information from full texts (OECD, 2009), our sample was retrieved in primary schools,
the initial stage of expertise development. To complete reading tasks, students might often benefit from
using basic strategies, as described in MDL (Alexander, 2003; Dinsmore & Alexander, 2016). In practice,
memorization is usually the first step that provides possibilities for expertise development in later phases
(Alexander, 2003). As was clearly required in the Chinese Curriculum Standards for Full-­Time Compulsory Education, the memorization tasks for Chinese primary students involve accumulating characters, vocabulary
and even sentences, which are the foundations for later reading achievement (MOE, 2011, 2022).

Longitudinal relationship between memorization, elaboration and control strategies
The reciprocal relationship between memorization and elaboration strategies was demonstrated. Positive
correlations between the two strategies have been found in previous studies (Chuy & Nitulescu, 2013;
Qi, 2020). This study thus expanded on previous findings that have thus far been obtained mainly by
investigating the longitudinal relationship between memorization and elaboration, enriching empirical
evidence from a developmental perspective.
Moreover, memorization and elaboration in Grade 4 positively predicted control strategies in Grade
6, but not vice versa. The results corresponded to the MDL (Alexander, 1997, 2004), which states that
strategies at the lower levels provide foundations for strategies at the higher levels, and the higher-­level
strategies rely on the lower-­level ones. It is reasonable to assume that memorization and elaboration

Gender differences in reading
Consistent with previous findings, girls had noticeable advantages in reading achievement at both waves
(Machin & Pekkarinen, 2008; OECD, 2014, 2016, 2019; Pekkarinen, 2012). For learning strategies, girls
used the three strategies more often than boys in Grade 4, whereas gender differences disappeared in
Grade 6. Boys seemed to begin to be more aware of using learning strategies in the later primary school
year; however, this increased awareness did not result in the same amount of improvement in achievement. Concerning maturational differences, boys showed relatively delayed development in reading
(Dwyer, 1973; Wei et al., 2015). Boys may need more time to improve their achievement by using more
learning strategies. Teachers may try to pay more attention to how boys use strategies, which was not
addressed in this study. Providing support on the quality of strategies used might be useful when boys
have reported more learning strategies but have not yet observed achievement increases.
In addition, the effects of prior reading achievement on subsequent learning strategies were stronger
among boys. This finding implies that boys might be more inclined to use strategies when they perceive
that they could promote learning results. Showing them that strategy use can promote performance and
designing tasks that empower them to use strategies effectively might be meaningful. Guo (2021) found
that teachers' verification and scaffolding feedback had stronger positive influences on boys' learning
strategy use. Encouraging boys to use learning strategies more effectively by making connections between strategy use and task completion might be a way to narrow the gender gap in reading.

Implication and limitations
Our study expands on previous cross-­sectional evidence to understand the longitudinal relationship
between memorization, elaboration, control strategies and reading achievement from Grades 4 to 6. It
also contributes to understanding the gender differences in this relationship.
In practice, this study sheds light on primary students' reading learning in China. First, the role of the
memorization strategy in reading is highlighted. This strategy not only promotes students' reading achievement but also provides the foundation for developing deeper-­level strategies. For primary students who
are in the early stages of expertise development, the active use of memorization may reinforce the students'
basic knowledge and deepen their understanding, thereby preparing them for higher-­order reading tasks.
Second, the positive effects of prior reading achievement on subsequent strategies were revealed. High
achievers are more likely to use strategies more frequently, according to previous positive feedback. For low
achievers, more learning support, such as showing the connection between effective strategy use and performance enhancement, is needed to reinforce their use of effective strategies. Such support might be more
important for boys, given their stronger connections between achievement and strategies. The possible way
to close the gender gap implied by this study might be to create a virtuous circle of strategies and achievement development by providing boys with more assistance to improve their learning process from test
outcomes and by encouraging boys to use more conducive learning strategies to obtain higher achievement.
There are several limitations in the present study. First, the study only examined the frequency while
neglected the quality of strategies use; thus, it may be limited in representing students' actual process of

C ONC LUSION
This study's primary findings add to the existing research on the longitudinal relationship between learning strategies and reading achievement and the gender differences in this relationship. The findings reconfirm the reciprocal relationship between memorization and elaboration and their roles as precursors
for higher-­level control strategies, as the MDL proposed. Unlike most previous studies based on middle
school students in Western contexts, this study reveals memorization as the most significant strategy to
promote Chinese primary students' reading achievement. The different implementations of memorization, language characteristics and developmental stage should be considered. Furthermore, prior reading
achievement increased the later learning strategies used. More importantly, stronger effects of prior reading achievement on learning strategies were found for boys. The findings help disentangle the complex
relationship between learning strategies and reading achievement and identify gender differences, thereby
enriching our understanding and providing directions for improving students' learning in reading.
