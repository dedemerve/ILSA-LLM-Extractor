A B S T R A C T
According to the social agency theory (Moreno et al., 2001), the social cues of artificial 
intelligence-powered pedagogical agents (AIPAs) hold detectable potential for enhancing 
learning. However, previous studies have reported inconsistent results regarding the effectiveness 
of these social cues. This study utilized a multilevel meta-analysis to integrate the results of 33 
experimental studies on the social cues of AIPAs and explore their effectiveness. The results 
indicated that the social cues of AIPAs were associated with statistically detectable positive effects 
on cognitive learning outcomes (k = 66, g = 0.359, 95 % confidence interval (CI) =
[0.191–0.526]), affective learning outcomes (k = 85, g = 0.519, 95 % CI = [0.226–0.812]), and 
behavioral learning outcomes (k = 23, g = 0.458, 95 % CI = [0.169–0.746]). No statistically 
detectable change in cognitive load was observed (k = 11, g = 0.039, 95 % CI = [-0.153–0.230]). 
Additionally, 16 potential moderating variables related to AIPAs and non-AIPAs were identified 
and discussed. For cognitive learning outcomes, the type of the pedagogical agent (F = 5.420, p =
.007, pattern: virtual agents with visual representation > physically embodied robots > virtual 
agents without visual representation), the image of the pedagogical agent (F = 7.782, p < .001, 
pattern: anthropomorphized > semi-anthropomorphized > non-anthropomorphized), gender of 
the pedagogical agent (F = 9.309, p < .001, pattern: N/A > male > female), instructional setting 
(F = 9.869, p < .001, classroom-based > laboratory > online), interaction type (F = 19.195, p <
.001, pattern: one-to-many > one-to-one), and educational level (F = 7.134, p < .001, pattern: 
K–12 > university <N/A < preschool) showed statistically detectable differences in effect sizes. 
For affective learning outcomes, only the publication type yielded a statistically detectable dif­
ference (F = 4.803, p = .031, pattern: conference papers > journal papers). The findings of this 
study provide insights into the utilization and design of social cues in AIPAs for educational 
purposes.

1. Introduction
In the era of digital teaching and learning, artificial intelligence-powered pedagogical agents (AIPAs) have been increasingly in­
tegrated into educational settings (Zhang et al., 2024a). AIPAs are instructional systems embedded with artificial intelligence (AI) 
technologies that can dynamically respond to learners’ behaviors, contexts, or emotional states during the learning process, thereby 
providing personalized and adaptive instructional support (Kim et al., 2021). Extensive studies, including meta-analyses, have 
consistently demonstrated the benefits of pedagogical agents for teaching and learning (Alemdag, 2023; Lee & Lee, 2022; Zhang et al., 
2023). However, technological availability does not guarantee felt relatedness. Many learners remain susceptible to machine heu­
ristics, construing machines as objective, emotionless, and purely functional (Sundar & Kim, 2019). This perception of “coldness” can 
weaken social bonding with the agent and erode sustained instructional engagement.
As Conaway et al. (2005) noted, students need to see their teachers as emotional educators with whom they can form emotional 
attachments, not solely as emotionless computers. More importantly, education involves much more than the mere transmission of 
information; it involves interpersonal interactions and emotional exchange. To address these challenges, researchers have drawn on 
the principles of social agency theory. This theory was initially proposed by educational psychologists such as Roxana Moreno. When 
educational technologies, such as virtual tutors or interactive robots, exhibits human-like social cues, such as verbal and nonverbal 
communication cues, emotional expressions, and personalized feedback, students are more likely to perceive these agents as genuine 
social actors (Moreno et al., 2001). Therefore, incorporating social cues such as expressive language, gestures, and facial expressions 
may therefore help AIPAs appear more socially and emotionally present.
1.1. Effectiveness of social cues by AIPAs
The rationale for integrating social cues into AIPAs is strongly supported by the principles of social agency theory (Moreno et al., 
2001). Social agency theory emphasizes that when learners perceive instructional agents as social partners with human-like qualities, 
they are more likely to develop positive emotional experiences and learning motivation, thereby enhancing their learning outcomes. 
By incorporating social cues, AIPAs can make interactions with learners more naturally and engage in deeper cognitive processing. For 
example, when a robot teacher uses eye contact, gestures, or changes in intonation to emphasize important content, it mimics natural 
human cues, helping students focus on and better retain the information (Ba et al., 2021). Additionally, empathy and personalized 
responses can meet students’ emotional and motivational needs to increase their persistence and satisfaction with the learning process 
(Alves-Oliveira et al., 2019).
However, the social cues of AIPAs have raised concerns and questions. According to the cognitive load theory (Sweller, 2011), the 
cognitive load during the learning process is divided into intrinsic load (the complexity of the learning material) and extraneous load 
(the way of learning or the environment). In the teaching process, teaching agents with emotional expressions may introduce addi­
tional extraneous load, as learners need to allocate cognitive resources to interpret the agent’s emotional and social cues. Since such 
cues are unrelated to the core learning content and may divert learners’ attention (Liao et al., 2024). Moreover, the introduction of 
affective agents might lead to improper resource allocation, where limited working memory resources are used to process emotional 
and social information that is not directly related to learning objectives, thereby reducing the capacity for the deep processing of 
learning material. Furthermore, if the emotional expressions of an agent are inconsistent with the learning content or appear unnatural 
(e.g., an overly enthusiastic teaching agent explaining a complex and serious topic), it could lead to cognitive conflict could ensure, 
which would further disrupt the learning process.
Empirical research has reported mixed results regarding the effectiveness of social cues in AIPAs. The social cues of AIPAs can 
detectably enhance student engagement (Schodde et al., 2019), motivation (Saerbeck et al., 2010), and academic performance (Zhang 
et al., 2024a). However, some studies have shown that these effects are not always positive. Some studies have found that social cues 
may have no detectable effect (Bai et al., 2025) and, in some cases, may even produce negative effects (Liew et al., 2016). The complex 
relationship between social cues and learning outcomes raises questions about the overall effectiveness of social cues in AIPAs. These 
contradictory results suggest a delicate balance between promoting positive emotions and learning motivation in students while 
avoiding over-socialization, which might distract or intimidate learners.
1.2. Previous and current studies
Although relatively limited, existing meta-analyses have explored the effects of emotional pedagogical agents in multimedia 
learning environments (Wang et al., 2023) and the effects of robotic social behaviors (Belpaeme et al., 2018; Wang & Cheung, 2024). 
Wang et al. (2023) investigated the instructional impact of affective pedagogical agents in multimedia environments. These agents 
conveyed positive emotions through facial expressions, body gestures, prosodic tone, and encouraging language. The findings showed 
that such affective pedagogical agents can promote learners’ positive emotions, motivation, and overall academic outcomes. Addi­
tionally, Belpaeme et al. (2018) conducted a systematic review on the use of social robots in educational settings. The study highlighted 
how the social behaviors of robots, such as gaze, gestural feedback, and affective responses, play a critical role in sustaining learner 
engagement, promoting contextual involvement, and supporting personalized instruction. In the field of language learning, as revealed 
by Wang and Cheung (2024), socially supportive actions, such as using a friendly tone, offering encouraging phrases, asking about 
learners’ feelings, expressing empathy, providing emotionally supportive responses, praising learners’ efforts, and showing interest in 
learners’ progress, have positively impacted language learning success, showing better results compared to neutral behaviors, with an 
effect size of 0.269. While these studies provided initial evidence of positive outcomes, they did not focus specifically on AIPAs, nor did

Compared with these previous meta-analyses, our study makes several contributions and extensions. First, unlike Wang et al. 
(2023), which did not clearly distinguish AI-driven agents from human instructors presented on video, our study restricts the scope to 
AIPAs. While the computers are social actors (CASA) paradigm (Nass et al., 1994) posits that people apply social norms to machines, 
studies by Edwards et al. (2016) and Zhang et al. (2024a) have consistently shown that students perceive and react differently to 
human teachers and machine teachers. Second, our study focused on social cues rather than purely emotional cues. As demonstrated by 
Yin et al. (2024), social cues are not necessarily expressions of emotion but can also be ways of conveying information. Furthermore, 
unlike studies that focus solely on physical robots (Wang & Cheung, 2024), our study integrates three types of AIPAs: embodied robots, 
virtual teachers with visual presence, and chatbots without physical or visual presence. Moreover, we scientifically assessed the effects 
of AIPAs on students’ cognitive learning outcomes, affective learning outcomes, behavioral learning outcomes, and cognitive load. 
Furthermore, to identify the conditions under which AIPAs social cues become effective, we organized potential moderators into two 
complementary domains: the agent-design domain and the implementation-context domain. The former focuses on how an agent 
conveys social cues through its appearance, behaviors, and interaction mechanisms; the latter examines how those cues play out for 
different learners, tasks, and instructional settings. This two-layer framework draws on work arguing that instructional effects are 
jointly shaped by (a) the affordances engineered into the agent by design and (b) the ways those affordances are perceived, interpreted, 
and enacted in specific contexts (Bower, 2008). Finally, in contrast to prior meta-analyses that used conventional two-level models 
(which typically require keeping only one effect size per study or aggregating multiple effects before analysis; Assink & Wibbelink, 
2016), this study adopts a three-level random-effects meta-analytic approach as a methodological contribution. We chose this model 
because many primary studies in our dataset reported multiple, dependent effect sizes from the same sample (across outcomes, 
conditions, or time points). Treating these as independent would violate model assumptions, inflate statistical significance, and bias 
pooled estimates. The three-level model addresses this dependence by partitioning variance into sampling error (Level 1), within-study 
heterogeneity (Level 2), and between-study heterogeneity (Level 3) (Cheung, 2014). This allows us to retain all eligible effect sizes 
without loss of information and to more accurately characterize variability at different levels. Within this framework, we report τ2 and 
I2 at multiple levels and provide prediction intervals to better reflect the uncertainty and generalizability of the pooled effects (Van den 
Noortgate et al., 2013).
Given the aims of the present study, we formulated the following research questions. 
RQ1: What impact do AIPAs with social cues have on students’ cognitive learning outcomes, affective learning outcomes, 
behavioral learning outcomes, and cognitive load compared with AIPAs without social cues?
RQ2: How do moderating variables influence the effects of social cues in AIPAs?
2. Method
In this meta-analysis, the extended PRISMA guidelines developed by Moher et al. (2009) were applied. The research commenced 
with an exhaustive literature search, employing both electronic database searches and snowballing techniques to collect relevant 
studies.
2.1. Power analysis
To assess statistical power, we conducted an a priori power analysis using the metapower R package (Griffin, 2021). Following 
common guidance for standardized mean differences (Cohen, 2013), we set the expected standardized mean difference to 0.40, the 
average sample size per study to 60, the anticipated number of included studies to 20, and assumed substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 75 
%), which is typical in psychological meta-analyses. Under these assumptions, the estimated power to detect an overall effect was 0.92 
for the random-effects model and approximately 1.00 for the fixed-effects model, indicating adequate power to detect 
small-to-moderate effects.
2.2. Database search and search terms
To locate relevant articles, a systematic search was conducted using Web of Science, ERIC, and PsycINFO. We selected Web of 
Science, ERIC, and PsycINFO because these are widely recognized and frequently used in systematic reviews and meta-analyses in 
education, psychology, and learning sciences. Web of Science offers multidisciplinary peer-reviewed sources, ERIC focuses on 
education-specific studies, and PsycINFO covers psychological and developmental research. Together, they offer broad and relevant 
coverage for studies involving AI-powered pedagogical agents and learning outcomes.
To ensure the quality of the review articles, screening was limited to peer-reviewed articles written in English. After screening the 
complete articles, we used a “snowballing” approach to identify articles that were not extracted by the search strings (Wohlin, 2014). 
We used Google Scholar and previous relevant review articles to search for specific articles. Specifically, for Google Scholar, we used 
keyword combinations such as “AI pedagogical agent” “social cues”. We focused on the first 10 pages of each search result (i.e., top 100 
results), following common practice in meta-analytic search protocols. Additionally, we screened the reference lists of key review 
articles and included studies to identify relevant publications that may not have been indexed in the three primary databases.
In the literature search and keyword identification process, we organized the search terms into two major categories. Boolean 
operators were used to combine terms: synonyms within each category were connected with OR, and the categories were connected

In the meta-analysis, we included studies that met the following criteria: (1) the use of AI-powered pedagogical agents was 
explicitly described, where “AI-powered” refers to systems that either (a) integrated at least one AI component (e.g., natural language 
processing such as speech recognition or dialogue management; computer vision such as facial expression or image recognition; 
machine learning or deep learning for personalized recommendation, emotion detection, or learner modeling; or knowledge tracing/ 
user modeling for adapting content based on learner behavior), (b) demonstrated AI-related functionalities in system behavior even

2.4. Data selection
Fig. 1 illustrates the entire data filtration process. Ultimately, 33 studies that met the inclusion criteria of this research were 
included. Two trained researchers in educational technology (one with a Ph.D. and the other a doctoral candidate) independently 
screened all records retrieved through the database search. The screening process followed a set of predefined inclusion and exclusion 
criteria. In cases of disagreement, the researchers discussed the discrepancies until full consensus was reached. The data included in 
this study can be found in the supplementary material.
To comprehensively evaluate the internal validity of the included studies, we employed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical 
Appraisal Checklist for Quasi-Experimental Studies (Barker et al., 2023), which comprises nine items designed to assess core aspects of 
study design. These items cover essential domains such as clarity in participant selection, presence and comparability of control 
groups, consistency in intervention implementation, reliability of outcome measurement, and appropriateness of statistical analysis. 
The same two researchers who conducted the study screening (one with a Ph.D. and the other a doctoral candidate) independently 
assessed all eligible studies using the JBI’s four-point response scale: “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” and “Not Applicable.” Any discrepancies 
in scoring were resolved through discussion until full consensus was reached.
The evaluation results indicated that all included studies received a total score of 6 or higher. According to prior literature (Rajati 
et al., 2022; Soroorikia et al., 2024), scores ranging from 1 to 3 typically indicate low-quality studies, 4–6 indicate moderate quality, 
and 7–9 reflect high-quality research. Based on this classification, the overall quality of the studies included in this meta-analysis can 
be considered high, which strengthens the credibility and robustness of the synthesized findings.
2.5. Effect size integration
Effect sizes provide a standardized method to evaluate the efficacy of treatments. Previous studies have used Cohen’s d (Cohen, 
2013) or Hedges’ g (Hedges & Olkin, 2014) to compute effect sizes. Although both metrics generally yield comparable outcomes, 
Hedges’ g is preferred when adjusting for small sample sizes (e.g., less than 50). For this analysis, Hedges’ g was employed. According 
to Cohen (2013), effect sizes of 0.20–0.49 indicate a small effect, 0.50–0.79 suggest a medium effect, and 0.80 or above denote a large 
effect.
To enhance interpretability for broader audiences, we translated Hedges’ g into the Common Language Effect Size (CLES), which 
expresses the probability that a randomly selected learner in the socially cued AIPAs condition would show a better outcome than a 
learner in the control condition. This approach follows the guidance of Brooks et al. (2014) and Ostermann and Anheyer (2023), who 
advocate for the use of CLES to make standardized effect sizes more understandable to non-technical readers.
Most of the studies included in our research examined the impact of the social cues of AIPAs on multiple dependent variables. To 
enhance the precision and rigor of our analysis, we categorized these dependent variables into four major types: cognitive learning 
outcomes, affective learning outcomes, behavioral learning outcomes, and cognitive load. We then conducted four separate meta- 
analyses for each category. This classification has been used in studies by Zhang et al. (2023) and Wei et al. (2021).
This comprehensive multilevel meta-analysis incorporated a total of 185 effect sizes, which were systematically categorized into 
four distinct groups: cognitive learning outcomes (66 effect sizes), affective learning outcomes (85 effect sizes), behavioral learning 
outcomes (23 effect sizes), and cognitive load (11 effect sizes).
2.6. Coding scheme
To identify the conditions under which AIPAs social cues are most effective, we adopted an “affordance–context fit” perspective and 
organized moderators along two complementary dimensions: technology and context (Bower, 2008). The underlying premise is that 
learning effects arise not only from what the technology affords, but also from how those affordances are perceived, interpreted, and 
enacted in specific instructional settings. Accordingly, we grouped moderators into two sets. The agent-design set characterizes what 
an AIPAs can signal and do through its appearance, behavioral repertoire, and interaction mechanisms, and thus its capacity to elicit 
social presence, personalization, and socially grounded human–AI interaction. The implementation-context set captures the conditions 
under which these signals are likely to translate into actual engagement and performance, including features of the learner population, 
task demands, and instructional arrangements that shape the effectiveness of social cues. Within this framework, we drew on prior 
syntheses (Wang & Cheung, 2024; Wang et al., 2023) and the characteristics of our corpus to specify operational definitions, coding 
rules, and decision criteria for each moderator. The same two researchers who conducted study selection independently coded all 
eligible studies, and any disagreements were resolved through iterative discussion until full agreement was reached. Definitions, 
categories, and decision rules are summarized in Table 1.

because the two paradigms differ fundamentally in interaction style and output generation: traditional AI typically follows rule- 
governed procedures and fixed templates, whereas generative AI produces open-ended natural language and adapts to context 
(Bandi et al., 2023). From an affordance–context fit perspective (Bower, 2008), the affordances a technology offers can shape how 
learners perceive and use social cues, and how effective those cues are across tasks and settings. Distinguishing AI types therefore helps 
examine whether underlying technological affordances alter the perception, interpretation, and uptake of social cues, with down­
stream effects on learning. Given the rapid diffusion of generative AI in education (Zhang et al., 2024b), drawing a clear distinction 
from traditional AI at the literature-review stage is warranted to explore how differences in technological affordances may influence 
the presentation and processing of social cues.
Type of pedagogical agent captures the format in which the AIPAs was presented, including physical robots, visually represented 
virtual agents, and text- or voice-based agents without a visual presence. This classification is grounded in embodied cognition theory, 
which posits that cognitive processing is influenced by sensorimotor experiences; therefore, more physically embodied formats may 
enhance immersion and engagement (Johnson-Glenberg et al., 2014). At the same time, the uncanny valley effect (Mori, 1970) 
suggests that highly humanlike but imperfect agents may elicit discomfort or psychological resistance, especially in the case of physical 
robots (Sato et al., 2024). In contrast, visually represented virtual agents, though lacking spatial embodiment, can convey social cues in 
a less intrusive manner. This variable was designed to examine how different levels of embodiment and modes of social cue delivery 
affect the instructional potential of AIPAs.
Image of the pedagogical agent refers to the extent to which the agent exhibits humanlike visual features. Drawing from social 
agency theory (Moreno et al., 2001) and media equation theory (Reeves & Nass, 1996), humanlike characteristics may trigger 
interpersonal processing and deeper engagement with the agent. While greater anthropomorphism can increase perceived social 
presence, it may also lead to discomfort if the agent’s design approaches human likeness without full realism. Moreover, excessively 
animated or complex behavior may impose additional cognitive load (St´arkov´a et al., 2019), particularly in content-heavy learning 
environments. This variable was included to investigate how varying levels of humanlike design influence learners’ interaction and 
processing depth.
Agent gender reflects whether the AIPAs was presented with a clearly identifiable gender (male or female) or with gender-neutral/ 
neutral/unspecifiedattributes. This coding draws on the notion that gendered design may activate learners’ gender-based expectations 
and stereotypes, which can influence perceptions of agent warmth, competence, or credibility (Veletsianos, 2010). For example, fe­
male agents are often perceived as more supportive, whereas male agents may be associated with authority (Armando et al., 2022). 
This variable was introduced to explore the influence of gender cues on learners’ responses to instructional agents.
Role of the agent indicates whether the AIPAs was presented as a tutor or as a peer. Prior studies suggest that tutor roles may 
enhance learners’ perceptions of authority and structured guidance, thereby fostering focused attention and task alignment. In 
contrast, peer roles may encourage collaborative behavior and reduce psychological distance (Baylor & Kim, 2005). This variable was 
included to examine whether role framing contributes to the differential impact of AIPAs on learning.
Social cue presentation includes two dimensions: the type of social cues (vocal, visual, or both) and the number of cues (single vs. 
multiple modalities). According to social presence theory, multimodal cues increase the sense of interpersonal involvement and 
engagement (Short, Williams, & Christie, 1976). This variable captures the richness and diversity of social signaling strategies 
employed by AIPAs.
Publication year and publication type were included as methodological covariates. Publication year served as a proxy for the 
technological context, recognizing that recent studies are more likely to incorporate advanced AI systems, particularly generative 
models. Publication type distinguished between conference papers and journal articles, accounting for possible differences in peer- 
review rigor and reporting standards. These two variables allow us to assess whether methodological evolution and temporal 
context influence reported effect sizes.
Research method differentiates between studies that employed the Wizard-of-Oz (WoZ) technique, where the agent was partially 
operated by human researchers, and those using fully automated agents. The WoZ approach is often used to simulate advanced in­
teractions not yet technically feasible (Dahlb¨ack et al., 1993). This distinction is relevant because the degree of automation may in­
fluence the perceived reliability and interactivity of the agent, thus affecting learners’ trust and engagement.
The country variable captures the cultural and educational context by distinguishing whether studies were conducted in Eastern or 
Western societies. This distinction is grounded in cross-cultural research indicating that cultural values shape learners’ attitudes to­
ward instructional technologies and social agents (Kou & Zhang, 2024). Drawing on Hofstede’s dimensions, individualism–collecti­
vism and power distance may condition how socially expressive AIPAs are interpreted, potentially leading to different receptivity 
across contexts (Hofstede, 2011). By including country as a moderator, the analysis aimed to explore whether these broader cultural 
orientations systematically influence how learners respond to AIPAs’ social cues.
The learning domain variable was coded to distinguish studies focusing on sciences, humanities, or other disciplines. This cate­
gorization acknowledges that learning objectives and communication practices differ across subject areas, which may interact with 
AIPAs design. For instance, science instruction typically emphasizes accuracy, procedural clarity, and unambiguous feedback, creating 
contexts where structured, directive social cues are particularly valuable. In contrast, humanities instruction often prioritizes 
reflection, dialogue, and emotional engagement, potentially amplifying the importance of affective and relational signals from the 
agent. Including this variable was intended to examine whether differences across learning domains moderate the relationship be­
tween AIPAs social cues and learning outcomes.
Instruction duration categorizes whether the intervention was short-term (e.g., a single session) or extended over a longer period (e. 
g., multiple sessions). Prior research suggests that longer exposure may allow learners to form more stable perceptions of the agent’s

role and to develop deeper interaction patterns (Johnson et al., 2000). This variable was therefore included to examine whether the 
length of engagement moderates the learning effects of AIPAs.
Instructional setting refers to the physical or virtual environment in which the AIPAs was deployed, including classrooms, labo­
ratories, and online platforms. Different settings carry distinct social and psychological affordances that may shape learners’ per­
ceptions of instructional agents (Moreno & Mayer, 2007). For instance, classroom settings may provide stronger social cues and 
normative structures, while online environments often lack such contextual grounding. This variable was included to explore how 
learning context affects learners’ responses to AIPAs intervention.
Interaction type describes the communication structure between the AIPAs and learners, categorized as either one-to-one or one-to- 
many formats. This variable was informed by Bloom’s (1984) seminal research demonstrating that one-to-one human tutoring sub­
stantially outperforms group instruction in promoting learning outcomes. The present study aimed to examine whether this pattern 
still holds in the context of AI-powered instructional agents. Considering that one of the most frequently cited advantages of AI is its 
capacity to provide personalized learning and targeted feedback, this comparison also sought to explore potential differences in the 
effectiveness of AIPAs between individualized and group-based instructional settings.
Educational level captures learners’ developmental stage, categorized as preschool, K–12, university level or others. Drawing on 
developmental psychology, particularly Piaget’s stage theory (Piaget, 1976, pp. 11–23) and prior research (Calvert, 1999), this var­
iable accounts for differences in cognitive maturity, social attribution tendencies, and sensitivity to social cues across age groups. It was 
included to examine whether learner educational level moderates the effectiveness of AIPAs.
Finally, we categorized the dependent variables into four distinct types of learning outcomes: cognitive learning outcomes (e.g., 
knowledge acquisition, conceptual understanding), affective learning outcomes (e.g., interest, motivation, self-efficacy), behavioral 
learning outcomes (e.g., task engagement, collaborative behavior), and cognitive load. This classification is grounded in widely 
accepted models of educational psychology, which conceptualize learning as a multidimensional construct involving knowledge 
processing, emotional engagement, and behavioral participation, alongside the cognitive demands of the learning task (Fredricks et al., 
2004). It also aligns with prior meta-analytic frameworks (e.g., Monib et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023), ensuring comparability across 
studies. Organizing the outcomes in this way allowed us to systematically investigate how the presence and nature of social cues 
embedded in AIPAs designs affect different facets of learning, while also enabling fine-grained analysis of both benefits and potential 
trade-offs across domains.
2.7. Outlier detection and influence analysis
Outliers can detectably distort the overarching results of a study, necessitating their removal to accurately understand changes in 
the main effect. We conducted an influence analysis following the methodology proposed by Viechtbauer and Cheung (2010). This 
approach identifies strong effects that qualify as outliers and assesses whether meta-analytic results hinge disproportionately on a 
single study or effect, thereby testing the robustness of the findings. Specifically, three critical metrics in influence analysis assist in 
detecting outliers and evaluating their impact. 
(1) DFFITS: Gauges the change in the standard deviation of the mixed-effects measure following the removal of a specific study.
(2) Cook’s Distance (Cook’s D): Akin to the Mahalanobis distance in traditional statistics, it quantifies the disparity in values with 
and without the study.
(3) Covariance Ratio (cov.r): Calculated as the determinant of the variance-covariance matrix with a study excluded over that of the 
full dataset. Notably, a cov. r value below 1 suggests that excluding the study yields a more precise estimate of the effect size, 
indicative of reduced heterogeneity.
In conclusion, further probing of individual indicators is generally unwarranted if outliers or influential cases do not affect the 
conclusions, bolstering confidence in the resilience of the meta-analytic findings against such anomalies.
2.8. Publication bias
Publication bias refers to the phenomenon in which studies with detectable results are more likely to be published, resulting in a 
body of literature that may not accurately represent the entire field (Rothstein et al., 2005, pp. 1–7). To assess potential publication 
bias, funnel plots and multilevel Egger’s regression tests were employed (Rodgers & Pustejovsky, 2021). A funnel plot was considered 
roughly symmetrical when visual inspection did not reveal apparent asymmetry. The multilevel Egger’s regression evaluates bias by 
testing whether the intercept detectably deviates from zero across levels of clustering (e.g., multiple effect sizes per study). A 
non-detectable p-value (p > .05) suggests no statistically detectable publication bias. Additionally, the trim-and-fill procedure was 
applied within each outcome domain to estimate and adjust for potentially missing studies due to bias (Duval & Tweedie, 2000).

3.5. Sensitivity analysis
3.5.1. Leave-one-out analysis
To evaluate the robustness of the meta-analytic estimates, we conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (Vehtari et al., 2017), 
in which each individual study was excluded in turn. Importantly, this procedure is not based on outlier exclusion but is intended to 
assess whether any single study exerts undue influence on the overall results.
Cognitive Learning Outcomes: We observed that the estimates of effect size ranged from 0.336 to 0.396 after excluding each in­
dividual study. The actual effect size in our results was 0.359, which falls within this range.
Affective Learning Outcomes: We observed that the estimates of effect size ranged from 0.470 to 0.562 after excluding each in­
dividual study. The actual effect size in our results was 0.519, which falls within this range.
Behavioral Learning Outcomes: We observed that the estimates of effect size ranged from 0.408 to 0.516 after excluding each 
individual study. The actual effect size in our results was 0.458, which falls within this range.
Cognitive Load: We observed that the estimates of effect size ranged from −0.011 to 0.072 after excluding each individual study. 
The actual effect size in our results was 0.039, which falls within this range.
These findings demonstrated that our results on cognitive learning outcomes, affective learning outcomes, behavioral learning 
outcomes, and cognitive load are robust.
3.5.2. Influence-based sensitivity (with vs. without flagged outliers)
Using the same three-level meta-analytic model, we compared pooled estimates including versus excluding the effect sizes flagged 
as influential in Section 3.1 (DFFITS, Cook’s distance, cov. r). For cognitive learning outcomes, the pooled standardized mean dif­
ference changed only marginally from g = 0.346 when the flagged effect was retained to g = 0.359 after its removal (absolute change 
= 0.013). For affective learning outcomes, the estimate shifted from g = −0.024 to g = −0.097 after excluding four flagged effects 
(absolute change = 0.073). For behavioral learning outcomes and cognitive load, no effect sizes were identified as influential; 
accordingly, no deletion-based recomputation was required and the reported estimates remain unchanged.
Taken together, both the leave-one-out results and the with-versus-without comparisons show only minor shifts, supporting that 
the conclusions are robust and not driven by a small number of influential observations.
4. Discussion
This multilevel meta-analysis investigated the overall effect of AIPAs’ social cues on students’ cognitive learning outcomes, af­
fective learning outcomes, behavioral learning outcomes, and cognitive load, and explored how potential moderators influenced their 
effectiveness. The results indicated that the social cues of AIPAs detectably increased cognitive, affective, and behavioral learning 
outcomes. No detectable influence on cognitive load was observed. Furthermore, the type of pedagogical agent, the image and gender 
of the pedagogical agent, as well as the instructional setting, interaction type, and educational level, were all associated with 
detectable differences in cognitive learning outcomes. In the case of affective learning outcomes, only publication type showed a 
statistically detectable effect.
4.1. Overall effect of social cues from AIPAs on different learning outcomes
The findings of our study provide detectable theoretical contributions from the perspectives of social agency theory (Moreno et al., 
2001) and cognitive load theory (Sweller, 2011). Social agency theory suggests that people ascribe sociality and agency to nonhuman 
agents (e.g., AI instructional agents) that exhibit certain human behavioral traits and thus interact with them similarly to human 
interactions (Moreno et al., 2001). This study shows that when AIPAs exhibit behaviors consistent with human social habits, they can 
effectively enhance students’ learning outcomes in the cognitive, affective, and behavioral dimensions. The results validate the 
applicability of social agency theory in human-computer interactions, further illustrating the importance and necessity of teaching 
agents’ social cues in promoting learning outcomes. This provides solid empirical support for the future design of AIPAs, clearly 
demonstrating their positive effects on students’ cognitive, affective, and behavioral learning outcomes.
Additionally, in contrast to previous claims that social cues in AIPAs might increase students’ cognitive load (Beege et al., 2020; 
Beege & Schneider, 2023), our research demonstrates that the presence or absence of such behaviors does not substantially affect 
cognitive load. This insight enhances our understanding of cognitive load theory by suggesting that appropriately integrating social 
cues into AI does not complicate the learning process. Thus, our research not only confirms the positive impacts of social cues in 
educational AI but also encourages a re-evaluation of incorporating these cues, advocating for their optimization to enrich learning 
experiences without negatively impacting cognitive load.
The positive impact of social cues exhibited by AIPAs on cognitive, affective, and behavioral learning outcomes highlights the 
comprehensive influence of AIPAs. Cognitive, affective, and behavioral outcomes are widely acknowledged as indispensable com­
ponents of effective learning (Post et al., 2019). The favorable impacts on these aspects suggest that AIPAs with social capabilities can

provide extensive educational support, meeting diverse learning needs. This finding underscores the robustness and versatility of social 
cues in AIPAs. By simultaneously enhancing cognitive understanding, emotional engagement, and behavioral activation, these agents 
fulfill multiple roles traditionally associated with effective human educators.
4.2. Effect of moderating variables
This study revealed multiple factors accounting for differences in cognitive learning outcomes, as well as a single factor associated 
with affective learning outcomes. Beyond statistical interpretation, these findings offer theoretical and practical insights into how 
instructional agents can be more effectively designed and applied in educational contexts.
4.2.1. Cognitive learning outcomes
First, this study finds a clear gradient in cognitive gains by agent form. Virtual pedagogical agents with visual representation show 
the strongest benefits, followed by physically embodied robots, while voice-only or text-only agents have minimal effects. This pattern 
indicates that visual presentation is a key driver of educational effectiveness. From a social presence perspective (Short, Williams, & 
Christie, 1976), visible “co-presence” is a prerequisite for learners to form social and emotional responses to an agent. Compared with 
purely auditory or textual interaction, a visible agent more readily elicits a sense of “otherness,” which can trigger interpersonal-like 
responses. Heightened social presence, in turn, supports sustained attention, emotional engagement, and deeper cognitive processing, 
leading to improved learning. Physically embodied robots can also strengthen social presence through spatial co-location and natu­
ralistic interaction (Belpaeme et al., 2018). However, prior research indicates that, relative to visually represented virtual agents, 
embodied robots are more prone to eliciting uncanny-valley responses (Mori, 1970), which can induce discomfort and cognitive 
interference. This asymmetry offers a plausible explanation for why robots did not outperform visually represented virtual agents in 
our results. By contrast, agents without visual representation provide too few social cues to reliably activate social processing, which 
constrains deep learning (Haresamudram, 2025).
Second, the degree of anthropomorphism in pedagogical agents shows a clear and robust impact on learners’ cognitive outcomes. 
Our meta-analysis reveals a graded pattern: the higher the level of anthropomorphism, the stronger the learning gains. This pattern 
aligns closely with social agency theory, which posits that nonhuman agents with humanlike attributes are more readily construed by 
learners as social actors, thereby triggering social-like information processing (Moreno et al., 2001). Within this mechanism, greater 
anthropomorphism more strongly elicits social presence, namely the learner’s psychological sense that the agent is socially “there.” 
Social presence, in turn, functions as a key mediating pathway, sustaining attention, task engagement, and depth of cognitive pro­
cessing. Hence, higher anthropomorphism is more likely to heighten social presence, promote deeper learning engagement, and ul­
timately enhance cognitive outcomes. This continuous increase from non-anthropomorphized to fully anthropomorphized agents 
captures the core mechanism and theoretical contribution of the present study.
Third, compared with agents explicitly presented as male or female, N/A AIPAs showed stronger associations with learning out­
comes. In our analysis, only the N/A group yielded a statistically detectable effect; agents labeled as male or female did not exhibit 
detectable positive effects. This pattern offers convergent evidence for the advantages of N/A design, which can reduce the influence of 
gender stereotypes, direct attention to the task itself, and support motivation and cognitive engagement (Barth, 2012; Feine et al., 
2020). From a social identity perspective, explicitly gendered agents may unintentionally trigger gender-based categorization, 
weakening learners’ psychological fit with the agent and constraining the depth and breadth of cognitive processing. By contrast, 
gender-neutral/neutral/unspecified agents avoid activating such category cues and are more readily accepted as a “neutral instruc­
tional other,” which can enhance applicability and inclusiveness across diverse learner groups and contexts. Practically, these findings 
suggest that gender-neutral/neutral/unspecified AIPAs, using androgynous visual design, non-gendered language, and ambiguous 
vocal characteristics, can mitigate bias risks and better serve cross-cultural, cross-age, and cross-disciplinary settings, thereby 
advancing more personalized and inclusive instructional goals.
Fourth, instructional context exerted a statistically detectable moderating effect on AIPAs effectiveness. AIPAs showed a moderate 
positive impact in classroom settings, whereas effects were not statistically detectable in laboratory or online contexts. This pattern 
indicates that effectiveness depends not only on the agent’s social design features but also on the degree of social structuring in the 
learning environment. As noted by Song, Kim, and Luo (2016), contextual psychological and social affordances shape how learners 
interpret and respond to instructional agents. Classrooms provide more than physical space; they typically include stable behavioral 
norms, clearly defined roles, and a history of ongoing interaction, forming a “socialized learning space.” In such settings, learners are 
more likely to treat AIPAs as legitimate participants in instruction or as teaching assistants (Fraser, 2012), which elevates the perceived 
authority of the agent’s input and encourages deeper information processing. The role clarity and interaction density of classroom 
environments can also strengthen social presence, the felt sense that the agent is “really there.” Prior work suggests that social presence 
is a key driver of social-agency mechanisms and socially oriented cognitive processing, supporting motivation, attention, and task 
engagement and thereby enhancing learning outcomes (Song et al., 2016). By contrast, laboratory settings, although highly controlled, 
often involve short tasks and narrowly scoped interactions and lack sustained social structures. Learners may therefore construe AIPAs 
as research apparatus rather than social partners, limiting activation of social-agency pathways (Linn & Eylon, 2011). Similarly, online 
environments frequently lack spatial co-presence and contextual cues, leading learners to treat AIPAs as information deliverers or 
technical interfaces, which constrains their social and interactive potential (Song et al., 2016). Taken together, these observations 
suggest that AIPAs design and deployment should emphasize contextual embeddedness. In instructional scenarios that stress 
collaborative interaction and social construction, integrating AIPAs into settings with clear role definitions and structured interaction 
is likely to move them from peripheral tools to socially recognized instructional participants, thereby maximizing their impact on

cognitive processing and learning.
Fifth, one-to-many formats tended to produce stronger learning outcomes than one-to-one formats, although both showed positive 
tendencies. This finding is inconsistent with the analysis conducted by Bloom (1984), which demonstrated that one-to-one tutoring 
detectably outperforms one-to-many classroom instruction in terms of academic achievement, with an improvement of approximately 
two standard deviations. However, in learning contexts driven by AIPAs, certain mechanisms distinct from traditional classroom 
settings may emerge, resulting in the observed advantage of one-to-many formats under specific conditions. We propose two potential 
explanations for this pattern. First, although AIPAs theoretically possess strong potential for highly personalized tutoring, their current 
technical capabilities in areas such as content adaptation, interaction diversity, and emotional modulation remain limited. Previous 
research has indicated that when instructional agents lack rich social cues or flexible feedback mechanisms, learners in one-to-one 
settings are more prone to experiencing boredom, disengagement, and diminished attention (Hashim et al., 2022; Murtaza et al., 
2022). In contrast, in one-to-many environments, the presence of a collective learning context characterized by shared pacing, social 
comparison, and group-level incentives (Cockrell et al., 2000) may partially compensate for the limited personalization of AIPAs and 
support more sustained learner engagement. Second, AIPAs in individual interactions often struggle to create the same degree of social 
presence and perceived authority as human instructors. Human teachers naturally command respect and a sense of responsibility 
through the formal structure of the classroom and their established social roles (Baylor & Kim, 2005; Reeves & Nass, 1996). In many 
cases, AIPAs are perceived primarily as functional tools rather than as legitimate instructional figures, a tendency that may become 
more pronounced in one-to-one contexts. In group-based settings, however, the shared experience, ritualized participation, and social 
comparison processes may contribute to attributing greater symbolic authority to AIPAs, which in turn can strengthen motivation and 
focus. These findings suggest that under current technological conditions, embedding AIPAs within group learning environments may 
more effectively unlock their instructional potential, offering practical guidance for designing efficient and scalable AI-supported 
learning models in future educational practice. However, it is important to note that the current body of research on one-to-many 
interaction formats remains relatively limited, and the generalizability of these findings requires further empirical validation and 
expansion.
Sixth, among all educational levels examined, only learners in the K–12 group exhibited a statistically detectable benefit from 
AIPAs interventions, whereas no comparable effects were observed for preschool or university learners. This pattern may be explained 
through developmental psychology and adaptive instructional design. Learners in the K–12 range are typically transitioning from 
concrete to formal operational thinking (Piaget, 1976, pp. 11–23) and possess emerging metacognitive skills and social attribution 
mechanisms. These developmental traits make them more responsive to social cues such as facial expressions, tone of voice, and 
interactive gestures embedded in AIPAs behavior. As a result, they are more likely to engage the social agency pathway proposed in 
media interaction theories (Reeves & Nass, 1996), leading to heightened motivation, strategy use, and comprehension. In contrast, 
preschool children’s limited capacity for abstract thinking and social decoding may lead to cognitive overload when exposed to 
complex social behaviors from instructional agents (Roman et al., 2014). Their attentional resources may be consumed by salient but 
non-essential features (e.g., agent appearance or voice), which can detract from processing core learning content. University students, 
on the other hand, tend to exhibit high levels of self-regulated learning and media literacy. They are more likely to perceive AIPAs as 
functional tools rather than interactive partners, reducing the likelihood of strong social engagement. This pragmatic stance may also 
give rise to a “cold technology” mindset, dampening trust and emotional connection with the agent (Wang et al., 2025). These findings 
suggest that learners’ developmental stage and prior media experience detectably influence how social design features are perceived. 
Instructional agents should therefore be tailored to different educational levels by adjusting their social complexity and interaction 
style. For instance, preschool learners may benefit from intuitive, low-complexity agents; K–12 learners may respond well to 
emotionally supportive and contextually rich designs; and university learners may prefer agents optimized for cognitive scaffolding, 
metacognitive cues, and collaborative task support.
4.2.2. Affective learning outcomes
In addition, this study identified one variable that appeared to influence affective learning outcomes, namely the type of publi­
cation. The reported effect sizes in conference proceedings were observed to be higher than those found in journal articles. This 
discrepancy may result from the fact that conference papers often emphasize novel findings or promising results to increase the 
likelihood of acceptance, which may contribute to the presence of positive reporting bias (Treanor et al., 2020). In contrast, journal 
articles typically adhere to stricter methodological standards, including larger sample sizes, detailed reporting, and more controlled 
study designs, which may lead to more conservative estimates of AIPAs’ emotional impact. This finding suggests that the publication 
venue should be taken into account when interpreting the emotional learning effects of AIPA-based interventions, and that caution is 
warranted when generalizing from findings reported in conference proceedings.
4.3. Noteworthy but non-detectable findings
Although our analysis did not reveal a statistically detectable moderating effect of AI type (traditional versus generative) on 
cognitive, affective, behavioral outcomes or cognitive load, the variable remains theoretically and practically important given the 
growing prominence of generative AI in education. Subgroup patterns were nonetheless clear: traditional AI showed stable, positive 
effects across the three learning domains, whereas estimates for generative AI were more variable, with wider confidence intervals and 
some crossing zero. Part of this variability likely reflects the evidence base: only two to three generative-AI studies met the inclusion 
criteria, which limits the precision of the pooled estimates. Beyond this data constraint, there are plausible mechanistic explanations. 
Traditional AI is rule-driven and structurally explicit, which facilitates alignment with clear instructional objectives and task-oriented 
S. Zhang et al.                                                                                                                                                                                                          
Educational Research Review 49 (2025) 100746 
18 

contexts, thereby supporting consistent and controllable learning support. Generative AI, while promising for open-ended inquiry and 
dialogue, produces less predictable outputs that can induce goal drift, information overload, or cognitive distraction, reducing the 
stability of observed effects in current studies. Taken together, the absence of a detected moderation effect should not be read as 
evidence that the two AI types are equivalent. It more likely reflects the early stage of empirical work on generative AI, where the 
evidence base is thin and study designs are heterogeneous. Future research should increase the number of generative-AI studies, 
employ more consistent design frameworks and controlled comparisons, and improve alignment with instructional goals through 
prompt design, interaction protocols, and feedback mechanisms. We also recommend prioritizing hybrid pedagogical agents that 
combine the structural reliability of rule-based components with the expressive flexibility of generative modules, orchestrated under 
clear task plans and risk controls, to strike a better balance between stability and adaptability and to enhance instructional 
effectiveness.
Besides, although cultural background did not emerge as a statistically detectable moderator in our analysis, it remains concep­
tually important for understanding learner–agent interactions across sociocultural settings. Subgroup analyses showed a general trend: 
studies conducted in Western contexts more often reported detectable positive effects of social cues on cognitive, affective, and 
behavioral outcomes, while studies in Eastern contexts showed more mixed or negligible effects. These findings, though not 
conclusive, suggest that culture may shape how learners perceive and respond to AIPAs. According to Hofstede’s cultural dimensions 
(Hofstede, 2011), learners from individualistic and low power-distance societies may view socially expressive AIPAs as approachable 
collaborators, which could enhance engagement with social cues. In contrast, learners from collectivist or high power-distance cultures 
may interpret these cues through more hierarchical or instrumental perspectives, reducing their relational value. Another possible 
explanation involves cultural display rules. In high-context cultures, where indirect communication and emotional restraint are 
preferred, overt social behaviors such as informal language or friendly gestures may seem inappropriate or inauthentic, lowering 
receptivity. This contrasts with low-context cultures, where expressiveness is more accepted and often welcomed.
Our cultural analysis, however, was based on a broad East–West classification and lacked more detailed coding of specific cultural 
values or individual-level data. As a result, the cultural interpretation remains mostly theoretical and needs stronger empirical evi­
dence to clarify the behavioral mechanisms behind learners’ responses. In addition, the studies in our review were situated in diverse 
educational systems that vary in curriculum centralization, assessment regimes, and teacher autonomy (Hardy et al., 2020). 
Combining findings across such structurally different contexts without considering these institutional factors may reduce the 
comparability and generalizability of results. Future research should refine cultural coding and include educational system features 
such as curriculum centralization, testing culture, teacher autonomy, and digital equity as potential moderators. Using international 
datasets such as the OECD’s PISA or UNESCO’s Global Education Monitoring reports could improve the contextual sensitivity of 
cross-national analyses. It is also important to collect learner-level variables, such as prior AI experience and attitudes toward tech­
nology, to build a more mechanism-based and context-aware explanatory framework.
4.4. Limitations and future studies
Although this study makes substantive theoretical and empirical contributions, it has limitations at both the content and meth­
odological levels. On the content side, the current evidence base leaves several gaps that constrain deeper analysis. First, primary 
studies rarely report individual-level variables. Learners’ prior experience with AI technologies and their ability to process social and 
affective information may shape how they perceive and respond to AIPAs social cues, yet most studies did not provide such background 
information, which prevented moderator analyses on these factors. Second, evidence on learner–agent gender matching is limited. 
Prior work suggests that gender matching can influence engagement and social presence (Ozogul et al., 2013; Putnam et al., 2022), but 
most included studies either did not report learner gender or did not distinguish specific matching conditions, which constrained tests 
of this mechanism. Third, to ensure coding consistency and adequate power for moderator analyses, we followed established practice 
and categorized social cues as “single” or “multiple.” This approach is practical but coarse, and it may mask differences among specific 
cue combinations, for example “voice plus facial expression” versus “voice plus gesture.” Finally, empirical research on generative AI 
in educational settings (e.g., ChatGPT) remains sparse, with only a small number of eligible studies, which limits the generalizability 
and comparability of our conclusions in this emerging context. Future work should provide more systematic and granular reporting on 
individual backgrounds, gender matching, cue combinations, and the design of generative-AI–driven interventions to support more 
precise moderator tests.
On the methodological side, several analyses were constrained by gaps in the primary data, which limited the scope for statistical 
modeling and inference. We initially planned to use meta-analytic structural equation modeling to examine the interrelations among 
cognitive, affective, and behavioral outcomes. This was not feasible because most studies did not report correlation matrices or 
covariance information among variables. We also explored meta-regression to estimate the joint effects of multiple moderators, but the 
combination of a modest number of studies and substantial design heterogeneity led to unstable estimation. We therefore report single- 
moderator models and note the uncertainty introduced by multiple comparisons. In addition, some outcome domains suffered from 
limited sample sizes, which affects statistical power and estimation precision. For example, the cognitive load domain included only 11 
effect sizes from 4 independent studies, well below our a priori power scenario (approximately 20 studies with an average sample size 
of about 60), which may reduce sensitivity and increase the risk of Type II errors. Even so, because cognitive load is central to assessing 
the cost–benefit profile of social cues, we retained this domain and transparently report its uncertainty. Going forward, larger and more 
completely reported evidence bases will enable more robust and integrative analyses, for example integrated meta-regressions when 
conditions permit, and will improve cross-study comparability and the generalizability of conclusions.

5. Conclusion
This study offers a clear and transferable framework for understanding how social cues in AIPAs relate to learning. Overall, social 
cues are associated with gains in cognitive, affective, and behavioral outcomes, and no statistically detectable change was observed for 
cognitive load. This pattern aligns with social agency theory (Moreno et al., 2001) and places social presence (Short, Williams, & 
Christie, 1976) as a proximal psychological pathway. Under typical instructional tasks and appropriate design, social interaction can 
be activated without adding processing burden. In this way, our contribution moves the conversation from “whether it works” to “how 
it works and under what conditions.”
The results also show several general boundaries and gradients. On the agent side, features track learning gains in an ordered way: 
visually represented virtual agents outperform physically embodied robots, and both outperform voice- or text-only agents; fully 
anthropomorphic agents outperform semi-anthropomorphic ones, and both outperform non-anthropomorphic agents; gender-neutral/ 
neutral/unspecified presentation helps reduce extra costs linked to social categorization and improves fit across contexts. On the 
context and learner side, effects are shaped by setting and audience: classroom settings, rather than laboratory or online settings, more 
often yield statistically detectable and more stable benefits; one-to-many interaction tends to exceed one-to-one; K–12 learners are 
more sensitive to social cues, while comparable detectable benefits were not observed at the preschool or university levels. Taken 
together, these findings indicate that the impact of social cues depends both on the perceptibility and coherence of the cues and on the 
social context and audience in which they are embedded.
These patterns translate into practical design guidance. In implementation, it is advisable to prioritize visually represented virtual 
agents and to provide stable, coherent, and moderate social cues to elicit social presence. Gender-neutral presentation can enhance 
inclusivity and cross-cultural fit. Deployment should emphasize contextual embeddedness, integrating AIPAs into classrooms and 
collaborative activities with clear roles and structured processes so that agents function as socially recognized instructional partici­
pants rather than mere interfaces. The relative advantages of one-to-many contexts should be interpreted within the current evidence 
bounds to avoid over-generalization.
Finally, we outline an agenda for evidence and method, focusing on AI type and cultural context. For AI type, although no sta­
tistically detectable joint moderation was found, subgroup patterns suggest that traditional AI shows more stable effects, while esti­
mates for generative AI are more dispersed. Future work should expand the number of generative-AI studies, use more consistent task 
designs and reporting standards, and improve alignment with instructional goals through prompt strategies, interaction protocols, and 
feedback mechanisms. Hybrid agents that combine the stability of rule-based components with the expressive flexibility of generative 
modules may help balance reliability and adaptability. For cultural context, although no statistically detectable moderation was found, 
subgroup trends suggest more frequent positive effects in Western settings. Cross-national research should refine the coding of cultural 
and institutional variables, such as curriculum centralization, assessment regimes, teacher autonomy, and digital equity, and include 
learner-level covariates such as prior AI experience and technology attitudes. When samples and paradigms are more mature, inte­
grated meta-regression can be used to test joint effects and to build a more context-sensitive explanatory framework.
