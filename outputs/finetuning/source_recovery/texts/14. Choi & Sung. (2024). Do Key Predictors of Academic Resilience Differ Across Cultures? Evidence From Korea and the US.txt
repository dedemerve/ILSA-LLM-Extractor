Article
Do Key Predictors of 
Academic Resilience 
Differ Across Cultures? 
Evidence From Korea 
and the US
Yeseul Choi1
 and JeeHun Sung2
Abstract
This study aimed to compare key predictors of academic resilience in the 
distinctive socio-cultural contexts of South Korea and the United States, 
employing a machine-learning-based random forest technique. Using 
PISA 2018 data, we focused on students in the bottom 25% of family 
socioeconomic status, totaling 1,654 in South Korea and 1,175 in the US. 
The results showed that individual factors, such as meta-cognition, joy of 
reading, self-concept of reading, and work mastery, were ranked highly in 
both countries. On the other hand, self-efficacy and students’ expectation 
of higher education were specific to Korea, while fixed mindset and meaning 
in life were specific to the US. Based on these findings, this study highlighted 
the need for culturally responsive policies to nurture academic resilience 
among disadvantaged youth.
Keywords
academic resilience, sociocultural context, disadvantaged youth, machine 
learning, comparative analysis

Introduction
Academic achievement is important for youth development, as it is strongly 
associated with school adjustment and later success in life. However, aca-
demic achievement has been shown to be strongly related to family socioeco-
nomic status (Sirin, 2005; von Hippel et  al., 2018), and overcoming this 
educational inequality across family backgrounds has been one of the most 
worldwide challenging issues. Surprisingly, within socioeconomically chal-
lenged contexts, certain students achieve high academic success, which 
researchers have captured in the concept of academic resilience (Morales & 
Trotman, 2004; OECD, 2010). Identifying the factors that promote academic 
resilience is crucial for developing effective policies and interventions to sup-
port disadvantaged students and thus mitigate inequalities in educational 
outcomes.
In resilience theory, researchers have paid attention to the influence of 
cultural norms and values in hindering or promoting resilience (Ungar et al., 
2007). Regarding this, Volante and Klinger (2023) caution against the risks of 
“policy borrowing” by applying the concept of academic resilience to educa-
tion systems that differ significantly from the cultural and historical context 
of the host society. This underscores the need for a deeper exploration into 
how academic resilience can be nurtured within nations of diverse socio-
cultural backgrounds, aligning with practical policy, and program interven-
tions. However, despite the accumulating body of research on personal and 
environmental factors influencing academic resilience, there is few compara-
tive analyses that investigate how these factors manifest similarly or differ-
ently across cultures.
While some recent studies have undertaken cross-cultural comparisons, 
but they have some limitations. First, few studies have conducted in-depth 
comparative analyses of countries with different cultures in the East and 
West. Previous research predominantly conducts comparisons within the 
same Asian culture (e.g., Cheung et al., 2014; Sandoval-Hernández & 
Białowolski, 2016) or classifies Eastern and Western countries into collective 
categories (e.g., Özcan & Bulus, 2022). For instance, Özcan & Bulus (2022) 
present a constraint as it oversimplifies and categorizes countries with dis-
tinct socioeconomic backgrounds, including economic magnitude and popu-
lation, into binary cultural classifications. This approach labels the United 
States and Finland as individualistic nations, while designating Hong Kong, 
Saudi Arabia, and Brazil as collectivistic counterparts. Such act of clustering 
and contrasting a wide array of countries encounters limitations in unraveling 
the intricate interplay of each unique socio-cultural context.
Second, the methodologies employed in previous studies are mainly based 
on selecting a small number of factors and comparing their statistical

significance. With the proliferation of international academic achievement 
data such as PISA published by the OECD, the potential for cross-border 
comparisons has expanded. However, even recent studies (e.g., Cheung, 
2017; Özcan & Bulus, 2022) are still dominated by approaches rooted in 
traditional regression analysis, which cannot include an extensive list of vari-
ables due to multicollinearity issues. It has an inherent limitation in selecting 
a limited number of variables for comparability.
This study addresses the limitations of these existing studies in two ways. 
First, it conducts an in-depth comparative analysis using the cases of South 
Korea and the United States, which possess unique characteristics of East 
Asian and Western cultures, respectively. South Korea, recognized for its out-
standing academic achievements in international assessments, stands as a top-
performing education system in East Asia (Teng et al., 2018). Meanwhile, the 
United States, traditionally known as a Western powerhouse, is acknowledged 
for its advanced education system that respects diversity and employs a holis-
tic approach (Shin & Koh, 2005). Due to these characteristics, both countries 
have demonstrated great interest in benchmarking each other’s education sys-
tems. However, previous studies indicate the possibility that fundamental dif-
ferences in socio-cultural environments between the two countries may 
influence the mechanisms of academic resilience (Choi, 2023; Kim & Park, 
2006; Pomerantz et al., 2008). Therefore, this study compares these two coun-
tries to provide insight into the factors influencing academic resilience in 
diverse cultural and educational contexts. By examining these two countries, 
this study also aims to offer critical reflections on the issues of international 
policy borrowing related to academic resilience.
Second, this study utilizes the recently developed machine-learning-based 
random forest technique. The random forest approach has the advantage of 
estimating the priority of closely related variables while avoiding the prob-
lem of multicollinearity, even with a large number of variables (Rothacher & 
Strobl, 2023). Therefore, in this study, we employed the random forest 
method to exploratively estimate the most influential variables in two coun-
tries, and compare the common and differential variables among the foremost 
predictors, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of academic resilience 
within the two nations.
Put together, the purpose of this study is to explore the similarities and 
differences between the top predictors of academic resilience in two distinct 
countries, South Korea and the United States. In doing so, this study contrib-
utes to the field by examining the predictors of academic resilience within the 
unique socio-cultural contexts of Korea and the United States, especially 
through the utilization of a new methodological technique that enables a 
comprehensive understanding of the large set of variables. Furthermore, the

The Concept of Academic Resilience
Academic resilience refers to the process or outcome of achieving aca-
demic success despite challenging circumstances (Morales & Trotman, 
2004; M. C. Wang et al., 1994). Since 1970s, developmental psychologists 
have been exploring the phenomenon of positive adaptation in the face of 
adversity or risky environments that hinder human development within the 
concept of resilience (Garmezy, 1991; Masten et al., 2021; Rutter, 1987; 
Werner & Smith, 1982; Zimmerman, 2013). In line with these studies, the 
concept of academic resilience focuses on the educational context of school 
adjustment and learning, which is crucial during the child and youth devel-
opmental period.
In research, academic resilience is usually measured through two primary 
criteria: risk factors and successful academic development, as established in 
resilience research (Masten, 2001; Windle et al., 2011). Although researchers 
have employed various criteria to operationalize academic resilience, many 
studies generally consider the family environment (often indicated by family 
socioeconomic status) as a risk factor due to its significant impact on aca-
demic achievement, and a certain level of high academic performance is used 
as a benchmark for successful adaptation (Rudd et al., 2021; Tudor & Spray, 
2017). For instance, the OECD (2018) and Agasisti et al. (2018) identified 
academically resilient students as those who fall within the bottom 25% of 
the socioeconomic status distribution but achieve scores in the top 25% or 
above level 3 for literacy and mathematics.
Resilience research has underscored a socio-ecological perspective that 
resilience is not solely shaped by individual traits, but also by the intricate 
influence of multidimensional environmental aspects (Masten, 2015; Ungar 
et al., 2013; Winfield, 1994). The promotion of academic resilience has also 
highlighted not only cognitive and non-cognitive factors within individuals 
but also the pivotal role of environmental components, such as family and

school, which exert a strong influence on academic achievement (M. C. 
Wang et al., 1994). Moreover, there is a growing interest in how the distinct 
socio-cultural norms and values of various countries impact academic resil-
ience (Li et al., 2017; Özcan & Bulus, 2022). Therefore, to comprehend the 
factors that foster academic resilience, it is essential to grasp the diverse lay-
ers of individual, environmental, and cultural elements within the societal 
context.
Individual and Environmental Factors for 
Academic Resilience
Previous studies have highlighted the strong connections between factors 
known to influence academic development and academic resilience. 
Individual factors such as cognitive aspects like meta-cognitive strategies 
(Koyuncu & Firat, 2020; F. Wang et al., 2022), as well as non-cognitive fac-
tors like positive self-efficacy, academic self-concept (Borman & Overman, 
2004; Cheung, 2017; Gabrielli et al., 2022; Waxman et al., 2003; Waxman & 
Huang, 1996), and joy of learning (OECD, 2011; F. Wang et al., 2022), have 
been found to be strongly linked to academic resilience.
Among environmental factors, the role of support from family and teach-
ers and school environments has been identified as crucial for academic resil-
ience (Fang et  al., 2020). For instance, students who exhibit positive 
engagement and attitudes toward school, and high feeling of school belong-
ingness are more likely to possess higher academic resilience (Alva, 1991; 
Borman & Overman, 2004; Finn & Rock, 1997; García-Crespo et al., 2021; 
F. Wang et al., 2023; Waxman et al., 1997). Furthermore, teacher instruction 
and support, a safe, orderly, and disciplined school climate have been recog-
nized as crucial protective factors fostering academic resilience (Agasisti 
et al., 2018; Erberber et al., 2015; Gabrielli et al., 2022; García-Crespo et al., 
2021; Sandoval-Hernández, & Białowolski, 2016).
Academic Resilience Across Different Cultures
Recently, interest in the factors that influence academic resilience has been 
expanding to less studied cultures. In particular, there is a growing body of 
research on East Asian cultures, which have gained international attention for 
their high academic achievements (Cheung, 2017; Cheung et al., 2014, 2022; 
Li et al., 2017; Sandoval-Hernández & Białowolski, 2016). Researchers have 
conducted comparative analyses of factors influencing academic resilience 
groups in East Asian countries that share a Confucian culture. For instance,

Youth & Society 56(7)
Cheung et al. (2014) discovered that joy of reading and meta-cognition were 
common factors influencing high reading achievement among disadvantaged 
students in Asian countries like Shanghai, Hong Kong, Singapore, and Korea. 
On the other hand, when it comes to the difference between disadvantaged 
resilient and non-resilient students, there are some different factors, such as 
immigration status and home language, which are only significant in Hong 
Kong but not in other countries, and the expected level of education, which is 
significant in Hong Kong, Korea, and Singapore but not in Shanghai. These 
results show that even in culturally similar Asian countries, there are some 
differences in the factors that affect academic resilience.
On the other hand, studies have also been conducted to compare and ana-
lyze countries from different cultures in the West and Asia. Recently, Özcan 
and Bulus (2022) categorized 34 countries, including 30 countries in North 
America and Europe as individualist countries and 34 countries in Latin 
America and Southeast Asia as collectivist countries, to analyze whether the 
factors affecting academic resilience in reading are different in the two cul-
tures. The study found that disciplinary climate in test language lessons, joy/
liking reading, perception of reading competence and meaning in life were 
significant protective factors in both cultures, while perception of difficulty 
in reading, perception of competitiveness at school, and perspective-taking 
were influential factors in different cultures. Yet, there are few studies that 
have conducted in-depth comparative analyses between contrasting countries 
such as Eastern and Western cultures.
Socio-cultural Contexts in South Korea and the  
United States
For a comparative analysis of academic resilience in Eastern and Western 
cultures, this study specifically focuses on South Korea and the United States 
as two countries that epitomize these cultural differences. Both nations dem-
onstrate a keen interest in benchmarking their education systems; neverthe-
less, they exhibit significant socio-cultural contrasts, impacting academic 
achievement, and the education system.
In terms of socio-cultural aspects, South Korea is traditionally rooted in 
collectivism and Confucian values. This Confucian heritage cultural founda-
tion leads to a strong focus on academic excellence and education, driven by 
societal and familial expectations (Lam et  al., 2002). Furthermore, South 
Korea’s socio-economic development, especially in the historical aftermath 
of the Korean War, has been characterized by rapid economic recovery and 
development, with a strong emphasis on human resource development and

education to ensure national competitiveness (J. K. Lee, 2006). The rigorous 
study culture and fierce competition among students emphasize the impor-
tance of standardized testing, especially for university admissions. This 
demanding environment and a strong emphasis on academic success are 
linked to intensive private education outside of schools (known as “shadow 
education”), raising significant societal concerns about educational dispari-
ties between different social classes (S. Lee & Shouse, 2011). Additionally, 
there are heightened concerns about high levels of stress and the well-being 
of students.
In contrast, the United States champions individualism and diverse per-
spectives, valuing personal freedoms and creativity. This cultural ethos 
extends to its education system, which aims to cater to a range of students’ 
talents and interests (Shin & Koh, 2005). The U.S. education system has long 
attempted to integrate a wide array of subjects, extracurricular activities, and 
practical skills to foster holistic development and critical thinking. However, 
the country’s ethnic diversity has raised concerns about significant achieve-
ment gaps among historically marginalized groups, non-English speakers, 
and social classes, as well as high school dropout and relatively low academic 
performance in international comparisons (Braun et al., 2010; Reardon, 2011; 
Rumberger, 2011). In response to this, education reform in the US, despite 
many criticisms, has introduced performance-based accountability to improve 
academic performance (Morgan, 2016). For instance, policies like No Child 
Left Behind focused on a national curriculum and standardized testing, simi-
lar to the approach in Korea (Baker, 2003; J. K. Lee & Park, 2014).
These socio-cultural differences are likely to influence the beliefs and 
practices associated with academic success, shaping the mechanisms that 
influence academic resilience in both countries. For instance, in Western cul-
tures such as the United States, there is an emphasis on integrating various 
competencies rather than solely focusing on cognitive academic performance 
(Yang & Rettig, 2003). In contrast, previous research (Kim & Park, 2006; 
Pomerantz et  al., 2008) demonstrated that South Korean students pursue 
good academic performance for filial piety and social success and tend to 
believe that hard work is crucial for academic success, irrespective of innate 
ability. Moreover, they persevere, even if they have a less positive perception 
of themselves compared to students in the West.
Indeed, a recent study (Choi, 2023) showed that the motivation for 
Korean disadvantaged students to work hard at their studies stems from 
Confucian ideas, including filial piety toward their parents and a desire to 
achieve social success by attending university. These students are often pre-
occupied with excelling academically for its own sake, rather than focusing 
on life experiences or ultimate life goals. These findings highlight the

as disadvantaged students’ perceptions of academic success significantly 
influence their value systems. Therefore, an empirical comparison of the 
factors that promote academic resilience in two countries would provide 
interesting insights into cultural influences.
Methods
Data and Sample
This study utilized PISA data from the OECD to compare protective factors 
for academic resilience between Korea and the US. PISA is an international 
survey conducted every 3 years, which assesses knowledge and skills of 
15-year-old students in reading, mathematics, and science across OECD 
countries. The study sample included a total of 6,650 Korean and 4,838 US 
students who participated in the PISA 2018 survey. Among them, we 
selected students who were from the bottom 25% of the index of Economic, 
Social, and Cultural Status (ECSC), which is a composite measure of stu-
dents’ family background, home educational resources, and parental occu-
pation and education to compare students’ international socioeconomic 
status, in each country. The final sample has 1,654 and 1,175 students, 
respectively in Korea and US.
Measures
Dependent variables.  Based on the previous studies (Agasisti et  al., 2018) 
using PISA data, this study measured academic resilience as achieving at or 
above level 3 in reading literacy despite belonging to the bottom 25% of the 
ESCS distribution in each country. Literacy is the most fundamental skill for 
achieving and developing knowledge and skills in school and beyond, mak-
ing it a critical skill for academic resilience. In particular, the PISA 2018 
cohort focused on variables related to literacy. Level 3 in reading literacy 
means that students can understand and compare texts of moderate length, 
deal with implicit information and identify the main idea in a text. PISA 2018 
estimated students’ proficiency in reading literacy by plausible values that 
are based on a statistical model that takes into account the uncertainty and 
variability of the test scores. Plausible values are important because they pro-
vide a more accurate estimate of students’ proficiency than simply using the 
raw test scores. Therefore, this study used 10 plausible values (PVs) of read-
ing literacy generated by OECD. The dependent variable of this study is a 
binary variable, which resilient students were coded as 1 and non-resilient 
students were coded as 0.

Independent variables.  Based on the literature review, this study selected the 
individual and environmental factors that affect academic resilience. In the 
Supplemental File, variable codes from PISA data, descriptions, the number 
of items, and details for each item and scale are presented. Each variable was 
subjected to verification by the PISA working group, ensuring high levels of 
reliability and validity (OECD, 2020). The Cronbach’s alpha for composite 
variables employed in both nations falls within the range of .74 to .95, attest-
ing to the robust reliability of the measurements. The respondents of the vari-
ables used in this study are all students, reflecting their perceptions of various 
aspects such as their personal traits, parents, teachers, and schools.
Individual factors include eudaemonia: meaning in life (EUDMO), general 
fear of failure (GFOFAIL), joy/like reading (JOYREAD), mastery goal orienta-
tion (MASTGOAL), meta-cognition: assess credibility (METASPAM), summa-
rizing (METASUM), self-efficacy (RESILIENCE), perception of competence 
(SCREADCOMP), perception of difficulty (SCREADDIFF), fixed mindset 
(ST184Q01HA), students’ educational expectation (ST225Q06HA), subjective 
well-being: positive affect (SWBP), meta-cognition: understanding and remem-
bering (UNDREM), work mastery (WORKMAST).
Environmental factors include attitude toward school: learning activities 
(ATTLNACT), adaptation of instruction (ADAPTIVITY), sense of belonging 
to school (BELONG), competitiveness (COMPETE), teacher-directed instruc-
tion (DIRINS), disciplinary climate in test language lessons (DISCLIMA), 
parents’ emotional support perceived by student (EMOSUPS), perception of 
competitiveness at school (PERCOMP), perception of cooperation at school 
(PERCOOP), perceived feedback (PERFEED), other language at home 
(ST022Q01TA), teacher’s stimulation of reading engagement perceived by 
student (STIMREAD), perceived teacher’s interest (TEACHINT), teacher 
support in test language lessons (TEACHSUP).
Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics and the t-test results of each vari-
able to compare resilient and non-resilient students in Korea and the US. 
Based on the average of PV1 through PV10 of reading achievement, 52% 
(860 out of 1,654 students) in South Korea and 40.3% (473 out of 1,175 stu-
dents) in the US demonstrated academic resilience. The t-test results showed 
significant differences between resilient and non-resilient students in terms 
of various individual and environmental factors. While similarities existed 
between Korea and the United States, differences also emerged.

Next, this study utilized a random forest analysis to identify the most 
influential predictors for academic resilience in both Korea and the US, 
thereby comparing these leading predictors across the two countries. Random 
forest, a machine learning approach introduced by Breiman (2001), combines 
multiple decision trees for purposes of classification or regression, effec-
tively handling intricate interactions and multiple predictors within large-
scale datasets. Random Forest techniques are known to outperform traditional 
statistical methods in predicting the classification of a dependent variable 
(Casanova et al., 2014; Churpek et al., 2016; Couronné et al., 2018). While 
traditional methods such as linear regression and logistic regression are sus-
ceptible to multicollinearity problems as the number of predictors in the 
model increases, the random forest method can be effectively employed even 
with a large number of predictors (Rothacher & Strobl, 2023). Due to these 
advantages, the random forest method has recently been used to explore the 
prioritization of predictors that strongly influence the dependent variable in 
fields with a large number of highly correlated independent variables, such as 
social science or education data (Alamri et al., 2020; Gutiérrez et al., 2019; 
Hardman et al., 2013). Indeed, this method has been actively employed in 
recent studies to analyze factors affecting academic achievement and resil-
ience as well (Bernardo et al., 2021; F. Wang et al., 2022, 2023). Therefore, 
this study employed the random forest method, which is suitable for explor-
ing and comparing variables with a greater impact on academic resilience 
across various factors in two countries.
The “randomForest” package in R 4.2.2 was used with the number of trees 
set to 5,000 considering error rate convergence. The default values for “node-
size” (the minimum size of terminal nodes) and for “mtry” (the number of 
variables randomly sampled as candidates at each split) were used. To evalu-
ate the performance of the model, the value of sensitivity, specificity, and 
accuracy were used. The variable importance was assessed using the 
“%IncMSE,” which presents the percentage increase in mean square error for 
each variable when that variable is permuted. The variables with high 
%IncMSE values were considered to be the important predictors of academic 
resilience. Random forest models were fitted separately to each of the depen-
dent variables calculated by 10 plausible values, and variable importance was 
assessed using the %IncMSE measure. The importance rankings of the vari-
ables calculated with 10 plausible values were scored and averaged, and the 
final variable importance ranking was calculated and presented. Missing val-
ues were imputed with the mode for categorical variables and the median for 
continuous variables using “na.roughfix,” as suggested by Breiman (2001) 
for datasets with less than 20% missing values.

Discussion
The present study aimed to examine the major variables that influence aca-
demic resilience in Korea and the United States. The findings reveal that 
there are some common predictors of academic resilience in both countries. 
However, there were also some variables that were unique to each country, 
which suggests that there are some cultural and contextual differences that 
influence academic resilience.

Common Top Factors in Korea and the US
This study reveals that certain common factors significantly influence aca-
demic resilience in both Korea and the United States. Individual factors, such 
as meta-cognition, joy of reading, self-concept of reading, and work mastery, 
were ranked highly in both countries. Scholars of resilience have long recog-
nized that overcoming adversity and achieving academic and emotional 
development does not necessarily require exceptional factors, but rather, 
these individual cognitive and non-cognitive factors that are essential for 
shaping personal development (Masten, 2001). Our results also demonstrated 
that even in cultures as diverse as Korea and the United States, the key per-
sonal variables that impact reading development are common factors with a 
strong effect on the academic resilience of disadvantaged students. This high-
lights the critical need to focus on and develop foundational skills that influ-
ence academic performance across different contexts to foster academic 
resilience in disadvantaged students.
While these personal factors emerged as the top predictors, factors related 
to home or school ranked lower than personal factors in either country. 
However, it is important to note that this does not diminish the significance 
of environmental influences for academic resilience. Extensive prior research 
(e.g., Waxman et al., 2003) has consistently shown that environmental fac-
tors, including family and school environments have a significant impact on 
the academic achievement of disadvantaged students. These environmental 
factors may play a mediating or moderating role, exerting a decisive influ-
ence on the cognitive and non-cognitive abilities that affect academic perfor-
mance. Although the random forest technique used in this study cannot 
analyze these complex interactions, our preliminary analysis using the t-test 
indicates that there is a significant relationship between academic resilience 
and home/school factors, suggesting the importance of environmental fac-
tors. Therefore, it is crucial to consider the impact of environmental factors 
on academic resilience and further investigate how these factors interact with 
individual characteristics in different countries.
Differential Factors in Korea and the US
This study highlights cultural and contextual differences between the two 
countries in terms of the predictors of academic resilience. Self-efficacy and 
students’ educational expectation were specific to Korea, while fixed mindset 
and meaning in life were specific to the US.
First, this study showed that fixed mindset is strongly important for being 
academically resilient for disadvantaged students in the US, but it may not

be critically related to Korean students. Sun et al. (2021) found that Chinese 
students tend to have a fixed mindset about intelligence, while US students 
are more likely to have a growth mindset. They showed that US student’s 
math grade were positively related to growth mindset, while for Chinese 
students, this association was slightly negative. Similarly, based on PISA 
2018 results, the United States is one of the countries that has the widest 
performance gap in reading where students with a growth mindset scored 
around 60 points higher in reading than their counterparts. However, East 
Asian countries such as Korea, Macau, and Taiwan, however, growth mind-
set was not as strongly associated with academic performance as in most 
OECD countries (Schleicher, 2019). These previous findings suggest that 
fixed mindset is not strongly related to academic outcomes in Asian contexts 
compared to Western cultures.
Possible explanation about this huge gap of importance is the cultural dif-
ferences in how students conceptualize the relationship between intelligence 
and academic achievement. Confucian heritage cultures (CHC), such as 
Korea, attribute academic success in school to hard work regardless of their 
intelligence since they were inherent to accept the value of effort in school 
success. Due to this different cultural belief systems, CHC students are less 
likely to associate tightly between academic achievement and malleable 
intelligence than US students do. Thus, our study findings also highlight the 
importance of understanding the nuanced cultural differences in intelligence 
mindset and that impact academic resilience.
Second, the association between academic resilience and meaning in life 
was found to be stronger in the United States than in Korea. Examining the 
results of this study, academically resilient students among disadvantaged 
students displayed lower levels of meaning in life compared to non-resilient 
students in both countries. Contrary to the conventional belief that higher 
levels of meaning in life would correspond to higher academic achievement, 
the PISA report (Schleicher, 2019) revealed that high-achieving students in 
OECD countries generally exhibited lower values in the index of meaning in 
life compared to low-achieving students. However, a comprehensive expla-
nation of the negative relationship between meaning in life and academic 
performance based on PISA data has not been clearly established or exten-
sively researched thus far.
One possible explanation, when considering the survey items regarding 
meaning in life in the PISA data (e.g., “My life has clear meaning or purpose; 
I have discovered a satisfactory meaning in life; I have a clear sense of what 
gives meaning to my life”), is that academically successful students may 
experience more pressure than satisfaction in their lives, and the time con-
straints associated with academic immersion may impede their pursuit of

Youth & Society 56(7)
life’s purpose. Alternatively, students with a clear sense of life’s purpose may 
also have higher interest or aspirations in areas beyond academics. 
Additionally, Korea, where academic achievement is generally emphasized 
among students, and such achievement is considered a measure of life’s 
meaning, represents a cultural context that differs from Western cultures like 
the United States, where diversity in life choices is often valued. Due to these 
reasons, it is possible that disadvantaged students in Korea may have a less 
correlated perception of their academic achievements and life’s purpose com-
pared to their counterparts in the United States. Further research is needed to 
provide a more detailed explanation, but the findings of this study suggest 
that the mechanism through which life purpose is translated into academic 
achievement may differ somewhat between Korea and the United States, par-
ticularly among disadvantaged students.
Third, self-efficacy demonstrated a relatively stronger association with 
academic resilience in Korea compared to the US. Previous research has 
highlighted the differences in how self-efficacy is perceived among students 
from Western and Eastern cultures. For instance, one study has shown that 
students from collectivist cultures tend to exhibit lower self-efficacy com-
pared to students from individualistic cultures (Klassen, 2004). Indeed, the 
analysis of PISA data (Schleicher, 2019) also reveals that, on average, 
Korean students exhibit lower self-efficacy than their American counter-
parts. However, an intriguing finding is that, unlike in the US, only in Korea 
was a significant correlation observed between self-efficacy and reading 
achievement. In line with these results, our study also demonstrates that the 
impact of self-efficacy on academic resilience varies in intensity between 
the two countries.
One possible explanation for this phenomenon is that self-efficacy may 
interact with academic achievement differently in the two countries. In 
Korea, where academic performance is emphasized more than a broad range 
of activities, self-efficacy may have a greater influence on the academic 
achievement improvement of disadvantaged student groups. Alternatively, 
Klassen (2004) discussed that students from Western cultures tend to per-
ceive self-efficacy more positively based on optimism and self-enhancement, 
while students from Asian cultures tend to evaluate self-efficacy based on 
self-criticism stemming from modesty. In this case, students in Asian cultures 
may have a more realistic assessment of their actual abilities compared to 
students in Western cultures, which may result in a stronger association with 
academic achievement. Therefore, these findings suggest the need for a 
deeper understanding of the mechanisms through which students’ self-effi-
cacy is linked to academic resilience, taking into account the cultural envi-
ronments of each country.

Finally, the stronger association between students’ educational expecta-
tions and academic resilience in Korea compared to the US may reflect 
cultural differences in aspirations for higher education. While both coun-
tries value higher education, there are some differences in how Koreans and 
Americans view its importance (Shin & Koh, 2005). In the US, pursuing 
higher education is often seen as a way to develop important life skills such 
as independence and intercultural competence, which can lead to personal 
growth and success in the modern world. On the other hand, in Korean 
society, higher education is considered as the primary means to achieve 
success, upward mobility, and social status. As a result, for many Korean 
students, attending college itself can be a goal of their studies. This differ-
ences in perspective may explain why the desire to attend college is more 
strongly linked to academic achievement among disadvantaged students in 
Korea than in the US.
Implications
The findings of this study have important implications for promoting aca-
demic resilience. First, policymakers and educators should consider the influ-
ence of cultural differences when creating intervention plans. For instance, 
self-efficacy and growth mindset are known to have positive effects on aca-
demic achievement or resilience, and many intervention programs have been 
developed on these ideas. However, our study shows that these factors might 
affect academic resilience differently in different socio-cultural backgrounds, 
such as Korea and the United States. This suggests that intervention programs 
that promote academic resilience should understand that the mechanisms by 
which the targeted factors work may differ depending on cultural norms and 
social and educational environments.
Second, this study contributes to the understanding of cultural influences 
in the field of resilience. Recent resilience studies have emphasized the 
importance of understanding how individuals navigate cultural norms and 
environments to positively adapt to challenges. The results of this study sup-
port these notions, as we compared two countries with distinct cultural back-
grounds, providing empirical evidence for the potential influence of culture 
on academic development. However, there is a lack of in-depth research con-
sidering various cultural settings within the field of academic resilience. 
Thus, this suggests the necessity to advance academic resilience theories 
through comprehensive research across specific cultural contexts.
Third, from a broader policy perspective, the results of this study highlight 
the significance of tailoring policy to suit the specific cultural and social 
characteristics of the target region. This resonates with the concerns raised by 

1256	
Youth & Society 56(7)
Volante and Klinger (2023), who caution the potential risks of adopting edu-
cational strategies from other countries without considering the cultural and 
historical context when applying the notion of academic resilience. 
Policymakers need to recognize that a uniform strategy for enhancing aca-
demic resilience might not yield the desired outcomes across different cul-
tural and educational settings. Hence, the findings of this study emphasize the 
significance of developing a culturally sensitive policy framework to foster 
academic resilience among disadvantaged youth.
Limitations and Future Research
Despite the significance, this study has some limitations. First, this study 
used cross-sectional survey data, thus the results should be interpreted with 
caution to infer causal relationships. To analyze whether the identified factors 
are indeed causal predictors, further research using longitudinal data or 
experimental designs is necessary.
Second, this study utilized limited variables included in the PISA data 
from an international perspective, which means it may not capture the full 
spectrum of unique variables that influence academic resilience in specific 
countries. Also, while this study selected key variables that have been identi-
fied in previous research to influence academic resilience, some variables 
with a high proportion of missing data in one country were excluded for more 
precise estimation. Therefore, to capture highly unique variables that influ-
ence academic resilience in different countries or cultural contexts, more in-
depth studies are needed, and future research should consider developing 
internationally recognized indicators to include these variables.
Third, this study focused on discussing eight variables that were ranked as 
highly influential based on the random forest technique. This method is valuable 
for exploratory analysis, allowing the comparison of top predictors in two distinct 
cultures. However, it has the limitation of assessing the relative influence of each 
variable without displaying the dynamic interactions between them. For instance, 
while environmental variables may exhibit less influence than individual factors 
in the results, they can still exert a significant impact through mediating or mod-
erating interactions. Therefore, future research should concentrate on examining 
how environmental variables interact with individual variables using diverse 
techniques to achieve a more comprehensive understanding.
Conclusion
Recent perspectives in resilience theory have shifted to focus on the cultural 
context that influences academic resilience. The study findings highlighted 
the need for culturally responsive policies to nurture academic resilience

among disadvantaged youth. In particular, the study urges educators, policy-
makers, and researchers to avoid a “one-size-fits-all” approach that borrows 
policies from other countries and to instead recognize the unique contextual 
factors of each country. Recognizing these potential cross-cultural differ-
ences in fostering academic resilience will be crucial to ensuring that disad-
vantaged students in various countries receive the customized prevention and 
intervention support necessary to overcome challenges and achieve academic 
success.

Author Biographies
Yeseul Choi is an Associate Research Fellow in the Office of Educational Welfare 
Research at the Korean Educational Development Institute (KEDI). Her primary 
research interests include educational policies, practices, leadership, and school 
improvement strategies to promote student growth and learning. Currently, her 
research focuses on uncovering the factors and mechanisms that influence academic 
resilience and improvement among disadvantaged students and exploring effective 
educational policies and interventions. 
JeeHun Sung earned his MS in Lifelong Education at the University of Seoul 
National University and is currently a researcher at the Korea Institute for Curriculum 
and Evaluation. His research focuses on educational data-mining and competency-
based assessment.
