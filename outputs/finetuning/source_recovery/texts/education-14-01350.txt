Abstract: The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) is a global survey conducted
by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) to assess educational
systems by evaluating the academic performance of 15-year-old school students in mathematics,
science, and reading. In PISA 2022, 13,437 students from Australia participated in the test. While
the PISA main questionnaire assesses the subject knowledge, the student background questionnaire
solicits contextual information such as school climate, learner characteristics, and socioeconomic
status. This study analyses how these contextual variables predict student achievement using the
machine-learning models Ridge Linear Regression, K-Nearest Neighbours, Decision Trees, eXtreme
Gradient Boosting, and Support Vector Machines, and it reports the evaluation matrices and the most
accurate model in predicting student achievement. The analysis shows that contextual variables
are associated with student achievement and account for 42% of the variance in achievement. In
addition to evaluating multiple machine-learning regressors, Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP)
analysis is conducted to explain the model predictions and evaluate feature importance. Using SHAP
analysis, this paper demonstrates how educators and school administrators may effectively interpret
the machine-learning results and devise strategies for student success.

1. Introduction
Secondary education coincides with early adolescence, a critical period marked by
significant psychological, biological, and social growth [1]. The importance of supporting
students during secondary education cannot be overstated. Ensuring that students receive
the necessary support during this phase of their education is crucial for their academic
and personal development. It is a fundamental concern for teachers, school administrators, politicians, and policymakers worldwide. However, students’ academic success is
determined by an intricate interplay of various factors, including school climate, learner
characteristics, economic and social status, etc. A positive school climate, characterized by a
nurturing social atmosphere and effective teaching practices, promotes student engagement
and enhances learning outcomes [2]. Learner characteristics could also significantly impact
determining academic achievement [3]. Furthermore, studies revealed that economic and
social status could be a crucial indicator of academic achievement [4]. Understanding the
interactive effects between these factors is essential for promoting academic success among
students from diverse backgrounds.

2. Theoretical Background
2.1. School Climate
The school climate plays a crucial role in shaping the overall learning experience
for students and significantly influences their academic achievement. The term “school
climate” generally refers to the social and emotional aspects of the school environment that
contribute to the overall demographic characteristics and cultural interactions within the institution. The connotation of the concept has acclimated acceptance as the contextual factor
encompassing the “total environment quality within a school building” [5,6]. Historically,
approaches to assessing the attributes of educational environments could broadly involve
either contrasting the alpha press (external observers’ observations) and beta press (milieu
participants) observational data and systematic coding of classroom communication and
events [7] or applying naturalistic inquiries like case studies, questionnaire surveys, and
interpretive research design [8]. Later, Murray’s taxonomy was extended by an idiosyncratic viewpoint, in which individuals all possess both private beta press (environment)
and consensual beta press (shared view) of a particular educational setting [9]. Since
personal and consensual beta press could be contradictory to one another, researchers need
to decide whether their research scope will involve the perceptions of individual students
or the overall knowledge of the whole class. By the late 1970s, researchers revealed that
school climate was positively connected with the difference in the mean outcomes between
schools, even when adjusting for race, SES, and other demographics [10]. School climate is
the most significant indicator of how students feel about the social circumstances within
the school.
Since the 1990s, studies have naturally located the unit of school climate as the school
as a whole, whereas the individual classroom could serve as the appropriate measurement
unit since students and teachers spend a tremendous amount of time in it. In this regard,
school climate was found to be linked with various aspects, including school achievement [11]; crime and delinquency [12]; and students’ emotional attachment, bonding,
connectedness, and achievement [13] Acknowledging the multi-facetedness of the constructs of school climate, its definition expands to include both the social and physical
domains of school, such as quality and character of school life, norms, goals, values, interpersonal relationships, and teaching and learning practices [14]. Notably, apart from
these common domains, some additional research areas appeared over time: academic
outcomes [15,16]; social relationships [17]; order, safety, and discipline [17,18]; school connectedness [19,20]; and specific educational settings, such as science laboratories [21] and
computer-assisted classrooms [22]. These domains offer additional clues for understanding
the composition of school climate and concurrently guaranteeing individuals’ social and
emotional well-being.
More recently, school climate has been seen as an essential indicator of student performance and overall well-being. Studies on school climate value and celebrate the diversity,
creativity, and individual learning environments that strengthen student motivation and
engagement [23,24]. A positive and supportive social and emotional school climate fosters
a conducive learning environment. When students feel safe, respected, and valued, they are
more likely to engage in the learning process [25]. Strong, positive relationships between
teachers and students are crucial for effective learning [26] and for encouraging a sense of
ownership and empowerment among students [27]. This sense of non-cognitive engagement could lead to individuals’ increased effort, persistence, and a higher commitment to
academic success.
A wide range of research methods have been used to evaluate and investigate school
climate: both sole methods of assessing and mixed methods approaches combine qualitative
and quantitative methods. Fraser [28] described three major research types in the area:
(1) Revealing the consistent, solid, and positive associations between the quality of students’
learning environments and their cognitive and non-cognitive outcomes; (2) Evaluation
of educational programmes and national curriculum reform; (3) Practical application of
students’ feedback for the improvements of teachers’ practices and the overall classroom

environments. Despite the call for mixed methods, the most frequently used approach in
the past has been that of highly validated questionnaires that investigate students’ and

host of emerging emotional and non-cognitive dimensions. Potentials include secondary
analysis of existing data, cross-national studies, links between different environments, and
typologies of learning environments [28].
2.2. Learner Characteristics
School climate could affect teaching and learning practices in the classroom environments. The knowledge and experience of the non-cognitive factors could also translate
into the classroom, and individuals are thus influenced by their current beliefs and intentions surrounding teaching [29]. Learner characteristics refer to an individual’s systematic
approach and the methods they use to process information, a metric for evaluating their
learning capabilities [30]. Central to this discussion is that every learner possesses unique
attributes that influence learning processes and outcomes. Concerning the context of this
study, learners’ characteristics mainly refer to individuals’ non-cognitive competencies.
Emotionally stable and socially connected students are better equipped to handle stress,
anxiety, and other challenges. Physical and mental health are essential for effective learning,
concentration, and problem-solving abilities, which are crucial for academic success [31]. Social and emotional factors are integral components of student achievement. They contribute
to the development of well-rounded individuals who are both academically proficient and
equipped with the skills and resilience needed for success in various aspects of life.
An increasing number of educators, trainers, and policymakers believe that social and
emotional skills are essential for a complete education and post-school success. Alzahrani
et al. [32] state that social and emotional competence affects children’s academic achievements and behavioural development. Studies have also revealed the impact of social
and emotional skills on various learning outcomes, such as better school achievement in
reading, writing, critical thinking, vocabulary, and behavioural skills [33–35]. Students
with positive emotions toward school and learning tend to be motivated and engaged
in the educational process. A sense of purpose, enthusiasm for learning, and a belief in
one’s abilities contribute to increased effort and persistence, leading to higher academic
achievement. Social and emotional factors contribute to developing skills such as communication, problem-solving, decision-making, and conflict resolution, which are all applicable
in academic and real-world contexts. Recognizing and addressing social and emotional
factors in education is essential for fostering a holistic approach to student development
and achievement.
The early attempts to measure personality traits included the Big Five Inventory
(BFI) developed by John et al. [36]. This 44-item English BFI was designed to provide an
efficient and flexible assessment of five personality dimensions: extraversion, agreeableness,
conscientiousness, neuroticism (emotional stability), and openness to experience. The Big
Five taxonomy offers a concise and comprehensive summary of individual differences in
all personality characteristics. Furthermore, the personality characteristics encompassed by
the Big Five are predictive of various outcomes. Since its introduction, numerous studies
have confirmed its effectiveness and utility in educational and workplace settings.
3. Specific Constructs Selected for the Study
3.1. Teacher–Student Interaction
The connection between teachers and students is critical in shaping students’ academic
success and well-being. Substantial research literature emphasizes the profound impact of
positive teacher–student relationships on students’ achievement [37]. The positive feedback
loop between teachers and students could increase student motivation, engagement, and
high-quality teaching. On one hand, students may be more motivated to learn when they
can feel mutual trust with teachers [38]; on the other, teachers are more likely to adapt

Educ. Sci. 2024, 14, 1350

4 of 21

higher-quality teaching practices to cater for the unique needs and interests of students [37]
Indeed, studies have confirmed that positive teacher–student relationships are connected
to higher grade point averages and test scores for students throughout their school years,
beginning in kindergarten and extending through high school [39,40]. Other researchers
found that positive teacher–student interaction was strongly linked to student engagement
but modestly related to achievement [41]; while others identified that its effects could
narrow the achievement gap of different ethnic groups of students [42].
3.2. Sense of Belonging
Psychologists view belonging as a fundamental motive in human beings [43]. A strong
sense of belonging is crucial for student success, impacting their academic performance,
engagement, and overall well-being [44]. A wealth of research underscores the positive
correlation between belonging and achievement, highlighting its significant role in fostering
a thriving learning environment. A higher sense of belonging could make students seek
out and use campus resources to a greater extent. Belonging may also help students buffer
from stress, improving mental health and lifelong success [45]. However, these benefits
are not equally shared, and the literature indicated that underrepresented racial–ethnic
minority and first-generation college students report a lower sense of belonging [46]. The
belonging functions differently regarding individuals’ diverse goals, life circumstances,
and student characteristics [47,48].
3.3. Feeling Safe
Feeling safe in school is a prerequisite for thinking and learning [49]. It is not just
a matter of physical safety but also the individuals’ emotional and social well-being.
Feeling unsafe in a classroom environment may hinder students’ higher-level thinking
and decrease academic performance [49,50]. It has been demonstrated that the school and
classroom environment may immediately affect students’ sense of safety, and it could be
more detrimental than external events that occur elsewhere [51]. However, feelings of
safety may vary between students of different races and ethnicities.
3.4. Teacher Support
Teacher support is crucial in creating a positive learning environment and promoting
students’ academic success [52,53]. It covers various behaviours and actions demonstrating
care, concern, and belief in each student’s potential—a comprehensive understanding
of how teacher support could impact student learning and school adaption. Previous
studies have linked teacher support with academic achievement [35]; positive academic
emotions [54], well-being [55], and mental health in youth [56]. Meanwhile, investigating
teacher support in different countries and cultures to coincide with adolescents during
their secondary education deserves more attention. Exploring teacher support in various
subjects is particularly needed.
3.5. Disciplinary Climate
The disciplinary climate in a school significantly impacts student achievement, creating
a learning environment that either fosters or hinders academic success [57]. It encompasses
the established rules, expectations, and methods to address student behaviour. Studies
measuring disciplinary climate at the classroom level indicate that disciplinary climate
has a significant relationship with cognitive outcomes among elementary students, and
gender differences exist in mathematics achievement [58]. Gender-specific perceptions of
the classroom environment could partially contribute to its association between disciplinary
climate and academic achievement [57].
3.6. Perseverance
Perseverance, often described as the ability to persist in facing challenges and setbacks,
is a crucial factor influencing students’ achievement [59]. Current literature highlights

the impact of perseverance on various aspects of academic success. Research consistently
highlights the critical link between perseverance and academic success, demonstrating its
effects in multiple ways. Results revealed that perseverance was more positively associated
with achievement in East Asian cultures than in Western cultures [60].
3.7. Cooperation
Cooperation in the classroom, where students work together towards shared goals,
significantly impacts student achievement. This collaborative instructional approach could
foster a dynamic learning circumstance where students can benefit from each other’s
knowledge, perspectives, and skills. Numerous studies have investigated the favourable
effects of cooperation on various aspects of student learning and development: cooperative
learning approaches are not extensively and faithfully used in classes due to teachers’
knowledge, beliefs, and some implementation challenges [61].
3.8. Emotional Control
Emotional control, the ability to regulate one’s emotions effectively, plays a vital role
in student achievement [62]. It empowers students to navigate the challenges of academic
life, maintain focus, and persevere in the face of adversity. Numerous studies have explored assessing the impact of emotional control on various aspects of student learning
and development. These include assessing the emotional climate in STEM classroom settings [63], investigating associations between emotional circumstances and teacher–student
relationships and attitudes [64] and links between home and school environments [65].
3.9. Self-Efficacy
Self-efficacy, introduced by Albert Bandura, refers to individuals’ confidence and
belief in their ability to achieve success in specific situations or accomplish a particular task.
Researchers found that academic self-efficacy mediates the association between school
climate and academic achievement in Israeli secondary students [2]. Analysing student
self-efficacy and achievement from different theoretical frameworks [66], self-efficacy is a
prime determinant in mathematics learning [67]. Although numerous studies have shown
a connection between school climate and academic performance, the mechanisms and
contributing factors behind this relationship still need to be examined.
3.10. Index of Socioeconomic Status
Socioeconomic Status (SES), encompassing factors like income, education level, and
occupation, significantly shapes students’ learning experiences and ultimately influences
their academic achievement. Research has consistently demonstrated a complex relationship between SES and student outcomes, highlighting direct and indirect effects. The
research findings indicate a moderate positive correlation between SES and academic
performance [68]. Although the literature often reports a positive correlation between
family SES and student academic achievement, the strength of this relationship depends
on various social contexts and education systems [69]. Another study showed that the
relationship between SES and academic achievement has gradually weakened over the
past few decades. Additionally, findings indicate that, among Chinese students, there
is a stronger correlation between Socioeconomic Status (SES) and language achievement
(e.g., Chinese and English) compared to science/math achievement or general academic performance [70]. However, the effects of different SES components on academic achievement
can vary [4].
4. Method
4.1. Data
The data for the study reported in this article were drawn from the Organization
for Economic Cooperation and Development’s (OECD) PISA 2022 database. The PISA
2022 assessments focus on measuring the knowledge and skills of 15-year-old students in

three core areas—mathematics, reading, and science. In mathematics, this latest iteration
emphasized mathematics reasoning and its application to real-life challenges. A total of
13,437 students in 743 schools in Australia completed the assessments representing 265,200
students (an estimated 90% of the total student population in this cohort).
4.2. Analytic Strategy
This study used machine-learning algorithms to predict mathematic achievement from
school climate, learner characteristics and economics, and the social and cultural status of
students. There has been a debate about whether or not machine-learning approaches are
better than conventional statistical methods. While there is no unanimous opinion, some
studies have shown that machine-learning techniques provide more accurate results. For
example, Ye et al. [71] report that machine-learning algorithms are superior to conventional
regression models in predicting the risk stratification of patients. Froud et al. [72] indicate
that, when predicting the quality of life and performance of school children in Norway,
machine learning outperformed linear regression. According to Bzdok et al. [73] statistics
draws population inferences from a sample, and machine learning finds generalizable
predictive patterns (p. 233).
In this study, five machine-learning regression models are used to predict mathematics
achievement. These are Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbour (KNN),
Decision Tree (DT), Elastic Net (ELN), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). Support
Vector Machine is a powerful supervised learning algorithm used for both classification
and regression tasks in machine learning. SVM performs well with data that have many
features, especially when compared to some simpler algorithms [74]. KNN is a simple
yet effective machine-learning algorithm used for both classification and regression tasks.
KNN is conceptually easy to understand, implement, and interpret by examining k nearest
neighbours and can be used to gain insight into why a specific prediction was made. A
Decision Tree is another powerful and adaptable technique used in machine learning
and data analysis for both classification and regression tasks. DT is often praised for its
simplicity, interpretability, and ability to handle complex relationships [75]. Elastic Net is a
regularized regression algorithm in machine learning that combines the strengths of two
popular techniques, L1 and L2 regularization, to achieve better performance and feature
selection. XGBoost is a powerful and versatile machine-learning algorithm that has become
a popular choice for many tasks due to its accuracy, speed, and flexibility. It builds upon
the concept of gradient boosting, which sequentially combines multiple weak learners
(decision trees) to create a stronger model. Each new tree focuses on correcting the errors of
previous trees, leading to a progressive improvement in accuracy [76].

4.4. Data Analysis
An open-source programming language and software environment R and R Studio
(Version 4.3.2) were used to perform the analysis [77]. Significant relationships were found
with all features and mathematics achievement (p < 0.01). Machine-learning package e1071
for the Support Vector Machine model, kknn for K-Nearest Neighbour, rpart for Decision
Tree, glmnet for Elastic Net, and xgboost for the eXtreme Gradient Boosting model were used
to perform the regression analysis. R package caret was also used for some models. A
stratified random sampling technique was used to split the dataset into 70% training and
30% testing sets, ensuring proportional representation of all classes, as the dataset size
warranted sufficient data for both training and unbiased evaluation.
Hyperparameter tuning is crucial for machine-learning models. Hyperparameters
control model complexity and influence its ability to capture patterns without overfitting
or underfitting the data. Tuning can also help uncover model biases and explore hyperparameter combinations that reduce biased outcomes [78]. Each model is tuned to obtain the
best complexity parameter before the prediction.
5. Results

Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) are used to
measure the performance of each model. While MSE and RMSE evaluate the difference
between predicted and actual values, MAE and MAPE measure the average magnitude of
the difference values and actual values. Table 1 shows the machine-learning models and
their evaluation metrics.
It was found that prediction using XGBoost has the lowest MSE and RMSE values
and the highest R2 and correlation coefficient. The model accounts for 43% of the variation

the school climate; learner characteristics; and economic, social, and cultural status of
students. In overall performance, XGBoost achieves the best performance across most
metrics, indicating its potential for the most accurate predictions among these models.
The SVM model follows closely, also demonstrating good performance. KNN, DT, and
ELN exhibit slightly lower performance, but they are still worth considering in the model
evaluation process.

To evaluate the performance differences among multiple machine-learning models,
we conducted the Friedman test by utilizing several performance metrics, including Mean
Squared Error (MSE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE),
Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), R-squared (R2 ), and correlation coefficients. The
Friedman test yielded a test statistic of 29.49 and a p-value of 0.00004922. Given that the
p-value is significantly smaller than the standard significance threshold (e.g., 0.05), we can
confidently conclude that there are statistically significant differences in the performance of
the machine-learning models across the assessed metrics.
Following the significant result from the Friedman test, we proceeded with the Nemenyi post-hoc test to identify which specific models differ in performance. The Nemenyi
test facilitates pairwise comparisons among all models to determine if the observed differences are statistically significant. In this test, the Critical Difference (CD) value was found to
be 1.39. Based on this value, we concluded that there is a statistically significant difference
in the performance of the models. Moreover, the results indicate that the XGBoost model
notably outperforms the other models, demonstrating superior performance across the
evaluated metrics.

5.2. Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP) Analysis
This section reports Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP) analysis to explain XGBoost
as the choice regressor to predict the achievement score. As provided in Table 1, our
10 predictors are teacher–student interaction (TSINTER), sense of belonging (BELONG),
feeling safe (FEELSAFE), teacher support (TEACHSUP), disciplinary climate (DISCLIM),
perseverance (PERSEVE), cooperation (COOPER), emotional control (EMOCON), selfefficacy (MATHEFF), and index of socioeconomic status (ESCS). The achievement score
(ACHIEVE) is the variable to be predicted.

First, the following section explains the grid search method to identify an effective set
of hyperparameter values for the XGBoost regressor. Using the effective set of hyperparameters identified by the grid search method, we train the XGBoost regressor. Second,
the section expounds on the global SHAP analysis to evaluate how the features affect the
prediction of achievement score, ACHIEVE. Third, the section discusses the local SHAP
analysis to determine how the features affect the prediction of ACHIEVE for a selected set
of feature values. The section concludes with an interaction study between two features,
MATHEFF and ESCS.
5.3. Grid Search for Hyperparameter Tuning and Training
The grid search method for hyperparameter tuning is employed in this analysis. While
several parameters can be tuned for the XGBoost regressor, we select the learning rate,
maximum depth, and the number of estimators for hyperparameter tuning. We consider
0.01 and 0.015 as the possible values of learning rate. We select small learning rate values
because they lead to more accurate model training updates. We examine 4, 5, and 6 as the
maximum tree depth values, which prevent model overfitting and reduce bias. We consider
the values 500, 600, and 700 for the number of estimators as plausible candidates for the
number of trees in the model. We use Scikit-learn’s Grid Search method (GridSearchCV) to
test the above parameter values.
The Grid Search method identifies the best hyperparameters for the XGBoost regressor
training as follows: learning rate equal to 0.01, maximum tree depth equal to 4, and the
number of estimators equal to 700. We split the data using the 70/30 rule, meaning that
70% of the data is used for model training and 30% is used for model testing. Our XGBoost
regressor achieves a Mean Squared Error (MSE) equal to 5296 and an R2 value equal to 43%
on the testing data.
5.4. Global Interpretation
The global interpretation in SHAP analysis refers to understanding the overall importance of each feature in a machine-learning model. It provides a high-level view of the
factors that are most influential in driving the model’s predictions. In summary, the global
interpretation provides a general overview of the model’s behaviour, helping us to understand which features are most critical and how they work together to make predictions.
The testing data set (30% of the entire dataset) is used to conduct the SHAP analysis.
Figure 1 shows the bar plot that provides the relative importance of the features. Specifically, it rates the features using their mean of absolute SHAP values. The observation
presented in this figure aligns with the domain under study that considers a cohort of
Australian students.
The SHAP analysis indicates that efficacy in mathematics learning has the strongest
influence (40.72) over achievement scores, followed by the student’s social, cultural, and
economic status (18.37). The other factors, including BELONG, DISCLIM, and TSINTER,
have similar importance for the achievement score prediction. For the Australian cohort,
sufficient statistical evidence suggests that their achievement scores can be predicted by a
simpler XGBoost regressor comprising MATHEFF and ESCS as the principal predictors.
The inclusion of the other features depends on the analysis. This simple model (trained
using a small set of important features) reduces the chances of model overfitting. Section 5.6
presents a detailed analysis of this simpler XGBoost regressor.
Figure 2 provides a global analysis of how all ten features in the XGBoost regressor

5.5. Local Interpretation
SHAP analysis offers both global and local interpretations of models. Globally, it
explains the overall importance of each feature. Locally, it focuses on how specific features
contribute to the predictions for individual samples. Local interpretation provides a
detailed explanation of the model’s behaviour for individual samples, explaining why a
specific prediction was made and how different features contributed to that outcome.
This section presents a local SHAP analysis highlighting individual features’ effects
on achievement score prediction. This analysis enables us to assess the students individually, highlighting the various pain points that adversely affect their achievement scores.
This analysis provides insights to educators and care providers to better cater to their
students’ needs.
Figure 3a–e provide the local SHAP analysis, where the SHAP values for five different
combinations of feature points are presented. Figure 3a,b provide the SHAP values of
feature points where the achievement scores (f(x)) are less than the expected value, E[f(x)].
Figure 3a shows a scenario where a student’s poor mathematics learning efficacy (MATHEFF), student–teacher relationship (TSINTER), and feeling of safety (FEELSAFE) severely
impact the achievement score (413 vs. 496 average). This observation indicates that this student needs academic and personal support for their success. Similarly, Figure 3b highlights
that the selected student has a low ESCS score, which adversely affects their achievement
score. This observation may prompt the care managers to devise accommodations for the
student that account for their socio-economic status.
Figure 3c highlights a scenario where a student scores near average achievement
score. For this student, the major pain points include low ESCS and COOPER scores.
Consequently, this student must be evaluated for their struggles due to socio-economic
status. Additionally, educators must analyse this student’s ability to cooperatively work
with other students. Figure 3d,e show two scenarios where two students have scored well
above the average (540 and 613, respectively). An analysis of such students highlights the
aspects that work well to promote high achievement scores. For these students, almost
all the features positively impact their performance, out of which MATHEFF and ESCS
stand out. This observation indicates that educators must focus on mathematics learning
efficacy and socio-economic and cultural status to promote high achievement scores. This
analysis demonstrates how educators and school administrators can employ the local
SHAP analysis to study individual students and identify pertinent solutions to obtain
better student achievement outcomes.

5.7. Additional Machine-Learning (ML) Analysis
In this section, we conduct additional (ML) analysis wherein we contrast the performance of the XGBoost regressor when trained using the important features (Figure 1)
obtained via SHAP analysis against the XGBoost regressor trained using all features. We
also evaluate the ML regressors trained using the important features identified by SHAP.
For the performance evaluation, we use a 70/30 data split for the training and testing data
and employ the following metrics for performance evaluation: Mean Squared Error (MSE),
Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), and R2 value. We
conduct this analysis using Python 3.8.19 and the scikit-learn library.
Figure 1 ranks the features based on their importance, as determined by the global
SHAP analysis. While MATHEFF and ESCS are among the most important features for
achievement score prediction, the other features, including BELONG, DISCLIM, and TSINTER, have similar importance. We specifically consider a scenario where educators wish to
focus on MATHEFF (SHAP importance 40.72), ESCS (18.37), and TSINTER (4.76). Using
these three features, we fit a simpler XGBoost regressor and achieve an R2 score equal to
0.401 and MSE equal to 5611.16. Figure 6 shows the residual plot for the resulting XGBoost
regressor when tested on a different set comprising 500 sample points. The figure shows
that the residuals (the difference between the test achievement score values and predictions)
are randomly scattered around the baseline versus the predictions. This observation implies

6. Discussion and Conclusions
This study explored the machine-learning approach to predicting mathematics achievement from school climate; learner characteristics; and the economic, social, and cultural
status of students. The school climate factors include student–teacher relationships, sense
of belonging, feeling safe in school, disciplinary climate, and teacher support. Emotional
characteristics include perseverance, cooperation, self-efficacy, and emotional regulation.
Social characteristics measure economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS). PISA 2022
student data from Australia are used for the analysis.

Educ. Sci. 2024, 14, 1350

17 of 21

6.1. School Climate
Various aspects of school climate, including student–teacher interactions and disciplinary practices, were identified as moderate predictors of mathematics performance.
Meanwhile, a sense of belonging to the school, teacher support, and feeling safe are low
predictors. When it comes to the link between school climate and student achievement, it is
important to remember that a positive climate can act as a powerful support system, fostering not only academic success but also overall well-being. In particular, student–teacher
interaction plays a crucial role in the educational process, impacting not just academic
achievement but also students’ social-emotional development and overall well-being.
Positive teacher interactions can nurture students’ sense of belonging, acceptance, and
competence, boosting their confidence in their abilities. A supportive teacher–student
relationship can also create a safe and comfortable learning environment, reducing stress
and anxiety for students. The skills and experiences gained through positive interactions
with teachers, such as communication, collaboration, and adaptability, prepare students for
success in their future careers and personal lives.
6.2. Learner Characteristics
Among the learner characteristics measured, students’ mathematic self-efficacy
emerged as the strongest predictor of their mathematic achievement. Studies show that
self-efficacy, that belief in one’s own ability to succeed in specific tasks, plays a crucial role
in academic achievement. Numerous studies have shown a positive correlation between
self-efficacy and academic achievement. Students with higher self-efficacy tend to score
better on standardized tests, have higher GPAs, and are more likely to graduate on time.
Students who believe they can succeed are more likely to set ambitious goals, persevere
through challenges, and expend effort on their studies. Students with high self-efficacy are
more likely to choose challenging tasks and embrace academic risks. They believe they
can handle complex material and do not shy away from opportunities to learn and grow.
It is important to nurture self-efficacy among students. By fostering self-efficacy, schools
and educators can empower their students to become more motivated, engaged, and
successful learners.
In this study, the extent to which students cooperate with each other in the class had
moderate predictive value to achievement. A cooperative atmosphere in the classroom
replaces the pressure of competition with a sense of shared responsibility and support. This
can lead to reduced anxiety and a more enjoyable learning experience, allowing students to
focus on understanding concepts rather than fearing failure. When students work together,
they are more likely to be actively involved in the learning process. They can share ideas,
ask questions, and build on each other’s understanding, leading to a deeper and more
meaningful learning experience. In this study, cooperation shows a moderate predictive
value to achievement. By creating a culture of collaboration and mutual support, schools
can equip their students with the skills and mindset they need to succeed, not only in their
academic pursuits but also in life.
Emotional control, or the ability to manage your emotions in a healthy and productive way, plays a crucial role in academic achievement. Students who can manage their
emotions are more likely to be respectful, empathetic, and supportive towards their peers
and teachers. This creates a collaborative and encouraging learning environment, which
can further enhance academic success. Perseverance, the quality of persisting despite
difficulty, setbacks, or discouragement, is an essential ingredient in the recipe for academic
achievement. Those who are persistent are less likely to give up easily. Perseverance is
having the grit and determination to keep going, even when faced with repeated failure.
There are many ways schools can promote the culture of perseverance in the classrooms.
Teachers can serve as role models by demonstrating their own perseverance in the face of
challenges, sharing their own experiences of overcoming difficulties and showing a passion
for continuous learning. Recognizing and applauding students’ hard work, dedication,

Educ. Sci. 2024, 14, 1350

18 of 21

and persistence, not just their final results, reinforces the value of continuous effort and
motivates them to keep trying.
6.3. Economic, Social and Cultural Status
The economic, social, and cultural status of the students has the second-highest predictive score toward achievement, but it does not mean that these factors determine a
student’s success. They can create certain advantages or disadvantages to influence their
educational endeavour. It is obvious that higher-income families often have more resources to invest in their children’s education, such as providing private tutoring and
educational materials. These resources could provide students with a learning advantage. Students living in poor neighbourhoods may have limited access to quality education and technology, which may put them at a disadvantage compared to students from
wealthier neighbourhoods.
Teachers face complex challenges in closing the achievement gap between students
from different socioeconomic backgrounds, but dedicated teachers can play a crucial role in
making a difference. Teachers need to build trusting relationships by developing positive
relationships with students from disadvantaged backgrounds and demonstrating empathy
and understanding. This creates a safe environment where students feel comfortable
asking for help and expressing themselves. Teachers can also adapt teaching methods
and materials to resonate with students’ cultural backgrounds and lived experiences. This
makes learning more relevant and engaging for students from diverse backgrounds.
Closing the achievement gap is not a one-time solution but an ongoing process that
requires continuous effort and collaboration. By employing these strategies and constantly
adapting their approach, teachers can create a more equitable learning environment and
empower students from all backgrounds to reach their full potential.
