The gender system: A cross-national perspective
Rafael Quintana *
School of Education and Human Sciences, University of Kansas, USA

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Gender
Graphical models

Quantitative researchers lack frameworks for conceptualizing and operationalizing gender as
different from sex. The present study uses the concept of a probabilistic boundary, derived from
graphical models, to define the constitutive elements of gender. This approach aligns with a
constructivist framework that views gender as a set of multidimensional attributes distinguishing
men and women in specific societies. Using this framework and data from the 2018 Program for
International Student Assessment (PISA, 2018), I identified the constitutive elements of gender
across 72 countries and 74 constructs using a structure learning algorithm. Additionally, I
examined whether country-level characteristics predict the presence of competitiveness and
empathy—two stereotypically gendered traits—as constitutive elements of gender. The findings
suggest that access to educational and economic opportunities and the country's region are
predictive of the presence of competitiveness and empathy, respectively. I discuss the advantages,
assumptions, and limitations of the proposed approach to operationalize gender.

1. Introduction
In social science research, the construct of gender plays a critical role in both theoretical and empirical inquiries. Gender is
considered a central construct that influences countless facets of humans’ lives and is crucial for comprehending a wide range of social
phenomena. Moreover, even when gender is not the primary focus of the investigation, it is routinely included in statistical models as
an important covariate or control variable. Yet traditional methods of conceptualizing and measuring gender have long raised
theoretical and methodological concerns. Key problems include the conflation of sex and gender, the tendency towards essentialist
interpretations, and more generally the difficulty of representing a complex social system that varies across time and space with a
single binary variable.
Sociologists have long argued that gender should not be reduced to a set of psychological traits but understood as a social system
that includes individual characteristics, cultural expectations, and social institutions (Risman, 2018; Risman and Davis, 2013). The
presumption is that social structures external to the individual, such as cultural beliefs or institutions like the legal or education
systems, play crucial roles in explaining gendered behavior (Lorber, 1995; Martin, 2004; Ridgeway and Correll, 2004; Risman, 2004;
Risman and Davis, 2013). Consequently, gender is seen as being produced across different levels—individual, interactional, and
institutional—and is deeply embedded within society (Connell, 1987; Lorber, 1995; Martin, 2004; Risman, 2004).
The complexity of gender, viewed as a multi-level and context-dependent system, makes it challenging to operationalize and study
using quantitative tools. This is reflected in the fact that, despite a clear conceptual distinction between gender and sex, quantitative
researchers keep using undifferentiated binary measure of sex/gender. Such practices can lead to essentialist interpretations and the

determined phenomena differ from biological traits. More generally, without a clear definition and operationalization of gender as a
social construct —distinct from sex, understood as a biological variable—, the term “gender” loses its analytical utility. Without a clear
operationalizing of gender, it becomes difficult to understand how gender varies across time and space, or how specific social institutions shape gendered behavior.
In this study, I propose a method for conceptualizing and operationalizing gender in a context-specific manner that enables the
investigation of how social environments shape gendered behavior. Importantly, the focus is on attitudes and behaviors that distinguish men and women across contexts; the study does not address gender identity or gender expression.
The approach proceeds in two steps. The first step, which is the main focus of the study, involves identifying a set of individual-level
attributes, such as attitudes and behaviors, that differentiate men and women within particular societies. This approach aligns with
constructivist frameworks that conceptualize social categories in terms of their “constitutive elements” (Kohler-Hausmann, 2018; Roth
et al., 2023; Sen and Wasow, 2016). The second step examines whether macro-level features predict the presence or absence of these
constitutive elements across societies. This perspective aligns with viewing gender as a social system, where societal characteristics
like cultural practices and regulations shape patterned behavior (Connell, 1987; Martin, 2004; Risman, 2004, 2018).
The proposed method begins, then, by identifying a set of individual-level attributes that distinguish men and women in specific
societies. Researchers working within constructivist frameworks have argued for moving beyond unidimensional measures of gender
toward multidimensional measures (Lindqvist et al., 2021; Morgenroth and Ryan, 2018). Thus, rather than relying on abstract and
unidimensional notions of masculinity and femininity, this approach focuses on identifying specific attitudes and behaviors, such as
empathy, competitiveness, or risk-taking, that might differentiate men and women within particular cultural contexts (Bauer, 2023;
Lindqvist et al., 2021; Nielsen et al., 2021).
However, it is not clear how to identify the elements or dimensions constituting gender. The problem is, fundamentally, how to
delineate the boundaries of gender. Sex categories are defined by clear biological markers including external genitalia, secondary sex
characteristics, gonads, chromosomes, and hormones (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2022). Gender, on
the other hand, is defined as a complex system emerging from an indefinite number of social norms, practices, behaviors, interactions,
and attitudes operating at different levels, from cultural expectations and gendered policies to individual identities and behaviors, that
are associated with sex traits (Martin, 2004; Ridgeway and Correll, 2004; Risman, 2004). The challenge is, then, to provide bounds to
gender categories amidst the vast array of differences that can exist between men and women in a specific culture. Simply put, the
challenge is to understand what the term “gender” as a social construct signifies in a given society.
In this study, I propose using probabilistic graphical models to conceptualize and operationalize the constitutive elements of
gender. Specifically, I use probabilistic graphical models to identify a set of strongly relevant variables that carry all information about
a target variable. If the target variable represents individuals’ sex, then this set of variables will (1) explain all sex differences in the
dataset, and (2) contain all information about the man/woman distinction in a specific context. This method inverts the traditional
approach: rather than using a presumably well-defined measure of sex/gender to explain a phenomenon of interest, it seeks to identify
which variables within the system “explain” the sex/gender categorical variable. The idea is to bound gender by finding the smallest set
of variables carrying all information about sex in a particular system.
The “constitutive elements of gender” are defined, then, as the minimal set of attributes that explain differences in outcomes and
are informative about sex categories in specific cultural contexts. Based on this definition, the empirical analysis addresses two
questions. First, how do the constitutive elements of gender vary across nations? Second, do country-level characteristics predict
whether specific elements are constitutive of gender? The objective is to explore how gender is constructed differently across societies,
and which societal structures predict these differences.
To address these questions, I used the 2018 Program for International Student Assessment (PISA, 2018) dataset and included 74
constructs across 72 countries or regions. PISA focuses on middle adolescence, which is a period with marked gender differences
(Chandra-Mouli et al., 2018; Heyder et al., 2021). A structure learning algorithm is used to search for the constitutive elements of
gender within each country. To interpret the results, I focus on a stereotypically masculine trait, competitiveness, and a stereotypically
feminine trait, empathy. Specifically, I investigate whether country-level features predict the presence or absence of these attributes as
constitutive elements of gender across societies. The results indicate that access to educational and economic opportunities predicts the
presence of competitiveness as a gender-differentiating factor. On the other hand, belonging to Asian countries is the strongest predictor of empathy functioning as a constitutive element of gender.
In summary, this study proposes a data-driven method for identifying the key attributes that distinguish men and women within
specific societies—what I refer to as the constitutive elements of gender. Gender is conceptualized here as the social system that
distinguishes men and women. As noted above, the study does not address gender identity or expression, nor does it seek to construct
unidimensional gender typicality scores (Ballering et al., 2024; Lippa and Connelly, 1990). Instead, the goal is to identify a set of
strongly relevant attributes related to gender within different social contexts. Identifying this set provides a foundation for conceptualizing gender as a multidimensional social construct, which in turn enables comparative analyses of how gender is constructed
across cultural contexts. While the empirical analysis illustrates this approach using PISA 2018 data, the proposed framework is readily
applicable to other datasets and research questions.
More broadly, this study contributes to the literature by introducing a methodological framework that conceptualizes gender—and,
by extension, other social categories—as multidimensional and context-dependent. The aim is not to develop a new theory of gender,
but to provide an empirical strategy for conceptualizing and measuring gender in a way that is compatible with constructivist and
multilevel theories of gender. While previous approaches have sought to move beyond binary classifications, they offer limited
guidance on how to determine which attributes should be considered constitutive of a social category. The method proposed here

2. The constitutive elements of gender
Persistent gender inequalities across a variety of health, educational, occupational, and economic outcomes have been extensively
documented (e.g., Buchmann et al., 2008; Kennedy et al., 2020; Levanon and Grusky, 2016). These “gender gaps” highlight systematic
disparities between men and women in relevant outcomes. Hence, a categorical distinction between men and women serves as a
valuable descriptive tool to quantify differences in outcomes between groups. Descriptive statistics can bring to our attention manifest
disadvantages in specific domains (Quintana and Correnti, 2020).
However, problems emerge when this categorical variable is used for explanatory purposes. Specifically, complications arise when
interpreting the “effect of gender” or when trying to explain why women and men differ in specific areas. Quantitative researchers
generally treat sex and gender as synonymous, which results in a conceptual muddle that can lead to essentialist inferences and the
perpetuation of gender stereotypes.1 This study focuses on the explanatory role of gender, rather than its descriptive function.
To explain gender inequalities, researchers have tried to identify key attributes that distinguish men and women and drive differences in outcomes. For example, there is an extensive literature suggesting that men tend to be more self-confident than women, and
that gender differences in self-confidence have important educational and occupational consequences (Exley and Kessler, 2022; Grosch
et al., 2022; Sterling et al., 2020). The question is how to think about these gender-related attributes. In particular, how are these
attributes related to gender?
Attributes such as self-confidence are often regarded as mediators, in the sense that they are influenced by sex/gender and affect
some outcome of interest. For example, self-confidence is posited as a mechanism by which sex/gender influences individuals' career
choice (Correll, 2001). However, these factors should not be considered mediators in the traditional sense. In traditional mediation
analysis, the goal is to determine how much of a treatment's effect on an outcome is transmitted through a particular mediator
(VanderWeele, 2015). This framework presents complications when applied to social categories such as gender or race because they
cannot be conceptualized as manipulable treatments.2 In summary, even if attributes such as self-confidence might explain differences
in outcomes across sex categories, they do not align with the traditional definition of a mediator.
Another important characteristic of these attributes is that they can be constitutively related to gender, i.e., part of what it means to
“be a man” or “be a woman” in a particular society (Hu and Kohler-Hausmann, 2020). In general, a constitutive claim accounts for a
system's capacities by reference to its parts and their organization (Ylikoski, 2013). Constitutive relations are central to constructivist
approaches to social categories, which treat categories as composed of specific elements—such as attributes, practices, meanings—that
together constitute what a category is in a given context (Brubaker, 2016; Kohler-Hausmann, 2018; Sen and Wasow, 2016). From this
perspective, a constructivist approach to gender (or any social category) can be understood as disaggregating the components that
constitute the category in a particular context. Rather than treating gender as a fixed, observed attribute of individual i, this approach
investigates the set of elements that jointly make up the category.3
Prominent theories of gender have conceived of gender primarily as what men and woman do –often summarized with the phrase
“doing gender” (West and Zimmerman, 1987). According to this view, gender refers primarily to the set of culturally-determined
attitudes and behaviors that distinguish men and women in a particular society (Connell, 2012; Martin, 2023; Wood and Eagly,

In Western societies, masculinity has been defined as a collection of attitudes and behaviors including competitiveness, independence, self-confidence, dominance, assertiveness, among others (Berdahl et al., 2018; Martin, 2023; Nielsen et al., 2021). On the

Culturally determined attributes such as self-confidence, competitiveness or agency have been considered, then, constitutive
features of gender (i.e., of masculinity and femininity) in Western societies. This claim has two important implications. First, it implies
that gender does not refer to an underlying “essence” that exists independently of these features, as the meaning of gender emerges
from these constitutive relations (Hu and Kohler-Hausmann, 2020). Hence, identifying these constitutive elements will clarify the
meaning of gender in a particular society. Second, the meaning of gender can change by altering its constitutive features. For instance,
the meaning of “masculinity” and “femininity” in Western societies would shift if attributes like communality and agency were no
longer associated with them. Prior research has in fact examined how definitions of masculinity and femininity have changed across
time and space (e.g., Boutyline et al., 2023; Cuddy et al., 2015; Scarborough et al., 2019).
In summary, gender is conceptualized as a collection of attitudes and behaviors which can influence outcomes, functioning
similarly to mediators. However, in the context of social categories the concept of mediation presents important challenges. Consequently, I will refer to these attitudes and behaviors simply as the “constitutive elements” (attributes, features, factors) of gender. In the
following sections, I will discuss the advantages of focusing on these elements, explain in what sense they are context-dependent, and
present a method for their identification.
3. Toward a constructivist approach to gender
Gender is commonly operationalized as a binary variable with two discrete categories: women and men. This categorical variable is
then used to explain or predict a variety of outcomes (e.g., Eagly and Wood, 2013). This way of conceptualizing gender has well-known
problems, notably the conflation of sex and gender and the promotion of essentialist inferences, i.e., the idea that differences between
men and woman —in terms of their abilities, interests, behaviors, etc.—are immutable and biologically determined (Levanon and
Grusky, 2016; Westbrook and Saperstein, 2015). Researchers have also argued that this conceptualization lags behind the current
understanding of gender as a multidimensional and context-dependent construct (Bauer, 2023; Lindqvist et al., 2021; Martin and
Slepian, 2021; Nielsen et al., 2021).
The first advantage of disaggregating gender into its constitutive elements is that it facilitates the assessment of whether specific
attributes that vary across sex categories are biologically or socially determined. Cross-cultural variation in constitutive elements
suggests that these elements are socially constructed. This variation can be leveraged to identify societal characteristics that predict
different constructions of gender.
Disaggregating gender into its constitutive elements can also deter essentialist inferences (Helms et al., 2005; Sen and Wasow,
2016). Unidimensional measures of gender, whether binary or gradational, imply an underlying essence of masculinity or femininity.
As Paechter (2006, p.260) puts it, “using ‘masculinity’ as a noun suggest that there is a thing that is being named, that masculinity is
something definite.” Treating gender as a single variable can lead to a search for the essence of masculinity and femininity. Multidimensional measures avoid this problem, as the constitutive factors are not intrinsically gendered. For example, there is nothing
intrinsically “masculine” or “feminine” about, say, self-reliance or dependability, yet these have been identified as constitutive attributes of gender (Kimmel, 2012). In other words, constructivist approaches do not define gender in terms of two (or three, or ten, or a
hundred) fixed categories. Instead, they view gender in terms of the attributes that, while not intrinsically associated with sex traits,
define gender within specific societies.
Another advantage of focusing on the constitutive attributes of gender (or other social categories like race) is that it can shed light
on the causal mechanisms driving inequalities in outcomes (Sen and Wasow, 2016; VanderWeele and Robinson, 2014). When gender is
treated as a composite variable, it obscures the multiple underlying processes that contribute to differences in outcomes. Disaggregating gender into its constituent elements allows for targeted interventions on each element, providing a more precise understanding of causal mechanisms (Quintana et al., 2025; VanderWeele and Robinson, 2014).
The final advantage of adopting a constructivist perspective is that it might point to attitudes and behaviors that were not known to
be constitutive of gender. Prior research stresses the importance of acknowledging certain attitudes and behaviors as integral components of gender. For example, competitiveness is frequently linked to masculinity (Berdahl et al., 2018; Bosson and Vandello, 2011).
That is, manhood is partly defined and demonstrated by being competitive, which affects labor market and educational outcomes and
environments (Berdahl et al., 2018; Buser et al., 2014). The authors’ claim is not simply that men are more competitive than women.
Rather, the authors suggest that individuals compete in an effort to demonstrate their manhood, and thus conform to societal gender
expectations (Berdahl et al., 2018; Bosson and Vandello, 2011). The claim is, then, that in Western societies competitiveness is a
constitutive element of masculinity.
Similar claims have been made about other attitudes and behaviors such as aggressive behaviors. Prior research suggests that

4. The gender system across nations
A notable limitation of the literature on the social construction of gender is its heavy reliance on samples from North America and
Western Europe (Connell, 2012; Fleming et al., 2015; Klein et al., 2022). However, it is well known that what is considered feminine or
masculine can vary with cultural context (Cuddy et al., 2015; Eagly and Wood, 2013; Wood and Eagly, 2015), as well as across time
(Begall et al., 2023; Boutyline et al., 2023; Diekman et al., 2005). Qualities commonly ascribed to men, such as assertiveness, or to
women, like compassion, are not universally held across cultures (Cuddy et al., 2015). For example, Gneezy et al. (2009) find that
while men are more competitive in a patriarchal society, women are more competitive in a matrilineal society. Assuming universal
gender attributes oversimplifies the complexity of gender and perpetuates essentialist beliefs. Conceptualizing gender as a system
–rather than a variable– moves the analysis of gender beyond personality traits to encompass larger societal structures (Martin, 2004;
Ridgeway and Correll, 2004; Risman, 2004; Risman and Davis, 2013).
Several theories have been proposed to explain how cultural and societal processes shape gender differences in attitudes and
behaviors. For instance, some theories claim that gendered behaviors are determined by the gender division of labor (Wood and Eagly,
2012), cultural values (Cuddy et al., 2015) or a tendency to copy self-similar others (Cross et al., 2023). In addition, notions such as
“gender norms” (Pearse and Connell, 2016), “gender stereotypes” (Ellemers, 2018), “gender ideology” (Begall et al., 2023) or “gender
roles” (Barr et al., 2023) have been proposed to explain how macro-level structures shape micro-level attitudes and behaviors.
The goal of this study is not to test any specific theory about how the constitutive elements of gender emerge in response to societal
structures. Rather, the primary objective is to identify these constitutive attributes –using a definition and assumptions described
below– and examine how these attributes vary across nations. The study is guided by two overarching principles. The first principle is
that national variations in macro-level structures (e.g., gender norms, cultural values, institutions, etc.) can lead to differences in the
defining elements of gender. The idea that gender is shaped by social and cultural factors is part of its definition, and this is why it is
referred to as a “social construction” (Krieger, 2003; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2022; West and
Zimmerman, 1987). Thus, one can expect differences in the construction of gender across societies. As Risman and Davis (2013, p.743)
put it, “just as every society has a political and economic structure, so, too, does every society have a gender structure.”
The second principle is that, just as gender, the concept of gender equality is multidimensional (Else-Quest and Hamilton, 2018;
Kurzman et al., 2019). By questioning the presence of a single latent construct of gender equality, researchers have increasingly
focused on different components or varieties of gender egalitarianism (Begall et al., 2023; Grunow et al., 2018; Knight and Brinton,
2017; Scarborough et al., 2019). For instance, a country might have high gender equality in the health domain, but low gender equality
in the economic domain (Else-Quest and Hamilton, 2018).5
This study investigates how the constitutive elements of gender vary across nations by following a two-step process. First, I
operationalize the constitutive elements of gender using probabilistic graphical models. The next section provides a more formal
definition of these elements and describe methods to identify them. Second, I examine whether country-level characteristics, such as
political, educational, occupational, or financial structures, predict the presence of specific attributes—competitiveness and
empathy—as constitutive elements of gender. The aim here is to determine if certain constructions of gender at the individual level are
more prevalent within specific societal structures.
The emphasis of this study is primarily on the first step, as current literature lacks clarity on how to operationalize gender. I argue
that the main challenge is defining the boundaries of gender, and that probabilistic models offer a principled solution to this issue.
Once the constitutive elements have been identified, the second step involves a more straightforward task of using a predictive model
to assess the strongest country-level predictors of these elements. This study is exploratory in nature and makes no causal or normative
claims.

5. Searching for the constitutive elements of gender
The constitutive elements of gender have been so far characterized as attributes that explain differences in outcomes between
women and men and that might shape the meaning of gender (i.e., masculinity and femininity) in a specific society. However,
establishing clear criteria to determine what differentiates a constitutive from a non-constitutive element of gender is not straightforward. Previous studies have found a myriad of sex/gender differences in areas ranging from cognitive skills (Else-Quest et al., 2010)
and health and wellbeing (Kennedy et al., 2020) to dietary preferences (Nakagawa and Hart, 2019) and cultural tastes (Lagaert et al.,
2017). Hundreds of meta-analyses and tens of thousands of journal articles focus on sex/gender differences in a variety of outcomes
across the social and behavioral sciences (Eagly and Revelle, 2022).6 Many of these differences are deemed important and impactful. In
addition, numerous gender stereotypes exist –for example, social psychologists have used over 100 traits and adjectives to measure
masculinity (Martin, 2023).
Given this vast array of gender differences and stereotypes, determining a definite set of constitutive features becomes a challenge.
Essentialist views on gender avoid this problem by viewing men and women as well-defined and biologically determined natural kinds.
Constructivism refutes the idea of natural kinds, and disaggregates social categories into socially-determined constitutive elements
(Kohler-Hausmann, 2018; Roth et al., 2023; Sen and Wasow, 2016). A key challenge for the constructivist approach is drawing a
boundary—determining which elements are constitutive of gender and which are not. Without clear boundaries, the concept of gender
risks losing its meaning, complicating our understanding of how gender evolves over time or varies across cultures.
A common analytic approach in the analysis of gender focuses on gender gaps, i.e., mean differences between sex categories. This
method presupposes that the gender measure, typically a binary variable, is well-defined—an assumption that brings complications
described above. Using this method to determine if an element is constitutive of gender presents additional problems. In particular, (1)
there is no clear threshold for defining a difference as “constitutive,” and (2) this approach is not sufficiently bounding, given the vast
array of documented sex/gender differences.
Now, it is worth noting that other non-essentialist or non-binary approaches to measuring or conceptualizing gender have been
proposed. One approach involves the use of gradational measures of gender self-identification—i.e., self-perceived degrees of femininity and masculinity—rather than binary categories of sex/gender in surveys (e.g., Magliozzi et al., 2016). However, this understanding of gender differs from the focus of the present study. As noted above, the emphasis here is not on gender or sex identification,
but on the attitudes and behaviors that distinguish men and women in specific contexts, irrespective of how individuals self-identify.
A method more closely related to the objective of this paper is the gender diagnosticity approach proposed by Lippa and Connelly
(1990) and implemented in several studies (e.g., Fleming et al., 2017; Mittleman, 2022; Yavorsky and Buchmann, 2019). This method
typically uses logistic regression to generate predicted probabilities of being male or female for each individual, based on a set of
covariates. These predicted probabilities are then interpreted as a unidimensional gender typicality score.
Importantly, this framework can also be used to evaluate how strongly different variables predict sex. However, as a tool for
identifying constitutive elements, this approach has notable limitations. First, there is no principled threshold for determining whether
a given association should be considered constitutive. Second, and more importantly, predictive associations may arise through
multiple pathways—direct, indirect, confounded, or via endogenous selection—which makes the results difficult to interpret.
The present study adopts a structural, probabilistic approach rather than a purely predictive one. The aim of this framework is to
identify a multidimensional set of strongly relevant variables, rather than collapsing them into a unidimensional score. Using graphical
models, I define the probabilistic boundary of sex as the minimal set of attributes that renders sex conditionally independent of all
others. This approach isolates only those variables directly tied to sex—excluding spurious, redundant, or downstream associations—which, as I argue below, aligns more closely with the aim of identifying the constitutive elements of gender.
5.1. Searching for constitutive elements using graphical models
This study uses probabilistic graphical models to identify which variables are constitutive elements of gender (Quintana, 2022).
Graphical models are powerful tools to represent in a compact way the structure of a complex system, characterized by the presence of
many interacting elements (Koller and Friedman, 2009). In particular, below I explain how graphical models can be used to identify a
set of strongly relevant variables that carry all information about a target variable.
A graphical model is a data structure composed of a set of variables (nodes) and a set of connections (edges) among the variables.
That is, given a set of random variables V = {X1 ,…,Xn }, a graph G = (V , E ) consists of a set of nodes V and a set of edges E . In a
directed graph, the edges are either Xi →Xj or Xj →Xi , and in an undirected graph all the edges are Xj − Xi . Whenever we have that Xi →
Xj ∈ E , we say that Xj is the child of Xi in G, and that Xi is the parent of Xj in G. Whenever a node is directly connected to another node,
either in a directed (Xi →Xj ; Xi ←Xj ) or undirected (Xi − Xj ) way, we say that Xj is a neighbor of Xi (Koller and Friedman, 2009).

Friedman, 2009; Pearl, 2009). The conditional independence of X and Y givenZ is commonly denoted as X ⫫ Y | Z. For instance, the
statement Sex⫫ Occupation|Competitiveness, Aspirations implies that Sex does not provide any additional information about Occupation
once we know Competitiveness and Aspirations. Or, analogously, that Occupation does not provide any additional information about Sex

Graphical models can be used, then, to find a subset of variables C = Xi ,Xj ,…, Xk , Xi , Xj , …, Xk ⊆ V , that render a target variable
S conditionally independent of all the remaining variables in the system: S ⫫ V \ C | C. In the present application, S represents individuals' sex, C represents the constitutive elements of gender, and V \ C represents the remaining variables. The target variable S
represents individuals’ sex because the gender system describes a set of socially-determined attitudes and behaviors that are built
around a two-sex difference (Ridgeway and Correll, 2004).7 Thus, C represents the subset of socially determined attitudes and behaviors that render S conditionally independent of all the other variables in the system. In graphical models, the smallest set of
variables containing information about S that cannot be obtained from other variables is referred to as a Markov blanket, denoted as
Mb(S) (Koller and Friedman, 2009). Conceptualized in this way, the constitutive elements of gender refer to the minimal set of variables that explain all the observed systematic differences across sex traits.
The Markov blanket of S is said to “shield” or “screen off” the variable S from other variables in the system (Pearl, 2009). This can be
interpreted from two perspectives, aligning with previous characterizations of the constitutive elements of gender. First, all sex differences can be fully predicted by knowing the values of Mb(S)—that is, once we know Mb(S), the man–woman (or male–female)
dichotomy does not provide any additional information. The Markov blanket can be interpreted, then, as a boundary that separates the
associations between S and the rest of the system. This interpretation is consistent with the idea that constitutive attributes explain
differences in outcomes. Second, Mb(S) contains all information about S that cannot be obtained from any other variable. This
interpretation aligns with the idea that constitutive attributes are informative about the man–woman distinction. Put differently, once
we know the constitutive elements Mb(S), the binary variable S is no longer informative.
Given the set of random variables V , one can search for the constitutive elements C of S by estimating a graphical model
compatible with the joint distribution P(X1 ,…,Xn ). As shown in Fig. 1, in a directed acyclic graph (DAG), Mb(S) consists of S’s parents,
S’s children, and S's spouses (i.e., other parents of S’s children). Now, even though these three nodes are part of Mb(S), it is important to
differentiate them as not all might qualify as constitutive factors. In particular, the spouses of S might not be considered as constitutive
elements. In Fig. 1, nodes X5 and X6 are marginally independent of S but become associated with S upon conditioning on common
outcomes X3 and X4 , which are referred to as collider variables. Thus, the variables X5 and X6 are related to S due to collider bias, also
known as “endogenous selection”, and therefore should not be considered constitutive elements.
To illustrate why these variables should not be considered constitutive elements of gender, consider the relationship between
children's gender and family socioeconomic status (SES), which one can assume are marginally independent. Prior research suggests
that there are gender differences in competitiveness, and that competitiveness varies with SES (Boneva et al., 2022). These relationships can be represented with the DAG Sex → Competitiveness ← SES. This DAG implies that SES and Sex will be associated by
conditioning on Competitiveness, which operates as a collider. This association can be considered spurious, as there is no theoretical
justification for considering SES a constitutive element of gender.
The constitutive elements of gender can be defined, then, as the variables that are directly related to S in a directed graph G, which
is the same as the neighbors of S, Nbr(S) in G. In Fig. 1, Nbr(S) = {X1 , X2 , X3 , X4 }. The set Nbr(S) is a subset of Mb(S), and excludes
variables that are associated with S through a collider variable. By defining the constitutive elements of gender as Nbr(S), rather than
the parents and children of S, one disregards the direction of the relationship. As previously mentioned, it can be problematic to
consider sex as a cause. However, I use directed rather than undirected graphs (popular in network psychometrics, e.g., Isvoranu et al.,
2022) to discard nodes spuriously associated with S through collider bias.
Following a constructivist approach, the present study focuses on the constitutive elements of gender—defined as Nbr(S)—rather
than on the binary variable S. It is worth noting, however, that the relation between Nbr(S) and gender can be conceptualized in
different ways. Some authors understand social categories as higher-level abstractions and their constitutive elements as lower-level
features (Huang et al., 2025; Mossé et al., 2025), while others specify gender as a latent construct measured by its constitutive indicators (Quintana, 2022). Providing a fully specified account of this constitutive relation is important but lies beyond the scope of the
present study.
In summary, the main challenge faced by constructivist approaches to gender—and social categories in general—can be described
as a bounding problem, i.e., differentiating between more or less relevant attributes. I argue that a principled solution to this problem
involves focusing on the constitutive elements of gender, operationalized as Nbr(S). This set of variables explains all sex differences in
outcomes and provides all information available about the male-female distinction. In the empirical application, I search for the

6. Competitiveness and empathy as gender-related traits
Different approaches can be adopted to investigate the constitutive elements of gender. One approach focuses on how gender is
constructed within specific cultures, aiming to understand how the constitutive elements are produced and expressed in that culture.
For example, there are in-depth qualitative analyses of masculinity and femininity within Latino (Mirandé, 1997) and Anglo-American
(Kimmel, 2012) cultures. The goal of this approach is to provide a nuanced and comprehensive portrayal of a specific gender system.
A different approach focuses on specific constitutive elements of gender and their potential variation across cultural contexts.
Instead of providing a comprehensive view of a gender system, the goal is to compare gender systems with respect to a specific
constitutive element. This approach facilitates exploring whether societal features predict the presence of certain constitutive
attributes.
This study adopts the second approach by investigating whether the constitutive elements of gender vary across nations and if
specific societal structures —such as political, educational, occupational, or financial systems—predict the presence or absence of
particular attributes. Specifically, I focus on competitiveness, a stereotypically masculine trait, and empathy, a stereotypically feminine trait. I focus on these two attributes because (1) they are considered stereotypically masculine and feminine traits, (2) there is
robust empirical evidence supporting sex differences in these attributes, and (3) these attributes are thought to influence relevant
individual and social outcomes. Consequently, understanding which societal features predict the presence or absence of these attributes as gender-differentiating traits becomes a relevant question. Below, I provide a brief overview of the literature on sex differences
in competitiveness and empathy, the differences in outcomes these traits are thought to explain, and whether the presence of these
attribute is influenced by societal features.
6.1. Competitiveness
A large body of observational and experimental research documents that in Western countries women tend to be less competitive
than men (Markowsky and Beblo, 2022; Niederle and Vesterlund, 2011). Women's distaste for competition and reluctance to select into
competitive environments has been found to predict gender differences in education and labor market outcomes (Buser et al., 2014;
Reuben et al., 2017). In particular, differences in competitiveness have been considered an important factor contributing to the underrepresentation of women in leadership roles and in math and science-intensive disciplines (Buser et al., 2014; Reuben et al., 2017).9
Yet sex differences in competitiveness are not universal. Gender gaps in competitiveness vary widely across societies, and in some

6.2. Empathy
Stereotypical feminine traits include prosocial attitudes and behaviors involving empathy, care, and nurturance towards others
(Beutel and Marini, 1995; Eagly, 2009). These attitudes and behaviors align with the traditional gender role of women as the primary
caregivers both in the home and in the marketplace (Beutel and Marini, 1995; Eagly, 2009). In particular, there is ample evidence on
sex differences in empathy, which is broadly defined as the ability to share and understand the affective states of others (Kamas and
Preston, 2021; Rochat, 2023).
The finding that women tend to be more empathic than men is thought to explain several individual and social phenomena. For
example, empathy has been proposed as a mechanisms explaining sex differences in aggression (Vachon et al., 2014), political attitudes (Sirin et al., 2017) and charitable giving (Willer et al., 2015).
Prior research has also examined how cultural contexts might influence empathy levels. Specifically, studies suggest that individuals in Eastern cultures tend to be more empathetic than those in the West (Atkins et al., 2016; Boven et al., 2013). This difference
is attributed to the collectivist nature of Eastern cultures, which emphasizes social relationships, in contrast to the individualistic
nature of Western cultures (Atkins et al., 2016; Boven et al., 2013).
Finally, it is worth noting that empathy is considered to consist of distinct components: an affective component that involves individuals’ emotional reactions, and a cognitive component that involves the capacity to understand how another person feels (Atkins
et al., 2016; Boven et al., 2013). As explained in the measures section, this study includes a measure of the cognitive component of
empathy, often referred to as “perspective-taking.”
7. Data
The data comes from the 2018 Program for International Student Assessment (OECD, 2019), which is a is a worldwide study
conducted by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). PISA 2018 gathered data on about half a million
15-year-old school students in 80 countries and economies. Lebanon and North Macedonia were excluded due to substantial missing
data, hindering reliable estimation of the constitutive elements (see below for details). Kosovo was also excluded because of missing
country-level data. To avoid double-counting samples, regions for which there is national data were removed. For example, the
samples from two Russian regions, Moscow and Tatarstan, were removed because there is a different representative sample of Russia.
All the remaining countries and regions were retained, leading to a sample of 570,876 students across 72 nations or regions.
The PISA 2018 dataset has three main advantages for the purposes of this study. First, it contains representative samples of a wide
range of countries. Second, as described below, it gathered measures of several gender-relevant attitudes and behaviors. Third, the
study focuses on middle adolescence, a period with marked gender differences (Chandra-Mouli et al., 2018; Heyder et al., 2021).

independence based on covariance relationships rather than on cross-country comparisons of mean differences. To the extent that noninvariance primarily reflects intercept (mean) shifts, its impact on the conditional independence structure should be limited.

8. Method
8.1. Structure-learning algorithm
Structure learning algorithms are used to find the graph that encodes the dependence structure of the data. The output can be either
a directed graph (DAG) or a completed partially directed graph (CPDAG), representing a Markov Equivalence class (Spirtes et al.,
2000). There are two main classes of structure learning algorithms: constraint-based and score-based algorithms. Constraint-based
algorithms construct a graph using conditional independence tests (Spirtes et al., 2000). Score-based algorithms explore the space
of candidate graphs and identify the graph that maximizes some score, such as the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (Spirtes and
Zhang, 2018). For a comprehensive review of structure learning methods, see Glymour et al. (2019), Scutari and Denis (2021), and
Spirtes and Zhang (2018).
The present study uses the Hill-Climbing (HC) algorithm, a score-based greedy search method implemented in the bnlearn R
package (Scutari and Denis, 2021), to estimate the underlying CPDAG for each country. A comprehensive simulation study identified
the HC algorithm as a top-performing structure learning method, both with and without data noise (Constantinou et al., 2021). The HC
algorithm begins with an empty DAG and computes its initial score. It then iteratively explores potential DAGs by adding, removing, or
reversing single edges, updating the graph whenever a modification increases the score, until no further improvements can be made.
This process continues until the algorithm identifies the graph that achieves the highest possible score (Scutari and Denis, 2021).
To ensure stability in the estimates, the DAG for each country was constructed using 200 bootstrapped samples. The threshold
above which an edge is retained (the “significance threshold”) was empirically determined based on the method proposed by Scutari
and Nagarajan (2013).11 The average resulting threshold across countries was 0.54, with a minimum of 0.51 and a maximum of 0.67.
This means that, on average, an edge was retained if it appeared on at least 54% of the bootstrapped samples, independently of the
direction of the edge.
After estimating the DAG, the constitutive elements of gender in each country were identified by selecting the neighbors of the
binary variable representing students’ sex.
8.2. Assumptions
In order to identify the neighbors (Nbr(S)) of a node S based on a DAG G using a set of random variables V , one needs to make the
following assumptions (Pearl, 2009; Spirtes et al., 2000):
Modularity: Each edge in the graph represents an autonomous mechanism, in the sense that one could change one relationship
without changing the others.12
Faithfulness: The conditional independence relationships observed in V are reflected in the underlying causal structure in G.
Causal sufficiency: There are no unmeasured confounders in V that influence both S and Nbr(S).
These three assumptions are required to interpret the set Nbr(S) as constitutive elements of gender. The modularity assumption
ensures the elements are separable, the faithfulness assumption ensures the dependencies reflect true causal mechanisms (instead of
random noise), and the causal sufficiency assumption ensures the edges are not spuriously generated by unobserved confounders. The
last is a strong assumption, thus strong causal inferences should be avoided.

9. Results
9.1. General findings
This study operates under the assumption that gender is a multidimensional construct that can manifest differently across cultures.
From this perspective, offering broad generalizations about the constitutive elements of gender can be problematic. At the same time, a
detailed analysis of individual countries is outside the scope of this study. Thus, I will present general patterns that can be observed
across nations.
An initial observation is that the number of estimated constitutive elements varies greatly across countries, from a minimum of 9
(Dominican Republic) to a maximum of 54 (Jordan). Across countries, the average number of neighboring variables is 27. This shows
that while the current method serves as a feature selection tool, reducing the number of features by over half, a substantial number of
variables remain. This underscores the complexity and pervasiveness of gender systems, and how the meaning of gender varies across
cultures.
9.2. Common constitutive elements across nations
The results also indicate that certain variables consistently appear to be constitutive of gender across nations. In particular, students' enjoyment of reading was identified as a constitutive element in all countries except for Japan. Additionally, the time individuals
spend reading for enjoyment was the second most common variable, found in 90 percent of the countries. It is also worth noting that in
all countries in the dataset, girls tend to enjoy reading more than boys. All standardized sex differences are larger than 0.1, with some
reaching up to 0.8, as shown in Fig. S1 in the Supplemental Materials. The same pattern is observed across other reading-related
variables, with girls consistently showing more positive attitudes towards reading than boys. These results suggest that attitudes toward reading are a key aspect of the construction of gender across countries’ educational systems. These findings align with prior
research suggesting that schooling in general, and language subjects in particular, have been constructed as stereotypically feminine

9.4. Predicting empathy
Fig. 4 displays the coefficients of a Lasso logistic regression predicting empathy, specifically individuals' ability to understand the

Comparative studies emphasize how cultural differences between Eastern and Western countries influence individuals’ empathy
levels (e.g., Atkins et al., 2016; Boven et al., 2013). The results presented in Fig. 4 indicate something different, namely that the

gender might play an important role in explaining the East-West differences in empathy. It is also worth noting that collectivism, which
is a common explanation for East-West differences, does not appear as an important predictor in this analysis.
It should be highlighted that Asia appears as an important predictor in the models predicting competitiveness (Fig. 2) and empathy
(Fig. 4), but in opposite ways. The results presented in Fig. 2 indicate that, compared to other regions and controlling for country-level
features, competitiveness does not tend to be a gender-differentiating attribute in Asian countries. Conversely, Fig. 4 shows that
empathy does tend to be a gender-differentiating factor in Asian countries. This illustrates how gender can be constructed differently
across different societies. Since none of the included societal indicators account for these geographical differences, future studies can
investigate the specific cultural factors within these countries that explain why these attributes are or are not part of the construction of
gender.
10. Limitations and robustness checks
10.1. Data limitations
An important limitation of the dataset is the potential for measurement error and omitted confounders. Datasets containing these
issues might cause the (conditional) independence relations in the observed data to differ from those produced by the true datagenerating process (Tu et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2017). Attempts were made to minimize measurement error by utilizing composites of items and reliable constructs developed by PISA. To minimize unmeasured confounding, I included most items available. Yet
these two risks will always be present in observational data.
The comparative nature of the study somewhat alleviates concerns related to these data limitations. Inferences are drawn across
countries with respect to the same dataset. For instance, regardless of potential unmeasured variables, competitiveness is directly
related to sex in some countries but not in others. This suggests that competitiveness appears to be more closely associated with gender

10.2. Robustness checks
The results of the analysis can also be sensitive to the specific search algorithm implemented. The goal was to identify Nbr(S), which
is the set of variables most predictive of S. Two approaches were used to test whether Nbr(S) provided most information about S in the
dataset. It is worth noting that the spouses of S, which are excluded from Nbr(S), contain additional information about S.
10.3. Assessing the added predictive value of sex
The first approach involved assessing the added predictive value of Sex when included in a model with Nbr(S). If Nbr(S) contains all
information about S, then adding Sex should not increase the model's accuracy. To measure accuracy, I used the Root Mean Square
Error (RMSE), which represents the standard deviation of the residuals. A substantial decrease in RMSE would suggest that Sex
contributes significantly to the model's predictive capability, above and beyond what is provided by Nbr(S).
To illustrate, consider the results from the first country in alphabetic order, Albania. The sample size for Albania is 6359. The
estimated graph includes 75 nodes (no missing variables), out of which 36 were identified as neighbors of S (Nbr(S)) and 38 as not
neighbors of S (¬Nbr(S)). Each element of ¬Nbr(S) was used as a dependent variable, Y. I then implemented two regression models. In
the first model, Y was regressed on Nbr(S), and in the second model Y was regressed on Nbr(S) and Sex. The difference in RMSE
between these two models indicates the added predictive value of Sex.

10.4. Conditional independence tests
The second approach involved testing each of the conditional independencies implied by the resulting graph related to S and
Nbr(S). If Nbr(S) provides all information about S, then ¬Nbr(S)⫫S | Nbr(S). These independencies were tested by examining the
correlation between the residuals for the regressions ¬Nbr(S) ∼ Nbr(S) and S ∼ Nbr(S). If the independence condition is met, the
residuals from these regressions should be statistically independent and, consequently, not correlated (Ankan et al., 2021).
To illustrate this approach, Fig. 5 (right panel) displays the Pearson correlation coefficient, along with the 95% confidence intervals, among the residuals for one country (Albania). A value near 0 suggests that the conditional independence holds in the data,
while a value approaching 1 or − 1 suggests otherwise. The fact that all correlations are below | 0.1|, with an average of the absolute
value of the correlation of 0.04, suggests that the conditional independencies hold in the data.
These results are consistent across countries. The largest average correlation observed in any country was 0.05 (SE = 0.005). Fig. S4
in the Supplemental Materials displays the average correlations based on absolute values by country, along with 95% confidence
intervals. It is important to note that, although the average correlations remain below | 0.1|, a few correlations within countries
exceeded | 0.1|. However, none of these correlations involved the two variables that are the focus of the analysis (competitiveness and
empathy).
11. Discussion
It is difficult to overestimate the relevance of gender in social science theorizing. However, quantitative researchers lack frameworks for conceptualizing gender and examining how gender shapes social outcomes. The concept of gender was originally introduced
to distinguish the social and cultural aspects that differentiate men and women (norms, expectations, stereotypes, preferences, etc.)
from biological distinctions based on anatomical and physiological traits (Haig, 2004). Yet despite this conceptual distinction,
quantitative research often relies on an undifferentiated binary measure of sex/gender (Bauer, 2023; Krieger, 2003). Conflating gender
with sex risks encouraging essentialist interpretations that portray differences between men and women as stable, discrete, and
biologically determined.
Researchers have therefore argued that gender should be studied through the sociocultural attributes that differentiate men and
women within particular societies (Greaves and Ritz, 2022; Horstmann et al., 2022; Lindqvist et al., 2021; Nielsen et al., 2021).
However, this perspective faces an important challenge: identifying which attributes should be considered constitutive of a social
category. Because numerous differences between men and women can be observed across domains, delineating clear boundaries for
gender is not straightforward.
This study uses the concept of a probabilistic boundary, derived from graphical models, to define the constitutive elements of
gender in a specific system. Specifically, I defined the constitutive elements of gender as the neighboring nodes in a directed graph.
Based on this definition, I searched for the constitutive elements of gender across 72 nations using a structure learning algorithm. The
number of constitutive factors ranged from a minimum of 9 to a maximum of 54, with an average of 27. The most prevalent elements
were attitudes and behaviors toward reading, suggesting that during adolescence these factors serve as a primary gender-defining
characteristic globally.
The analysis also examined whether country-level features predict whether certain attributes—competitiveness and empathy—function as constitutive elements of gender. The results indicate that competitiveness is more likely to differentiate boys and girls
in countries where women have greater access to educational and economic opportunities. By contrast, belonging to Asian countries
emerged as the strongest predictor of empathy functioning as a gender-differentiating attribute. Together, these findings highlight how
cultural and institutional contexts shape which attributes become gendered, cautioning against essentialist interpretations such as the
assumption that men are inherently competitive or women inherently empathetic.
Identifying the constitutive elements of gender can serve several purposes. First, it provides a foundation for examining crosscultural variation in how gender is constructed. Second, it helps illuminate potential mechanisms underlying gender inequalities by
identifying proximal factors that differentiate men and women. Third, it can function as a data-reduction strategy by isolating key
attributes associated with gender, which may be used, for example, to construct gender typicality scores (Ballering et al., 2024; Lippa
and Connelly, 1990).
Several limitations should be noted. Because the analysis relies on observational survey data, omitted variables may generate
spurious associations. Moreover, the available measures may not capture all relevant attributes that shape gender systems. Addressing
this limitation is challenging; in order to conceptualize gender as a system rather than a variable, comprehensive measures of that
system are required.
In addition, it is important to note that the constitutive elements of gender as defined in this study should not be interpreted
normatively. Being identified as a constitutive element of gender does not necessarily mean that the factor is socially relevant or
important. To make these assessments, one should consider their social impact, the magnitude of the gap, among other relevant

As with other data-driven approaches, the inferences depend on the specific characteristics of the data. The present study draws on
PISA, which focuses on middle adolescence. Given the emphasis in the data on attitudes and behaviors related to learning and
schooling, the features identified here reflect how gender is constructed within schools during this specific period. Other insights about
gender might emerge from datasets that capture different life stages or emphasize other domains (e.g., health, political attitudes, labor
markets, or family life).
The approach can also be extended to longitudinal datasets, enabling analyses of how the construction of gender evolves over time
(Quintana, 2022). This is important because gender is presumably not only context-dependent but also time-dependent. For example,
school-related attributes such as interest in reading or mathematics may be more salient in middle adolescence than in later life. By
identifying which attributes become or cease to be constitutive, researchers can trace how the elements of gender shift in salience
across the life course. In addition, the approach can be extended to other social categories such as race, political affiliation, disability,
etc.
The present study focuses on sex differences in attitudes and behaviors and the societal structures associated with those differences.
Other approaches concentrate on different aspects of the gender system, notably gender stereotypes. Advances in computational text
analysis have greatly expanded the scope of these investigations, as researchers can now systematically measure cultural meanings in
large-scale text data (e.g., Arseniev-Koehler and Foster, 2022). These approaches are largely complementary. Text analysis is
particularly useful for tracing cultural stereotypes over time, especially where survey data are unavailable (Boutyline et al., 2023).
However, such analyses often rely on specific textual sources that may not represent broader populations and may therefore be less
suited for cross-national comparisons.
Taken together, the findings suggest that gender is best understood not as a fixed individual attribute but as a context-dependent
social system. Rather than treating gender as a single explanatory variable, research should focus on the constellation of attitudes,
behaviors, and institutional arrangements that differentiate men and women within particular contexts. This perspective shifts the
central question of research—from measuring gender differences to explaining how gender itself is socially constructed. More broadly,
the approach illustrates how social categories can be studied by identifying the attributes and practices that give them meaning in
specific social contexts.
