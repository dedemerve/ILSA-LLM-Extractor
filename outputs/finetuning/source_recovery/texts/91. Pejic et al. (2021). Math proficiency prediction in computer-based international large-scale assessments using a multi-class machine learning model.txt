Abstract—This study examines whether a multi-class 
predictive machine learning model can be used to predict 
students' math performance in computer-based international 
large-scale assessments. Raw data from the PISA 2012 
computer-based 
assessment 
was 
used 
to 
conduct 
the 
experiments. Three-class neural network and random forest 
machine learning models were developed and compared in 
prediction 
performance 
for 
students' 
proficiency 
in 
mathematics. The three levels of math proficiency were low, 
mediocre and high. Both machine learning models relied on 
background variables from student, parental and school 
questionnaires, as well as on the student math assessment data. 
Manual and a few automatic feature selection methods were 
explored while developing the machine learning models. Due to 
the imbalance in the three-class dataset Cohen's Kappa 
coefficient and ROC AUC were used as main metrics of the 
model performance. 
Keywords—multi-class, predictive model, machine learning, 
computer-based assessment 
I. INTRODUCTION  
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 
is a large-scale educational assessment conducted by The 
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development 
(OECD) in a three-year cycle since year 2000 amongst 15-
year-old students as the target population. In 2012 the survey 
was administered in sixty-five countries all over the world. 
Beside the traditional paper-based assessment (PBA), an 
optional computer-based assessment (CBA) was also 
administered, in which forty-four countries participated. 
PISA primarily evaluates students' skills in core school 
subjects of reading, mathematics and science. The 2012 
assessment also measured students' financial literacy and 
problem-solving skills. Furthermore, the problem-solving 
survey was conducted only in CBA form, alongside of digital 
reading and computer-based mathematics. Although both 
PBA and CBA contained reading and mathematics items, 
they were made of different sets of items. 
Each PISA cycle places one of the three main assessment 
competence domains into focus - reading, mathematics or 
science. Besides scoring the students' performance, the PISA 
survey also collects information on students' social and 
economical background through a questionnaire. There are 
also questions about students' attitudes, behavior and 
motivation in relation to education. Furthermore, parents and 
schools also participate by answering questionnaires, thus 
providing more background information about students' 
home and school environment. The domain in focus accounts 
for about the half of the total number of items in the 
assessment, while the other domains share the second half. In 
2012 the survey focus was on mathematics, meaning that the 
students were typically administered more mathematics items 
than others. 
OECD has recognized three main groups of technological 
challenges for development of future PISA assessment 
instruments: first, including a wider range of competencies 
besides the three main domains, second, interactive and 
dynamic delivery of assessments, and third, relevance of 
assessment tasks. The answer for these challenges was the 
development 
of 
computer-based 
PISA 
assessment 
instruments. PISA's transition from PBA towards CBA mode 
was started in 2006, when the science assessment was 
delivered for the first time in electronic form. It was followed 
up in 2009 with digital reading assessment, and in 2012 with 
computer-based 
mathematics 
and 
problem-solving 
assessment [1]. In 2015 for the first time the preferred mode 
of assessment was computer-based, when almost all 
participating 
countries 
have 
administered 
the 
PISA 
questionnaires and surveys only by computer. 
ICT usage in education and educational data mining has 
recently come into focus of many researches [2], [3], [4], [5], 
[6]. Various statistical and machine learning methods on 
PISA datasets were studied in [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], 
[13], [14], [15]. 
Because of the large amount of background variables, 
feature selection was needed before classification with 
machine learning models. There are several algorithm types 
which compute the ranking of the predictor variables, making 
it possible to choose the best ones for the dataset to train the 
class predictor model [16], [17]. 
The collection and digitization of data on student success 
in school, as well as the existence of data on students that are 
not directly related to school and schooling but can influence 
the results of their schooling, enabled the use of data mining 
techniques. Machine learning techniques were used in [18] to 
predict the results of students in public examination. In [19]  
social and economic background variables available from 
statistics gained from data collected by schools and 
universities were used to predict success in different school 
subjects in Portugal. 
Data 
mining 
techniques 
were 
used 
to 
student 
performance predictions in [20], [21]. Language and 
mathematics performance level was predicted by school, 
teacher, parents and students' personal variables with neural 
networks in [22]. In [23] 194 PISA background variables 
were chosen and investigated about their effect on anxiety 
with mathematics.

Predictor importance was investigated on PISA 2015

investigated were from the survey about student anxiety, 
student motivation and epistemological beliefs, and as the 
target value PISA's own plausible values were used. 
Plausible values (PVs) are in essence estimates for 
population proficiency for the given PISA assessment 
domain [25]. 
Effects of background variables on plausible values on 
mathematics score based on collected data in PISA 
assessment was investigated in many researches [26], [27], 
[28]. 
The subject of this study is to examine whether a multi-
class machine learning model would be viable for students' 
math proficiency prediction in computer-based large-scale 
education assessments. Rather than estimating the population 
proficiency (plausible values in PISA database), in this study 
the focus is on the proficiency of a single student. Supervised 
machine learning models based on neural network and 
random forest algorithms were trained from a dataset 
assembled 
from 
PISA 
cognitive 
test 
answers 
and 
questionnaire answers. Most relevant background variables 
were identified using automatic feature selection algorithms. 
The labels for training the supervised machine learning 
models were engineered from the scoring data available in 
the PISA database and relevant information in PISA 
codebooks. Such a machine learning model could be used to 
address the third group of PISA technological challenges 
identified by OECD, as it would enable the assessment 
instrument to interactively adapt to the ability of students and 
provide a more adequate and engaging assessment 
experience. 
Section II. of this paper describes in detail the 
preprocessing of data and the resulting experimental dataset 
used for training and performance measuring of the machine 
models. In Section III. the scientific methods of the study are 
presented with details about the machine learning models and 
feature selection methods, the implications of the imbalanced 
dataset, and specifics about the chosen performance 
measurements. Results of the conducted experiments and 
measurements are presented in Section IV. Finally, the 
conclusions are drawn in Section V. 
II. EXPERIMENTAL DATASET 
For this study the PISA 2012 CBA mathematics dataset 
was used for two main reasons. First, the focus of the 2012 
assessment was mathematics, thus the majority of the 
administered test questions were mathematics, resulting with 
more relevant data in the dataset which can be used to train 
the machine learning models. Second, the CBA dataset was 
picked over the PBA dataset, although it contained less 
records (271323 students participated in CBA, while 485490 
students participated in PBA), because of the relevance of the 
computer-based environment.  
The PISA 2012 computer-based assessment covered three 
domains: problem-solving, digital reading and computer-
based mathematics. The participating countries either 
administered only the problem-solving computer-based 
survey, or all three of the computer-based surveys. There 
were 24 varieties of the problem-solving and computer-based 
literacies (CBAL) test forms, each form composed of two 
item clusters. CBAL refers to digital reading and computer-
based mathematics. The two clusters were either from the 
same survey domain, or mixed, with two clusters from 
different survey domains.  
The total number of computer-based mathematics items 
was 41, grouped into 4 item clusters, each cluster containing 
10 or 11 items. Clusters CM1, CM2 and CM4 have 10 items 
each, while cluster CM3 has 11 items. The number of 
mathematics test questions given to a student varies 
depending on the administered test form. In the case of test 
form with one mathematics cluster and one problem-solving 
or digital reading cluster, it was either 10 or 11 test questions. 
In the case of test form with two mathematics clusters it was 
either 20 or 21 test questions.  
Since PISA is an international large-scale assessment, the 
available data files are capturing all of the differences 
between 
the 
countries, 
including 
economic, 
social, 
educational and cultural aspects. Because of many country 
specific factors, the PISA data is usually analyzed and 
interpreted on a country level. In this study the focus is on 
examining the performance of the machine learning models, 
hence the whole dataset with 271323 student records was 
processed, rather than a country specific subset of data which 
would contain significantly less data. 
The dataset was assembled by combining the PISA 2012 
CBA scored cognitive item response data file with student, 
school and parent questionnaire data files. After initial 
preprocessing, data cleaning and selecting relevant data the 
data files were joined into a final dataset containing records 
for 58753 students. 
Label for the supervised machine learning and 
classification algorithms was created from the data available 
in the final dataset and the scoring information for computer-
based mathematics items in the codebook. For each student 
in the dataset the number of administered computer-based 
test questions was collected and the maximum score for those 
test questions was determined. Most of the test questions 
were scored with 1 point, with a few exceptions, where the 
test questions were scored with 2 point. The number of 
achieved points was divided with the number of maximal 
points to obtain the test success coefficient. This coefficient 
was further used to assign a low proficiency, mediocre 
proficiency or high proficiency label to the given student. 
The A, B and C classes were named based on these labels. 
Data in the final dataset was shuffled and then divided 
into two groups, train cases and test cases, in order for the 
prediction models to be tested on unseen data. The dataset 
was split into train and test sets in the ratio of 70 to 30. Only 
the training part of the data was used in feature selection 
algorithms. During the neural network model creation, the 
training data was further divided randomly into new training 
data and validation data. Validation data was used during the 
learning 
process 
for 
checking 
whether 
the 
model 
performance is decreasing on validation data while 
improving on the training data. To avoid overfitting, early 
stopping was implemented in the learning process. After the 
learning process was finished, testing was made with the test 
dataset which was unseen by the model previously, not even 
for validation purposes.

To be able to compare the relevance of manually chosen 
and automatically chosen features on the models, the 
automatic feature selection algorithms were configured to 
also choose 101 features. However, the scope of the 
background variables was broadened by allowing the 
automatic feature algorithms to choose variables from all 
three questionnaires. Besides the student questionnaire, 
parents and school questionnaires were also included. The 
reason for this was the assumption that the automatic feature 
selection algorithms shall choose the most significant 
background variables across all questionnaires and thus 
improve the performance of the machine learning models. A 
small number of generic variables were filtered out manually. 
They were identified by the automatic feature selection 
algorithms as significant, but due to their non-relevant 
context they were removed from the dataset and the selection 
was repeated. 
The random forest feature selection method combines the 
advantages of the filter method and the wrapper method. It 
calculates the significance of individual features in such a 
way that each decision tree also calculates the significance 
for a part of the data. Features selected at the top of the 
decision tree are more significant than those at the lower 
branches. Finally, the mean value of the individual results is 
calculated to obtain the final result of the random forest 
algorithm. 
The chi-squared test is used in feature selection because 
its value determines how interdependent the two variables 
are. In the case of feature selection, the two variables are 
individual features and targets. Greater dependence between 
the two variables indicates a better predictor. 
Recursive feature eliminator is a method that is 
independent of the type of features. Multiple models with 
different features are created and performance is compared. 
This method is the most demanding of all in terms of 
computer power and the amount of computing time. 
Each automatic feature selection algorithm was executed 
five times. From the feature selection results a set of 101 
features was assembled using the features which were chosen 
the most times. As the most significant features we have 
identified the variables which were chosen all five times by 
all three algorithms (Table III.). 
The background variables chosen by the automatic 
feature selection algorithms were manually examined and 
found reasonable and fitting for the context of the study. The 
rest of the automatically chosen variables, which were 
chosen less times, were also manually verified and found to 
be relevant. Due to space constrains only the 5 most 
significant background variables from the total of 101 chosen 
by each feature selection algorithm are shown in Table II.

Random forest is a classification algorithm which relies 
on the decision tree method. Applying multiple decision 
trees, each decision tree is trained, and each of the results is 
taken into account when computing the overall classification. 
In this case 1000 decision trees are used, none of them sees 
the complete dataset, and therefore overfitting can not occur. 
In case of the decision tree algorithm, subsequential 
decisions are made to split the input features into classes 
according to the target. 
Feedforward neural network with two hidden layers, each 
containing 75 neurons, is used as class predictor for three 
classes. Since multi-class prediction is the case, the output 
layer has 3 neurons matching the number of classes. The 
output neurons have the softmax activation function, which 
returns a probability value between 0 and 1. The activation 
functions of the hidden layer are of the ReLU (Rectified 
Linear Unit) type, and the initialization of these neurons is of 
the He type [29]. Stochastic gradient descent was applied as 
the learning optimizer. In the phase of preprocessing, the 
dataset has to be encoded with respect to the output of the 
neural network. The target data is encoded by one hot 
encoder, where each class belongs to one specific output 
neuron, which has value 1 for the belonging class while all 
the other output neurons have value 0. This way, a multi-
class classification can also be understood as a multiple 
binary classification, because each output is bivalent. Prior to 
the training process, the data set is divided into training data 
and test data. During the training, the model is constantly 
checked, and the accuracy after each individual epoch is 
compared for both the training set and the test set. In that 
epoch of training in which the accuracy for test data begins to 
deteriorate, training should be stopped because it is a sign 
that the model is overfit. To avoid overfitting, early stopping 
was used.

The dataset used in the study was highly imbalanced 
because the high proficiency students' class contained a very 
low number of samples compared to the low and mediocre 
proficiency classes. In order to overcome this issue, 
Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Technique (SMOTE) 
[30] was used to produce artificial samples in target dataset. 
Classes with fewer samples were oversampled for the 
purpose of the training process. Label A stands for the class 
with low proficiency level in mathematics, B stands for 
mediocre proficiency class and C for the high proficiency 
class (Figure 1).

The accuracy, recall and F1 score are standard metrics for 
classification models. However, in the case of the two 
models examined here, the results of these metrics are rather 
weak. The reason behind it is that these metrics are optimal 
for binary classification models with two classes. In this 
study three-class models were used, which affected the 
interpretation of these metrics, as well as their overall fitness 
as a metric. For this reason Cohen’s Kappa coefficient and 
ROC-AUC values were chosen as the performance measure 
of the models. 
V. CONCLUSION  
Prediction of students' mathematics proficiency on PISA 
computer-based assessment has been accomplished with 
machine learning method based on multi-class neural 
network and random forest models. Three math proficiency 
classes were used: A – low proficiency, B – mediocre 
proficiency, and C – high proficiency. The dataset was 
imbalanced because of relatively low number of students 
with high mathematics proficiency. SMOTE technique was 
applied to achieve a balanced dataset. Automatic and manual 
feature selection was compared. Automatic feature selection 
was used to select a set of most relevant features from the 
PISA background questionnaires. Performance of the models 
was measured with different metrics. Cohen’s Kappa 
coefficient was used to compare the model performance.

Both models demonstrated good performance. Overall the

Based on the results of the study, it can be argued, that a

large-scale computer-based assessment instrument, such as 
PISA, to interactively adapt to the skills of the students, and 
thus provide a better and more interesting assessment 
experience. 
The topic of the study offers a number of possibilities for 
future work: use of log-file databases for released PISA 2012 
computer-based items for improving the machine learning 
models and analysis of the student performance, further 
experimentation

network to improve performance, further exploration and 
optimization of multi-class models with imbalanced datasets, 
and at last, examination of more recent PISA CBA datasets 
from years 2015 and 2018 with science and reading as main 
competence domains.
