From theories of task-oriented reading we derived the hypothesis that digital reading has characteristics of a
problem for poor comprehenders, while it poses a routine task for skilled comprehenders. It follows that problem
solving predicts navigation and digital reading over and above comprehension, and there is an ordinal interaction between comprehension and problem solving. This interaction means that effects of problem solving on
navigation and digital reading are stronger in poor, and weaker in skilled comprehenders. Effects of problem
solving, comprehension and their interaction on digital reading are assumed to be mediated through navigation.
These hypotheses were tested using PISA-Data (N = 13,080), employing two indices of navigation, “Precision”
and “Adaptive processing”. Results confirmed our hypotheses with the only exception that Adaptive processing
was not predicted by the interaction between problem solving and comprehension. Our results suggest that
digital reading constitutes a routine task for skilled, but a problem for unskilled comprehenders.
Educational relevance statement: In the 21st century virtually every student and adult is confronted with the task to
search for, read, understand and use information on line on a daily basis for personal, educational or occupational purposes. For some readers this task is routine, which they easily accomplish. For others, this task is a
complex problem, which they cannot readily solve. The present research shows that good comprehension skill, as
taught in advanced reading instruction, puts readers in a position to proficiently find and adequately process
information on line. In the face of lacking comprehension skill digital reading becomes a problem, which can be
overcome when readers are in command of good problem solving skill. This means that besides efficient reading
instruction, designing curricula to improve problem solving skill is vital to teach students how to navigate the
digital 21st century information landscape.

In this article, we argue that comprehension skill plays a critical role
in determining whether for any individual digital reading tasks can be
accomplished as a routine, or are turned into a problem – in the sense
that no clear path to that problem’s solution is readily available.
We refer to digital reading as an activity that comprises decoding,
integrating, and evaluating textual, and possibly in addition pictorial,
information from multiple documents that are accessed via a digital
interface to serve informational and/or communicative needs. The term
“document” here has a broad meaning, and can refer to a whole text (e.g.

a newspaper article) as well as a single graph or picture, which can be
accessed e.g. through a hyperlink embedded in a text. It is important to
note that this definition of digital reading goes beyond reading a single
text on a digital device. Specifically, it employs the need to access
multiple documents according to task demands that may either exist
already at the outset, or emerge during the reading process. The access
and sequencing of documents from an opaque digital information
environment has been termed “navigation” (Lawless & Schrader, 2008;
Salmerón, Strømsø, et al., 2018), and described as a distinct cognitive

In the following, we use the term “comprehension” for the process of
forming a situation model from a single text, which can vary in length
from a single sentence to a linear text composed of several paragraphs or
pages. Such a text might also comprise graphs, images or tables that are
readily available to the reader, and need not be accessed as a separate
document. Comprehension as a process thus involves the decoding of
textual information, the retrieval of word meanings, syntactic parsing,
semantic integration of the sentence level, establishing local and global
coherence, and forming a text base and a situation model (see Kintsch,
1998). We refer to comprehension skill as the ability to execute these
processes, and to a skilled comprehender as an individual who is able to
execute these processes efficiently.
Our use of the term “digital reading” is in line with the component
processes of earlier models of digital reading, such as the Information
Problem Solving on the Internet (IPS-I) model, put forward by BrandGruwel et al. (2009): These authors delineated computer skill, reading
skill, and evaluation skill as a basis for information problem solving, and
indeed computer skill has been found to have positive associations with
digital reading (Goldhammer et al., 2013), as has evaluation skill
(Incognito & Tarchi, 2024) and reading skill (Hahnel et al., 2016, 2018;
Salmerón, García, & Vidal-Abarca, 2018). A strong relation between
reading skill and performance in digital reading is to-be-expected on the
grounds that digital texts are texts in the first place and thus digital
reading involves all processes involved in reading single texts. In the
present article however we propose that digital reading requires
comprehension skill on the basis of a mechanism that reaches beyond
the fact that digital reading and reading single texts draw on a number of
shared processes (i.e. comprehension). Rather, we argue that comprehension skill contributes to digital reading by making it a routine task for
skilled, but a problem for unskilled comprehenders. This notion then
also implies that in the case of lacking comprehension skill, problem
solving skill becomes of particular importance for a reader faced with a
digital reading scenario. In the following, we derive these ideas from
recent models of task-oriented reading, and a discussion of problem
solving, and test them using data from 32 countries that participated in
the OECD’s Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA, see
OECD, 2014a).

problems (e.g. Kanniainen et al., 2021). From the perspective of the
problem-solving literature, a problem occurs when there is a barrier
between a given and a goal state, without obvious means of overcoming
it (Mayer & Wittrock, 2006). Thus, problems and problem solving can
appear in virtually every domain – be it mathematics, engineering,
writing, planning a trip – or digital reading. A problem can be distinguished from a routine task by the criterion that in a routine task there is
also a discrepancy between a given and a goal state, but no barrier,
because some clear path to solve the task is at hand. The same situation
might pose a problem for one person, while it represents a routine task
for another, if these persons differ in which means of task solution are
available to them. Thus, within the same person, through skill acquisition, a situation that represents a problem in the present might become a
routine task in the future (Anderson, 1987).
Complex problems differ from static problems in that not all information required to solve the problem is available on its onset. Accordingly, complex problems are at least to some extent opaque and require
exploration in order to generate information. This is often true for
problems that involve search for information. Through the course of
solving such a problem a person will repeatedly encounter new states of
affairs as to which information is available, missing, or how newly acquired information is to be judged according to its redundancy, usefulness, trustworthiness and plausibility.
The idea that digital reading can represent a problem is suggested by
the processes involved in problem solving, Representing, Planning and
Monitoring, Executing, and Self-Regulating (Mayer & Wittrock, 2006).
Representing means that an internal representation of an externally
presented problem, such as a word problem in a math class, is created,
entailing both the given and the goal state, as well as possible intervening states. Planning and Monitoring implies conceiving means of
bridging the gap between the given and the goal state, such as breaking
down the problem into smaller pieces, or recognizing which kinds of
knowledge might be missing for the problem to be solved, and evaluating whether the means devised and put into action are appropriate.
Self-Regulation refers to the fact that the solution process as a whole will
be unforeseeable at the outset. Thus, for instance, new sub-goals and
cognitive activities to pursue them might have to be instigated, while
others are discarded. Executing means the execution of procedures like
carrying out calculations to solve an arithmetic problem, or manipulating a slider to figure out the rules as to which a dynamic system works
(Fig. 1).
These processes overlap with processes in digital reading, where
readers have to represent the problem, i.e. create some cognitive representation of the given and the goal informational states, as well as
possible legal intervening states (e.g. how might missing information be
acquired). They have to form a plan as to which sources are appropriate
and accessible. They have to monitor whether incoming information is
appropriate, and to regulate whether an initially set sub-goal, e.g. to
access a specific document, has to be replaced (e.g. because the document is behind a paywall). They have to execute cognitive operations
involved in reading as well as operating the electronic interface.
It is important to note that the term regulation in Mayer and Wittrock’s (2006) model has a narrow meaning. In conceptualizations of
self-regulated learning such as those proposed and applied by Greene and
Azevedo (2009) or Winne (2017), (self-) regulation is the umbrella term
for processes like planning, monitoring, or coping with task difficulties
(cf. Greene & Azevedo, 2009, p.19). As such however, models of selfregulated learning are largely compatible with Mayer and Wittrock’s
framework. E.g. the “planning” component in Mayer and Wittrock
(2006) corresponds to the “planning” component in Greene and Azevedo
(2009), or phase 2 in Winne’s (2017) model, where “the learner forges
goals for working on the task and drafts plans to approach those goals.”
(p. 39).

1.1. Models of reading as problem solving
Reading has been described in terms of elaborate cognitive models of
how individuals decipher and integrate information from written text (e.
g. Kintsch, 1998). With the advance of the Internet, the construct of
reading has shifted from a textual to an intertextual level, beginning e.g.
with Rouet et al.’ (1996) seminal investigation of how students use
conflicting evidence from multiple texts in historic reasoning. A number
of models have been proposed to describe the interactions of processes
in multiple text reading (Rouet et al., 2017; Rouet & Britt, 2011), or
digital reading (Brand-Gruwel et al., 2009) that commonly label digital
or multiple text reading scenarios as problems. Rouet et al. (2017) choose
Reading as Problem Solving (RESOLV), Brand-Gruwel et al. (2009) coin
their model Information Problem-Solving on the Internet (see above).
Despite the explicit reference of these models to problem solving, to date
there is no study (to the best of our knowledge) that investigates the role
of problem solving skill and its interaction with comprehension skill in
digital reading. The aim of the present article is to make a first step
towards filling this gap. In the following, we will outline commonalities
between reading digital reading and problem solving.

To date, only indirect evidence supports the notion that digital
reading comprises elements of problem solving, and that navigation can
be conceived as a means of overcoming the barrier between a given and
a goal state. Among this is a study by Goldhammer et al. (2014). These
authors analyzed time-on-task effects in the domains of reading and
problem solving in technology-rich environments. They found that timeon-task effects were positive especially in hard problem solving tasks,
while they were negative especially in easy reading tasks. They interpreted this finding in a dual-processing framework, arguing that problem solving tasks need cognitive effort to be invested for the problem to
be solved, while the reading tasks could be solved by automatic cognitive processing to a large extent. Extending this line of research, Naumann and Goldhammer (2017) found that time-on-task-effects in digital
reading resembled those that had been previously found for problem
solving, and especially if these digital reading tasks had high navigation
demands. This finding points to navigation demands being among the
variables that can turn a digital reading task into a problem. They also
showed that comprehension skill could alleviate positive time-on-task
effects. This result supports the idea that digital reading tasks constitute a problem for poor comprehenders, and a routine task for skilled
comprehenders. From this perspective, problem solving skill should be
predictive of navigation behavior, and in turn digital reading, and
comprehension skill should moderate the effect of problem solving skill
on navigation behavior and digital reading in a way that effects of
problem solving skill are stronger in poor and weaker in strong
comprehenders.

1.3. Task models in information problem solving and the role of
comprehension skill
In digital reading, problem solving skill might not be of equal
importance for all persons alike. Following Frensch and Funke (1995),
the fact that a reading scenario might constitute a problem rather than a
routine task, is grounded in the opacity of the given and the goal state, as
well as the opacity of possible means to bridge the gap between them. In
the context of digital reading, the representation of a goal and a given
state, and the means to overcome a barrier between them, has been
described as a “task model” (Rouet et al., 2017). Building a task model
involves the definition and updating of a desired end state, the setting
and updating of reading and information access goals, the identification
of information needs, the identification of available means, and the
handling of obstacles (see Rouet et al., 2017, Fig. 2, p. 6). According to
RESOLV these processes are in particular reliant on text decoding and
passage comprehension. Proficient text decoding and passage comprehension is what defines a skilled comprehender (see also Afflerbach
et al., 2008). From this perspective, a skilled comprehender will build a
transparent representation of the goal state, the discrepancy between
the given and goal state, and possible means to bridge the two. An unskilled comprehender, in contrast, will create a more opaque task model,
and possible means to overcome the barrier between the given and goal
states will be less clear, meaning that this lesser skilled comprehender
will be faced with a problem, rather than a routine task.
These ideas are backed by research showing that skilled comprehenders create and maintain more adequate task models. Hahnel et al.
(2018) showed that skilled comprehenders made better choices than
poor comprehenders when they had to decide whether a hyperlink
pointed to useful or trustworthy information. Mañá et al. (2017) found
skilled comprehenders to make better decisions as to when to search for
information. Moreover, in their study one condition had a delay between
the presentation of the text and the questions. This condition prompted
students to more carefully search for information while answering the
questions, and give more correct answers. This effect however occurred
only for skilled comprehenders – in line with the idea that skilled
comprehenders were able to build an adequate task model in the
“delayed” condition, realizing gaps in their understanding, and closing
these by adequate search decisions. Results by Rouet et al. (2011), and
Salmerón et al. (2015) point in the same direction. They provided evidence that better comprehenders are better equipped to make appropriate search decisions in the face of distracting cues, such as
capitalizing (Rouet et al., 2011), or a lexical overlap between a question
and a hyperlink that is in fact irrelevant to the question (Salmerón et al.,
2015). An adequate task model will not only specify when and where to
find information, but also the scrutiny with which it is to be processed.
In line with this idea Naumann (2019) found that in digital reading more
skilled comprehenders were better in adapting the time they expended
reading task-relevant materials to task difficulty. Also much in line with
this, Kaldes et al. (2024) found that adults scoring high in digital literacies spent more time on average on overall difficult digital reading
items than adults low in literacy.

2. Method
2.1. Sampling and subjects
According to PISA’s stratified sampling scheme, first schools were
sampled within each country, and then 35 students matching PISA’s
target population were randomly sampled from each school. We selected
all those students who participated in the PISA 2012 Computer-Based
Assessment (CBA), who completed one cluster of digital reading and
one cluster of complex problem solving each, and who were assigned a
minimum of one cluster print reading (N = 13,080, 32 countries,
country-wise 218 ≤ N ≤ 1313). Since booklet rotations are assigned
randomly, these students form a random sample of the whole data set.

metafor, version 4.6–0 (Viechtbauer, 2010). Random-effects metaanalysis decomposes an effect into a fixed effect common to all studies, a
random study-specific effect, and a random effect that captures variance
due to sampling error. Thus, other than fixed-effects meta-analysis, it
does not assume the existence of one common effect size, but rather
assumes a distribution of effect sizes with a variance that emerges from
the studies differing in methods, materials, or – as in our case – populations. The effect sizes can, but need not be, independent from each
other. In our case, we integrate only one effect size per country per
analysis, meaning that effect sizes are treated as independent from one
another. The model we employed can be written as follows (see e.g.

2.2.3. Problem solving skill
In PISA 2012, Problem solving skill was measured with 16 units
comprising 42 items (OECD, 2014a). To measure complex (as opposed
to static) problem solving, we used 27 items that required active
exploration, containing a problem space and an item space (items
classified as “interactive” in the item classification given in OECD,
2014b, Table A6, p. 417). In the item space, students were presented
with a question. In the problem space students could work on the
problem, explore it, and generate information. The different units were
designed to contain as little content-related prerequisites as possible,
and to minimize reading load (OECD, 2013). The unit “Climate Control”
is an example of a unit measuring complex problem solving (see Fig. 1).
In order to find out how the climate control works, students are
instructed to explore it and subsequently work on two items within the
unit that allude to the relations between the controls and the outcomes
(Item 1) and to setting humidity and temperature to pre-specified settings (Item 2). The EAP reliability was 0.76.

4. Discussion
The present study is the first to investigate the combined association
of comprehension and problem solving skill with navigation and performance in digital reading. Our investigation followed suggestions that
accomplishing digital reading tasks entails not only comprehension, but
also cognitive processes theoretically associated with problem solving.
This is true especially in digital reading scenarios where it is not clear
from the start which sources to access, and integrate into the task solution. Consistent with this, we found evidence that navigation behavior
(as a means of bridging the gap between an informational given and goal
state), and eventual digital reading performance, is predicted by problem solving skill over and above comprehension skill. In addition, we
found evidence that both skills interact in a compensatory fashion in the
prediction of navigation precision and digital reading: Students for
whom digital reading poses a problem due to weak comprehension skill
might nevertheless adequately navigate the information space, and
eventually solve the task, when they are in command of good problem
solving skill. On the other hand, if students are in command of good

empirical level have to provide experimental evidence for a beneficial
role of problem solving skill in digital reading, but also on a conceptual

A second limitation pertaining to the data-base used in the present
research is that the data was collected already in the year 2011. While
we did have good reasons to work with this particular data set to answer
our research questions as this data set is still the only large-scale data set
that contains all three variables, (interactive) problem solving,
comprehension skill, and digital reading, the question stands how
generalizable our findings might be over time. The rise of social media as
a mass and global phenomenon began in parallel to design of the digital
reading assessment materials used in this research. Thus, assessment
materials designed today would contain more texts such as social media
or microblog posts. However, on a more structural level, the problems of
navigation and evaluation, which can make an information space opaque and turn a digital reading task into a problem, have stayed the same,
and if any gained additional importance. From this perspective, we assume that the present results would hold today. Another issue is that
student’s reading skills and habits might have changed. For example,
due to the increasing availability of digital devices, students’ navigation
skills might be practiced now so intensely on an every-day basis that
navigation behavior is no longer predicted by comprehension skill and
problem solving. We deem this, however, unlikely. First, the increasing
use of digital devices is not per se carried out in a manner that contributes to deep learning and deep reading (Altamura et al., 2025), and
to practicing the cognitively demanding activity of task-oriented navigation. Second, there is evidence that students still differ widely in their
navigation skill. For instance, He, Borgonovi, and Suárez-Álvarez (2023)
analyzed navigation on the basis of one single multiple text reading item
from PISA 2018, and found ample variance in students’ navigation
behavior. Moreover, navigation profiles matched theoretical expectations such as students visiting all relevant pages without ‘rushing’ obtained the highest scores on the reading proficiency scale. This result is
also interesting as these students would have had a high score in Precision (visiting all relevant pages) and Adaptive processing (not rushing)
in the present research. Another result that indicates that while youths
ever more frequently interact with digital text, their competences in its
use do not necessarily increase, are meta-analytic results from Delgado
et al. (2018). Analyzing comprehension between digital and printed
text, these authors found year of publication to be a moderator to the
“screen-inferiority effect”, thus students performing worse with digital
than with printed text. Running against the idea that with the increasing
prevalence of digital text respective competencies increase, they found
that the later a study’s data was collected, the larger the screen inferiority
effect was, i.e. the worse participants’ performance with digital text was
relative to their performance with printed text. However, a (conceptual)
replication of the present results with data from any more recent
assessment that measured the constructs of interest in this research
would be a valuable avenue of further research.

4.1. Educational implications
Taken at face value, the present results have clear educational implications. In the 21st century, participation in society in private,
educational, and professional contexts is hardly conceivable without the
ability to navigate opaque information spaces. Thus, making the ‘right’
navigational choices is as vital as adequately processing information. To
achieve this, besides prior content knowledge and adequate instruction
(Wineburg et al., 2022), as the present research once again demonstrates, good comprehension skill is vital (see also Hahnel et al., 2016;
Naumann & Salmerón, 2016; Salmerón, Strømsø, et al., 2018).
Comprehension of text has been extensively studied on many layers of
the reading process, such phonological awareness and letter knowledge
(Schneider et al., 2000), reading individual words (Tunmer & Nicholson,
2011), or passage comprehension (Duke et al., 2011). Also, there is a
host of well-researched intervention programs to specifically train
comprehension such as iStart-2 (Snow et al., 2016), or TuinLEC (VidalAbarca et al., 2016). The present study highlights that these skills are
important not ‘only’ for the comprehension of traditional paper-based
linear texts, but also for accomplishing the problem-solving that often
comes with digital reading. This is because good comprehension skill
helps readers to build, maintain and update an adequate task model
entailing what information has been acquired, is relevant, is trustworthy, or is lacking, and if it is lacking, what to do to acquire it. This
means for educators that teaching proficient comprehension is a crucial
task to prepare students for the challenges of the 21st century knowledge
society.
The present research also showed that problem solving might alleviate negative effects of lacking comprehension skill to some extent. It is
however important to note that effects of problem solving were
completely alleviated only for students at the upper end of the
comprehension skill distribution. Thus, for a majority of students, being
proficient problem solvers will help them to adequately navigate. This
again points to a need for instructional curricula to foster students’
competencies to deal with complex problems, and to the need for
research into approaches to directly train students’ complex problem
solving skill, which are only in their infancy (e.g. Greiff et al., 2014;
Kretschmar & Süß, 2015). This is even more so, as many approaches to
foster complex problem solving are targeted at average or even gifted
learners with good language abilities (Glazewski & Ertmer, 2020) –
while those with poor language abilities are those that need problem
solving the most when they navigate digital spaces. An optimistic note in
this vein is that some instructional designs to foster complex problem
solving, such as the explicit teaching of meta-strategic knowledge, have
been found to work especially well in low-performing students (Zohar &
David, 2008).
4.2. Limitations and directions
The claim that strengthening students’ problem solving skill might
also strengthen their proficient navigation and digital reading comes
with a number of caveats. First and foremost, the present data is
correlational, rendering causal claims risky at the very least. More
specifically, since reading was not a major domain in PISA 2012, the
present study had to miss out on a number of potentially relevant variables that are predictive of navigation and digital reading performance.
Most prominent among these is knowledge of metacognitive strategies
such as comprehension monitoring. These strategies facilitate the
13

J. Naumann et al.

“cold” cognitive processing only. However, Precision and Adaptive
processing might also be seen as instances of motivated and perseverant
task engagement, or “behavioral engagement”, following Guthrie et al.’s
(2012) model of reading engagement. According to these authors,
engagement in reading is directly dependent on motivational variables
such as topic interest and reading enjoyment. These ideas are supported
by findings indicating that reading motivation predicts reading amount
(Guthrie et al., 1999), that information-oriented online reading
engagement predicts task-adaptive navigation (Naumann, 2015), that
topic interest predicts persistent reading and through this comprehension (Ainley et al., 2002), that students higher in reading enjoyment
better align time on reading tasks to the tasks’ difficulties (Naumann,
2019), and that students higher in reading enjoyment are less prone to
“position effects” (Nagy et al., 2018), meaning that the frequent performance decline over the course of an assessment (Borgonovi & Biecek,
2016) is less pronounced for students high in reading enjoyment. From
these perspectives, proficient comprehension and problem solving skills
might be seen as a necessary, but not sufficient condition for precise and
adaptive navigation.
Moreover, as pointed out in List and Alexander’s Cognitive Affective
Engagement Model of Multiple Text Comprehension (List & Alexander,
2017a), besides motivation attitudes towards a text’s stance might
impact processing: A reader might be able to navigate precisely. However, if they might tell already e.g. from the description of a hyperlink
that their attitudes will be inconsistent with the stance of a text that may
be accessed through this hyperlink, they might discard it for reasons of
maintaining cognitive consistency (Maier & Richter, 2014). A similar
consideration holds for Adaptive processing: As Social Psychology has
pointed out, attitudes influence the processing of textual information to
the effect that attitude-inconsistent information is processed with more
scrutiny, while attitude-consistent information is readily accepted (Lord
et al., 1979), or to the effect that attitude-consistent information is integrated more easily into the situation model (Maier & Richter, 2013).
Future research will have to seek out how interest or enjoyment in
reading, and attitudes, affect the selection and processing of sources in
digital reading scenarios.
A fourth limitation is that some effects were significant in (almost) all
individual countries, like the main effect of problem solving skill on
Precision. Other effects, particularly the interactive effects of problem
solving and reading skill, while being significant meta-analytically,
turned out significant only in roughly half of individual countries. In
the present research, we had no hypotheses regarding the interaction of
proficient navigation and digital reading with features of countries and
educational systems. One observation is however that the interaction
between problem solving and reading skill predicted navigation especially in countries performing high in problem solving and lesser in low
performing countries: The correlation between the interactive effect of
reading and problem solving skill on Precision and the country mean for
problem solving in the present sample of countries amounts to − 0.74.
This suggests some kind of “threshold” model: If the average level of
problem solving skill is too low on average within a population, even
relatively high levels of problem solving skill within that population
cannot level out failing comprehension when it comes to selecting
relevant contents from an opaque information environment.
Finally, the present study tapped only the surface of how problem
solving processes enter navigation and digital reading. For example, we
used only scored problem solving item responses as a predictor. However, many tests of problem solving skill, the paradigms employed in
PISA among them, allow for the theory-based derivation of problem
solving processes too (e.g. Han et al., 2023, Molnár et al., 2022; see also
He, Shi, & Tighe, 2023). From this perspective, in a direct extension of
the present research, behavior based indicators of problem solving
might be used as a predictor of navigation in addition to scored responses. Also, navigational logs are but one data channel to investigate
navigation, or problem solving behavior in digital reading (Järvelä &
Bannert, 2021). Finer grained future studies could utilize additional data

channels (Fan et al., 2022), e.g. building groups of poor vs. strong
problem solvers, combined with poor vs. strong comprehenders, and
investigate how persons that are strong in either, but poor in the other
skill engage with digital reading problems using methods such as eyetracking or think-aloud protocols, thus opening the black box of how
exactly problem solving and comprehension skills compensate for each
other in navigation and digital reading.
