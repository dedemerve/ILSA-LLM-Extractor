A B S T R A C T
Collaborative problem-solving (CPS) has become essential in today’s society because it provides
opportunities for deep learning and critical thinking. During CPS, students tend to spontaneously
adopt different roles that facilitate the interactive process. Previous studies have focused on the
influence of emergent roles on learning outcomes and CPS skills, but have rarely used procedural
approaches to analyze the diversity of and changes in emergent roles during CPS. The availability
of naturally occurring educational discourse data provides the opportunity to identify emergent
roles at a fine-grained level. Therefore, this study identified emergent roles based on fine-grained
coding and used hierarchical regression and epistemic network analysis to explore the influence
of emergent roles on learning outcomes through CPS activities and their co-occurrence with CPS
skills. The identification of emergent roles was based on observations of 54 undergraduates and
postgraduates. The results showed that the participants played different, flexible roles during the
CPS process. Roles focused on content and performance appeared more frequently, while those
related to social emotion and evaluation were relatively less frequent. Furthermore, knowledge
seeker and distractor significantly impeded the success of CPS activities, and harmonizer was
conducive to high-quality learning outcomes. In addition, information giver always co-occurred
with the skills of maintaining team function. Content- and performance-focused roles usually
appeared together with negotiation and coordination. These findings highlight the importance of
emergent roles during the CPS process, and provide some practical suggestions for improving
learning outcomes and CPS skills.

better equipped to deal with complex problems (Liu, Liu & Zhang, 2019). Previous studies have developed various frameworks to

Previous studies have identified the relationship of roles, learning skills and outcomes in collaborative inquiry learning and
problem-based learning (Fiore et al., 2010; Heinim¨aki, Volet & Vauras, 2020). Although a few studies have explored the overall
relationship between emergent roles and CPS skills (Dowell et al., 2020), most of them have categorized emergent roles into fixed
categories through clustering or social network analysis, which might be difficult to capture dynamic changes in roles and how they
relate to CPS skills and outcomes from the procedural perspective. Thus, it is necessary to conduct fine-grained analysis to explore the
dynamic patterns of multiple emergent roles and CPS skills.
The purpose of this study is to analyze the emergent roles that occur in CPS and their relationships with learning outcomes and CPS
skills based on fine-grained procedural data.
2. Literature review
2.1. Emergent roles
According to the social identity theory, roles represent individuals’ unique identifications and behaviors, which are established
through affective perceptions of the community (Wu et al., 2022). Previous studies have divided roles into two categories: scripted
roles and emergent roles. Scripted roles are pre-defined and assigned to regulate learners’ participatory behaviors, while emergent
roles refer to the function and responsibility of individuals to actively manage their behaviors based on learning tasks or needs (Strijbos
& Weinberger, 2010). Although scripted roles can promote high-quality collaborative learning, they may interfere with the natural
collaborative process (Raes, Schellens, De & Wever, 2016). Given that collaborative learning groups are often formed without
pre-defining individual tasks (Kirschner & Erkens, 2013), this study focuses on the unrestricted emergent roles adopted by learners.
There are many ways to identify emergent roles. Some studies have summarized emergent roles by analyzing the interview data.
For example, Wang and Li (2021) summarized 5 types of emergent roles in science-learning activities through thematic analysis of
interviews. However, this approach was relatively difficult to reflect the dynamic changes in roles during collaboration. Therefore,
some studies have used social network analysis or cluster analysis to evaluate emergent roles. Ouyang and Chang (2019) summarized
the emergent roles in online discussion as those of a leader, initiator, influencer, mediator, regulator, and peripheral participant using
social network analysis. Dowell et al. (2020)) used cluster analysis to divide roles in CPS into five categories: driver, follower,
influential performer, lurker, and marginalist. Although these roles reflected the intrinsic characteristics of the collaboration to some
extent, they still ascribed learners to a fixed role, which might be difficult to reflect the diversity of roles. To solve this problem, some
studies put forward coding schemes for emergent roles based on data related to the dialogue or behavior in the collaborative process. It
was widely accepted that roles could be described as being task-related and social-emotional (Heinim¨aki, Volet, Jones, Laakkonen &
Vauras, 2021). Volet, Vauras, Salo and Khosa (2017) further subdivided task-related roles into content-focused, performance-focused,
and evaluation-focused roles. These studies laid the foundation for the fine-grained analysis of emergent roles in collaboration,
providing a novel perspective for increased understanding of the CPS.
2.2. CPS
2.2.1. Learning outcomes in CPS
CPS refers to the attempt by two or more learners to share their skills and knowledge to obtain a unified solution to a problem
(Roschelle & Teasley, 1995). Nieswandt, McEneaney and Affolter (2020) proposed a triple problem-solving space model, which
regarded CPS as an interplay of three dimensions: cognitive/content, social/relational, and affective. In CPS activities, students
brought individual resources to the group from these three dimensions, and adjusted their behaviors accordingly through the inte-
gration of information from self and others. Students enacted various socio-cognitive behaviors and identified their positions in
complex interactions, thus forming a variety of roles (Dowell et al., 2020).
To measure the effectiveness of CPS activities, many studies have focused on learning outcomes, such as written reports, solutions,
or proposals (Dowell et al., 2020). However, learners always achieve uneven learning outcomes in CPS activities (Lin et al., 2016).
Thus, some studies have explored the individual factors influencing learning outcomes, such as achievement motivation and prior
knowledge. For example, Shan (2015) pointed out that the learning outcomes from CPS are affected by the motivation of collaborators
to participate in CPS. In addition, Bulu and Pedersen (2012) found that scaffolding could support learners with low prior knowledge to
achieve better results in CPS tasks. Recently, the focus of CPS has been evolving from highly individualistic products to a more

2.2.2. CPS skills
CPS skills refer to the ability of learners to effectively participate in the CPS process, which are achieved through effective
communication and interaction (OECD, 2017). Previous studies have focused on developing theoretical frameworks to explain and
evaluate CPS skills. Most frameworks highlighted the significance of both social and cognitive processes in CPS. Traditional studies
evaluated social and cognitive aspects of CPS skills through questionnaires. For example, Liu (2021) designed a questionnaire and
divided CPS skills into four areas: cooperation consciousness, cooperation skills, problem-solving ability, and self-efficacy. Although
this approach can provide valuable information, it affords a limited understanding of the actual process of collaboration. With the
development of computer technology, some researchers have focused on how to accurately assess CPS skills in interactions with online
platforms. The Program for International Student Assessment (PISA) divided CPS skills into cognitive skills and social skills, and
evaluated CPS skills based on students’ choices in interacting with a computer program (OECD, 2017). This was viable for large-scale
standardized assessments, but some researchers criticized it could not replicate a natural interactive process because the computerized
responses were pre-programmed (Herborn, Stadler, Mustafi´c & Greiff, 2020). Recently, more researchers have called for in-depth
analysis to assess CPS skills in interactions with real people (Care et al., 2016). For example, Sun et al. (2022) divided CPS ability
into three dimensions based on the learners’ discourse in real interactions: building shared knowledge, negotiating and coordinating,
and maintaining team functions, which provided a reference for the fine-grained evaluation of CPS skills.
Since CPS involves complex cooperation and information exchange between individuals, CPS skills could be better cultivated in the
course of an interactive process (Griffin, Care & McGaw, 2012). Consequently, the relationships between CPS skills and factors
reflecting interactive processes should be carefully considered, one of which pertains to emergent roles.
2.3. Emergent roles, learning outcomes, and CPS skills
Some studies have initially explored the relationship of roles, learning skills and outcomes in collaborative inquiry learning and
problem-based learning. For example, Heinim¨aki et al. (2020) found that higher-achieving groups in collaborative science-inquiry
settings usually exhibited versatile science-oriented role configurations. Fiore et al. (2010) indicated that uncooperative partici-
pants influenced interactions in problem-based learning. Compared with these contexts, CPS places a stronger emphasis on the
collaborative process and the development of interpersonal skills (Ouyang, Xu & Cukurova, 2022). Although CPS was an essential and
unique context in collaborative learning, a systematic review of the influence of roles on CPS skills suggested that few researchers
focused on the effects of emergent roles (He, Shi, Choi & Zhai, 2023). An exploratory study used cluster analysis to identify emergent
roles and developed a linear mixed-effect model of roles, CPS skills, and learning outcomes (Dowell et al., 2020). This method classified
emergent roles into fixed categories but was less focused on dynamic changes in roles during CPS processes. However, the emergent
roles would fluctuate dynamically among participants (Heinim¨aki et al., 2021). A student enacted various emergent roles during the
collaboration. Furthermore, individuals’ roles might be changed under the influence of others (Wu et al., 2022). Therefore, it is
necessary to investigate the emergent roles dynamically and explore their impacts in CPS through a fine-grained procedural
perspective to deepen the understanding of CPS.
The purpose of this study is to analyze the emergent roles that develop during CPS activities and their relationship with learning
outcomes and CPS skills based on fine-grained procedural data. The specific research questions are as follows:
RQ1.
What emergent roles (content-focused, performance-focused, evaluation-focused, and social-emotional roles) do learners
adopt during a CPS process?
RQ2.
How do emergent roles influence learning outcomes in CPS after controlling for prior knowledge and achievement motivation?
RQ3.
How do emergent roles develop in conjunction with CPS skills?
3. Method
3.1. Participants and context
The participants in this study were 54 undergraduates and postgraduates from a normal university in Beijing (mean age 21.17
±1.40 years; 46 females). Because achievement motivation and prior knowledge can affect learning outcomes in CPS (Bulu & Ped-
ersen, 2012; Shan, 2015), we used questionnaires to measure these two factors. The participants were divided into 18 heterogeneous
triads based on their questionnaire responses. The groups then undertook a three-hour CPS activity with the theme of “migration of
workers” (see Fig. 1). Specifically, based on population data for Shangrao, a city in China, the participants were required to collab-
oratively develop a multiple regression model to predict the change in the population of Shangrao from 2023 to 2028, analyze the
reasons for the change, propose measures to attract more people to the city, and write a group solution. We chose this theme since it
was a hot topic in Chinese society and was sufficiently complex to require CPS. Because of the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, one

3.2. Procedure
Before the CPS activity, the participants completed a questionnaire designed to measure their achievement motivation and self-
reported knowledge of statistics. Then, they were assigned to one of 18 heterogeneous triads based on their questionnaire re-
sponses. The participants were also given reading material regarding the CPS task and told they could search for additional information
online.
At the start of the activity, the participants were required to complete an initial personal mind map within 30 min using the
materials provided, which was used to measure their prior knowledge and gave them an opportunity to prepare for the CPS activity.
Next, they engaged in a 120-minute CPS activity, working collaboratively to solve the problem. Finally, they spent 30 min completing a
second personal mind map, which reflected their learning outcomes as a result of the CPS activity. The experimental procedure is
shown in Fig. 2.
3.3. Data collection and analysis
3.3.1. Coding of emergent roles
To enable fine-grained analysis of the emergent roles, we transcribed the videos of the groups’ CPS activities into text. The coding
was performed at the turn level, which referred to all the continuous utterances of a speaker in a period of time (Hennessy, Howe,
Mercer & Vrikki, 2020). The end of a particular turn was marked by the switching of speaker and listener or the silence of all par-
ticipants. The coding scheme for emergent roles was proposed by Volet et al. (2017) (see Table 1). Only one emergent role from the
coding scheme was assigned to each turn. Because turns were typically brief, only one role emerged in most turns. In some rare longer
turns, we followed Volet et al. (2017) and coded only the dominant functional role in the given turn. Two coders independently coded
all of the data, and the kappa value was 0.851. Following the independent coding, the two coders discussed any discrepancies until
consensus was achieved. The coding of CPS skills followed the same steps.
3.3.2. Pre-test questionnaire and measurement of achievement motivation
Given that achievement motivation and prior knowledge can affect learning outcomes in CPS, we conducted a pre-test to perform
heterogeneous grouping. The structure of the pre-test questionnaire is shown in Fig. 3. The self-reported prior knowledge was
developed in conjunction with this CPS context and reviewed by an experienced expert in educational technology, which included 4
items. The achievement motivation was tested by the Achievement Goals Questionnaire (Elliot & Church, 1997), which consisted of 16
items and was applicable to Chinese student samples (Zong, Zhang & Yao, 2017). The questionnaire had a high degree of reliability
(Cronbach’s α = 0.906). Responses were measured using a five-point Likert-type scale where 1 = “completely inconsistent” and 5 =
“completely consistent.”

3.3.3. Measurement of prior knowledge and learning outcomes
Since the mind maps were relatively objective to reflect knowledge acquisition in CPS than the self-reported data, we used initial
mind maps to evaluate prior knowledge, and the mind maps created following the CPS activity to evaluate learning outcomes. Two
trained coders scored the participants’ mind maps based on the criteria proposed by Docktor, Dornfeld, Frodermann, Heller and Yang
(2016) (see Table 2). The inter-rater reliability was satisfactory (Cohen’s kappa = 0.845).
3.3.4. Coding of CPS skills
The coding of CPS skills was also performed at the turn level. We adopted the coding scheme for CPS skills proposed by Sun et al.
(2022), which divided CPS skills into three major dimensions and was applicable to analyze discourses of the collaborative learning
process (see Table 3). Two coders independently coded all of the data, and the kappa value was 0.928.

3.3.5. Statistical analyses
Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the types and distribution of emergent roles in the CPS process. To further analyze the
relationship between emergent roles and learning outcomes, a hierarchical regression model was developed. Hierarchical regression
divides independent variables into a series of sequences, and regression analysis is undertaken sequentially (Qiu, 2009). By comparing
the determination coefficients (R2) of the regression models based on different sequences, the effects of additional variables can be
analyzed (Mao & Song, 2018). We used prior knowledge and achievement motivation as the first sequence of independent variables of
Model 1. The normalized data on the proportions of emergent roles were added as the second sequence of Model 2. The learning
outcomes were the dependent variables of both models in this study. Then, the effect of emergent roles on learning outcomes was able
to be analyzed by comparing the R2 of the two models. To identify the patterns of emergent roles and CPS skills, we used the epistemic
network analysis (ENA) web tool (Marquart, Hinojosa, Swiecki, Eagan & Shaffer, 2018). ENA provides insights into the connections
among different factors by measuring their co-occurrence and representing the relationships in dynamic network models, and has been
widely used to explore qualitative and quantitative research questions (Shaffer, Collier & Ruis, 2016). We established an ENA model to
explore the co-occurrence of emergent roles and CPS skills.

4. Results
4.1. Emergent roles in the CPS process
The descriptive statistical analysis was adapted to analyze the frequency and proportion of various emergent roles (see Table 4).
There were several emergent roles in CPS activities, but they were not evenly distributed. Roles focused on content and performance
were most common, such as information givers (35.93%), opinion providers (13.44%), information seekers (13.44%), and knowledge
providers (9.54%). In contrast, roles related to social emotion and evaluation, such as harmonizer (1.80%), distractor (1.11%), and
challenger (0.20%), appeared less frequently.
4.2. The influence of emergent roles on learning outcomes after controlling for prior knowledge and achievement motivation
The hierarchical regression models were developed to identify the influence of emergent roles on learning outcomes. Before
developing the models, we adapted the correlation analysis (see Table 5). Prior knowledge (PK) and harmonizers (HA) had significant
positive correlations with learning outcomes (LO) (p < 0.05), while knowledge seekers (KS) and distractors (DI) had significant
negative associations with learning outcomes (LO) (p < 0.01). The results reflected it was necessary to establish the regression models
to provide stronger evidence for the relationship of these factors. Then, we tested several assumptions of hierarchical regressions:
linearity, homoscedasticity, and normality. In addition, we eliminated the “information giver (IG)” to ensure that the variance inflation
factors (VIF) coefficients were less than 3, thus there were no multicollinearity problems in the regression models (Slinker & Glantz,
1985).
After testing the assumptions, prior knowledge and achievement motivation were entered into the first variable sequence to create
Model 1, resulting in an insignificant R2 of 6.6% (see Table 6). It indicated that more factors influencing learning outcomes should be
considered in addition to prior knowledge and achievement motivation. Then, the proportions of emergent roles were added into the
second variable sequence to create Model 2, and the R2 increased to 21.0%. The results for Model 2 (p < 0.05) showed that the addition
of emergent roles had a significant impact on learning outcomes.

4.3. The relationship between emergent roles and CPS skills
ENA was used to analyze the relationship between emergent roles and CPS skills (see Fig. 4). Primarily, the information giver (IG)
often appeared simultaneously with the skills of maintaining team function (MTF), indicating that the role of providing information
was necessary to maintain communication during the CPS process. To deepen our understanding of the connections, we selected some
typical examples in CPS. The co-occurrence of the information giver (IG) and maintaining team function (MTF) mainly occurred when
participants shared their materials to provide support or guidance to others. For example, when Student 1 asked for some support
regarding the analytical method, Student 2 shared some resources that provided additional information and detailed instructions for
each step (see Table 7).
The information seeker (IS), information giver (IG), knowledge provider (KP), and opinion provider (OP) all displayed together
with negotiation and coordination (NC) (see Fig. 4), reflecting the potential for content- and performance-focused roles to promote in-
depth thinking and coordination. In the example shown in Table 8, Student 1 asked questions about the choice of independent var-
iables, and Student 2 briefly explained that they should gradually focus on specific variables. Student 3 proposed that they should
divide up the work, which helped to solve the problem quickly.
5. Discussion
This study aimed to identify emergent roles in CPS and their relationships with learning outcomes and CPS skills based on fine-
grained procedural data. Using descriptive statistics, hierarchical regression analysis, and ENA, we made several important findings.
5.1. Focus on the diversity and contextuality of emergent roles
The results revealed the overall distribution of emergent roles in CPS. The emergent roles played by individuals differed and were
flexible, reflecting the participants’ interactions and role variations in CPS (Salazar, 1996). Content- and performance-focused roles
appeared more frequently, while those related to social emotion and evaluation appeared less often. The results differed from those
obtained in previous studies, which might be because the distribution of emergent roles was context-dependent (Driskell, Driskell,
Burke & Salas, 2017). For example, there were more roles related to evaluation and social emotions in scientific argumentation
(Maloney, 2007). This context usually includes cognitive and social-emotional conflicts, which requires participants to critically
evaluate their logical consistency and deal with various social-emotional challenges (Isoh¨at¨al¨a, N¨aykki, J¨arvel¨a & Baker, 2018).
Compared with previous studies, CPS tends to prompt learners to construct and maintain unified solutions (OECD, 2017). The
inherently social nature of CPS leads to fewer challengers and distractors (Mayer, 1998). Furthermore, complex problems require
multiple inputs to get a solution (Unal & Cakir, 2021), leading more content-focused people to seek out plenty of information and
knowledge. At the same time, CPS follows a series of structured steps (Nelson, 1999), stimulating more performance-focused par-
ticipants to express procedural opinions to facilitate the activities. The findings of this study highlighted the unique features of
emergent roles in CPS and confirmed the situational nature of role distribution, which enriched our understanding of CPS and
reminded us to analyze emergent roles in combination with specific contexts.
5.2. Eliminate negative influences of knowledge seekers and distractors on learning outcomes, and facilitate more harmonizers
In this study, we examined the influence of emergent roles on learning outcomes by developing a hierarchical regression model and
found that knowledge seekers were not conducive to high-quality learning outcomes. This finding was inconsistent with a previous
study, which found a positive relationship between collaborative learning outcomes and knowledge seekers (Volet et al., 2017). This
might be caused by the different proportions of knowledge seekers and knowledge providers. The number of knowledge providers was
much larger than that of knowledge seekers in the previous study (Volet et al., 2017), which might satisfy the needs of knowledge
seekers by prompting the discussion into a virtuous circle. Conversely, the relatively small number of knowledge providers in our study

were unable to meet the needs of the knowledge seekers, resulting in their function of stimulating deeper cognitive content processing

dently, enriching our understanding of role interactions and their impact. This also meant that when knowledge providers were
insufficient, appropriate scaffolding needed to be provided to supplement the knowledge necessary for CPS.
In addition, the distractors significantly hampered the CPS activities. Although the influence of distractors on collaborative learning
has been explored, the results have been mixed. Some studies have found that distractors can enhance learning outcomes because
appropriate off-task talk can support coordination and resist marginalization (Langer-Osuna, 2016). However, other studies have
reported a negative impact of distractors on collaboration (Maloney, 2007). The results of our study support the latter view, confirming
that distractors in CPS had a negative impact on learning outcomes. Notably, the studies advocating the positive effect of distractors
often included various interventions, such as guiding students to reflect on the collaborative process, or allowing them to choose their
partners (Langer-Osuna, Gargroetzi, Munson & Chavez, 2020). Hence, the activities should be carefully designed to avoid interference
from distractors and regulate the negotiation atmosphere, such as guiding reflections on learning processes or allowing flexibility in
choosing peers (Langer-Osuna et al., 2020). The results of this study enhanced our understanding of the impact of distractors in CPS
and demonstrated the importance of providing interventions to avoid the negative impacts of distractors.
Meanwhile, harmonizers had a positive impact on learning outcomes. This result was in accordance with that of a study by Chang,
Chen and Wang (2011), who found that harmonizers positively affected individual grades in online discussions. Our study further
verified the positive effect of harmonizers in the context of CPS. Harmonizers focused on building teamwork, and accounted for
positive social behaviors, such as joking and encouraging (Benne & Sheats, 2007). These behaviors were important aspects of
socio-emotional regulation, which was beneficial in collaborative learning (N¨aykki, Isoh¨at¨al¨a & J¨arvel¨a, 2021). The positive effects
were seen when an individual successfully promoted social regulation and equal participation (Rogat & Adams-Wiggins, 2014).
Therefore, it might be effective to facilitate more harmonizers and promote learning performance in CPS by providing socio-emotional
regulation scripts.
5.3. Cultivate more content- and performance-focused roles to promote CPS skills
This study observed a strong connection between emergent roles and CPS skills. First, information givers tend to use skills for
maintaining team function. This finding supported previous claims that less productive roles were more engaged in maintaining
communication, which identified the overall patterns of emergent roles and collaborative learning skills through cluster analysis and
linear mixed-effects modeling (Dowell et al., 2020). In this study, we specifically identified the co-occurrence between information
givers and maintaining team function based on fine-grained analysis of discourse data. Maintaining team function involved efforts to
maintain a functional team by assuming individual responsibilities, taking initiative, and co-regulation (Rosen, 2017). In this study,
although the information givers were less involved in the cognitive process (Heinim¨aki et al., 2020), they still actively shared materials
or information to provide guidance, which promoted cognitive regulation and problem-solving. Meanwhile, one important aspect of
maintaining team function was the provision of task-relevant information (Sun et al., 2022), which was the main responsibility of
information givers.
Additionally, roles focused on content and performance always emerged in conjunction with negotiation and coordination. Pre-
vious studies have found that emergent roles are conducive to participants constructing knowledge and negotiating solutions
(H¨am¨al¨ainen, Niilo-R¨am¨a, Lainema & Oksanen, 2018). The results of our study were similar to those of previous studies, which found
that drivers contributed impactful knowledge and excelled in negotiations regarding ideas (Dowell et al., 2020). We also observed
connections between content-focused roles and negotiation, and further emphasized the negotiation and coordination skills of
performance-focused roles. On the one hand, this indicated that rich information, knowledge, and opinions had the potential to elicit
further questions, explanations, and justifications from other participants (Ding, 2009). On the other hand, in-depth negotiation and
coordination might inspire participants to produce a collective solution by integrating different information, sharing knowledge, and
allocating labor as required (Hayashi, 2018). In conclusion, the above results enhanced our understanding of the connection between
emergent roles and CPS skills, which is beneficial for the in-depth analysis of the CPS process and the design of interventions aimed at
cultivating CPS skills. The conclusions also suggested guiding students to provide more information, knowledge, and opinions in the
CPS process, which was beneficial to promote maintaining team function, negotiation and coordination.
6. Application and further research
6.1. Educational application
This study expands our understanding of the CPS process from the perspective of emergent roles. The findings reveal the diversity
and contextuality of emergent roles, which help teachers understand the dynamics of emergent roles during the CPS process and
enhance the sensitivity to CPS contexts. As preparation for CPS activities, it will be useful if teachers can provide detailed introductions
about the characteristics of different roles and encourage flexible adoption by students.
Besides, this study explores the relationships of emergent roles, learning outcomes and CPS skills. Therefore, it will be beneficial to
design interventions aimed at helping students adopt roles positively associated with learning outcomes or CPS skills. For example,
teachers can stimulate more content- and performance-focused roles by guiding students to provide more valid information, knowl-
edge, or opinions. Another way is to provide socio-emotional regulation scripts to promote harmonizers. Some measures can also be
designed to avoid the emergence of roles that may hinder the CPS process, such as guiding reflections on learning processes or allowing

6.2. Limitations and further research
Although we adopted rigorous procedures in this study, some limitations remained. First, the research context and brief duration of
the experiment limited the generalizability of the findings. The study involved a carefully designed CPS activity aiming to conduct an
in-depth analysis of emergent roles. All participants were from a normal university in China and the sample size was relatively small.
The uneven gender ratio in this normal university resulted in more female samples. Therefore, care should be exercised when applying
the findings of this study to other contexts. In addition, in this study, we mainly analyzed emergent roles at the individual level. Future
research could focus more on emergent roles in CPS at the group level. Finally, in this study, we mainly analyzed transcribed text, thus
some information might be lost. Future studies should combine transcripts with videos and physiological data to enable a more
comprehensive analysis.
