Numerous studies have documented gender differences in self-reported personality, suggesting that women score
higher than men particularly on the Big Five domains of Agreeableness and Neuroticism. Previous studies also
indicate that gender differences can vary across facets of the same personality domain. However, whether these
gender differences generalize across countries or instead vary across countries is not yet clear. The inconclusiveness of extant evidence has been attributed to methodological differences in the samples and the questionnaire adaptations. We therefore revisited gender differences in Big Five personality domains in 27 countries
based on comprehensive samples representative of the respective adult populations (total N = 143,313) and on
questionnaires adapted using consistent translation methodology. Results revealed gender differences in all
domains except Extraversion. The most pronounced differences emerged for Neuroticism and Agreeableness,
with effects of d = 0.40 and.35, respectively. These differences varied in magnitude across countries and were
aggregated across domains higher in more individualistic countries. Interestingly, this pattern was inverse for the
Openness with smaller gender differences in more individualistic countries. Investigating gender differences in
scores on 15 more specific, facet-level traits in a subset of countries, we found that typically one facet per domain
showed the most substantial gender differences.

Numerous studies over the last decades have documented gender
differences in self-reported personality. Personality assessments using
the Big Five taxonomy (e.g., John & Srivastava, 1999) have found
gender differences in scores on all five domains, albeit of different
magnitude (e.g., Feingold, 1994; Hofmann et al., 2025). Typically, these
differences are most pronounced for Neuroticism and Agreeableness,
with women scoring higher than men by around 0.5 SD (e.g., Costa et al.,
2001; Feingold, 1994). For the other three domains, effects have been
smaller—with women scoring higher than men on Conscientiousness,
Openness, and Extraversion by about 0.1 SD in each case—but still
rather robust (e.g., De Bolle et al., 2015). Going beyond these global
personality differences, recent studies investigating gender differences
on a more nuanced level, that of personality facets, indicate that especially for domains with less pronounced differences, namely Extraversion, Conscientiousness, and Openness, gender differences occur only in
some of their facets, for example, the Extraversion facets Dominance or
Assertiveness (e.g., Costa et al., 2001; Hofmann et al., 2025; Lee &
Ashton, 2020). This suggests that investigating more specific, facet-level
traits in addition to the Big Five domains contributes to the

understanding of gender differences.
Two broad lines of explanation have been proposed for these robust
gender differences: Evolutionary factors are assumed to cause dispositional differences through processes of natural selection (e.g., Buss,
1999). Alternatively, several researchers have hypothesized that universal processes of gender-role socialization elicit common stereotypes
about men and women, resulting in gender differences in personality
through socialization effects (e.g. Eagly & Wood, 1999). However, with
differences in personality traits being determined both by genetic factors
and environmental influences (e.g., Schwaba et al., 2026), gender differences in self-reported traits likely reflect the joint operation of
evolutionary and socialization processes.
The approaches result in different assumptions regarding the crosscultural generalizability of gender differences in personality. Whereas
an evolutionary perspective would primarily predict general, cultureindependent gender differences in personality (for an alternative
assumption, see Schmitt et al., 2008), a socialization perspective would
expect gender differences in personality to be smaller in cultures with
more egalitarian gender roles, gender socialization, and sociopolitical

gender equality (Schmitt et al., 2017).
However, evidence on systematic country gender differences in
personality does not support either of these two hypotheses and rather
varies across studies, likely reflecting the idea that gender differences in
traits are multiply determined (e.g., Falk & Hermle, 2018). In a
comprehensive (but highly selective as it only included British Broadcasting Corporation (BBC) website visitors) study of three Big Five traits
(Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism) based on data of over
200,000 respondents from 53 nations, Lippa (2010) found that only
gender differences in Agreeableness varied substantially across countries. Similarly, in their comparison of gender differences in personality
among adolescents in 23 cultures, De Bolle et al. (2015) found that
personality trait differences between boys and girls showed only limited
variation across cultures.
By contrast, other studies (e.g., Costa et al., 2001; Hofmann et al.,
2025; Lee & Ashton, 2020; Schmitt et al., 2008) showed systematic
cultural or country differences in the magnitude of gender differences in
personality. Broadly, results of these studies indicate that gender differences in scores on the aggregated Big Five are more pronounced in
more developed countries, which is contrary to what the socialization
hypothesis would suggest (see Schmitt et al., 2008, 2017), or that they
are more similar in Western countries compared with Asian and African
countries (e.g. Costa et al., 2001), and thus in countries with more equal
opportunities for both genders.1
Why do the results of studies investigating cross-country variations
in gender differences in personality traits differ so greatly? One possible
explanation is that methodological differences in the study designs
might introduce bias (Berggren & Bergh, 2025). Differences in sample
composition and questionnaire adaptation could affect the degree to
which a study identifies gender differences. To our knowledge, all crosscountry comparative studies of gender differences in adult personality to
date have been based on self-selected samples (e.g., online access
panels), or on highly educated, i.e. student samples, thus reducing the
socio-demographic variation within the sample even further. Consequently, the question remains to what degree the found gender differences in personality indeed generalize to the total adult population.
Further, as the language adaptations of the Big Five instruments used
in the different studies have often been of different or even unknown
quality, variations in gender differences in personality self-reports could
be due, at least partly, to variations in translation quality. Support for
this reasoning comes from studies that investigated gender differences
across countries with either a single questionnaire administered in English only (Lippa, 2010) or separating the set of countries into Englishspeaking and non-English speaking nations (Lee & Ashton, 2020).
Both studies showed no major or substantially reduced (compared to
multi-lingual set of countries) cross-country variation in the gender
differences thus indicating that methodological biases due to questionnaire adaptations can overpronounce cross-country variations in gender
differences.
The aim of the present study is to contribute to resolving these
questions by revisiting gender differences in personality traits and cross-

cultural variation in these gender differences using large, non-selective,
multi-national samples based on a probability-based sampling design
and measurement instruments whose cross-cultural comparability has
been ensured through a rigorous, consistent translation process. For this
purpose, we used recent large-scale and international comparative data
representative of the adult population in the 27 nations investigated
(total N = 143,313). For a subset of 11 countries we could also investigate 15 more specific, facet-level traits, allowing us to explore withindomain variations indicated by earlier studies, both aggregated across
countries and separately by country.
1. Method
1.1. Sample and design
Data were collected as part of PIAAC (OECD, 2024), an established
international comparative large-scale study program initiated by the
OECD. It is designed primarily to inform countries about the performance of their adult populations in key cognitive skills, such as literacy
and numeracy, and how it compares internationally. Therefore, a crucial
aim of PIAAC is that the design and all measures used are comparable
across countries and that the samples optimally reflect the total adult
population in each country. To achieve this, PIAAC strives for rigorous
methodological standards (OECD, 2025; for the Big Five assessment, see
also Rammstedt et al., 2025).
Thirty-one countries participated in the second cycle of PIAAC
(referred to as “PIAAC 2023” (OECD, 2024) fielded in 2022–23 and
twenty-nine in the optional assessment of the Big Five. To date, data of
27 of these countries are available as public use files from the OECD
website (https://www.oecd.org/en/data/datasets/piaac-2nd-cycle-data
base.html, see Table S1 https://osf.io/z2uqj).
In each country, probability samples of the adult population were
drawn with a sample size of typically 4000 to 5000 respondents per
country (Ntotal = 143,313 for the 27 countries). The mean age was 41.4
years; 50% of all respondents were female and 27.54% had a higher
education degree. Details on the sampling design are provided in S1,
sample characteristics by country in Table S1.
1.2. Instruments
1.2.1. Personality traits
BFI-2-(X)S. In PIAAC 2023, the Big Five personality traits were
assessed either with the short (BFI-2-S) or the extra-short (BFI-2-XS)
form of the Big Five Inventory–2 (Soto & John, 2017). The BFI-2-XS
comprises three items per Big Five domain to be answered on a 5point rating scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly
agree). The BFI-2-S includes all 15 BFI-2-XS items and 15 additional
items (six items per domain). This ensures that in addition to the Big Five
domain scores, scores for three facets per domain can be computed. To
facilitate readability, all items of the BFI-2-(X)S were adapted slightly to
form full sentences rather than phrases. All PIAAC instruments,
including the BFI-2-(X)S, underwent a rigorous, multi-step translation
process (see Rammstedt et al., 2025).
Sixteen of the 27 analyzed countries implemented the BFI-2-XS, 11
countries the BFI-2-S. The average reliability of the scale scores of the
three item BFI-2-XS scales was α = .45, of the six-item BFI-S scales was
α = .68. (For results per country and domain see Table S2).
The public-use data files do not include item-level information but zstandardized Big Five scale scores, standardized within each country.
Measurement invariance analyses revealed that the Big Five domains
and facets reached at least partial metric invariance across the 28
countries using traditional methods (see OECD, 2025). Furthermore,
most items had highly similar loadings on their respective factors when
using the alignment method (OECD, in press) thus ensuring that relations of the Big Five traits with other variables can be compared across
countries without major bias elicited by differences in loadings across

2.2. Variations in gender differences in personality across countries
As outlined above, earlier studies have been inconsistent regarding
cross-country variation in gender differences in personality (e.g., Costa
et al., 2001; Lippa, 2010). Our study supports the existence of substantial cross-country differences (see Fig. 2): Across all five personality
domains, MD varied across the 27 countries investigated (SD = 0.09).
The smallest gender differences across all five domains were found in
Singapore (MD = 0.41), followed by Israel (MD = 0.51), and Lithuania
(MD = 0.53). By contrast, gender differences in personality were as
much as twice as high in Germany, Norway, and Estonia, with MD values
of around 0.80.
Focusing on the domain-specific differences, gender differences in
almost all countries were largest for Neuroticism and Agreeableness and
markedly lower for the remaining three dimensions. Among the Big Five
domains, the degree to which the gender differences varied across
countries varied, with Agreeableness and Openness showing more crosscountry variations in the gender differences (both SD = 0.11) than did
the other three domains (SD < 0.1).

1.2.2. Gender
Gender/sex was assessed in PIAAC with a binary item (male/female).
1.2.3. Country-level indicators
We used a set of well-established indicators of countries' economic
development and culture (i.e., individualism and gender egalitarianism), which are based mostly on official statistical data and have been
applied in previous studies (e.g., Schmitt et al., 2008). The indicators
used were (a) GDP per capita, (b) the Human Development Index (HDI),
(c) a country's average life expectancy at birth, (d) the Gender Inequality
Index, and (e) the country's score on Hofstede's individualismcollectivism index (for details see S2).
Information on the statistical analysis is provided in the Supplemental Materials S3. All code implemented for the current analyses is
provided on https://osf.io/z2uqj.

2.3. Associations between gender differences in personality and countrylevel economic and cultural indicators

2. Results

As several earlier studies have indicated that gender differences in
personality are more pronounced in more egalitarian, Western countries
(e.g., Costa et al., 2001; Lee & Ashton, 2020), we next investigated if
country indicators were related to the gender differences in the Big Five.
As outlined above, we used a set of related indicators that reflect key
economic and cultural differences and that have been used in previous
studies (e.g., Lee & Ashton, 2020; Schmitt et al., 2008). Table 1 shows
the correlations of the different indicators with the gender differences
for the five domains as well as for the aggregated multivariate gender
difference. Separate results for each indicator are displayed in Fig. S3 in
the Supplemental Material.
Generally, the gender differences tended to show rather pronounced
correlations with the various indicators, ranging between r = − 0.61 and
0.53, with an absolute average of 0.27. However, it should be noted that
these correlations were based on a sample size of N = 27 countries,
leading to large standard errors and susceptibility to outliers. Focusing
on how the overall, multivariate gender differences (MDs) were related
to the country-level indicators, the only significant association found
was for scores on Hofstede's individualism-collectivism index, with 17%
explained variance (MD = 0.41), which indicates that gender differences
in personality were greater in more individualistic countries. Investigating the variations in the correlations of the country indicators with
gender differences in personality separately for each Big Five domain,
associations were highest for the gender differences in Openness
(average |r| = 0.50).
Our results thus indicate that gender differences in personality across
the set of 27 investigated countries were indeed related to a country's
level of individualism while for all other indicators, no substantial associations with the aggregated gender differences in personality
emerged. In line with previous studies (e.g. Costa et al., 2001; Schmitt
et al., 2008) gender differences in aggregated personality were more
pronounced in more individualistic countries. Moreover, our results
show that for the different personality domains associations with individualism markedly vary: While the positive association were reflected
for Neuroticism (0.53) and Extraversion (0.49), Openness was negatively associated with individualism (− 0.52), and no significant relations emerged for Agreeableness and Conscientiousness. Thus, for
Openness, results point in the opposite direction to what the aggregated
findings across domains suggest: Gender differences in Openness were
less pronounced in more developed, individualistic countries.

2.4. Big Five facets' contribution to the understanding of gender
differences in personality

Neuroticism, the domain with generally highest gender differences, the
strong gender difference was due primarily to women scoring markedly
higher than men on Anxiety (d = 0.47). By contrast, for the other two
facets of this domain, Depression and Emotional Volatility, gender differences were markedly smaller (d = 0.21 and d = 0.27, respectively).
Regarding Agreeableness, the second domain showing comparatively large gender differences overall, women on average reported
more than one third of a standard deviation higher scores on Compassion (d = 0.37), whereas the effect sizes for Respectfulness and Trust
were lower (d = 0.24 and d = 0.19, respectively).
For Openness, where women scored 0.15 of a standard deviation
higher than men on the domain level, the largest effects were found for
the facet Aesthetic Sensitivity, with women scoring about a quarter of a
standard deviation higher (d = 0.28), whereas no gender differences
were found for the facets Creative Imagination and Intellectual Curiosity

Because earlier studies (e.g. Costa et al., 2001; Hofmann et al., 2025)
have indicated that the more specific, facet-level traits substantially
contribute to the understanding of gender differences in self-reported
personality—with facets in the same domain sometimes showing
gender differences even in opposite directions—we also investigated the
extent to which gender differences differ among the three facets per
personality dimension as assessed with the BFI-2-S (based on a subset of
11 countries; see Table S1).

2.5. Variation of facet-level gender differences across countries
The above-described rank order of the gender differences by facet
within a domain emerged not only aggregated across countries but also
robustly for most of the 11 countries for which facet-level data were
available (see Fig. 4). For example, in all countries, Sociability showed
the strongest gender difference of the three Extraversion facets.
One exception to the rather homogeneous pattern across countries is
the effect for the Openness facet Intellectual Curiosity for Germany: In
contrast to what has been found in earlier studies (e.g. Hofmann et al.,
2025; Lee & Ashton, 2020) this facet showed small to negligible effects
in all countries but Germany, with women mostly scoring higher than
men. Only for Germany the previously found effect of men scoring
markedly higher than women on this facet could be supported (− 0.31).
Similar to the procedure on the domain level, we then tested the
degree to which countries varied systematically in the facet-level gender
differences. One should bear in mind that facet-level data were available
only for a subset of 11 of the 27 countries. Therefore, the domain-level
and facet-level results are not directly comparable and variation in the
country-level indicators was somewhat reduced. Table 2 shows the associations of the gender effects, both on the facet and domain level, with
the country indicators for the subset of 11 countries (see Fig. S4 for
separate results by indicator).

3. Discussion
In this study, using large-scale data from 27 countries, we revisited
gender differences in scores on the Big Five personality domains and
their facets, as well as cross-national variation in these gender differences. Our study is the first to gauge these gender differences using
comparable, population-representative samples and comparable instruments translated and adapted using a rigorous translation methodology. The consistent sampling and translation methodology allowed us
to rule out important caveats regarding sample selectivity and comparability. Our results replicate earlier findings suggesting that men and
women do indeed differ in their personality self-descriptions.

limited as the sample investigated consisted primarily of OECD countries, which exhibit limited variation in key indicators such as economic
development and gender equality. Against this background, the country
variations—and especially the associations with country indicators on
the level of the separate Big Five domains—found in the present study
are even more notable and worthy of further investigation using high
quality samples from a more diverse set of countries.

indicators. Thus, it would be interesting to investigate these facet-level
gender differences in a more comprehensive set of countries in future
research.

Our findings also support the previously identified tendency that the
more-specific, facet-level personality traits substantially contribute to
the understanding of gender differences. For each of the five domains,
the corresponding three facets showed differential patterns regarding
the gender differences. For Neuroticism and Agreeableness, the two
domains with the largest gender differences, these differences were due
especially to the Neuroticism facet Anxiety and the Agreeableness facet
Compassion, a finding that is in line with earlier studies (Costa et al.,
2001; Hofmann et al., 2025).
3.1. Cross-country variations in gender differences
Our study aimed to contribute to the question of whether countries
vary systematically in gender differences in personality and, if so,
whether such systematic cross-country variations emerge across all five
domains as indicated by several studies (Costa et al., 2001; Hofmann
et al., 2025; Schmitt et al., 2008) or only for Agreeableness, as suggested
by Lippa (2010) or only for Emotionality/Neuroticism as indicated by
the findings of Lee and Ashton (2020). Our findings show that gender
differences in personality indeed vary across countries. Across the 27
countries examined, multivariate gender differences in personality
observed in Germany, Norway, and Estonia were roughly twice as large
as those found in Singapore; and similar cross-country variation
emerged at the domain and facet level.
Further, our results support the idea that variations in the size of
gender differences are systematically related to the country's level of
individualism with more pronounced gender differences found in more
individualistic countries. Notably, our study further revealed that this
effect was not uniform across the five personality domains. While for

In sum, our study showed for the first time based on comprehensive,
comparable, and population-representative samples that: (a) as indicated in earlier research, on average women scored higher than men on
self-reported Neuroticism, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, and
Openness but not on Extraversion, with the most pronounced differences
for Agreeableness and Neuroticism; and (b) countries clearly differed in
the magnitude of the gender differences found. Further, our findings
showed, for the first time in this clarity, that the systematic association
of gender differences with cultural values varies across the personality
domains, not only in size but also in direction. For all domains but
Openness, gender differences tended to be larger in more individualistic
countries, thus supporting the general trend reported in earlier studies
(e.g. Costa et al., 2001). In contrast for the Openness gender differences
are less pronounced in more egalitarian countries domain thus supporting the socialization hypothesis (e.g., Eagly & Wood, 1999). From a
more general perspective our results indicate that cultural and socioeconomic factors affect gender differences not uniformly across personality domains. Future studies should therefore be reluctant to
analyze such associations on the aggregated level across all personality
domains only.
Further we could support (c) that the investigation of personality
facets clearly contributes to the understanding of the gender differences.
We found that mainly one facet per domain was most affected by gender
differences. Thus, our results indicate that, on average, women not only
describe themselves as more neurotic and agreeable than men, but also
particularly as more anxious and more compassionate. Finally, (d) these
more sensitive facets per domain tended to covary more strongly with
cultural differences in the subset of 11 countries for which facet-level
data were available.
