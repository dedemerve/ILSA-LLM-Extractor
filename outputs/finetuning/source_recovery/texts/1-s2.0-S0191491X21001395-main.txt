The effects of individual- and class-level achievement on attitudes towards
mathematics: An analysis of Hong Kong students using TIMSS 2019
Xin Chen *
Unit of Teacher Education and Learning Leadership, Faculty of Education, The University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Attitudes towards mathematics
Mathematics achievement
Big-fish-little-pond effect
Hierarchical linear model
TIMSS

Using the TIMSS 2019 data from fourth (N = 2947) and eighth graders (N = 3265) in Hong Kong, this study
investigated the link between individual- and class-level mathematics achievement and students’ attitudes towards mathematics (ATM; i.e., self-concept, perceived mathematical value, enjoyment and boredom). Student
achievement was positively connected to their ATM, whereas class-level achievement was negatively related.
Meanwhile, the connections between individual- and class-level achievement and ATM were more robust in the
eighth grade. Besides, cross-level interaction effects indicated that individual performance and gender played
moderating roles in the class-level achievement and self-concept/boredom. More specifically, the link between
class-level achievement and self-concept was stronger for underachievers in both the fourth and eighth grades,
but the connection between class-level achievement and boredom was stronger only for girls and underachievers
in the eighth grade.

1. Introduction
Affect is an increasingly important area in mathematics, especially
attitude towards mathematics (ATM; Dowker et al., 2019; Vandecandelaere et al., 2012), a multidimensional construct comprising
emotional experiences, competence beliefs and vision with respect to
mathematics (Di Martino, 2019; Di Martino & Zan, 2010; Dowker et al.,
2019). A large body of research has demonstrated the positive relationship between ATM and academic performance (e.g., Dowker et al.,
2019; Peixoto et al., 2017). Furthermore, longitudinal studies uncovered
the reciprocal promoting effects between student achievement and ATM
(Pekrun et al., 2019; Pinxten et al., 2014). Nevertheless, international
comparisons have consistently shown that East Asian students obtain
superior mathematics achievement while their ATM, on the other hand,
is lower than those of their peers, with higher math anxiety, less
enjoyment, and less confidence in math (Dowker et al., 2019; Mullis
et al., 2020). Surprisingly, if, as most studies have revealed, academic
achievement positively predicts attitudes, why does East Asian students’
affective performance fail to outperform their peers as does their math
achievement?
Although being in a higher-performing classroom may contribute to
students’ academic success, the big-fish-little-pond effect (BFLPE) suggests that students in higher-achieving settings may have a lower self-

concept than their counterparts in lower-achieving classrooms (Marsh
et al., 2007; 2019). Various studies have assessed the extensive adaptability of the BFLPE worldwide – not just in East Asian countries –
considering that student attitude is a multifaceted construct, whereas
whether such an effect can also apply to other student attitude constructs such as emotions and perceived value of mathematics is still
unknown. This study set out to survey the association among student
and class-level achievement and ATM to determine whether students
with the same competence in a higher-achieving environment have less
positive attitudes than those in lower-achieving classrooms in Hong
Kong. Hong Kong is a contemporary Chinese society where traditional
Chinese culture and values collide with Western school education ideas
(Sun, 2015). As a crossroads of cultures, choosing Hong Kong as the
major research environment might lead to some intriguing results.
Another vital purpose of the study is to examine whether the relationship between achievement environment and ATM varies based on
students’ performance or gender. Indeed, considerable gender differences in math-related affective factors still exist despite the gradually
declining gender differences in math achievement (Holm et al., 2017;
Parr et al., 2019). Evidence has shown that female students generally
reported fewer positive ATM (e.g., Frenzel et al., 2007). Even when girls
achieve academic success, they seldom report more positive learning
experiences (Stipek & Gralinski, 1991). Nonetheless, whether a

found. As some research has pointed out, differences in the correlation
between mathematics attitudes and academic results exist in different
cultural backgrounds (e.g., Dowker et al., 2019). In a comparative study
of first-grade students in China and England, Dowker et al. (2019) found
that, for the English sample, self-rating and enjoyment were significantly related to arithmetic scores; however, no correlation was found
between attitudes and mathematics achievement for the Chinese participants except for self-rating. Furthermore, limited research suggests
that the connection between affect-related variables and achievement
appears to alter with time (e.g., Liou, 2014). Specifically, a more vital
link between self-concept and mathematics has been observed in the
middle classrooms compared to the elementary classrooms despite the
well-documented literature showing that students’ attitudes towards
mathematics decline over the years (e.g., Metsämuuronen & Tuohilampi, 2014; Michael et al., 2010). Consequently, further research is
required on the relation between ATM and student achievement,
focusing on different classroom settings and age groups.

2. Literature review
2.1. Attitudes towards mathematics
The concept of attitude originates from social psychology and is used
to predict the behavior of individuals in a preference-based selection
environment. Neale (1969) first formally proposed the crucial role of
attitude in mathematics education and emphasized that positive attitudes might involve students more in math learning. As more study into
the connotation of ATM is conducted, most definitions of ATM
increasingly concentrate on its components. For instance, studies based
on the Mathematics Attitude Questionnaire (MAQ) defined mathematics
attitudes as mathematics anxiety, a liking for mathematics, unhappiness
with poor math performance and self-rating in mathematics (Dowker
et al., 2019; Thomas & Dowker, 2000). Concerning the variety of definitions of ATM, Di Martino and Zan (2010) believe that a broader
perspective on the attitude is enriching and significant for researchers
because different definitions serve different research questions. By
employing the grounded theory method, they finally obtained a
three-dimensional representation of students’ attitudes comprising
emotional experience, beliefs about mathematics and perceptions of
mathematical competence. Likewise, a study carried out by Vandecandelaere et al. (2012) has shown similar constructs of the attitude (i.
e., self-concept, mathematical value and enjoyment).
Taken together, the variety of meanings attributed to the attitude
construct suggests that it is not a unidimensional factor. ATM in the
present study was mainly measured by the TIMSS (2019) scales, which
reflected the views of the participating countries rather than the theoretical consensus. Therefore, this study defined ATM as an umbrella
term including academic self-concept and perceived value in mathematics learning as well as emotional experiences - namely, enjoyment
and boredom, both of which have been identified as critical learningrelated emotions (Pekrun, 2006). Self-concept mainly refers to the students’ self-judgment of their competence in mathematics learning;
enjoyment or boredom denotes a liking for or dislike of mathematics.
The perceived value of mathematics stands for the perceived importance
or usefulness of mathematics.

2.2.2. Group-level achievement and attitudes towards mathematics
Teachers, parents, and students are all concerned about academic
success, therefore most schools and classrooms have a strong culture of
assessment and comparison. Often, the evaluation of a student’s performance depends not only on his or her own abilities but also on the
performance of other students (see Pekrun et al., 2019). That is, individuals evaluate themselves not in terms of their absolute abilities but
in terms of their relative level in relation to their peers. Given the current
dependence of evaluation results on academic performance, achievement comparison among students is typical behavior. Such social comparisons may help students evaluate their own academic performance
and abilities and, as such, may profoundly affect students’ learning in
both positive and negative ways. For instance, numerous studies have
shown that the better the peer group in a school or class, the better the
individual’s academic achievement (Hanushek et al., 2003; Stäbler
et al., 2017). From the sociological perspective, however, peer groups
serve another function – "social contrast," which may lead to “discouraging effects” because peers not only learn from each other and imitate
each other, but they also compare themselves with each other (Kelley,
1952). This argument is supported by Marsh (1984), who proposed the
BFLPE assuming that students compare their own abilities to those of
their peers and utilize this social comparison impression to develop
self-concepts; more specifically, academic self-concept was positively
linked to individual achievement but negatively correlated with class

2.2. Achievement and ATM
2.2.1. Individual-level achievement and ATM
ATM is seen as vital for students’ mathematical learning and
problem-solving, therefore better understanding of its antecedents is
needed. Positive relations between students’ mathematics performance
and their cognitive appraisals (i.e., perceived value and competence)
have been widely demonstrated (Dowker et al., 2019; Van der Beek
et al., 2017). For instance, Bouchey and Harter (2005) surveyed middle
school students and found an unambiguous relationship between
self-concept and mathematics achievement. However, self-concept in

At the same time, such comparison may cause students to feel
dissatisfied with their academic achievements, and they may thus
experience more negative learning-related emotions. In addition to
focusing on the negative effect of a high-performing environment on
students’ academic self-concept, recent studies examining the influence
of group-level achievement on emotions in math classrooms have begun
to emerge; however, no consistent conclusions have been reached. Parr
et al. (2019), for example, examined the influence of class average
mathematics achievement on enjoyment and boredom, and the results
showed no significance. Similar conclusions were obtained in another
study examining the impact of group-level mathematics achievement on
academic emotions among a sample of Finnish eighth-graders, showing
that class-level achievement only had a significant negative effect on
students’ pride but no significant effect on enjoyment and boredom
(Holm et al., 2020). Conversely, some researchers have reported a significant influence of class-level mathematics achievement on enjoyment
involving 5th - 10th - grade students in Germany (Pekrun et al., 2019).
Thus, it is obvious that there is no consensus on the impact of group-level
achievement on students’ learning-related emotions in math classes, and
further study is needed.
Even though no relevant studies examine the effect of group-level
achievement on students’ perceptions of the mathematical value, findings have shown that such a relationship might exist. Academic value is
usually divided into intrinsic and extrinsic value. The former is closely
related to the mastery goal while the latter is more related to the performance goal (Pekrun, 2006). Students with mastery-oriented thinking
and learning generally show great course interest and employ deep
learning strategies. Personal interests usually dominate their study efforts, causing them to overlook less fascinating topics in favor of their
favorites, detrimental to test score development (see Senko & Miles,
2008). However, in a higher-achieving environment, solid academic
rivalry tends to devalue these students’ academic values. Students who
are influenced by ’bad money drives out good’ are more inclined to give
up interest to get excellent grades. On the other side, in the performance
goal-oriented, students with lower mathematics scores often attach less
importance to mathematics (Solomontos-Kountouri et al., 2013; Trautwein et al., 2006). Coupled with the fact that students tend to judge their
relative ability rather than their actual ability when in the context of a
highly competitive structure, it may exacerbate students’ lower assessment of the value of mathematics, especially in a higher-achieving
environment (Guo et al., 2018; Marsh et al., 2015). Moreover, existing
evidence suggested that the link between students’ beliefs about
competence and perceived value strengthened with age (Jacobs et al.,
2002). It is, thus, inferred that the BFLPE might extend to perceived

On the other side, existing research on the interaction effect between
individual and class-level achievement on academic value and emotions
is inadequate. In line with the control-value theory, positive emotions
are closely related to students’ academic success, whereas negative
emotions, such as boredom and anxiety, are generally associated with
academic failure (Pekrun, 2006). Thus, it is highly likely that
lower-performing students develop these emotions in environments
with higher achievement levels. Surprisingly, limited evidence has
revealed that BFLPEs on enjoyment and boredom are more closely
linked to high-performing students in higher-achieving classrooms
(Holm et al., 2020). There is obviously a need for additional study to
discover whether able students are more strongly linked to BFLPE.
Lastly, although few studies have specifically examined the moderation
effect of individual achievement between perceived value and
group-level achievement, this relationship might exist. Empirical evidence has demonstrated that perceived value is an important antecedent
of academic performance (Muis et al., 2015). When students realize the
meaning of mathematics, they are more likely to persevere in their
studies and choose more challenging mathematics activities to expand
their understanding (Wigfield, & Eccles, 1992), ultimately promoting
their academic achievement. Instead, underachievers seldom appreciate
the value of mathematics, especially in the higher-achieving environment where academic success is more difficult than in the average
classroom (e.g., Solomontos-Kountouri et al., 2013). Therefore, the
BFLPE may have a greater relationship with the academic value among
lower-performing students in higher achievement settings.
2.4. Gender as a moderator in the achievement environment and ATM
Although few studies have looked at the role of gender in the relationship between achievement environment and ATM, prior research
has shown that girls are more involved in social comparison and hence
more susceptible to the adverse effects of the achievement environment
(Guimond et al., 2007). On the contrary, some researchers suggest that
boys’ greater sense of competition may promote their more negative
emotions in higher-achieving contexts (Baudoin & Galand, 2017),
consequently being influenced more by the BFLPE. That argument is
supported by neuroscience, with the evidence showing that activation of
the female brain’s right hemisphere is linked to empathy levels; however, no such link has been discovered in the male brain (Rueckert &
Nyabar, 2008). On the other side, the assumption that male students are
better at math may cause males in higher-performing classrooms to have
unpleasant experiences because they think they are not living up to the
expectations (Holm et al., 2020).
Given the mixed arguments on the moderation role of gender in the
achievement environment and attitudes, scarce empirical evidence may
provide helpful reference values. Recent research looked at the interaction effect of class-level performance and gender on academic emotions, showing that the negative relationship between class average
math achievement and shame was more robust among boys, but no
gender moderating impact on enjoyment and boredom (Pekrun et al.,
2019). Similar conclusions have been obtained in the study of Holm
et al. (2020). In addition, evidence suggests that the predictive impact of
group-level achievement on math self-concept is also not
gender-dependent (Marsh et al., 2007). In terms of the perceived value
of mathematics, studies indicated that boys in mathematics courses reported less emotional cost and greater personal importance than girls,
but girls valued the utility of mathematics higher than boys (Gaspard
et al., 2014; Solomontos-Kountouri et al., 2013). To this sense, the association between math value and class-level achievement may tend to
be stronger for girls. To summarize, there is a scarcity of research on the
interaction effect of gender × class-level achievement on ATM, and the
great majority of studies are conducted in western settings, making it
difficult to make assumptions. Consequently, the lack of an interaction

2.3. Individual performance as a moderator in the achievement
environment and ATM
Evidence has shown that students with math difficulties and low
mathematics performance reported more math-related boredom and
anxiety but less enjoyment (Holm et al., 2017; Van der Beek et al.,
2017). Thus, examining whether individuals’ mathematics performance
moderates the achievement environment - attitudes relationship is
essential. Through literature review, most studies examined the influence of group-level achievement on students’ self-concept (i.e., BFLPE)
than that on other constructs of attitudes. For instance, evidence has
shown that the BFLPE had a less impact on high-achieving pupils’
self-concept than it did on low-achievers (Trautwein et al., 2009), in
contrast to Marsh et al. (2007), who showed that the BFLPE had a
greater association with self-concept in able students. Besides, Marsh
et al. (2019) found comparable results in a second investigation,

2.5. Current study
The current study aims to broaden prior knowledge by examining the
relations between individual and class-level achievement and ATM with
a focus on mathematics classrooms in Hong Kong. Besides, whether such
relationships vary across students of different genders and math ability
is also examined. As a core subject in the school curriculum worldwide,
mathematics is particularly emphasized with the popularity of STEM
education, and math-related skills and competency are seen as critical
for any possible career in today’s market (Tze et al., 2021). Previously
published studies verified larger BFLPEs on self-concept in mathematics
than that in reading and science (e.g., Guo et al., 2018). In combination
with the domain specificity of emotional dispositions (Goetz et al., 2006)
and competence beliefs (Bong, 2001), the present study aims to address
the posed issues with a focus on mathematics classrooms. The current
study, on the other hand, used data from both fourth and eighth grades
to assess the results’ consistency. Based on prior work, this study mainly
investigated the following three questions.

3.2.2. ATM
ATM in the current study consisted of enjoyment, boredom, value
and self-concept. Enjoyment was measured by seven items in the Students Like Learning Mathematics (SLM) scale (e.g., ‘I look forward to
mathematics class’). Compared to the study of Min et al. (2016), who
selected five items to assess enjoyment by using the SLM scale, the two
extra items in this study are newly added in the SLM scale (i.e., ‘I like any
schoolwork that involves numbers’ and ‘I like to solve mathematics problems’). Besides, two items were selected to assess students’ boredom (e.
g., ‘Mathematics is boring;’ Tze et al., 2021). Four items in the Students
Confident in Mathematics (SCM) scale were selected to examine students’ mathematics self-concept (e.g., ‘I learn things quickly in mathematics;’ Min et al., 2016). The Students Value Mathematics (SVM) scale
(nine items) was adopted to examine students’ perceived value of
mathematics in terms of students’ self-assessment of the importance and
usefulness of mathematics learning (e.g., ‘I think learning mathematics will
help me in my daily life’). It is worth mentioning that the fourth-grade
survey did not include any value-related assessments. All survey items
utilized a four-point Likert scale with a range from 1 (disagree a lot) to 4
(agree a lot), meaning that the higher the score, the more likely a student
had positive attitudes towards mathematics.
To examine the structural validity of the scales used in the study,
exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was first used. The Cronbach’s alpha
(α) coefficients for enjoyment, boredom, self-concept and value were
0.95, 0.79, 0.89, and 0.92 in the eighth grade, respectively, and .94, .76
and .86 in the fourth grade, suggesting good internal consistency of data.
Then, given that the items used in this study were chosen from several
scales, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used. The results of the
CFA of the scales involved in the study are presented in Table 1. The chisquare statistics and several fit indices were used to evaluate model
testing (e.g., CFI, TLI, SRMR, and RMSEA). TLI and CFI values ≤ .95,
RMSEA values ≤ .06, and SRMR ≤ .08 were considered good model fit
indicators (Hu & Bentler, 1999).

3. Method
3.1. Participants
This study selected fourth- and eighth-graders in Hong Kong math
classrooms from the TIMSS 2019 database for analysis. After deleting
null data, a total of 2947 fourth-grade students were incorporated into
the final analysis, with 1372 girls (46.60 %) and 1573 boys (53.40 %)
and two students who did not report their gender. Fourth-grade students
were from 140 classes within 139 schools who participated in TIMSS
2019. In addition, 3265 eighth-grade students participated in this survey, with 1484 females (45.50 %), 1773 males (54.30 %), and eight
students who did not report their gender. Eighth-grade students were
from 140 classes within 136 schools who participated in TIMSS 2019. In
the Hong Kong data set, almost all randomly selected schools had one
class participating in this investigation except for one school with more
classes involved in the survey. Considering the possible stronger frogpond effect from class-level than school level (see Pekrun et al., 2019),
the study employed average class achievement as a reference. The mean
number of participating fourth-grade students per classroom was 21.05
(minimum = 8, maximum = 30) while that for the eighth grade was
23.32 (minimum = 7, maximum = 33).

3.2.3. Covariates
Gender is worthy of mention due to the significant gender disparities
in students’ attitudes towards mathematics (Dowker et al., 2012; Vandecandelaere et al., 2012). Gender was controlled in the study and was
coded female = 0 and male = 1. Furthermore, family-related variables
appear to be another critical element impacting students’ attitudes,
generally operationalized using socioeconomic status (SES), including
home educational resources and parental education (Hu et al., 2018).
For instance, Chen et al. (2012) found a positive relationship between
home resources and students’ attitudes towards science. In line with
prior work, book ownership in the current study was used to assess individual student’s home resources (Hu et al., 2018). The example item is
“About how many books are there in your home? (Do not count magazines, newspapers, or your school books)."

PISA and NAEP all employ plausible values to estimate students’
potentially general ability. That is to say, plausible values represent the
range of possible abilities of the student (Wu, 2005). Marchant (2015)

3.3. Data analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using two-level hierarchical linear
modeling (HLM), which is beneficial to deal with the nested data
structure and cross-level moderation effects (Raudenbush & Bryk,
2002). All continuous variables before analysis were standardized;
meanwhile, predictors at the two levels were uncentered, given that
standardization is equivalent to being grand centered (Aiken & West,
1991). Before data analysis, handling missing data is an important issue
to consider. Observations with missing data are deleted using listwise
deletion in the HLM software when creating an MDM file. Missing data
estimation, in this sense, produces an unbiased estimation of the complete data (Raudenbush et al., 2016). Recent evidence has shown that
some widespread missing data handling procedures like multiple imputations might result in bias involving multilevel regression models
with random coefficients (Enders et al., 2020). More specifically, a null
model was first constructed to obtain the proportion of within- and
between-class variance and the intraclass correlations (ICC1) of students’ attitudes, which helps to understand how much of the variance
was caused at the classroom level and then to determine whether
multilevel modeling was acceptable. Second, individual-level variables
(i.e., mathematics achievement and covariates) and the classroom-level
variable (i.e., class average mathematics achievement) were then added
into the models (see the following equations). There was no reason to
presume varying slopes between classrooms, so more efficient and
parsimony constant slope models were created (Snijders & Bosker,
1999).
The HLM 7 software was used to analyze the relevant seven models
separately, and maximum likelihood estimation was employed to estimate the parameters. MAij represents the mathematics achievement of
the individual student i in the classroom j, and the same goes for the
outcome variable (Yij) as well as covariates (MALE and SES). CMAj
stands for class-level math achievement of school j. In addition, β0j, β1j
and β2j are regression coefficients at the student level, while γ00, γ01, γ10,
and γ20 are regression coefficients at the classroom level. rij is a studentlevel residual, and u0j, u1j, and u3j are the random effects related to the
intercept and slope of the student level.

4. Results
4.1. Results of preliminary analysis
Tables 2 and 3 present the preliminary analysis of the student-level
variables in the fourth and eighth grades, respectively. Correlation
analysis suggested that variables measured in the present study were
connected except SES. Notably, fourth-graders showed more enjoyment
and academic self-concept and value and reported less math-related
boredom than eighth-graders.
4.2. Between-class correlations and explained variance
First, according to the null model results, the intraclass correlation
coefficients (ICCs) for enjoyment, boredom, perceived value, and selfconcept of eighth-graders (fourth-graders) were .07 (.06), .04 (.03),
.06 and .06 (.02), respectively, indicating that a significant proportion of
students’ attitudes towards mathematics was due to between-classroom
differences. Although some ICC1 values were somewhat below .05
(LeBreton & Senter, 2008), the study decided to retain the model
because some studies have pointed out that the use of a Likert scale with
a wider range of options (i.e., 7 or 9 points) could significantly reduce
the negative impact on the estimation of ICC1 when conducting multilevel analysis (Beal & Dawson, 2007; Biemann et al., 2012). In other
words, the value of ICC1 is significantly underestimated when using the
five-point Likert scale, whereas the scale used in this study contained
only four options, which could be inferred as a possible underestimation
of the ICC1 value. Next, individual- and classroom-level achievement
and covariates were added into the model.

5. Discussion
The current study contributes to the existing knowledge by investigating the association between student- and class-level achievement and
ATM in Hong Kong math classroom settings. To sum up, in fourth and
eighth grades, the data showed a positive association between student
achievement and ATM, but a negative relationship between class-level
achievement and ATM; furthermore, in eighth grade, such connections
are increasingly relevant. Lastly, the relationship between class-level

grew more prominent with age, and as a result, competence beliefs
across various content areas became more distinct and influenced by the
academic environment. In this regard, one possible explanation could be
offered from the perspective of the internal/external frame of reference
model (I/E model; Marsh, 1986), stating that in addition to comparing
their achievements with others (e.g., the BFLPE focusing on external/social comparison), students will also compare their achievements in
different subjects so as to form their competence beliefs in a particular
subject. They found a significant negative correlation between academic
achievement and academic self-concept in different subject areas (e.g.,
math achievement and Chinese self-concept). As the grade level progresses, the topic content becomes more complex; as a result, some
students eventually suffer learning difficulties and are unable to balance
the study. Usually, girls have a higher level of achievement in language
or literature, which will impair their mathematical self-concept according to the I/E model and, in turn, lead to more boredom in line with
the control-value theory (Goetz et al., 2008; Pekrun, 2006).

5.1. Achievement and ATM
Corroborating with prior work, students with higher math achievement reported more positive ATM (e.g., Peixoto et al., 2017). Meanwhile, the connections between student achievement and ATM become
much more robust with time despite that students’ ATM declines,
implying that affective outcomes may play a powerful role in students’
academic success and school education, especially in the higher grade.
The most robust finding to emerge from the analysis is that the
BFLPE is not merely applicable to academic self-concept but also academic value and enjoyment and boredom. These findings supported
earlier research by Marsh et al. (2019) and Pekrun et al. (2019), who
investigated the “Big/Happy fish in little ponds” hypothesis and
discovered negative relationships between school/class-average
achievement and self-concept/math-related emotions. Meanwhile, the
BFLPE exists in both grades, which is consistent with the findings of
Cremeens et al. (2007), who discovered that after the age of seven or
eight, young children’s self-rating of ability is impacted by social comparisons. Furthermore, the data revealed a greater relationship between
class-level mathematics achievement and ATM in eighth-graders
compared to fourth-grade pupils. Combined with the preliminary results showing that pupils’ decline in ATM following primary school, one
possible explanation is that early elementary school children have a high
level of confidence in their abilities in mathematics, with less dependence on external indicators like achievement (Marsh et al., 2015).
Children grow increasingly capable of comprehending and integrating
experiences linked to abilities as their cognitive capacities mature, such
as identifying their skills and understanding their relative strengths and
shortcomings through grades and test scores (Guo et al., 2018). Festinger (1954) argued that the lack and ambiguity of critical evaluation
criteria might lead to individuals’ feeling uncertain about themselves.
Hence, they tend to refer to others’ achievements and abilities as
objective criteria to understand themselves better. Usually, they refer to
generalized others with a common attribute as comparative objects,
such as their classmates, in order to obtain a relatively objective
self-evaluation.

5.3. Individual performance moderates in group-level achievement and
self-concept/boredom
Individual performance was found to play a moderating effect in the
link between group-level achievement and self-concept in both fourth
and eighth grades, as well as boredom among eighth-grade pupils,
indicating that the BFLPE on self-concept and boredom is more stronger
among lower-performing students. In line with Trautwein et al. (2009),
lower-performing students’ mathematical self-concept is more closely
related to the BFLPE than higher-performing ones. In terms of academic
emotions, the findings contradict earlier research that showed that in
higher performance contexts, high-achieving students reported less
enjoyment but more boredom (e.g., Holm et al., 2020). They proposed
an explanation for this unexpected result from the perspective of
teachers’ teaching: because teachers believe that high-performing students understand math-related content well, they move quickly in
higher-performing classes, rarely giving further explanations or allowing students to explore the meaning of different approaches, resulting in
increased boredom and less fun for high-performing students. Whereas
in Chinese classrooms, math teachers will take into account pupils of
various abilities and present in a step-by-step manner. They usually offer
detailed explanations, present various solutions to test the validity of
their responses, and emphasize issues that students do not understand
(Gu et al., 2004). Therefore, social comparison might explain these
different findings, in combination with the fact that such stronger
relationship between BFLPE and boredom is only observed in the eighth
grade but not the fourth grade.
Comparison has become a common social phenomenon, one of the
social-psychological foundations of the current fierce educational
competition in Chinese education (e.g., Ho, 2009; Leung, 2001).
Accordingly, students are accustomed to comparing themselves to peers
who have achieved academic success. Also, parents expect their children
to keep up with or surpass their better-performing classmates and even
put pressure on their children. Thus, even if there is a high degree of
differentiation in academic performance among peer groups, everyone
may be inclined to compare themselves to the overachievers, at least to
the average of the group. Such comparison makes individuals more
concerned about their comparative advantages or disadvantages among
peers. The comparative pressure increases accordingly, making individuals more likely to doubt their abilities and have lower self-concept
and negative learning experiences. Furthermore, for the substantial link
between class-level achievement and boredom among eighth students
rather than fourth graders, one plausible explanation is that rather of
stressing more systematic and abstract mathematical knowledge in
eighth grade, the primary school curriculum is meant to promote children’s enthusiasm and interest in learning. Meanwhile, fourth graders
do not have to deal with high-stakes examination and/or entry pressure

5.2. Gender moderates in group-level achievement and boredom
Results corroborate the findings of a great deal of the previous work
in which considerable gender differences in mathematics-related affective factors still existed, with females having more negative ATM
(Frenzel et al., 2007; Holm et al., 2017; Parr et al., 2019). Furthermore,
data revealed that gender only moderated the relationship between
group-level achievement and boredom in the eighth grade, implying
that the BFLPE on boredom was stronger for girls than for boys.
Whereas, such moderation effects were not observed in other constructs
of the attitude and among fourth graders. Boredom is commonly
induced by a lack of confidence in one’s academic control and a high or
low sense of academic value (Pekrun et al., 2002). One plausible
explanation for the stronger class-level achievement-boredom relationship among girls might be their lower competency beliefs and domain
values and more immense expectations of success (Frenzel et al., 2007).
Alternatively, influenced by the stereotype that mathematics is a
male-dominated discipline (Spencer et al., 1999), girls are more likely to
devote a significant amount of time and effort to learning math and
maintaining high academic achievement. However, such a significant
time commitment is seldom proportional to academic achievement,
particularly in a higher-achieving classroom where individual possibilities to excel relative to others are limited, leading to fewer favorable
self-perceptions during social comparison (Pekrun et al., 2019).
Notably, the stronger relationship of BFLPE in math boredom for
girls is only observed in the eighth grade but not the fourth grade. Dicke

6. Limitations, conclusions and practical implications
Given the lack of attention on the influence of group-level achievement on students’ attitudes, this study is encouraging and significant to
the literature by exploring the relationship between students’ ATM and
class-level achievement in a non-Western context. However, limitations
remain in the current study. First, concerning the sample selection, this
study merely used data in Hong Kong, ignoring the survey of participants from different countries/areas. Thus, the conclusions of this study
may primarily constrained to Hong Kong samples. In future studies,
including samples from different countries and regions to conduct
comparison analysis could help resolve this issue. At the same time, it
might be possible to examine whether a high-achieving environment is
one plausible explanation of the comparatively negative attitudes towards mathematics of East Asian students. Second, the study did not
employ specialized scales to examine students’ self-concept and enjoyment/boredom. Instead, several items in the questionnaire of TIMSS
were chosen to operationalize students’ attitudes towards mathematics.
Inconsistency in using measurement scales, participants, and analysis
methods may result in different conclusions concerning the link between
attitudes towards mathematics and achievement. Future research may
refer to widely applied scales, which could help reduce the bias from
instruments and help researchers to make objective comparisons and
judgments. Third, limited by the items in the questionnaire, this study
only examined two emotions and was thus unable to determine if similar
conclusions could be found in more diverse emotions such as pride and
anger. For future research, discussing other academic emotions in
mathematics classrooms could help understand the whole picture concerning the effect of achievement on students’ emotional experience.
Finally, one source of weakness in this study was exploring the linear
relationship between achievement and attitude. Longitudinal research
should be considered in future studies to establish the viability of the
cause-and-effect relationship.
Overall, the present results successfully identify that learning with
excellent peers may result in a decrease in some learners’ positive
learning experience. Although students’ mathematics achievement
positively related to their ATM, their peer classmates’ achievement
plays a negative role in their ATM. Meanwhile, the relationship between
individual-level (class-level) achievement and math attitude becomes
stronger as the grade increases. Furthermore, the test indicated that
lower-performing students’ self-concept and boredom, as well as females’ boredom, are important elements to consider in higherperforming classrooms.
Findings also provide practical implications for schools and teachers
and parents. First, the shift in the link between academic success and
ATM from fourth to eighth grades requires special attention, particularly
at the class level. As a result, some of the current practices of overperformance orientation, such as pursuing enrollment rates or school
rankings and even dividing students according to their ability into
different classrooms, may need to be reconsidered. The findings of this
study indicate that a less stratified school system would be more
conducive to student development. Second, while continually pushing
students to attain excellent academic success, school instructors and
parents may need to be cautious. To put it another way, non-cognitive
outcomes of students, as well as their potential adverse effects on
other aspects of youth, are not insignificant and should be given the
same level of attention as cognitive results. Ample evidence has suggested the influential role of attitudes in affecting students’ knowledge
acquisition, career choices and even well-being (e.g., Marsh et al., 2018;
Pekrun et al., 2017). As a result, cultivating favorable attitudes about
math and math-related problems is even more essential. Third, the
findings suggested that teachers should prevent female students and
lower-achieving pupils from being bored in high-performing math
