ABSTRACT

ARTICLE HISTORY

Many educational studies have examined the relationships
between school science literacy achievement and various
school-level factors, including school resources, teacher
quality, and class size. The primary purpose of the current
study was to investigate the predictive ability of student-,
teacher-, and principal-reported variables for school science
literacy achievement using a machine learning approach.
Using data from 238 US schools collected by the 2015
Program for International Student Assessment, we
examined the relative importance of 48 predictors in
predicting school science literacy achievement. The
machine learning algorithm identified ten relatively
important predictors of school science literacy outcomes.
Of the ten predictors, seven were reported by the students.
We discussed the results concerning implications for school
leaders in setting program priorities.

It is well understood that science literacy has become a fundamental skill in an
increasingly complex world, and enhancing students’ science literacy is a core
focus in public education (Anderson et al., 2007). Success in science, technology,
engineering, and mathematics (STEM) is a national priority, and numerous
efforts and resources have been dedicated to enhancing student achievement
in STEM in the United States (Bybee, 2018; Young et al., 2018). Specifically,
schools are the place to build students’ content knowledge and science literacy
skills to meet the demands of today’s and tomorrow’s dynamic and evolving
challenges. In the United States, significant pressure and emphasis are placed
on continually improving science achievement for all students, and schools
are held accountable for implementing scientifically based interventions. The
research on school science literacy achievement has increased in the past
several years (e.g., Beese & Liang, 2010; Belland et al., 2017; Holzberger et al.,
2020).

Because school science literacy achievement is a function of various characteristics involving schools, the teaching process, and students’ personal and
family backgrounds, research in this area focuses on potential variables that
predict outcomes (Holzberger et al., 2020). Examining the important predictors
of school science literacy can suggest some of the reasons why schools are, or
are not, achieving at optimum levels. As with gauging the economy’s health
using indicators such as unemployment rates and stock market performance,
we can assess the relative importance of different predictors of school science
literacy achievement. The No Child Left Behind Act of 2002 and the Every
Student Succeeds Act of 2015 require states to conduct annual assessments
of learning outcomes and for schools to be evaluated based on those results.
The Common Core standards in mathematics and Next Generation Science
Standards are national standards for teaching mathematics and science in the
United States. Within this coherent framework, school programs aim to foster
greater STEM interest and understanding among students. Although assessment outcomes may not be the best indicators of a school’s achievement
level (Chingos & West, 2015), schools still use these outcomes to enhance
public perceptions of school quality (Dee, 1998; Heck, 2000). As a result, 48
states and the District of Columbia include or plan to include learning outcomes
in their school accountability system (Data Quality Campaign, 2019), although
significant differences exist concerning the relative emphasis placed on these
outcomes (Achieve, 2019).
According to the Next Generation Science Standards (2013), science is at the
heart of this country’s ability to continue innovating, leading, and creating the
jobs of the future. All students, whether they become technicians in a hospital,
workers in a high-tech manufacturing facility, or Ph.D. researchers, “must have a
solid K-12 science education” (p. xiii). Understanding what works and what has
failed to work for schools concerning science literacy outcomes is crucial in the
United States and many other countries. Numerous studies have examined the
association between various factors and student science learning outcomes
(e.g., Ainley & Ainley, 2011; Cairns & Areepattamannil, 2017; Holmlund et al.,
2010; Rubie-Davies et al., 2012; Soltani, 2020). Because students, teachers, and
school administration are all part of the school system, and school science literacy achievement is an essential measure of school quality in the United
States (U.S. Department of Education. National Center for Education Statistics,
2000), school leaders should understand whether all these various factors simultaneously predict science learning outcomes. From the pragmatic perspective
of school policy and practice, school administrators and teachers may be interested in what the data can reveal about school practices that may or may not be
related to school science literacy outcomes. Although such data do not account
for student differences in science learning outcomes, they can be used to
examine relationships between potential predictors and school science achievement status, thereby improving school practices and priorities.

Because there are many possible predictors, the primary purpose of this
study was to examine the differential predictive ability of student-, teacher-,
and principal-reported variables for school science literacy outcomes in US
schools, based on 2015 PISA data, using a machine learning approach. This
study builds on previous studies and contributes to the literature in three
ways. First, most previous studies using PISA data have focused on a few independent variables, typically employing the hypothesis-testing approach (e.g.,
You et al., 2021). In contrast, this study includes a large number of predictors
to identify the most important variables at the school level. Thus, our approach
is more exploratory. Second, this study focuses on school science literacy outcomes rather than student-level outcomes, as we aim to include student-,
teacher-, and principal-reported variables in our analysis. This focus can help
teachers and school leaders better understand what works for schools in
terms of science literacy outcomes. Third, methodologically, this study builds
on the machine learning framework, which will be discussed in more detail in
subsequent sections. The machine learning approach may help identify new
predictors that have not been previously considered, providing predictive information that can help expand theories by incorporating new insights.

Literature review
The use of data in educational decision-making has been a recurring theme in
educational reform agendas across many countries (Schildkamp & Lai, 2012). In
the United States, decision-making based on data has been linked to school
accountability systems, shaping how the data is used (Datnow et al., 2018).
Because school achievement outcomes are influenced by teachers and leadership, school climate, resources, and students’ social and emotional health, many
studies have examined predictors of student science learning outcomes, particularly individual variation in students’ science learning. Caro et al. (2016)
suggested that students with positive dispositions (e.g., high motivation, interest in science, and positive attitudes toward tasks) exhibit higher levels of
science literacy. Student science self-efficacy is a critical student disposition
that can be seen as a key factor in facilitating science learning (Dang &
Nylund-Gibson, 2017; Hyytinen et al., 2018). Tytler (2007) asserted that
science education should not ignore students’ own interests and feelings. A
positive disposition has been found to contribute to student enjoyment and satisfaction with science learning (Alsop & Watts, 2003; Ma, 2018; Renninger & Hidi,
2011). The research suggests that different emotional states can enhance or
disrupt information processing (Pekrun et al., 2002). Negative emotions, such
as anxiety, can lead to debilitating thinking, while positive emotions, like enjoyment, can facilitate science learning by supporting an exciting learning environment (Zeidner, 1998; Zembylas, 2004).

Recognizing the importance of student disposition in achieving science
learning outcomes, researchers have explored the factors that contribute to a
positive disposition toward science learning. Hascher (2010) suggested that
student learning involves teaching and learning practices that elicit a range
of emotional states, including frustration and happiness. Other studies have
found a positive association between teacher support, instructional strategies,
and inquiry-based, hands-on activities and student enjoyment, motivation,
and satisfaction (Bulunuz et al., 2012; Renninger & Bachrach, 2015). Indeed,
teacher classroom practices and teacher characteristics are commonly examined in the research on learning. Some researchers argue that inquiry-based
teaching is most effective when it involves higher levels of feedback or guidance, whereas others contend that teacher-directed instruction is more beneficial for science learning (Rosenshine, 2012; Zhang, 2016). Teacher
collaboration was found to be positively associated with math and reading
achievement at the school level (Goddard et al., 2007). Dorman and Fraser
(2009) examined ten dimensions of teacher classroom practice (e.g., teacher
support, involvement, task orientation, and computer use) and found that
enhancing teachers’ classroom practices can improve student outcomes.
Studies conducted over the past 30 years have provided clear evidence that
teacher classroom practice significantly predicts student learning (Dorman &
Fraser, 2009; Kerr et al., 2006).
Another line of research identifying predictors of science learning achievement has focused on teachers’ beliefs and perceptions of the school environment. These beliefs and perceptions shape psychological functioning in
schools and the instructional strategies that result, which in turn influence students’ learning experiences (Holzberger et al., 2014; Huppert & So, 2013; Woolfolk et al., 2009). It has been found that teacher perceptions regarding the
school are linked to teacher well-being, motivation, job satisfaction, and organizational commitment, each of which is associated with dedication and effective
teaching (e.g., Collie & Martin, 2015; Holzberger et al., 2014; McLean & McDonald
Connor, 2015). In addition, teacher contextual beliefs, such as perceived autonomy and support, are also considered aspects of the school environment that
impact teacher behavior (Collie et al., 2016). Learning outcomes have also
been found to be associated with teacher qualifications; the most effective teachers have high academic degrees, teach in the areas in which they were trained
(Fitchett & Heafner, 2018; Vesterinen & Aksela, 2013), and participate in professional development programs (Eren-Şişman et al., 2020). Research reviews
have provided compelling evidence of a positive association between the
school environment (including leadership) and student achievement, although
this association is not specific to science literacy.
While the preceding review identifies predictors at the student, teacher, and
school levels, it does not articulate how these levels relate to one another structurally. Bronfenbrenner’s (1979) ecological systems theory offers a valuable lens

for organizing these predictors within a coherent hierarchy. The theory posits
that human development is shaped by a set of nested environmental
systems, each embedded within the next: the microsystem (the immediate
setting in which the individual participates), the mesosystem (interactions
among microsystems), the exosystem (settings that indirectly affect the individual), and the macrosystem (broader cultural and policy contexts).
Applied to the school context, student-level variables such as science selfefficacy, enjoyment, and achievement motivation reside within the microsystem, the immediate learning environment as experienced by the student.
These dispositions are not formed in isolation; they develop through direct
interactions with teachers, peers, and classroom instructional activities.
Teacher-level variables, including instructional strategies, feedback practices,
perceptions of resource adequacy, and professional collaboration, can be situated at the boundary between the microsystem and the mesosystem. Teachers
shape the microsystem that students inhabit, and their practices are themselves
influenced by interactions across settings, for example, by how professional
development opportunities connect to classroom instruction or by how collegial relationships affect pedagogical decisions (Bronfenbrenner, 1979). Principal-reported variables, such as class size, teacher qualifications, school
climate, and resource allocation, operate at the exosystem level. Principals’
decisions about staffing, budgets, and organizational structures create conditions that constrain or enable teacher practices, which in turn shape the learning experiences available to students (Bronfenbrenner, 1994). Although
students rarely interact directly with these administrative decisions, the
effects cascade downward through teachers and classrooms.
This ecological framing implies a hierarchical structure among the three
levels of predictors examined in the present study. School-level (principalreported) factors set the structural conditions within which teachers work;
teacher-level factors translate those conditions into instructional practices and
classroom climates; and student-level factors capture how learners perceive,
internalize, and respond to those proximal experiences. Accordingly, the
influence of exosystem variables on science literacy may largely interact with
teacher practices and, ultimately, through students’ motivational and emotional
engagement. This perspective suggests that student-reported variables may
emerge as the most proximal predictors of achievement, not because school
and teacher factors are unimportant, but because their effects are channeled
through the student’s immediate experience. At the same time, the ecological
framework cautions against interpreting strong student-level predictions as evidence that school and teacher factors can be neglected; rather, these outerlayer variables may be necessary preconditions for the student dispositions
that most directly predict science literacy.
By situating the predictors within Bronfenbrenner’s nested systems, the
present study moves beyond simply cataloging variables at different levels.

Instead, it provides a theoretical rationale for expecting differential predictive
salience across levels, which the machine learning analysis can then empirically
examine.

Analytical framework
A landmark study by Dawes et al. (1989) demonstrated that the validity of statistical prediction equaled or exceeded that of clinical judgment across tasks
ranging from predicting college grades to predicting responses to therapy.
Similarly, Grove et al. (2000) found that statistical prediction was about 10%
more accurate than clinical judgment, and Ægisdóttir et al. (2006) found that
statistical prediction was consistently as accurate, if not more accurate, than
clinical judgment. Garb et al. (2018) found that statistical prediction can identify
individuals likely to have poor mental health or behavioral outcomes in military
settings. In addition, as in business and medicine, the application of artificial
intelligence in education has grown in recent years (Chen et al., 2020; Roll &
Wylie, 2016). These advances include intelligent tutoring (Hwang et al., 2020),
machine learning for classification (Musso & Cascallar, 2009), and machine learning for predictive modeling (Musso et al., 2020; Musso & Cascallar, 2009). Identifying important predictors of school science learning outcomes and achieving
predictive capability could provide information for positive interventions and
aid in determining the teaching practices that have the greatest positive
impact on student learning (Musso et al., 2013) and school-level achievement.
A key contribution of machine learning, a form of artificial intelligence, is its
ability to develop predictive models of school-level outcomes using various
machine learning algorithms. One such algorithm is the multivariate adaptive
regression spline (MARS) (J. H. Friedman, 1991). MARS allows the use of all
linear, nonlinear, and interaction terms of predictor variables, if needed, to estimate the outcome variable more accurately. MARS also allows the analysis of
many predictors when the ratio of cases to variables is small. In the present
study, MARS was chosen not because machine learning is inherently superior
to traditional approaches, but because it was particularly well-suited to the
exploratory, high-dimensional nature of our research question. With 48 predictors and only 238 school-level observations, the case-to-predictor ratio falls well
below the commonly recommended thresholds for stable estimation in standard multiple regression (e.g., Harrell, 2015). Moreover, if nonlinear effects
and pairwise interactions were to be examined, as our research question
required, the number of parameters would increase dramatically, far exceeding
what the sample size could reliably support in a conventional regression framework. MARS addresses these challenges through its built-in forward selection
and backward pruning with generalized cross-validation, which automatically
identifies relevant predictors, detects nonlinear thresholds, and evaluates

potential interactions while guarding against overfitting without requiring the
analyst to manually specify every functional form in advance.
Critically, the value of MARS in the present context extends beyond merely
confirming which predictors matter, a question that traditional regression and
prior literature have already addressed to a considerable degree. Rather, the distinctive contribution of MARS lies in its capacity to reveal precisely how predictors matter through its hinge-function architecture. Standard linear regression
assumes that a one-unit increase in, say, self-efficacy has the same effect on
science literacy regardless of whether the school is at the low or high end of
the self-efficacy distribution. MARS relaxes this assumption by estimating piecewise-linear segments separated by empirically determined cutpoints (knots),
thereby identifying specific regions of the predictor space where associations
with the outcome strengthen, weaken, or change direction. These threshold
effects are not merely statistical artifacts; they carry direct practical implications.
For example, if a hinge function reveals that science self-efficacy has a
modest association with achievement up to a particular level but an accelerating association beyond that point, school leaders gain actionable information
about where to concentrate intervention resources, such as pushing schoollevel self-efficacy past the identified threshold rather than distributing efforts
uniformly. Similarly, if teacher feedback shows a negative association with
achievement only beyond a specific intensity level, this suggests a qualitatively
different dynamic in schools with very frequent feedback, a nuance that a single
linear coefficient would obscure. Thus, the MARS approach is intended not to
confirm or replace theory-driven hypothesis testing but to complement it by
generating empirically grounded, nonlinear insights via specific threshold
values and piecewise slopes that can both refine existing theoretical predictions
and inform targeted, evidence-based interventions at the school level.
Nevertheless, a lack of conceptual and methodological understanding of
machine learning hinders its use among educational researchers, as substantive
theoretical frameworks (e.g., social cognitive learning theory) and analyses
grounded in them have typically been preferred and emphasized (Bonsaksen,
2016). However, Pogrow (2017) pointed out that “there is absolutely no empirical evidence that education practices and decisions based on academic theory
produce better results than those that are not” (p. 13). Tukey (1977) suggested
that “today, exploratory and confirmatory can – and should – proceed side by
side” (p. vii). The author also asserted that “unless exploratory data analysis
uncovers indications, usually quantitative ones, there is likely to be nothing
for confirmatory data analysis to consider” (p.3). The point here is to suggest
that machine learning offers a conceptual and methodological framework
that can be a more effective way to analyze one’s data. In a recent article, Alyahyan and Düstegor (2020) proposed a framework for applying data mining techniques for predicting student success. Following this framework, we used

Current study
This study was motivated by a practical need facing school leaders and educators. In the current accountability landscape, schools are expected to make evidence-based decisions about how to allocate limited resources, including time,
funding, and professional development, to improve student outcomes (Datnow
et al., 2018; Schildkamp & Lai, 2012). However, the sheer number of factors that
may influence science literacy outcomes presents a genuine challenge for practitioners. School administrators must simultaneously weigh decisions about
class sizes, teacher qualifications, instructional approaches, student support programs, and resource allocation, often without clear guidance on which factors
deserve the greatest attention. Traditional research, which typically examines
a small number of theoretically specified variables, provides valuable but piecemeal evidence that can be difficult to translate into coherent school-level
priorities.
A data-driven approach that simultaneously evaluates a broad set of candidate predictors and identifies those most strongly associated with school
science literacy outcomes can complement theory-driven work by offering practitioners an empirically grounded basis for prioritization. For example, if student
motivational dispositions emerge as more salient predictors than specific
instructional practices at the school level, this information could guide school
leaders to invest in programs that systematically cultivate student interest
and self-efficacy in science, rather than focusing exclusively on particular teaching techniques. In this sense, the present study aims to bridge the gap between
large-scale assessment data and actionable information for school improvement, providing school leaders with a clearer picture of where their efforts
may yield the greatest returns for science literacy outcomes.
Through a literature review, 48 predictors were identified as “candidate variables” because they may have predictive power as informed by theory or previous studies. These predictors included school science learning variables that
principals reported (e.g., science-specific resources), teaching practices and
aspects of job satisfaction that teachers reported (e.g., self-efficacy related to
teaching science content), and student-related variables that students reported
(e.g., enjoyment of science learning). Teacher and student variables were aggregated to the school level because both were nested within schools.
These four types of predictors could be conceptually grouped as follows
based on what they assessed: (a) student characteristics and student perceptions of teaching practices, (b) school features, (c) teacher characteristics and
teacher perceptions of school teaching practices, and (d) school characteristics
concerning science learning. In doing so, we aimed to identify the most

Alyahyan and Düstegor (2020), which has been applied to machine learning
studies (e.g., Rodríguez-Hernandez et al., 2021). This section describes stages
appropriate for our machine learning analysis and explains the design decisions
made for implementing the MARS algorithm.

Data source
The data used in this study were drawn from PISA 2015. PISA operates on a 3-year
cycle; achievement areas are assessed in each cycle, but one area is the focus of
that cycle’s assessment. In 2015, science performance was the focus of the assessment. According to PISA, science literacy is “the ability to engage in reasoned discourse about science and technology, which requires the competencies to:
explain phenomena scientifically, evaluate and design scientific enquiry, and
interpret data and evidence scientifically” (OECD, 2017, p. 22). In this study, we
used 2015 PISA data from 266 US schools. In addition to collecting assessment
data, questionnaire data were collected from students, teachers, and principals.

Initial preparation
We included 48 predictors. These predictors were derived from the student,
teacher, and principal questionnaires of the 2015 PISA. The predictors were
categorized in this study based on what they assessed. Specifically, predictors
derived from the principal school questionnaire included school resources,
school climate, school leadership, the quantity of teaching staff, school type,
school responsibility, and extracurricular activities at school. Predictors
derived from teacher questionnaires included educational resources, job satisfaction, school leadership, teaching and assessment practices, and teaching
and teacher collaboration. Predictors derived from student questionnaires
included student disposition toward collaborative work, interest in science,
science learning in school, student motivation, and science-related disposition.
Table 1 lists and describes all the predictors.

Data preprocessing
After deleting missing values in the original data set, the final sample included
238 schools. No outliers were found. In this study, we did not discretize the
outcome variable because we did not want to lose information by categorizing
schools in some arbitrary way (e.g., low or high).
In this study, we aimed to use variables reported by students, teachers, and
principals (i.e., school personnel) to examine school-level science literacy outcomes. Although the PISA 2015 data had a hierarchical structure with students
and teachers nested within schools, students were not nested within teachers;
therefore, we could not use multilevel analysis in this study. To utilize all variables
reported by students, teachers, and principals, we aggregated student- and
teacher-reported variables to the school level by averaging them across students
and teachers within each school, enabling us to use all three informant sources to
predict schools’ science literacy outcomes. Although this aggregation means that
all predictors operate at the school level, the variables nevertheless retain their

Model implementation
Multivariate adaptive regression spline (MARS) (Jerome H. Friedman, 1991), the
machine learning algorithm used in this study, is a nonparametric regression
method that makes no assumptions about the relationships between the
dependent variable and the predictors, and it can automatically take nonlinearity and interactions among predictors into consideration if necessary, particularly for data with high dimensionality. When nonlinear relationships exist in
the data, MARS offers a convenient approach to capturing them by assessing
knots (cutpoints), similar to piecewise functions. The procedure assesses each
predictor knot point and fits a linear model for the segmentation of that data
point. The result is a hinge function, h(x – c), where c is the cutpoint value or
a knot. A MARS model can be written as

Once the first knot is found, the procedure continues to search for a second knot,
then a third, and so on. Once the complete set of knots (forward pass) has been
identified, knots that do not contribute significantly to predictive accuracy are
sequentially removed (backward pass). This process, known as “pruning,” continues until the optimal number of knots is determined by minimizing generalized
cross-validation (GCV) error. GCV produces an approximate leave-one-out crossvalidation error metric (Golub et al., 1979). MARS works well with many predictors,
automatically detecting nonlinearity and interactions between predictors.
In order to identify the best model with the optimal combination of degree
(i.e., main effect or interaction) and the number of terms (i.e., hinge base function
and nonlinearity) retained in the final model, MARS assesses all potential hinge
functions across all predictors and then prunes to the optimal number of knots
based on the change in R 2 of less than 0.001. In addition, to select the optimal
number of knots, the MARS algorithm also assesses potential interaction

effects between different hinge functions. Since we do not know the best model
with the optimal combination of degrees and terms, we need to explore a range
of possibilities using machine learning and select the optimal model. This process
of searching for the best model (i.e., a grid search) is called model tuning, and the
parameters that govern it are known as hyperparameters.
The MARS algorithm has two hyperparameters: the degree for interactions
and the number of terms to retain. This study performed a grid search for up
to third-degree interactions and 100 terms, with ten evenly spaced values,
resulting in 30 (3 × 10) combinations of degree and the number of terms to
retain in the final model. Then, the grid search was validated using 10-fold
cross-validation to identify the best model with the optimal combination of
these two hyperparameters that minimizes prediction error.

Model evaluation
Model evaluation was based on predictive accuracy, using cross-validation to
assess generalization performance and to measure error on independent
data. Indices for predictive accuracy in MARS include the residual sum of
squares (RSS), R 2, GCV, generalized R 2 (GRSq), root-mean-square error (RMSE),
and mean absolute error (MAE), as reported by the R caret package (Kuhn,
2011). The GCV and GRSq are measures of the model’s generalization ability,
indicating how well it predicts outcomes on a different dataset. If the GRSq is
satisfactory, we can have reasonable confidence in the model as a whole.
After the final model was determined, we evaluated the importance of the
predictor. According to Milborrow (2021), predictor importance is defined as
the strength of the relationship between observed predictor values and the
observed response. To operationalize this definition, MARS estimates predictor
importance based on two criteria: (1) the number of times a predictor is involved
in a term and (2) the extent to which it leads to a reduction in RSS and GCV. GCV
and RSS are normalized so that the largest net reduction is 100. That is, the relative importance of each predictor is normalized: 100 corresponds to the most
important predictor, and the other predictors have importance values measured
relative to it. Predictor importance can be visualized with graphs.
The analyses in the current study were conducted using the caret package in
R (Kuhn, 2011).

Overall, the student-reported motivational variables showed strong and largely
monotonic relations with school science literacy. Self-efficacy emerged as the
most powerful predictor: science literacy increased steadily as average schoollevel self-efficacy rose, with an especially steep increase once school-level
self-efficacy exceeded roughly 1.4 SD. Achievement motivation also displayed
a consistently positive association with science literacy. Schools in which students reported higher achievement motivation tended to have higher school
science literacy, with a nearly linear pattern across the full range of the motivation distribution. Enjoyment of science was positively related to achievement
once enjoyment rose above an extremely low level (about 1.2 SD below the
mean), indicating that, except for the very least engaged schools, higher enjoyment was consistently associated with better performance. By contrast, instrumental motivation showed the opposite pattern: up to about 1.3 SD above the
mean, schools in which students more strongly endorsed studying science for
its usefulness tended to have lower science literacy, and beyond that point,

further increases in instrumental motivation were not associated with additional

The student-reported classroom practice variables showed negative associations with science literacy. For teacher help, science literacy was essentially
flat at very low levels of perceived help, but once students perceived at least
a moderate amount of teacher help, higher levels of help were associated
with lower achievement. A similar pattern held for inquiry-based teaching
and teacher feedback. Across most of their range, schools where students
reported more frequent inquiry-based activities or more teacher feedback
tended to have lower science literacy, with somewhat stronger negative
relationships at the lower end of these practices. These patterns are consistent
with the interpretation that such practices and supports in schools are provided
more intensively in lower-achieving settings, rather than implying that they are
inherently detrimental.
At the school level, teacher-reported material shortage showed a clear detrimental association with performance. When shortages were at or below a
modest level, material resources were not strongly related to achievement,
but once shortages exceeded roughly 0.4 SD above the mean, greater shortages
were associated with noticeably lower school science literacy. Principal reports
yielded two additional nonlinear effects. For class size, only very small classes
were associated with achievement: schools with classes substantially smaller
than average tended to have lower science literacy, whereas within the
typical and larger range of class sizes, there was little systematic association
with performance. Finally, the proportion of science teachers with ISCED 5A
qualifications showed a threshold pattern. At very low proportions, there was
little or even a slightly negative association with science literacy; however,
once a modest proportion of highly certified teachers was reached, schools
with more science teachers with ISCED 5A qualifications tended to have
higher science literacy.
Together, these hinge-function patterns indicate that the key student,
teacher, and school predictors generally have monotonic but piecewise-linear
relations with school science literacy, with important changes in the strength
of association at specific regions of the predictor distributions.

Discussion
The primary aim of this study was to identify the relative importance of predictors of school science literacy outcomes in the United States using a machine
learning approach with a large number of predictors. We used the term’ importance’ with respect to the predictors included in the final model, rather than the
absolute importance of the predictors. The MARS predictive model effectively
predicts school science literacy outcomes, with ten predictors accounting for
72% of the variance. The findings of this study highlight three key points to

20

C. DING

consider when predicting school science literacy outcomes. First, although prior
work has linked science achievement to student-reported learning environments (Soltani, 2020), teacher characteristics (Rubie-Davies et al., 2012), and
school-level indicators such as pupil–teacher ratios (Dearden et al., 2002),
seven of the ten most important predictors in our analyses were studentreported variables.
Second, among the school- and teacher-level variables, only teacherreported educational material shortage was retained in the final model. In contrast, principal-reported material shortage was not, despite a moderate correlation between the two indicators (r = .46). This pattern suggests that school
science literacy is more closely linked to teachers’ perceptions of resource adequacy than to principals’ reports, perhaps because teachers experience material
constraints more directly in their day-to-day instruction. Notably, no other
teacher-reported variables, such as teaching and learning interactions, emphasis on science, assessment practices, or satisfaction with the work environment,
emerged as important predictors in the final model. The present findings
suggest that, relative to student-reported variables, teacher-reported factors
tend to play a more secondary role in explaining between-school differences
in science literacy.
Third, only two principal-reported variables were retained in the final model:
class size and the proportion of science teachers with ISCED 5A qualifications
(i.e., a bachelor’s degree with a major in science). Interestingly, smaller-thanaverage class sizes were negatively associated with school science literacy
achievement. This aligns with prior work showing mixed effects of class-size
reduction on achievement (e.g., Jepsen & Rivkin, 2009) and suggests that
smaller classes may not be inherently beneficial, particularly if they are more
common in lower-achieving or remedial settings or are staffed by less well-prepared teachers. Consistent with this interpretation, schools in which principals
reported that fewer than approximately two-thirds of science teachers held
an ISCED 5A qualification tended to have lower science literacy than schools
where at least 67% of science teachers were so qualified. These findings indicate
that the benefits of smaller classes for science achievement may depend critically on having a sufficient number of well-qualified science teachers. In contexts where class sizes are reduced without a corresponding increase in
teacher qualifications, smaller class sizes may not necessarily translate into
higher levels of school science literacy.
Given these findings, the ecological systems framework adopted in this study
provides a theoretical account of this pattern. Bronfenbrenner’s (1979) theory
predicts that the most proximal influences on individual outcomes will be
those operating within the microsystem, the immediate setting in which the
learner participates. Student-reported variables such as self-efficacy, achievement motivation, and enjoyment of science capture precisely these proximal
experiences and dispositions. By contrast, teacher-reported variables (e.g.,

EDUCATIONAL RESEARCH AND EVALUATION

21

material shortage) and principal-reported variables (e.g., class size, teacher qualifications) operate at more distal layers of the ecological system, functioning as
exosystem conditions that shape but do not directly constitute the student’s
learning experience. The finding that only three of the ten retained predictors
came from teacher and principal reports is consistent with the ecological expectation that outer-layer variables exert their influence on achievement primarily
through their effects on the proximal conditions students encounter. For
instance, having a sufficient proportion of well-qualified science teachers (an
exosystem condition reported by principals) likely matters for science literacy
because such teachers create more effective microsystem experiences, richer
instruction, stronger feedback, and more supportive classroom climates,
which in turn foster the student dispositions identified as important in our
model. Similarly, teacher-reported material shortage may have been retained
precisely because resource constraints are experienced directly in classroom
instruction, placing this variable closer to the microsystem boundary than
other school-level indicators.
Thus, rather than suggesting that school and teacher factors are unimportant, the ecological interpretation implies that their effects are largely channeled through student-level dispositions and perceptions, which emerged as
the dominant predictors. This understanding has practical significance for
school leaders: interventions targeting structural conditions (e.g., improving
resource adequacy, ensuring teacher qualifications) are unlikely to enhance
science literacy unless they translate into improved proximal experiences that
strengthen students’ motivation, self-efficacy, and engagement with science.
Beyond identifying which predictors are important, the MARS hinge-function
results offer a level of specificity that conventional linear models cannot
provide, and it is in these nonlinear threshold patterns that the present study
makes its most distinctive empirical contribution. Consider science selfefficacy, the most powerful predictor retained in the model. Rather than
showing a uniform positive slope, the hinge functions reveal that the association with science literacy is moderate (−0.33 per standard deviation) across
most of the distribution but accelerates sharply (1.26 per standard deviation)
once school-level self-efficacy exceeds approximately 1.4 standard deviations
above the mean. This threshold effect suggests that schools hovering just
below this critical region may realize disproportionate gains in science literacy
from even modest increases in collective student self-efficacy, a finding that a
single regression coefficient would mask entirely.
Similarly, the hinge functions for teacher feedback and inquiry-based teaching reveal that the negative associations with achievement are concentrated at
moderate-to-high levels of these practices, while at very low levels the relationship flattens. This piecewise pattern lends nuance to the otherwise counterintuitive finding that more feedback and more inquiry are associated with lower
performance (e.g., Jerrim et al., 2019): it is not that these practices are uniformly

22

C. DING

harmful, but that their intensive use tends to co-occur with lower-achieving
school contexts. The nonlinear specification makes the region-specific nature
of this association visible. In the face of material shortages, the hinge function
indicates that modest shortages are not associated with achievement.
However, once shortages exceed roughly 0.4 standard deviation above the
mean, each additional unit of shortage is associated with a steep decline
(−0.65 per standard deviation) in science literacy, offering school leaders a concrete threshold beyond which resource deficiencies become critical. These
specific cutpoints and piecewise slopes translate directly into actionable guidance. Rather than pursuing broad, uniform improvement strategies, school
leaders can target interventions at the regions of the predictor distributions
where changes in school conditions are most strongly linked to differences in
science literacy outcomes. In this way, the MARS approach complements
prior confirmatory research by moving from the general conclusion that motivation and self-efficacy matter to the specific, empirically derived thresholds at
which they matter most.

Limitations
This study demonstrates the utility of machine learning in analyzing highdimensional educational data within the US context. At the same time,
several limitations should be noted. First, student dispositions toward science
learning emerged as the most important predictors in the final model.
However, we did not explicitly model potential indirect or moderating pathways
through which other variables (e.g., instructional practices, teacher collaboration) might influence achievement via student motivation and emotions.
Although the MARS procedure did not retain any interaction terms in the
final model, the absence of detected interactions in this framework does not
preclude more complex mediated or moderated relationships that could be
examined with alternative analytic approaches.
Second, with 238 schools and 48 initial predictors, the case-to-predictor ratio
is modest, which raises legitimate concerns about model stability and potential
overfitting. Several features of the analysis help mitigate these concerns. The
MARS algorithm incorporates regularization through its backward pruning
step, which uses generalized cross-validation (GCV) to penalize model complexity and remove terms that do not meaningfully improve predictive accuracy.
This built-in mechanism reduced the model from a potentially large number
of candidate terms to just 16 retained terms (including the intercept) based
on 10 predictors, with no interaction effects, a parsimonious solution relative
to the initial predictor space. Furthermore, the hyperparameter grid search
was validated using 10-fold cross-validation, and the resulting generalized R²
(GRSq) of 0.72 provides an estimate of out-of-sample predictive performance
that accounts for model complexity.

23

That said, two of the ten folds (Folds 3 and 4) yielded comparatively large
RMSE values, which inflated the mean cross-validated error. This variability
suggests that a subset of schools or a particular sample partition may have
exerted disproportionate influence on fold-specific performance. To assess
whether these anomalous folds compromised overall model selection, we
examined cross-validation results with and without them. We found that the
same hyperparameter combination (degree = 1, nprune = 23) was selected in
both cases, indicating that the model structure is robust to fold-level instability.
Nevertheless, we acknowledge that future replication is important, particularly
with larger school-level samples or with PISA data from subsequent assessment
cycles, which could provide an independent test of whether the same set of predictors and threshold patterns emerge.
Third, our analysis was limited to 48 variables. While this choice helped maintain interpretability, it also means that the resulting model may not capture the
full range of factors relevant to school science literacy. One implication is that
the machine learning results should be interpreted as contingent on the
initial variable selection; additional or alternative indicators (e.g., more detailed
measures of instructional quality, resources, or policy context) might alter the
pattern of important predictors.
Finally, with respect to methodology, this study relied primarily on a single
class of algorithms, given its focus. There remains a clear need to compare
and integrate multiple machine learning approaches for modeling the
complex, multilevel determinants of school science achievement, while maintaining a grounding in educational theory. Although machine learning can
provide substantial gains in predictive accuracy, its greatest value in education
will come from coupling flexible algorithms with theoretically informed model
specification and interpretation.

Conclusion
The machine learning approach employed here provides an exploratory framework for identifying the predictors most strongly associated with school science
literacy outcomes. This information can help school leaders and teachers target
efforts to improve the learning context in ways that are most likely to enhance
science achievement. At the same time, the predictive model should be treated
as a foundation for subsequent, theory-driven work, in which the identified predictors and relationships are further tested and refined within explicit theoretical frameworks.
