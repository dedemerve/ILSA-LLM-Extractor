Abstract This study delves into the multifaceted predictors of mathematics performance among Singaporean students, as assessed by PISA 2022, by analyzing both student-level and school-level factors
to elucidate the foundations of Singapore’s educational excellence. Utilizing a multi-level analytical
approach on a dataset of 6482 students from 157 schools, the research reveals critical insights into the
dynamics of educational achievement. The intercept-only model, indicating an intraclass correlation
coefficient (ICC) of 0.3049, suggests that approximately 30.49% of the variance in mathematics scores
is attributable to inter-school differences. Subsequent analysis through a comprehensive model identifies
pivotal student-level predictors, including the disciplinary climate in mathematics classes, exposure to
mathematics reasoning and twenty-first-century tasks, mathematics self-efficacy across both formal/
applied and reasoning/twenty-first-century skills, and a deep familiarity with mathematical concepts.
Moreover, at the school level, the analysis highlights the significance of the total number of full-time
teachers and nuances in teacher support as essential to educational outcomes, with teacher participation also emerging as a factor of some significance. The elucidated model accounts for 64.25% of the
between-school variance and 26.04% of the within-school variance, underscoring the substantial impact
of the investigated predictors on mathematics performance variance among Singaporean schools. The
findings of this study not only contribute to the academic discourse on mathematics education but also
offer actionable insights for policymakers and educators aiming to replicate Singapore’s success or
enhance mathematics education globally.

Introduction
Singapore’s exemplary mathematics performance in PISA assessments is attributed to high-quality teaching, a rigorous curriculum, and a focus on problem-solving and knowledge application (OECD, 2017a,
2023a, b; Wang et al., 2023). Research highlights the importance of teacher qualifications, instructional
practices, and a supportive learning environment in student achievement (Heng & Song, 2020; Leung,
2006). The Opportunity to Learn (OTL) theory (Carroll, 1963) and Self-Efficacy Theory (Bandura,
1997) are key to understanding the success of Singapore’s education system.
OTL theory suggests that students’ learning outcomes are shaped by their exposure to essential content and instructional practices. In Singapore, this is reflected in a structured and rigorous curriculum
supported by the National Curriculum Framework, which balances conceptual understanding, skill
proficiency, and problem-solving (Ministry of Education Singapore, 2021; Skemp, 1976). Studies like
those by Hiebert and Grouws (2007) emphasize that effective teaching practices, focused on problemsolving and critical thinking, are crucial to maximizing learning opportunities and contributing to
Singapore’s mathematics success.
Self-Efficacy Theory (Bandura, 1997) offers a psychological perspective on students’ beliefs in their
ability to succeed. Singapore’s education system promotes mastery learning and a growth mindset
(Boaler, 2016), with formative assessments and feedback designed to boost students’ confidence in
mathematics. Research by Pajares and Miller (1994) links high self-efficacy to better achievement,
a connection supported by OECD (2013), which found strong correlations between self-beliefs and
PISA performance. Further, the relationship between formative feedback and mathematical reasoning,
as explored by Smit et al. (2022), underscores the role of feedback in enhancing self-efficacy and, consequently, mathematical achievement. These theoretical frameworks underline the need to identify and
prioritize predictors that influence student achievement in mathematics.
This study explicitly examines which predictors are most influential in shaping mathematics performance in Singapore, providing a focused summary of the predictors under investigation. These include

A multi-level analysis approach is crucial for accurately modeling the complex relationships between

analysis (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). Ensuring the validity and reliability of the analysis will involve
rigorous statistical testing and validation against established benchmarks.
Transferring findings from Singapore to other educational systems requires careful consideration of its
unique socio-cultural and educational context. This study will explore the universality and specificity of
identified predictors, offering insights into how differences in governance, cultural values, and societal
expectations may influence their applicability across settings (Muijs et al., 2004; Stewart, 2012). Singapore’s education system, distinguished by strong government support, a well-structured curriculum, and
a culture valuing academic excellence, provides an ideal case study. Understanding how these systemic
factors interact with student-level variables, like self-efficacy and classroom experiences (Peters, 2013),
can offer valuable insights for educators and policymakers seeking to replicate Singapore’s success or
enhance mathematics education elsewhere.
This study’s findings are expected to contribute significantly to mathematics education, educational
administration, and educational psychology. The research will offer actionable strategies for educators
and policymakers, emphasizing tailored interventions addressing both student and school-level factors.
Insights from Singapore’s system could serve as a model for other nations aiming to improve their
educational outcomes in mathematics.

Research Question
This study seeks to answer the following central research question: What are the student-level and
school-level predictors that most significantly influence mathematics performance among Singaporean
students in PISA 2022? By addressing this question, the research aims to provide a nuanced understanding of the factors contributing to Singapore’s educational success, offering insights that can inform policy
and practice both within and beyond Singapore.

Literature Review

theoretical foundation in a diverse set of frameworks that align with the variables under investigation.
The selection of these specific variables and theoretical constructs is informed by their relevance to
the context of Singapore’s education system and their empirical validation in prior research. While
a broad range of variables is included in the PISA dataset, the focus on these particular predictors—
disciplinary climate, cognitive activation, self-efficacy, teacher support, school autonomy, and parental involvement—is driven by their demonstrated impact on student outcomes in high-performing
educational systems, particularly in the context of mathematics education.
The variables chosen for this study are not arbitrary; they are derived from a thorough review of
the literature and an understanding of the Singaporean education context. This study’s theoretical
foundation rests primarily on Opportunity to Learn (OTL) theory (Carroll, 1963) and Self-Efficacy
Theory (Bandura, 1997; Schunk, 1991), both of which offer key insights into the mechanisms influencing student learning outcomes. OTL theory posits that learning outcomes are directly influenced
by students’ exposure to content and instructional practices. This theory underpins the inclusion
of variables like exposure to formal and applied mathematics tasks. This alignment is particularly
relevant in Singapore, where structured curriculum design ensures consistent and equitable exposure
to essential content across schools (Ministry of Education Singapore, 2021).
Cognitive activation strategies, such as fostering reasoning and encouraging mathematical thinking, are crucial for deepening students’ mathematical understanding. These strategies engage students
in higher-order thinking, promoting a more profound comprehension of mathematical concepts
(Stein & Lane, 1996). The relevance of cognitive activation is further validated by contemporary
studies that link these strategies to sustained student interest and proficiency in mathematics in highachieving systems like Singapore (Kaur, 2014; OECD, 2023a, b). These variables are included in the
analysis because they represent key instructional practices that are actively employed in Singapore’s
classrooms to enhance student engagement and achievement.
Mathematics self-efficacy, encompassing both formal/applied mathematics and reasoning/twentyfirst-century skills, significantly contributes to performance by influencing students’ beliefs in their
ability to succeed in mathematics tasks (Bandura, 1997). The critical role of self-efficacy is supported
by both classical and contemporary research, including Usher and Pajares (2009), which emphasize
its importance in competitive academic environments such as Singapore’s. The decision to focus on
self-efficacy is driven by its strong correlation with academic achievement, as documented in numerous
studies across different educational contexts. Bandura’s framework further underscores how mastery
experiences, verbal encouragement, and structured feedback contribute to self-efficacy development,
factors that align closely with instructional practices in Singapore (Bandura, 1997; Boaler, 2016).
School-level predictors such as funding, the total number of full-time teachers, and school autonomy
are also included based on their demonstrated impact on student performance. Research has consistently
shown that the allocation of resources, including government funding and the presence of a sufficient
number of qualified teachers, directly impacts the quality and accessibility of educational opportunities
(Levin, 2012; Darling-Hammond, 2000). School autonomy, coupled with effective instructional leadership, allows schools to tailor their strategies to meet the specific needs of their students, a practice
that has been shown to be particularly effective in Singapore (Chua et al., 2019; Robinson et al., 2008).
The inclusion of these variables is also guided by broader theoretical frameworks. Constructivism, as
proposed by Piaget (1976), emphasizes the role of active engagement and the construction of knowledge
through experience. This theory supports the inclusion of cognitive activation strategies and exposure
to diverse mathematical tasks, aligning with Singapore’s emphasis on deep conceptual understanding
and student-centred learning (Tan et al., 2022). Similarly, Bourdieu and Passeron’s (1990) sociology
of education offers insights into how social structures and power relations within educational settings
influence learning outcomes. This perspective justifies the inclusion of school-level predictors such as
funding and autonomy, which reflect the social and institutional context of education.

Finally, Epstein’s (2011) theory of overlapping spheres of influence justifies the inclusion of parental

Despite the advancements in this field, gaps remain in the literature, particularly concerning the
nuanced effects of certain predictors and the interplay between student-level and school-level factors in

such as student attitudes towards learning or school infrastructure. These were not included due to
their less direct relevance to the theoretical frameworks guiding this study or due to limitations in data
availability. However, their exclusion does not diminish the study’s contribution to understanding the
predictors of mathematics performance in Singapore.
Existing studies may not fully account for the unique educational landscape and cultural factors
influencing mathematics performance in Singapore. This research aims to fill these gaps by employing a
multi-level analysis approach to explore the predictors of mathematics performance among Singaporean
students participating in PISA 2022. This approach enables a comprehensive analysis that considers the
hierarchical nature of educational data, allowing for a nuanced exploration of how different layers of
predictors—ranging from individual attributes to school-level determinants—collectively shape educational success (Ngware et al., 2015; Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002; Snijders & Bosker, 2012).

Research Method
The research method section of this study provides a comprehensive approach to analyzing the multilevel predictors of mathematics performance among Singaporean students, as assessed by the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022. This section details the procedural steps
undertaken to manage, analyze, and interpret the data through multi-level analysis, utilizing the R
programming language and relevant statistical packages.
Sample Description and Data Structure
The PISA 2022 assessment involved a complex sampling design aimed at representing 15-year-old
students across different countries, including Singapore. The target population consisted of all students
aged between 15 years and 3 months and 16 years and 2 months at the time of assessment, who were
enrolled in an educational institution and had completed at least six years of formal schooling.
In Singapore, a two-stage stratified sampling design was used. The first stage involved the random
selection of schools, with the probability proportional to the size of the school in terms of the number
of eligible students. In the second stage, a random sample of 15-year-old students was selected within
each sampled school. This sampling approach ensures that the sample is representative of the population
of 15-year-olds in Singapore, covering various school types, including government, government-aided,
independent, and specialized schools.

The final dataset for this study comprised 6482 students from 157 schools in Singapore. These
students participated in the PISA 2022 assessment, which included a mathematics test as the primary
domain, along with questionnaires collecting detailed contextual information. The data structure is
hierarchical, with students nested within schools, allowing for the application of multi-level modeling
techniques to explore both student-level and school-level predictors of mathematics performance.
Data Preparation and Preliminary Analysis
The study commenced with the extraction of data from the PISA 2022 dataset, focusing specifically
on Singaporean students and their school principals. The data were carefully processed to ensure quality and completeness. Missing data were addressed using list-wise deletion, ensuring the integrity of
the dataset for subsequent analyses. This preprocessing step was crucial to maintain the validity of the
statistical inferences.
To account for the complex sampling design of PISA, sampling weights provided in the dataset
were applied during analysis. These weights adjust for the probability of selection and ensure that the
results are representative of the broader student population. Additionally, plausible values were used
to handle the uncertainty in the measurement of student performance, as recommended by the PISA
2022 Technical Report (OECD, 2017, 2024). Each plausible value represents a possible score that the
student might have obtained if they had taken the entire test, allowing for more accurate estimates of
population parameters.
Details on Abbreviations and Index Construction
The abbreviations used in this study, such as DISCLIM (Disciplinary Climate), TEACHSUP (Math
Teacher Support), and MATHEFF (Math Self-Efficacy), correspond to derived variables (DVs) constructed through PISA’s robust scaling and validation procedures (OECD, 2023a). Derived variables
are indices created using either simple transformations, item response theory (IRT) scaling, or complex
composite calculations to measure latent constructs that cannot be observed directly (OECD, 2023b). For
example, DISCLIM is calculated using responses to items that assess students’ perceptions of classroom
disruptions and their impact on learning. These items are scaled using the generalized partial credit
model (GPCM) to ensure comparability across populations and groups (OECD, 2023b). Summarizing
the construction process, each item contributing to DISCLIM is weighted according to its discrimination
parameter, and the final index reflects a latent score standardized to have a mean of 0 and a standard
deviation of 1 among OECD countries.
Nuances of Teacher Participation
Teacher participation (TCHPART), which evaluates the extent to which teachers are involved in decisionmaking processes within schools, is derived from responses in the School Questionnaire. This index
is based on scaled responses to items related to collaborative planning, curriculum development, and
instructional leadership (OECD, 2023b). Examples include teachers’ contributions to strategic planning
or their involvement in pedagogical committees. Such activities are critical in fostering a supportive and
responsive educational environment, as they align teaching strategies with student needs (OECD, 2023a).
Multi‑level Analytical Framework
To capture the nuanced relationship between student-level and school-level predictors on mathematics
performance, a multi-level analysis was employed. This approach is particularly suited to the hierarchical

structure of PISA data, where students are nested within schools. The lme4 package in R was utilized
to estimate both fixed and random effects within this hierarchical data structure.
The analysis was conducted in two main stages. The first stage involved an intercept-only model
to calculate the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which indicates the proportion of variance in
mathematics scores attributable to differences between schools. The ICC for this study was found to
be 0.3049, suggesting that approximately 30.49% of the variance in mathematics performance is due
to inter-school differences. This initial model serves as a baseline, helping to understand the extent to
which school-level factors influence student outcomes.
In the second stage, a full model was developed, incorporating both student-level and school-level
predictors. At the student level, significant predictors identified included the disciplinary climate, exposure to mathematical reasoning and 21st-century tasks, self-efficacy in mathematics, and familiarity
with mathematical concepts. At the school level, the total number of full-time teachers and nuances in
teacher participation were found to be significant predictors of mathematics performance.
In the full model, the “Mean Estimate” refers to the average estimated coefficient for a given predictor
across all plausible values, capturing the overall effect of that predictor on mathematics performance.
The standard error (SE) represents the variability of this estimate, accounting for the uncertainty in the
measurement process due to the use of plausible values. To ensure robustness, the model was estimated
separately for each of the ten plausible values of mathematics performance, and the results were aggregated using Rubin’s (1987) rules for combining estimates from multiple imputations (Huang, 2024).
This approach provides a more reliable estimate of the effect sizes and their associated standard errors,
allowing for more accurate inferences to be drawn from the data.
Statistical Methodology
The statistical methodology employed in this study leverages the advanced capabilities of the lme4
package in R (Bates et al., 2015), which is known for its robustness in multi-level modeling. This study
also utilized plausible values, a feature of large-scale assessments like PISA, to handle the uncertainty
in measuring student performance. For each of the ten plausible values of mathematics performance,
separate intercept-only and full models were computed. The results were then aggregated using Rubin’s
(1987) rules for combining estimates from multiple imputations, ensuring the integrity and credibility
of the statistical analysis.

Given the complexity and depth of the analysis presented in the study on the predictors of math performance among 15-year-old Singaporean students participating in PISA 2022, this discussion delves
into the implications of the findings, grounding our discussion in the broader literature of mathematics
education, educational administration, multi-level analysis, and educational psychology. This exploration is aimed at unpacking the dynamics of student-level and school-level predictors in shaping math
performance, leveraging empirical studies and theoretical frameworks to elucidate these relationships.
The significant yet negative coefficients observed for Cognitive Activation: Reasoning (COGACRCO),
Cognitive Activation: Math Thinking (COGACMCO), and Math Self-Efficacy: Reasoning/21st Century
Skills (MATHEF21) warrant careful interpretation. While counterintuitive, these negative coefficients suggest that as students engage more with reasoning and math thinking tasks, or as their self-efficacy in twentyfirst-century skills increases, their overall performance may decrease. This could be due to several factors.
First, the negative coefficients for Cognitive Activation: Reasoning and Cognitive Activation: Math
Thinking suggest that these strategies, while theoretically beneficial, may have unintended consequences

318

Can. J. Sci. Math. Techn. Educ. (2025) 25:308–323

if not implemented with careful consideration of student readiness. Cognitive Activation involves engaging students in higher-order thinking and complex problem-solving tasks, which research has shown can
enhance deep understanding and critical thinking skills (Hattie, 2009; Stein & Lane, 1996). However,
these benefits are contingent upon students’ ability to manage cognitive demands. When students are not
adequately prepared or lack the necessary foundational skills, these tasks can lead to cognitive overload,
which impairs performance (Kalyuga, 2007; Sweller, 1988).
In the context of Singapore, where academic expectations are extremely high, the pressure to perform
well in such challenging tasks may exacerbate stress and anxiety among students (Hembree, 1990).
Studies have highlighted that in high-stakes environments, students are more prone to experiencing
negative emotional responses, which can undermine their ability to perform optimally in cognitively
demanding tasks (Pekrun et al., 2009; Putwain & Symes, 2011). This suggests that, while Cognitive
Activation strategies have the potential to foster deeper learning, they need to be carefully calibrated to
align with students’ current cognitive capacities. Providing scaffolding, such as breaking down complex
problems into more manageable steps or offering additional support resources, could mitigate the risks
of cognitive overload and help students engage more effectively with these tasks (Kirschner et al., 2006).
Similarly, the negative coefficient for Math Self-Efficacy: Reasoning/21st Century Skills suggests that
students with higher confidence in these areas may take on tasks that exceed their current capabilities,
leading to lower performance. Self-efficacy is generally regarded as a positive predictor of academic
achievement, as it reflects students’ beliefs in their ability to succeed in specific tasks (Bandura, 1997).
However, overly high self-efficacy can sometimes result in overconfidence, where students underestimate
the difficulty of tasks or overestimate their own abilities (Vancouver et al., 2001). This misalignment
between perceived and actual competence can lead students to engage in tasks that are beyond their
preparedness level, thereby exposing gaps in their understanding and resulting in poorer performance
(Schunk & Pajares, 2004).
In the Singaporean educational context, where students are often encouraged to excel in a competitive academic environment, fostering balanced self-efficacy is crucial. Interventions should aim to cultivate realistic self-assessment, encouraging students to accurately gauge their strengths and areas for
improvement. This could involve integrating formative assessments that provide feedback on students’
current performance levels and readiness for more advanced tasks, thereby helping students to make
more informed decisions about the challenges they undertake (Black & Wiliam, 1998; Zimmerman,
2002). Such approaches would help prevent the potential downsides of inflated self-efficacy and support
students in achieving their full academic potential.
The Role of Positive Predictors: The positive and significant coefficients for Disciplinary Climate
in Math (DISCLIM) and Familiarity with Math Concepts (FAMCON) emphasize the crucial role of
a supportive learning environment and a robust understanding of foundational concepts in fostering
high math performance. A positive disciplinary climate contributes to a reduction in classroom disruptions and creates an atmosphere conducive to learning, enabling students to focus more effectively on
mathematical content. This is consistent with the extensive literature on classroom management, which
highlights the importance of a structured, respectful, and orderly classroom environment in promoting
academic success (Hattie, 2009; Wang & Degol, 2016). Moreover, a positive disciplinary climate has
been linked to increased student engagement and motivation, which are critical factors in academic
achievement (OECD, 2013; Marzano et al., 2003). In the context of Singapore’s rigorous educational
system, maintaining a disciplined and supportive classroom environment may be particularly effective
in helping students manage the high academic expectations they face.
Familiarity with Math Concepts (FAMCON) also plays a pivotal role in student achievement.
When students have a deep understanding of fundamental mathematical concepts, they are better
equipped to apply these concepts to solve complex problems, which is essential for success in standardized assessments like PISA. Research indicates that a strong foundation in basic mathematical

Can. J. Sci. Math. Techn. Educ. (2025) 25:308–323

319

concepts not only improves problem-solving skills but also enhances students’ ability to transfer
knowledge to new contexts, thereby improving overall performance (Hiebert & Grouws, 2007;
Rittle-Johnson & Schneider, 2015). This finding suggests that educational interventions focusing
on reinforcing foundational mathematical concepts could be highly effective in boosting student
performance, particularly in environments where students are expected to excel in high-stakes
assessments.
Exploring Non-significant Predictors: The non-significant predictors, such as Math Teacher Support (TEACHSUP) and School Funding sources (SC016Q01TA, SC016Q02TA, SC016Q03TA), offer
valuable insights into the complexities of educational outcomes. The lack of significance for Math
Teacher Support (TEACHSUP) may reflect inconsistencies in how support is perceived or delivered
among different classrooms and schools. It is possible that the quality of teacher support varies widely,
leading to mixed effects on student performance (Hattie, 2009). Alternatively, this finding might suggest that, in highly structured educational environments like Singapore, where students have access to
various learning resources and are often highly motivated, peer collaboration and self-directed learning could be more influential factors than teacher support (Zepke & Leach, 2010). This highlights the
need for future research to explore the relative importance of different types of support in enhancing
student outcomes.
Similarly, the non-significance of school funding sources (SC016Q01TA, SC016Q02TA,
SC016Q03TA) could indicate that it is not the amount of funding that matters most, but how those funds
are allocated and utilized. Prior research suggests that targeted investments, such as professional development for teachers, innovative teaching materials, and infrastructure improvements, are more likely
to have a direct impact on student achievement than general funding increases (Brendefur et al., 2022;
Darling-Hammond, 2010; Levin, 2012). This finding underscores the importance of strategic resource
allocation in educational policy and suggests that policymakers should prioritize funding initiatives that
directly enhance the quality of teaching and learning in the classroom.
The analysis underscores the critical roles of both student-level and school-level predictors in explaining variances in math scores. The significant reduction in variances upon introducing these predictors
highlights the importance of a supportive learning environment, high-quality instruction, and resources
tailored to students’ needs (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). For policymakers and educators seeking to
replicate Singapore’s success in mathematics education, these findings suggest that efforts should focus
on maintaining disciplined and supportive classrooms, reinforcing foundational mathematical concepts,
and strategically utilizing resources to support high-quality teaching practices (Schleicher, 2019).
Unexamined predictors might include parental involvement, students’ out-of-school learning experiences, and technological integration in teaching, which could account for the remaining variance in
math scores (Epstein, 2011; Cheung & Slavin, 2013). Future research should investigate these factors
to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of math performance. Additionally, longitudinal studies could examine how these predictors influence math performance over time,
providing insights into the long-term effects of different educational strategies.

Limitations of Study
This study acknowledges several limitations. First, while PISA measures mathematical literacy, this
may not fully align with the procedural emphasis seen in some curriculum areas in Singapore. This
potential misalignment could influence the interpretation of results. Second, the study’s reliance
on self-reported variables, such as teacher support and self-efficacy, may introduce response bias.
Finally, while the hierarchical model captures much of the variance in scores, unmeasured factors,
such as socio-cultural influences or unobserved school policies, may still play a role.

Implications
Policymakers should prioritize continuous professional development for mathematics teachers, focusing on enhancing content knowledge, pedagogical strategies, and integrating twenty-first-century
skills into the curriculum as these were shown to significantly impact student outcomes in the results.
Effective resource allocation frameworks are essential, targeting areas like technology integration,
materials that deepen mathematical understanding, and infrastructure supporting collaborative learning. The results highlighted that adequate teacher staffing (e.g., full-time teacher totals) was a significant predictor of mathematical outcomes, underscoring the need for targeted resource allocation
frameworks to address staffing and professional development needs. Encouraging school autonomy
in curriculum design, pedagogy, and resource management—balanced with accountability—can
foster innovation tailored to student needs. The nuanced significance of teacher participation in
decision-making processes, as reflected in the results, suggests that empowering educators through
collaborative decision-making platforms can enhance instructional practices and student outcomes.
Platforms for sharing best practices are recommended to build a collaborative professional community, supporting this participatory approach.
Teachers should create an engaging learning environment by fostering a positive disciplinary climate,
integrating real-world problems, enhancing students’ self-efficacy, and deepening familiarity with mathematical concepts. The results demonstrated that a positive disciplinary climate and high levels of selfefficacy were significant predictors of student success in mathematics. A supportive atmosphere, clear
expectations, consistent feedback, group work, and problem-based learning are key to promoting respect
and curiosity in mathematics. Real-world problems and interdisciplinary projects make mathematics
relevant, inspiring students and demonstrating its practical value. For example, the significant role of
exposure to reasoning and twenty-first-century tasks) suggests that real-world problems and interdisciplinary projects can further enhance students’ engagement and achievement in mathematics. Building
self-efficacy through achievable challenges and constructive feedback fosters resilience and confidence.
Differentiated instruction, using varied strategies like visual aids and technology, deepens students’
understanding, ensuring they can apply mathematical concepts in diverse contexts. This aligns with findings that familiarity with mathematical concepts was a critical predictor of mathematical performance.
This holistic approach strengthens mathematical skills and equips students for the twenty-first century.
