Despite the renewed emphasis in equity for SDG4, Indigenous learning gaps persist. Indigenous barriers for
learning are intersectional -a combination of multi-layered and heterogeneous causes. In this paper, we use data
from PISA for Development to estimate the Indigenous learning gap in Guatemala, Paraguay and Senegal for out
and in school samples. We employ machine learning which allows to employ numerous controls and their interactions, accounting for intersectionality. We find that negative learning gaps remain for both samples (with
some differences by level by of performance) even after controlling for around 66–217 covariates, showing the
extent of Indigenous-driven inequality and discrimination.

1. Introduction
Assessing the extent of existing learning disadvantages for Indigenous1 children and youth in comparison to non-Indigenous populations
is vital. This concern has formally being taken-up since the adoption of
the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (in 2007) (as
regards to Indigenous groups agency and ideas of development) and,
then, by the follow-up SDGs development agenda, which has placed the
concept of Indigeneity as a core dimension to address (Yap and Watene,
2019) because of being a powerful source of inequality in education
systems from the global south. In particular, SDG4 (Quality Education)
and its leading monitoring target (equity: target 4.5), highlights the
degree of vulnerability of Indigenous groups warranting a special focus;
namely: “By 2030, eliminate gender disparities in education and ensure
equal access to all levels of education and vocational training for the
vulnerable, including persons with disabilities, Indigenous peoples and
children in vulnerable situations.”2
Yet, in spite of the renewed emphasis of Indigeneity in the development agenda, striking Indigenous inequalities persist. In the case of
Latin America, for instance, studies find Indigenous gaps of around 15
points in math and language test scores for most countries in the region

(UNESCO-LLECE, 2010) and, worryingly, research has shown for the
same region that learning gaps between Indigenous and non-Indigenous
groups hold even after controlling for family socioeconomic levels (see:
Treviño et al., 2016; Murillo and Román, 2008).
The root of learning inequality for Indigenous groups is multidimensional (Valenzuela et al., 2017). Indigenous students barriers are a
combination of multi-layered and heterogeneous causes within an
intersectionality process. Some of the leading barriers are the language
spoken at home which is different to the medium of instruction (Khan,
2014) and the larger rate of poverty among Indigenous groups (and so
the higher proportion of uneducated parents and weakened
cross-generational transfer of schooling for this group). This larger
poverty rate has a geographical location on rural and peripheric zones
lacking resources and infrastructure (Gayoso de Ervin, 2021; Gruijters
and Behrman, 2020; Valenzuela et al., 2017), as well as having an
under-developed labour market of low-paid jobs in rural areas (Tarallo,
2019), with these areas being mostly populated by poor Indigenous
students. Other layers of Indigenous educational inequality are gender
(for instance, early marriage and teen pregnancy prevalence rates being
larger for Indigenous girls) and school resources which highly vary by
urban and rural locations, with rural schools having low infrastructure

Historically and contextually combined, each of these barriers creates multiple combinations of disadvantages that reinforce and exacerbate each other (Kabeer and Santos, 2017), pushing back the learning
levels that Indigenous groups can potentially achieve through social
exclusion and a discriminatory process (Van Dijk, 2009). The leading
concept linked to Indigenous groups educational disadvantage is
“intersectionality”, that is, the combination and accumulation of layers
of influence behind educational gaps which spread through, both within
and between levels (Codiroli Mcmaster and Cook, 2019).
Here, we offer new evidence on the Indigenous learning gap, both for
youth and students who had either left school or are attending school,
for three countries from the global south (i.e., Guatemala, Paraguay and
Senegal). These countries have resources-constrained education systems
with multiple marginalised∕poor students (OECD, 2018a). Guatemala
has the lowest rate of enrolment and attainment in Latin America, and
the educational performance of Indigenous groups (40 % speaks an
Indigenous language) is low, with 48 % being illiterate (Richardson
et al., 2019; Rubio, 2006). In Paraguay, around 40 % of the Indigenous
population over 15 years of age is illiterate and, on average, this population has been studying only for 3 years (Elías et al., 2013), whereas
for non-Indigenous groups the illiteracy rate is just 5 % and their average
years of schooling is around 8 years. In Senegal, almost 90 % of the
population does not master the language of instruction: French (Martín-Chazeaud and Celaya, 2020).
In this paper, we rely on data from the 2017 PISA for Development
(PISA-D) learning survey, which has been designed to be more relevant
to poorer education systems, to measure the Indigenous∕non-Indigenous
learning gaps. We employ two sets of data to estimate the Indigeneity
learning gap: the out of school sample (out of school youth aged 14–16
not attending school regularly who are the far-off disadvantaged youth
within education systems) and the in school sample (students attending
school at grade 7; 15 years-old).
Furthermore, we extend the empirical literature by formally
addressing “intersectionality” and compounding shortcomings (which
operate at different levels trailing the Indigeneity learning gap) through
machine learning techniques. Machine learning (ML) approaches depart
from standard econometric techniques by deriving the structure of the
data without necessarily knowing -ex ante- the effect of X on Y. ML allows power of covariates, numerous interactions among key covariates
yielding a very significant number of final chosen covariates (those with
higher predictive power), a high dimensional number of covariates
which is not possible to include in standard micro-econometrics models
(Hastie et al., 2009; Smith et al., 2020).
All in all, ML approaches permit a better identification of the treatment effect (here, measured by the Indigeneity status) by including more
covariates and cross-level interactions in the model and so the outcome
being more likely to be independent of the treatment. In fact, a key
concern about causality claims on the learning gaps of Indigenous students is to isolate the effects of observed (and unobserved) differences
behind them (McEwan and Trowbridge, 2007). ML is able to account for
the complexity behind the Indigeneity learning gap because of its capacity to handle a large number of explanatory variables and their interactions (Storm et al., 2020) and so, through the wide range of selected
covariates and combinations (in our case the set reaching 252 for the out
of school sample and 380 for in school sample covariates to chosen
from), lessening the chances of the omitted variable bias driving the
achievement and Indigeneity association. Conceptually, too, by accounting for numerous interactions across levels (students, family,
school), we are capable to amalgamate the diverse theoretical domains
of intersectionality underscoring the Indigeneity learning gap.
The paper is structured as follows. Section 2 provides a review on key
elements of the research (i.e., intersectionality, Indigeneity conceptualisation, as well as an overview of educational barriers facing
Indigenous youth for the three countries). In Section 3 we present the

data and its descriptive statistics. Section 4 describes the ML method and
how it is applied to estimate the Indigenous learning gap, including
additional robustness checks. Section 5 includes the estimations and we
conclude in Section 6.
2. Background
2.1. Intersectional inequalities and machine learning
Intersectionality “refers to the idea that social categories, principally
those that involve inequality or power, such as gender, race or ethnicity,
and social background, are almost always permeated by one another”
(Codiroli Mcmaster and Cook, 2019, p. 273). Intersectionality is a
multi-layered and heterogeneous combination of disadvantages that
vary in terms of temporality and location.
Even though socioeconomic status (SES) has been traditionally
addressed as one significant detriment standing in the way of quality
education (Garcia Palomer and Paredes, 2010), other characteristics
have a played role such as gender, ethnicity, family background, structural forms of discrimination and inefficiencies, and school characteristics. That is why the educational arena calls for a multidimensional and
intersectional understanding of inequalities (Justino et al., 2004; Kabeer
and Santos, 2017; Kabeer, 2010).
Intersectionality as theory and methodology has been broadly
attributed to qualitative research, and oftentimes this perspective has
been deemed incompatible with quantitative research (Scott, 2010;
Codiroli Mcmaster and Cook, 2019). However, ML techniques are highly
compatible with intersectionality because of their high versatility,
extensive use of data, and unexplored potential for development studies
(De-Arteaga et al., 2018). The use of ML techniques has increased in
both size and attention within educational research. Yet, the lack of
massive datasets and their incompleteness made the application of ML
techniques in the developing world challenging (Codiroli Mcmaster and
Cook, 2019). De-Arteaga et al. (2018) address this issue by pointing out
the emergence of a brand new and independent field of ML techniques:
“Machine Learning for the Development World” (ML4D), which could be
beneficial to tackle both educational inequalities and context-specific
dynamics. Beyond data limitations, ML4D might be “understood as an
interdisciplinary domain in the intersection of ML and development
research” (Codiroli Mcmaster and Cook, 2019, p. 3). That said, a
growing body of research has shown how ML techniques highly benefits
research in development settings (Mgala and Mbogho, 2015). For recent
outputs of ML4D, see, for instance: Afonja et al. (2021) and papers
therein.3
Here, we rely on the possibilities opened up by the combination of
ML4D and intersectionality. Thus, our paper fits within this growing
body of literature which aims at finding contextual and culturally relevant solutions to tackle learning inequality due to segregation and
discrimination, which in our case is based on Indigeneity through ML
techniques.

2020), and it is estimated that only six out of ten young people will be
completing secondary education by 2030, the SDG deadline (UIS, 2019).
Yet, educational quality is instrumental in increasing school retention
and preventing the phenomenon of out-of-school youth (Adelman and

primary language of instruction. Most secondary students have difficulties expressing themselves and conversing in French and almost 90 %

Given these three linguistic horizons, the theory of “diglossia” allows
to understand language predominance in a society and the internal hierarchy between languages in a specific context. Let L1 be the language
spoken at home (the mother tongue) and L2 the mainstream colonial
language (Spanish or French). What makes L2 hierarchically superior to
L1 is its spread use among the state and communication apparatuses, i.
e., the “official” domain (Baker, 2006). Yet, a consistent body of literature (e.g., Corbetta et al., 2020; Corbetta, 2021; Hynsjö and Damon,
2015; Marshall and Sorto, 2012) reports the benefits of L1 as a medium
of instruction (MoI): it successfully sustains cognitive development,
boosts self-esteem, maintains a positive attachment to one’s cultural
identity, and boosts achievements in the long run (Cummins, 2001;
Khan, 2014; May, 2003; Martín-Chazeaud and Celaya, 2020). Furthermore, when the knowledge and proficiency in L1 are well established,
the transfer of such skills improve the learning outcomes in L2 (Alidou
and Brock-Utne, 2011; Cummins, 2001; Skutnabb-Kangas and Dunbar,
2010). That is why vague bilingual policies systematically apply submersion systems (which are aimed at either eliminating L1 with immediate exposure to L2 as MoI or offer two or three years of L1 and then
switch to L2) are deemed to fail. This system is applied in the three
countries considered in this study.

2.3. Indigeneity - language
We address the concept of Indigeneity with a seemingly simple
approach: the language spoken at home versus the language of instruction. There are several approaches5 to define Indigeneity; and the
categorisation might be cultural, linguistic, ethnical, locational, selfidentification, activism, and cultural re-appropriation (Bailey, 2009;
DeLugan, 2012; Gellman and Bellino, 2019; Telles, 2004). However, the
linguistic dilemma has been pointed out as a major barrier to development, especially in the educational domain (Khan, 2014), thus the
approach we follow.
The issue of language opens up an interesting yet challenging
dilemma regarding policy implementation for the three countries under
study. Guatemala is officially a multicultural and plurilingual country,
with approximately 40 % of the total population considered Indigenous
(Del Popolo et al., 2009), whose diversity and Indigenous language
rights are stated in its Constitution and educational policy. Yet, according to Veliz Catalan (2020), many governments from the 19th
century promoted policies aimed at the “assimilation” of the Indigenous
groups and the abandonment of their culture. In Guatemala, the differences in educational results are not only found when comparing
Indigenous and non-Indigenous, but also a great dispersion in the results
can be seen according to ethnicity (Murillo and Román, 2008).
Paraguay, on the other hand, is a bilingual country representing an
unique case within the Latin America (López, 2009) because the majority of the population (around 80 %) speaks Paraguayan Guarani
(Jopara), and its usage is common in informal and semi-formal environments (Ito, 2012); though, conversely, only 2 % identify themselves
as Indigenous (Del Popolo et al., 2009). Monolingual Guarani speakers
and other Indigenous language speakers are among the most disadvantaged in Latin America (López, 2009; Gayoso de Ervin, 2021). Illiteracy
rates among Indigenous people reach 39 %, with only 21 % of Indigenous children completing primary school (López, 2009). Indigenous
education in Paraguay has consisted of Western-style schooling in order
to incorporate Indigenous communities into national society (Gaska,

peripheric areas are the most disadvantaged because of a lack of resources and infrastructure, populated mainly by Indigenous and poor
students (Valenzuela et al., 2017). The scarcity of resources is mirrored
in the lack of school buildings and adequate transportation (Gruijters
and Behrman, 2020). Rurality associated with Indigeneity is connected
to lower-wage jobs, primarily agricultural and other high-intensity jobs
(Orozco and Valdivia, 2017; Tarallo, 2019). In Guatemala and Senegal,
the job market struggles because of a low accumulation of human capital, instability due to migration and cultural, racial, and linguistic
barriers when attempting to access more rewarding jobs (de la Garza,
2016; Orozco and Valdivia, 2017), contributing to the cyclical reproduction of poverty and inequality.
Another layer of education inequality among Indigenous populations
is gender, with illiteracy rates among Indigenous women being much
larger when compared to non-Indigenous people and their male counterparts (López, 2009; Patrinos and Velez, 2009). In Guatemala, 74 % of
Indigenous women are literate compared to 85 % of males (Orozco and
Valdivia, 2017), with the poor implementation of bilingual education
programs further marginalising women, as too the discontinuity of education due to early marriage, intensity of chores and care activities
(Adelman and Székely, 2017; Orozco and Valdivia, 2017). Early marriage and teenage pregnancies are a massive hurdle; Latin America faces
huge educational disengagement due to early pregnancies (Murphy-Graham et al., 2020; Taylor et al., 2019), while the prominent dropout
reason for Senegalesan girls is early marriage (Delprato et al., 2017;
Lloyd and Mensch, 2008; Newman, 2020).
School variables are vitally important to boost educational opportunities, especially in extreme poverty environments where basic
educational material is lacking and the marginal utility of additional
school quality is much higher (Gruijters and Behrman, 2020). School
resources highly vary by geographical location, with rural schools
(mainly attended by the Indigenous students) having lower budgets,
fewer materials and weak infrastructure and high pupil-teacher ratios
and, vitally, because of their isolation, they are not attractive to teachers
(Grasso, 2018; Hernandez-Zavala et al., 2006; Ito, 2012; Mulkeen et al.,
2008). Also, research indicates inadequate and poorly targeted training
as one of the most outstanding issues, particularly concerning bilingual
programs (Gruijters and Behrman, 2020; Martín-Chazeaud and Celaya,
2020), with some teachers not speaking the language of the community
or having a limited knowledge of it (de la Garza, 2016). Furthermore, in
each of three countries, bilingual educators often maintain a racial
orientation that considers Indigenous language and culture inferior and
as barriers to learning (de la Garza, 2016; Khan, 2014).

3. Data and descriptive statistics
3.1. Data sources: in and out school samples
For the empirical analysis, we employ data from the 2017 PISA for
Development (PISA-D). Given that our overall aim is to quantify the
Indigenous learning gap, with the additional challenge for those Indigenous youth not currently attending school, we rely on two versions of
PISA-D: (i) the in-school sample 8 targeting students attending school at
grade 7 (15 years-old) which includes seven countries: Cambodia,
Ecuador, Guatemala, Honduras, Paraguay, Senegal and Zambia; and (ii)
the out-of-school sample 9 comprising OOSY aged 14–16 years old using a
household-based survey in five countries: Guatemala, Honduras, Panama, Paraguay and Senegal. Out of the countries included in the two
PISA-D samples, we excluded Panama (not included in the in-school
sample) and Honduras (with a low Indigenous rate ≈ 0.8 % in the inschool sample). Thus, our working sample is composed of three countries: Guatemala, Paraguay and Senegal, with a total number of observations∕students of 3613 (out-of-school) and 13,924 (in-school).
The in-school PISA-D survey is designed to describe the reality of
resource-constrained education systems which have numerous marginalised∕poor students (OECD, 2018a). This is achieved by using different
test instruments and collecting contextual information about specific
bottlenecks of schooling processes in the global south (OECD, 2018b).
Among the leading drivers to assess equality and equity, Indigeneity is
one of the five key background factors besides gender, disability,
migrant status and household poverty (OECD, 2018a). Moreover,
PISA-D out-of-school sample (or the OOSY survey) is a spin-off from the
main PISA-D school-based assessment covering those not attending
school regularly, thereby acting as a complement to the main survey.
The OOSY survey offers a tool to identify the far-off disadvantaged youth
within education systems: those who left or at risk of leaving education
(alongside an assessment of the reasons for exclusion) aged 14–16 (who
were either not in school or were in school in grade six or below).
3.2. Key covariate - Indigenous
The definition employed for establishing whether a student∕youth is
classified as Indigenous is based on the language spoken at home as
there is no information of self-reported ethnicity either from parents or
students in PISA-D. Specifically, in the two samples the question asked to
students, which is the basis for our definition of Indigeneity, is: “What
language do you speak at home most of the time?”, with the options
“language of the test” (i.e., non-Indigenous), and “other” (i.e., Indigenous). For the in-school sample questionnaire there are options for local
languages. In the case of two Latin American countries included in the
working sample, that is, Guatemala and Paraguay, Indigenous students
are those whose home language is Maya and Guarani, respectively, and
non-Indigenous students those whose speak Spanish at home, while for
Senegal those who speak French at home (the language of the test) are
considered as non-Indigenous and those who speak either Pular, Serer,
Diola or Wolof are classified as Indigenous.
Based on the language definition, the Indigenous average rates in the
out and in school samples are 55.4 % and 45.8 %, correspondingly.10

4. Methods
We rely on machine learning (ML) techniques to estimate the I-NI
learning gaps for the two school samples. Our underlying focus is on the
identification of treatment effect τ (τ = 1 if youth∕student is Indigenous,
and τ = 0 if non-Indigenous) on academic performance, plainly, the
Indigenous learning gap. Importantly, being aware of the full array of
disadvantages and how their interact across different domains
(youth∕students, family, teachers, school) behind the Indigenous
learning inequalities, ML is an appropriate technique to account for the
complexity behind the I-NI learning gap. This is due to ML’s ability to
deal with large number of explanatory variables and their interactions
(Storm et al., 2020), with the latter incorporating the concept of
intersectionality.
4.1. Machine learning - overview
ML techniques construct algorithms that can learn from the data, and
so they are capable of analysing patterns through learning, making
predictions and yielding data-driven insights. Our interest is on supervised ML type (standard econometric models) where there is an outcome
Y and a set of covariates X and the algorithm starts the model by learning
from the relationship (Breiman, 2001). There are an array of ML
methods, such as random forests, lasso, boosted regression trees, and
various hybrids of these methods (Storm et al., 2020; Athey and Imbens,
2019; Mullainathan and Spiess, 2017; Hastie et al., 2009, 2015).
Our methodological focus is on using ML for causality (Athey and
Imbens, 2019, 2017; Peters et al., 2017; Schölkopf, 2019), that is, for the
Indigenous status impact on learning, in a context of high dimensional
data: many predictors and relatively small N. Typically, the number of
regressors increases if we account for non-linearity, interaction effects,
parameter heterogeneity, or other functions of the original covariates.
Consider a generic formulation of the partial linear model to estimate
the Indigenous learning gap. The outcome is yi which denotes the binary
math and reading achievement levels (so, allowing us to examine how
the learning gap changes across the learning distribution), and the covariate of interest is the treatment effect (τγ), whilst the impacts of the
set of potential controls (x β ) are not of direct interest. Rather, we want
to address omitted variable bias by including as many potential controls

the teacher and school levels for the in school sample analysis (Panels C
and D, Table 3). These new set of variables plus their interactions results
in a larger number of possible covariates which lasso needs to select
from: 408 for the out of school sample, and 676 for the in school sample.

4.4. Double machine learning - treatment effects
The problem which may arise from lasso estimates is the sparsity
assumption which requires that the true model includes only a few
predictors. Double machine learning (DML) relaxes this assumption,
allowing to select more variables in the models and still be valid
(Chernozhukov et al., 2018, 2017). So, for robustness, we reassess the
main I-NI learning gap based on treatment effects with lasso by using
DML. DML employs cross-fitting estimation, a split-sample techniques
when combined with a potential outcome estimator requires a weaker
sparsity condition providing more reliable inference about processes
that include more controls and, further, it reduces the impact of covariate selection on the estimator for τ. DML combines the predictive power
of ML with an approach to address regularisation bias (Storm et al.,
2020).
DML proceeds into two generic steps: (i) it partitions the sample into
K folds; (ii) it uses the postlasso estimates computed using the observations not in a specific fold to fill in the moment conditions for the
observations in that fold.22 For the split sampling technique we employ 4
folds, so randomly partition the out of school and in school samples into
K= 4 subsamples (folds). Also, we resample these partitions to remove
the dependence of the estimator on any specifically selected folds. We
use S= 20 resamples, so randomly selecting each fold 20 times.

5.1.3. Selected covariates and tuning parameters
The tuning parameters determine which covariates lasso will include
and the plug-in methods, BIC, cross validation, and the adaptive lasso
are used to select the tuning parameters. A comparison of total selected
covariates (for two outcomes equations and the treatment final specification) across Table 4 (Panels A to D) shows how this number increases
from around 4 (plug-in) to 10 (BIC), and from 66 to 77 (adaptive) to
91–99 (CV). This is expected. Plug-in methods are the more parsimonious method including only important covariates but risking excluding
covariates with large coefficients. Adaptive lasso (a multi-step version of
CV) is efficient at including not only important covariates but also some
extra covariates that do not belong in the model (as well as CV which
chooses a λ that minimises the out of sample prediction), with BIC being
an intermediate case (though being more similar to the number selected
by the plug-in method).
We also provide some examples for the choice of λ with the number
of selected covariates for the out of school sample in Appendix B.

6. Conclusions
Equity (measured within SDG4) is a key feature of the new SDG
agenda and, thus, the concern of which are the groups most left behind
facing exclusion in resource constrained education system, is essential.
One of the most vulnerable groups in the global south, which had long
faced exclusion and discrimination, are Indigenous youth∕students.
Shrinking the learning divide between Indigenous and non-Indigenous
groups is needed for a steady progress of SDG4, but, in turn, this requires of a robust assessment through the consideration of the multiplicity of factors and their compounding effects behind this achievement
gap at different points of the learning distribution. In this sense, machine
learning (ML), with the capability to deal with a large number of
explanatory variables and interactions between drivers (operating at the
student, family and school levels) is a fitting technique to formally bring
about the intersectionality of disadvantages linked to Indigeneity
learning gaps.
Hence, in this paper, we employed ML to estimate the treatment
effect of Indigeneity (treated = Indigenous, and non-treated = nonIndigenous) on learning brackets of performance for three countries
(Guatemala, Paraguay and Senegal) based on the PISA-D learning survey. Our initial ML specification includes between 252 and 380 direct
and cross-over interactions effects of individual and family covariates to
chosen from. We also estimated the Indigenous and non-Indigenous (INI) learning gap by adding schooling conditions for attendance (out of
school sample) and teachers and schooling characteristics (in school
sample), as well as by employing double robust ML (allowing even more
predictors).

6.2. Policy implications
Empirically, any progress on narrowing the Indigenous learning gaps
requires, to begin with, a robust evaluation of the degree of educational
inequality accounting for the intersectionality influence behind these
disadvantages and, moreover, an assessment on which are drivers
behind this inequality (in other words, when the learning gap disappears
when comparing similar Indigenous and non-Indigenous groups based
on observable characteristics). Specifically, knowing which subpopulations of Indigenous youth are those furthest behind (either
those outside or inside education systems and to which level of
achievement are more prominent), is central for an effective targeting.
Our results point towards a two-stage process: (i) because of the
presence of the largest learning gap, targeting Indigenous students at the
risk of being out of school show be prioritised with additional pedagogic
and financial support to weaken the factors leading to their dropout of
school as once this group is outside of school, narrowing learning gap
with non-Indigenous counterparts is very challenging; (ii) for those in
school, policies should be concentrated on those Indigenous students
above the average performance level, with the aim of lessening the
differential in schooling conditions and resources with those schools
which are more likely to be attended by non-Indigenous students. That
is, for out of school populations policies could be seen as prevention
measures against dropout while for those in school in levelling resources
and infrastructure between schools. Yet, because of the historical and
discriminatory process against Indigenous populations, these policies
would still face significant challenges as we found persistent gaps on
achievement for Indigenous and non-Indigenous groups even after accounting for huge range of factors behind access and the quality of
learning.
There are, nevertheless, some promising initial steps to narrow this
inequality, at least broadly. First, the existing legal framework of each
country could have the potential to implement multicultural and bilingual education for Indigenous peoples (see review in Section 2). However, this requires a less vague legal framework acknowledging
