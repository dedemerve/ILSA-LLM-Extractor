Research on the influence of teachers’ ICT Capacity on classroom
teaching quality: An intermediary model based on self-efficacy
Feng Zhang , Qinghao Li *
Department of Education Management, Faculty of Education, East China Normal University, Shanghai, 200062, China

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Digitalization of education
Classroom teaching quality
Teachers’ ICT capacity
Threshold effect

As the core participants and implementers of the digital transformation in education, enhancing
teachers’ proficiency in Information and Communication Technology (ICT) emerges as a crucial
lever for catalyzing educational reform. Based on the technology integration theory and
constructivism theory, using the data from the Teaching and Learning International Survey
(TALIS) as the research sample, the OLS multiple regression model, Shapley value decomposition
method, mediation effect model, quantile regression model, and threshold regression model were
used to empirically examine the empowerment effect, mechanism effect, heterogeneity effect, and
threshold effect of teachers’ ICT capacity on classroom teaching quality. The study found that,
compared with OECD countries, there is much room for improvement in the ICT capacity of
teachers in Shanghai, China, where teachers receive adequate ICT training, but there is a serious
lack of digital technology literacy among teachers, and the phenomenon of “acceptance in reason
but detachment in practice” is significant. The ability of teachers to utilize information and
communication technology (ICT) significantly influences classroom teaching quality, with
teachers’ self-efficacy playing a partially mediating role. Enhancing teachers’ ICT capacity has the
strongest impact on the “cognitive mobilization” dimension, while its effect on the “classroom
management” dimension is comparatively weaker. Moreover, the empowering effect of information and communication technology increases as the quality of teachers’ teaching improves,
and to some extent, there is a “Matthew effect” that leads to a widening of the gap in the quality of
classroom teaching. Furthermore, teachers’ ICT capacity in the process of promoting the
improvement of classroom teaching quality is characterized by the double-threshold effect of
“stair-step rise”. With the improvement of teachers’ ICT capacity, its effect on the improvement of
classroom teaching quality shows marginal incremental characteristics, breaking the “ceiling
effect”. Based on these findings, recommendations include enhancing the multi-principal
ecosystem of digital education, developing a digital profile of teachers’ teaching, providing
supportive resources in a hierarchical and categorized manner, and fostering the establishment of
a teacher-learning community.

With the iterative upgrading of technology, the digital transformation of education has become a global trend. This extensive
integration of digital technology within the educational sphere has the potential to not only revolutionize the entire educational and

teaching process, thereby fostering high-quality development, but also to enable the widespread dissemination of superior educational
resources. Such advancements are instrumental in effectively offering new opportunities for classroom teaching reform. As of October
2023, the Internet access rate across all educational levels and types of schools in China has achieved a remarkable milestone of 100 %.
This comprehensive connectivity is complemented by the achievement of wireless network coverage in over three-quarters of these
institutions, alongside the establishment of multimedia classrooms in 99.5 % of schools. This significant progress in the digital
infrastructure of educational institutions underscores a continuous trajectory of improvement and upgrading. Scholars have identified
a consequential “amplification effect” of digital technology on teaching practices, facilitated by the enhanced informatization facilities
(Beardsley et al., 2021). This effect realizes the effective integration of pedagogical knowledge and technological knowledge into
specific subject content teaching as emphasized by the Technology Integration Theory, facilitating the creation of diversified interactive environments for teacher-student engagement. It encourages students to collaboratively construct knowledge through cooperation, thereby enhancing the quality and efficiency of classroom instruction. However, in reality, in China’s educational
environment, only about 24 % of teachers often let students use ICT for project learning or classroom work (OECD, 2019), and a
significant number of school teachers in China still possess only surface-level ICT skills. Their information literacy is notably inadequate (Sang et al., 2010), and many teachers face challenges such as low recognition of technology and barriers to integrating
human-technology interactions (Hennessy et al., 2005; Malinina, 2015). Due to the disparity between teachers’ ICT capacity and
digital skills, their additional working hours undoubtedly increase to some extent, thereby exacerbating workload and job stress. This
disproportionate allocation leads to excessive intrinsic cognitive load (related to subject complexity), extraneous cognitive load (from
instructional design), and germane cognitive load (for schema construction), as defined in Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller, 1988).
Such overloads hinder the optimization of instructional design and impede the creation of high-quality instructional materials,
consequently obstructing the improvement of classroom teaching quality (Luo, 2023; Arabaci & Deveci, 2022). In light of these circumstances, teachers in the new era find themselves confronted with the dilemma of digital technology simultaneously enhancing
quality and efficiency while increasing workload. However, we must realize that this difference is an inevitable product of the digital
transformation of education. Only by fundamentally improving teachers’ ICT capacity can we truly release the great potential brought
about by the digital transformation of education. Therefore, it is essential to examine whether teachers’ ICT capacity has a “positive” or
“negative” impact on the quality of their classroom teaching. In fact, to some extent, it reflects the theoretical controversy between
technology integration theory and cognitive load theory. Under what theory is the influence of teachers’ ICT capacity on classroom
teaching quality more suitable for the Chinese situation, which is the key issue of this paper?
The imperative for contemporary educational policymakers, administrators, and scholars is to investigate methodologies that
empower basic education teachers to transcend the constraints—referred to as “technological shackles”—and to harness the full
potential of technological advancements in classroom instruction, thereby facilitating a transition towards intelligent education.
Nonetheless, a critical review of extant scholarly work reveals a predominant inclination within the academic milieu towards the
theoretical explication of teacher teaching quality evaluation frameworks (Feldman, 1977; Centra, 1979), the dissection of variables
affecting classroom pedagogy and student learning (Shulman, 1986; Noben et al., 2022), and the development of ameliorative strategies (Fryer, 2011; Cong & Yu, 2020). There is limited literature available that explores the impact of teachers’ ICT capacity on the
quality of classroom teaching, and the specific mechanisms underlying this impact are largely unknown. Therefore, there is a need for
evidence-based investigation. The relationship between teachers’ ICT capacity and classroom teaching quality is not only a regional
educational topic but also a significant concern for the education community globally. While the reality of teachers’ ICT capacity is
widespread across different countries, it is notable that the majority of research on this subject primarily originates from Western
educational discourse, potentially leading to certain cultural differences in the conclusions drawn. Consequently, this study aims to
examine the situation of teachers’ ICT capacity in Shanghai from an international perspective, utilizing assessment data released by the
OECD. Based on this analysis, it will reveal the process through which teachers’ ICT capacity influences classroom teaching quality in
the local context of China.
From this perspective, several research questions merit further exploration. Firstly, what is the present state of teachers’ ICT capacity in China during the initial phase of educational digital transformation? Are there any disparities compared to teachers in OECD
countries? Secondly, does teachers’ ICT capacity influence the quality of classroom teaching? Is this influence empowering or
detrimental? Thirdly, how does teachers’ ICT capacity impact the classroom teaching quality, and what is the pathway of this influence? Lastly, does teachers’ ICT capacity have a substantial and heterogeneous effect on classroom teaching quality?
Given the limitations of existing literature, this paper aims to empirically investigate the relationship between teachers’ ICT capacity and classroom teaching quality, using data from the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS). Furthermore,
considering the variations in teachers’ individual circumstances, there is a possibility of heterogeneous effects on the impact of
teachers’ ICT capacity on classroom teaching quality. Therefore, this study also explores these heterogeneous effects using quantile
regression and threshold regression models. The objective is to provide empirical evidence that supports the promotion of high-quality
development in the global education system and the achievement of educational informatization goals.
2. Literature review and research hypothesis
2.1. Relationship between teachers’ ICT capacity and classroom teaching quality
Teachers’ ICT capacity refers to their professional competence in utilizing information technology to enhance the effectiveness of
their work, improve students’ learning outcomes, and support their own sustainable development. Strengthening teachers’ ICT capacity is an essential prerequisite for promoting the seamless integration of IT and teaching methods, ultimately leading to the

modernization of education (Cabero-Almenara et al., 2021). More and more evidence shows that teachers’ ICT capacity is significantly
related to teaching ability (Lin et al., 2023), self-efficacy (Ghazali et al., 2024), and students’ classroom participation (Hou, 2024).
Guided by social constructivism and technology integration theory, this study develops a theoretical framework linking teachers’
ICT capacity to instructional quality through the dual lenses of the nature of learning and teacher capacity. Specifically, social
constructivism emphasizes that learning is an active process of knowledge construction shaped by interactions between individuals
and their social environment (Jovana, 2011). High-quality teaching should therefore create supportive learning environments that
enhance students’ knowledge building and social engagement. In the digital era, ICT offers significant potential to enrich such environments. However, the effective integration of technology into teaching does not occur automatically—it imposes new demands on
teachers’ professional capabilities. The Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) framework, developed by Mishra and
Koehler (2006), offers a critical analytical perspective. A central goal of TPACK is to promote effective teaching and learning (Jammeh
et al., 2024) by emphasizing the integration of technology, pedagogy, and content knowledge. The deeper the integration among these
three domains, the greater the potential for teacher professional growth (Cetin-Dindar et al., 2018) and the more effectively ICT tools
can be utilized. Teachers strong in TPACK engage with technology proactively and reflectively (Koehler et al., 2014), continuously
strengthening their ICT capacity. This enables them to dynamically co-construct knowledge and practice, enhance their instructional
expertise, and reorient the classroom toward student-centered learning. Such an approach fosters greater autonomy and effectiveness
in student learning (Angeli & Valanides, 2009), thereby ultimately improving overall instructional quality.
In conclusion, to provide a more nuanced examination of how teachers’ ICT capacity influences classroom teaching quality, the
following three aspects will be analyzed: Firstly, enhancing teachers’ ICT capacity can facilitate clear and effective instruction in the
classroom. The three dimensions of understanding, preparation, and application of teachers’ ICT capacity can correspond to the
technical knowledge, teaching method knowledge, and subject content knowledge in the TPACK model under the technology integration theory and can play an important role in education (Mishra & Koehler, 2006), teaching, and educational environment construction. Teachers’ mastery of information technology application skills can stimulate teaching innovation (Koh et al., 2017), can help
teachers adapt to the changes brought by information technology in teaching, lead teachers to change their teaching ideas and
methods, and create knowledge with each other according to the environment in the class (Lai et al., 2022), optimize the classroom
teaching structure, carry out personalized teaching activities for students, continuously enrich their own teaching resources, and
realize clear teaching in the classroom, thus effectively improving the quality of education and teaching.
Secondly, enhancing teachers’ ICT capacity can facilitate teacher-student dialogue, collaboration, and social interaction, thereby
further stimulating students’ cognitive engagement and active participation in classroom instruction. The theory of social constructivism highlights the cooperative nature of knowledge construction, often initiated through dialogue (Tsoukas, 2000). By utilizing
information technology, teachers can overcome temporal and spatial limitations, facilitate dialogue and the exchange of teaching
experiences among exemplary educators, foster effective professional collaboration, partially mitigate the negative impact of “information asymmetry”, expand the boundaries of educational information and teaching methodologies (Abilleira et al., 2021), and
strengthen the integration ability of effective information technology. Teaching practice based on the TPACK framework enables
educators to design instructional activities that engage students cognitively and promote active participation. It also supports more
effective selection and use of ‘ICT tools that foster students’ inquiry, collaboration, and problem-solving skills (Mgeladze et al., 2024).
As a result, this approach significantly enhances students’ learning motivation, cultivates critical thinking, and consistently activates
cognitive engagement in the classroom, thereby further improving the overall quality of instruction.
Thirdly, classroom management is the key factor to integrate technology into teaching meaningfully (Durak & Saritepeci, 2017).
Teachers with a high level of ICT capacity are better at managing classes (Runge et al., 2023), and they can better carry out
student-centered teaching activities and enhance students’ constructivist learning experience (Rakes et al., 2006). Information
technology empowers teachers by providing them with platforms and tools for professional development. It reshapes and optimizes the
process of teaching and learning in the classroom, accelerates the adoption of new teaching concepts and instructional approaches, and
establishes a new type of teacher-student relationship. To a certain extent, this can make classroom teaching turn more to students,
arouse the enthusiasm of students’ classroom participation, realize the interaction between teachers and students in the classroom, and
achieve the effect of class management, ultimately leading to a significant improvement in the quality of classroom instruction
(Rockoff, 2004; Timperley & Alton-Lee, 2008). Based on the aforementioned analysis, in order to explore whether teachers’ ICT capacity will affect classroom teaching quality, this study presents the following hypotheses:
H1: Teachers’ ICT capacity has a significant contributory effect on the classroom teaching quality.
2.2. The mediating role of teachers’ self-efficacy
In the teaching process, technical knowledge, teaching methods, and related skills are very necessary, but this is not enough.
Teachers need to have a sufficient sense of faith when using technology to teach (Taimalu & Luik, 2019), and teachers’ value cognition
and use of technology are closely related to their sense of self-efficacy (Anderson et al., 2012). Teachers’ self-efficacy is derived from
Bandura’s theory of self-efficacy, which mainly refers to the teacher’s judgment of his or her ability to successfully carry out an
instructional behavior and the presumption of the possible outcome of that behavior (Tschannen-Moran et al., 1998). Enhancing
teachers’ ICT capacity yields several benefits. Firstly, recent studies have shown that teachers’ information technology knowledge
covered in the TPACK model of technology integration theory is related to self-efficacy (Yildiz Durak, 2019). It enhances teachers’
sense of professional identity, alleviates or even reduces burnout, improves their overall happiness, and boosts job satisfaction,
consequently enhancing their self-efficacy (Ortan et al., 2021). Secondly, it strengthens professional collaboration among teachers and
enhances their overall competence, emotional well-being, and teaching beliefs (Tsai & Chai, 2012), positively influencing their

pedagogical thinking and further bolstering their self-efficacy (Dilekli & Tezci, 2020). Existing research indicates that teachers’
self-efficacy is the key driving force of teaching quality and professional growth, and it is positively related to classroom management
and student participation (Karaiskos et al., 2024). Specifically, there is a significant correlation between high emotional value and
knowledge acquisition. Teachers with high self-efficacy are more adept at accurately gauging the pace of instruction and daring to
employ innovative teaching methods. These approaches help students overcome learning difficulties and enhance their enthusiasm
and participation in class through high emotional value (Liu et al., 2024). This strengthens students’ knowledge acquisition and
emotional experience, thereby improving the overall quality of classroom teaching.
Furthermore, teachers’ self-efficacy, as a critical internal motivational mechanism, affects their physical and mental well-being,
professional development, and teaching practices. The theory of social constructivism emphasizes student-centered learning, and
students actively construct knowledge through interaction and collaboration. On this basis, teachers are more likely to apply information technology to classroom teaching. Teachers with high self-efficacy possess a precise perception and evaluation of their teaching
abilities, exhibit unwavering dedication to teaching and professional growth (Künsting et al., 2016), and shape their instructional
content, organization, and behaviors at a foundational level (Evans et al., 2014). By building a good teacher-student relationship,
strengthening the knowledge link and emotional experience with students, we can further enhance the interactivity in the classroom
teaching process, and achieve higher academic achievements when encountering challenging learning content (Liu et al., 2025), thus
improving the quality of classroom teaching. Based on the aforementioned analysis, to explore the ways through which teachers’ ICT
capacity affects the quality of classroom teaching, the study presents the following hypotheses:
H2: Teachers’ ICT capacity affects the classroom teaching quality through teachers’ self-efficacy.
3. Materials and methods
3.1. Data sources
The data for this study originated from the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) project conducted by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) in 2018. TALIS 2018 collected relevant information on over 260,000
junior high school teachers from 48 countries/regions, including 3976 teachers in the Shanghai region of China. In this paper, based on
the variables of teachers’ ICT capacity, teachers’ self-efficacy, and classroom teaching quality needed for the study, the Shanghai
sample of TALIS 2018 was subjected to data responsiveness screening, and after removing the missing values, we finally obtained a
sample of 2945 valid teachers in the Shanghai region.
3.2. Research instruments
In this study, the Teacher’s ICT Capacity Scale, Teacher Classroom Teaching Practice Scale, and Teacher Self-Efficacy Scale were
used to investigate and study the teachers in Shanghai. All of the above scales adopt Likert’s 4-point positive scoring system (1 means
completely non-compliant, 4 means completely compliant), and the scores of each topic are summed up to take the mean value, and the
higher the score, the better the development of each variable respectively.
3.3. Reliability test
In order to confirm the validity of the data, all scales were tested for reliability in this study. The results indicate that the Cronbach’s
alpha of the internal consistency test for the total scale of the questionnaire is 0.912, and the Cronbach’s alpha of the internal consistency test for each scale and sub-dimension also exceeds 0.800, which makes the questionnaire’s reliability better. On this basis, this
study further tests the structural validity of each variable to lay the foundation for subsequent in-depth empirical analysis. After
testing, the sampling appropriateness KMO of each variable exceeded 0.900, and the Bartlett’s spherical test P < 0.001; further
validation factor analysis was conducted, and the results showed that the RMSEA ＜ 0.080, the CFI ＞ 0.900, the SRMR ＜ 0.080, and
the TLI ＞ 0.900, which conformed to the fitting standards of existing studies, and the structural validity of the questionnaire was good.
In addition to this, this study used Harman’s one-factor method to conduct an unrotated exploratory factor analysis of all variables
included, and the results showed that KMO = 0.937, Bartlett’s test of sphericity P < 0.001; the amount of methodological explanation
of the first factor also did not exceed the critical criterion of 40 % (Organ, 1986). Therefore, it is less likely that this study was affected
by common method bias, and subsequent data analysis can be conducted.
3.4. Variables
3.4.1. Dependent variables
TALIS 2018 employed a four-level Likert scale to assess teachers’ self-reported perceptions of classroom teaching quality. Using the
results of question responses, TALIS 2018 conducted several steps, including internal consistency testing, validated factor analysis,
method testing, and composite dimension fitting. As a result, it synthesized teaching option strategies into three dimensions: “classroom management”, “clear teaching", and “cognitive mobilization”. The goodness-of-fit tests demonstrated that the model fits well
within the group of teachers in Shanghai for this study, making it a suitable measure of classroom teaching quality. The scientific
validity of the scale had been widely recognized in the Chinese context and showed good adaptability (Lin & Zhou, 2022).

3.4.2. Independent variable and mediating variable
We assessed teachers’ ICT capacity across three dimensions: understanding, readiness, and application (Liang, 2020). The “understanding” dimension included two questions: “It is very important to invest in ICT in schools” and “The need for training in ICT
teaching skills”; the readiness dimension consisted of questions such as “Whether they are ready to use ICT in their teaching practice”;
and the application dimension included questions such as “Supporting students in using ICT to complete projects and projects”. The
scale was found to have good internal consistency and was chosen to measure teachers’ ICT capacity.
The mediator variable in this study is teachers’ self-efficacy, measured in strict adherence to the TALIS 2018 survey design, which is
grounded in a robust theoretical and empirical framework. This construct is derived from the Teacher Self-Efficacy Scale (TSES)
developed by Tschannen-Moran and Woolfolk Hoy (2001), widely recognized and used internationally. Extensive psychometric
studies have confirmed the stability and reliability of its three-dimensional structure. Consistent with this framework, TALIS 2018
operationalizes teacher self-efficacy into three dimensions: teaching efficacy, classroom management efficacy, and student engagement efficacy. The official TALIS 2018 technical report supports the construct’s validity through cross-national confirmatory factor
analysis (CFA). Recent studies using TALIS 2018 data have further validated this structure, with research on the Shanghai sample
providing direct localized evidence for the measurement choices in this study (Liu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025).
Referring to the established studies and considering that classroom teaching quality may be affected by teachers’ own factors, to
better explain the mechanism of teachers’ ICT capacity on classroom teaching quality, this paper includes four levels of information,
namely, teachers’ gender, age, qualification, and years of teaching experience, as control variables in the regression model. The
descriptive statistics of the relevant variables are shown in Table 1 and Table 2.

3.5.4. Threshold regression model
Both the multiple linear regression model and the quantile regression model solely focused on the linear influence of teachers’ ICT
capacity on classroom teaching quality. However, in reality, the impact of teachers’ ICT capacity on classroom teaching quality may
not exhibit a singular linear pattern but undergo continuous dynamic changes. Thus, to examine the occurrence of a threshold effect on
classroom teaching quality as teachers’ ICT capacity improves, this study utilizes the concept of threshold regression proposed by
Hansen (Hansen, 1999) to investigate the nonlinear interaction between teachers’ ICT capacity and classroom teaching quality. The
formula for the threshold regression model is presented below:

4. Results
4.1. Current status and international comparison of teachers’ ICT capacity
The OECD assesses teachers’ utilization of information technology for teaching and learning in the classroom across five dimensions: “dimension 1: Teachers reporting a high level of need for professional development in ICT skills for teaching”, “dimension 2:
Principals reporting shortages or inadequacy of digital technology for instruction”, “dimension 3: Teachers who ‘frequently’ or ‘always’ let students use ICT for projects or class work”, “dimension 4: Teachers for whom the ‘use of ICT for teaching’ was included in
their formal education or training”, and “dimension 5: Teachers who felt ‘well prepared’ or ‘very well prepared’ for the use of ICT for
teaching”. This study compares teachers from OECD countries as a whole with teachers in Shanghai, providing a comprehensive
understanding of the differences between the two groups through international comparisons involving various teacher samples.

Shanghai was chosen as the research site for the following reasons: firstly, Shanghai is in the highland of education in China. As the
experimental zone of comprehensive education reform in China, Shanghai took the lead in implementing the “Education Informatization 2.0 Action Plan,” which is typical and can be studied. Secondly, Shanghai (China), as the only city in China that continuously
participates in TALIS, can provide structured data comparable to that of OECD countries. Thirdly, Shanghai’s teachers’ group can
provide a unique case for cross-cultural comparison by integrating the Eastern educational tradition with Western technological
concepts. To sum up, this study chooses Shanghai (China) as the research site, which has strong Chinese characteristics and research
value.
Based on Fig. 1, noticeable variations exist in the scores of Shanghai teachers and teachers from OECD countries regarding the use
of information technology in teaching across different dimensions, with scores ranging from 17.7 to 79.1. Shanghai teachers outperformed their OECD counterparts in all dimensions, except for dimension 2 and dimension 3, where their scores were lower. More
specifically, Shanghai teachers obtained the highest score in the dimension of “incorporating ICT training into teacher training” and
the lowest scores in the dimensions of “teachers who regularly use ICT for projects or classroom work” and “principals who report
shortages or deficiencies in digital technology for teaching.”
To provide a more detailed analysis of the differences, this study utilizes relative comparison to determine the ranking of ICT
utilization in teaching among Shanghai teachers, building upon the overall comparison. Table 3 showcases the rankings of 45 countries
in the TALIS based on their scores in teachers’ use of ICT for teaching and learning (there are 48 participating countries, and 3
participating countries have missing values. In order to ensure the accuracy of the study, they are deleted.). Notably, china achieved
the highest score in the dimension of “teachers for whom the ‘use of ICT for teaching’ was included in their formal education or
training” and ranked fourth among all countries. Conversely, the dimensions of “principals who report shortages or deficiencies in

4.2. Teachers’ ICT capacity significantly and positively affects classroom teaching quality
The results presented in Table 4 demonstrate that, even after controlling for individual characteristic variables, the estimated
coefficients of teachers’ ICT capacity exhibit significant positive values and pass the 1 % significance level test. This signifies that
teachers’ ICT capacity has a substantial positive impact on classroom teaching quality, thereby confirming hypothesis 1. By enhancing

their ICT capacity, teachers can integrate ICT resources more effectively, create an engaging and interactive learning environment, and
guide students in active participation during classroom activities to foster the exploration and construction of knowledge systems.
These improvements collectively contribute to the enhancement of classroom teaching quality.
With regard to the sub-dimensions of classroom teaching quality, the impact of enhancing teachers’ ICT capacity varies across the
dimensions of “cognitive mobilization,” “clear teaching,” and “classroom management.” Firstly, among these dimensions, the most
substantial effect of teachers’ ICT capacity was observed in the “cognitive mobilization” dimension. After controlling for other variables, each one-unit increase in teachers’ ICT capacity resulted in an average increase of 1.365 units in the “cognitive mobilization”

Constructivist learning theory emphasizes the active participation of learners in the process of constructing knowledge (Allen,
2008). With the use of ICT tools, teachers can design and present challenging and inspiring learning assignments, develop students’
independent exploration and critical thinking skills, create a conducive learning environment, and stimulate students’ learning potential and initiative. Thus, these efforts contribute to the “cognitive mobilization” dimension of improving classroom teaching quality.
In contrast, teachers’ ICT capacity had the weakest effect on the “classroom management” dimension. After controlling for other
variables, the average increase in the “classroom management” dimension was 0.697 units for each unit increase in teachers’ ICT
capacity. This difference may be attributed to the fact that teachers’ responsibility for leading and coordinating classroom management, which relies heavily on their organizational and managerial skills, as well as their discipline management strategies and
classroom management techniques, is weakly associated with their ICT capacity.
4.3. Contribution of various factors to differences in the teacher classroom quality
Table 5 presents the results obtained from the Shapley decomposition method, which analyzes the contribution of total variation in
the sub-dimensions of teachers’ classroom teaching quality. The data reveal that the primary factors influencing the variance of “clear
teaching” ability in classroom teaching quality are ICT capacity and teachers’ gender, contributing 85.43 % and 13.39 % respectively.
The contribution rate of ICT capacity to the “cognitive mobilization” ability of teachers’ classroom teaching quality far exceeds that of
other factors, at 97.40 %. It is followed by teachers’ age (1.80 %), while the contribution rate of other individual characteristics of
teachers’ variables remains below 1 %. Furthermore, the variance in teachers’ classroom teaching quality regarding “classroom
management” ability is mainly due to the application of information technology, contributing 80.03 %. Additionally, teachers’ years of
teaching experience and age account for a certain contribution rate of 13.59 % and 5.89 %, respectively, while the contribution rate of
other variables is relatively low.
In summary, effective strategies to enhance the quality of teachers’ classroom teaching encompass two aspects: Firstly, improving
their ability to apply information technology is crucial. Schools should provide comprehensive training and services to help teachers
master and proficiently utilize various ICT tools and platforms, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of classroom teaching. Secondly,
teachers’ individual characteristics and experiences influence classroom teaching quality. Therefore, schools should offer personalized
support and guidance to assist teachers in identifying their strengths and weaknesses, ultimately improving the quality of their
classroom teaching.

utilizing technology effectively in the classroom, and seamlessly integrating technology with instructional objectives and teaching

4.6. Mechanistic path of teachers’ ICT capacity on classroom teaching quality
To elucidate the mechanism through which teachers’ ICT capacity affects classroom teaching quality, we employ a mediation effect
model to examine the involved pathways. The empirical findings are presented in Table 9.
The empirical results indicate a significant mediating effect of teachers’ self-efficacy in the relationship between teachers’ ICT
capacity and classroom teaching quality. Following the standard steps of the mediation effect test, the regression results presented in
Table 9 are analyzed. Firstly, in model (1), the effect of ICT capacity on teachers’ classroom teaching quality is significantly positive at
the 1 % level, allowing the test to proceed. Secondly, in model (2), the coefficient representing the effect of teachers’ ICT capacity on
teachers’ self-efficacy is 0.396, and in model (3), the coefficient representing self-efficacy is 0.221. Both coefficients have passed the
significance test at the 1 % level. Thus, a partial mediation effect is observed, confirming Hypothesis 2 of this paper, which posits a
transmission chain of “enhancing teachers’ ICT capacity → promoting teachers’ self-efficacy → improving teachers’ classroom
teaching quality”.
To further validate the mediating role of teachers’ self-efficacy in the relationship between ICT capacity and classroom teaching
quality, this study employed both percentile and bias-corrected Bootstrap methods, performing 5000 resampling iterations. The results
in Table 10 show that the 95 % confidence intervals for the indirect effect from both methods are [0.074, 0.102] and [0.093, 0.127],
respectively, both excluding zero. This confirms that teachers’ self-efficacy partially mediates the relationship between ICT capacity
and teaching quality, strengthening the robustness of the findings. Moreover, the 95 % confidence interval for the direct effect also
excludes zero, indicating statistical significance. This further reaffirms that self-efficacy partially mediates the link between ICT capacity and classroom teaching quality.

TALIS 2018 survey conducted among teachers in Shanghai, China. Additionally, we employ Shapley’s value decomposition method to
empirically examine the key factors influencing classroom teaching quality and investigate the multidimensional heterogeneity effect
of teachers’ ICT capacity on classroom teaching quality using quantile regression and threshold regression models. Based on the
empirical findings, this paper reveals the following key findings, presents the following conclusions, and provides corresponding

that teachers’ ICT capacity significantly and positively influenced classroom teaching quality, resulting in an improvement of 0.297
units. Among the sub-dimensions of classroom teaching quality, “cognitive mobilization” showed the greatest impact on teachers’ ICT
capacity, while “classroom management” had the least effect. When controlling for other variables, every one-unit increase in teachers’
ICT capacity led to an average increase of 1.365 units in “cognitive mobilization” and 0.697 units in “classroom management”.
This shows that teachers’ ICT capacity has a significant positive correlation with classroom teaching quality. Specifically, the
higher the teacher’s ICT capacity, the better the effect of improving the quality of classroom teaching. This research result breaks
through the main conclusions of previous studies and is consistent with similar research conclusions (Nelson et al., 2019; Ajani &
Govender, 2023). At the same time, the theory of technology integration emphasizes that teachers are not only the transmitters and
auxiliaries of teaching but also the participants and practitioners of teaching activities. Teachers can also play and create with students
in the classroom and integrate into the learning collective from the perspective of educational practice and learning participation.
Therefore, teachers with stronger ICT capacity can better integrate information technology in the classroom and improve classroom
teaching quality. However, we must realize that the gap between teachers’ ICT capacity in reality is essentially a dialectical relationship between “educational noumenon” and “technical instrumentality.” Only by correctly treating the relationship between them
and making technology truly serve teachers’ development can we achieve twice the result with half the effort.
ICT capacity positively contributes to the quality of classroom teaching, indicating that improving teachers’ ICT capacity can play a
role in upgrading the quality of teaching. Based on this, it is necessary to establish a sound digital education system, integrate advanced
information technology resources, build digital curriculum platforms, online learning resource banks, etc. To provide teachers with
more tools, accelerate the integration of information technology resources, and create a rich and interactive learning environment.
Since the beginning of the twenty-first century, the Ministry of Education in China has placed significant emphasis on enhancing
the information literacy and information technology teaching proficiency of primary and secondary school teachers. As part of this
effort, the ministry has implemented a series of national action plans aimed at promoting the capacity building of teachers in information technology. Nevertheless, it is important to highlight that comprehensive improvement of teachers’ ICT capacity requires
strengthening the development of digital infrastructure and establishing and enhancing the digital education system. Research shows
that nearly 70 % of the knowledge acquired by adults comes from work experience and problem-solving. Consequently, a mature and

5.2. Comprehensively carry out digital portraits of teachers’ teaching, explore the establishment of a personalized and differentiated teacher
management system, and accurately empower classroom teaching quality
Research reveals a dual-threshold characteristic in the impact of teachers’ ICT capacity on the enhancement of classroom teaching
quality. Furthermore, as teachers’ ICT capacity improves, its effect on the improvement of classroom teaching quality follows a
marginal incremental pattern.
This shows that the influence of teachers’ ICT capacity on classroom teaching quality is nonlinear, and this research result breaks
through the boundary of previous research (Cheng & Ma, 2024; Hong et al., 2024) and is also the research innovation of our article.
Previous studies have discussed that the application of information technology can improve classroom teaching quality, but no one has
elaborated and analyzed the improvement effect in detail. However, in our research, it is found that the influence of teachers’ ICT
capacity on classroom teaching quality is a marginal double-threshold effect, which shows that when teachers integrate technology
into effective learning, teachers centered on TPACK can better actively connect and interact with technology (Koehler et al., 2014).
When a certain knowledge element in teachers’ TPACK is improved, teachers’ professional development comes into being (Nugraheni
& Srisawasdi, 2025), which in turn affects the improvement of classroom teaching quality.
Therefore, to address the evolving demands of teachers’ ICT capacity in teaching practice, it becomes imperative to develop a
comprehensive digital profile of teachers’ teaching practices. Additionally, a tiered training plan should be formulated based on
teachers’ individual characteristics, and a differentiated teacher management system should be explored to effectively support
classroom teaching quality at various levels. In essence, for teachers with limited IT application skills, providing fundamental training
is essential. This training aims to help them acquire proficiency in basic technical tools and facilitate the digitalization of fundamental
classroom teaching practices. By doing so, teachers can overcome the traditional resistance to change, resulting in a substantial
improvement in the quality of classroom teaching. Teachers with moderate ICT capacity receive intermediate training that emphasizes
the development of technology integration skills. This training also aims to expand the scope and depth of teachers’ professional
growth and personal development opportunities. Teachers with advanced ICT capacity undergo advanced training to enhance their
digital literacy and proficiency. This training aims to maximize the potential of digital technology in classroom teaching and pave the
way for cultivating subject matter experts. Moreover, considering the ongoing development of teachers’ ICT capacity, it is crucial to
establish a long-term training mechanism. This mechanism should offer regularly updated training content to ensure teachers remain
up to date with technological advancements and changes in teaching methods.
5.3. Strengthen the echelon of teachers, focus on the demonstration effect of master teachers, and promote the construction of a teacherlearning community
The quantile regression model yielded empirical results, revealing that teachers’ ICT capacity had parameter estimates of 0.203,
0.265, 0.245, 0.345, and 0.372 at each quartile. These estimates suggest that a one-unit increase in teachers’ ICT capacity enhances the
classroom teaching quality by 0.203 units at the low quartile and by 0.265, 0.245, 0.345, and 0.372 units at the low-moderate,
medium, high-moderate, and high quartiles, respectively. In other words, the effect of strengthening teachers’ ICT capacity on the
gain in the classroom teaching quality at intermediate and high levels is more obvious. At the same time, teachers’ self-efficacy plays a
partial intermediary role between teachers’ ICT capacity and classroom teaching quality.
This indicates that the self-efficacy and self-evaluation abilities of pre-service teachers in applying technology to teaching are
closely related to their attitudes and intentions regarding technology integration. When teachers apply information technology to
teaching, their sense of self-efficacy can greatly affect teachers’ attitudes and willingness to integrate technology. When teachers
possess a higher capacity for ICT, their self-efficacy enhances their awareness of using technology in classroom instruction (Tondeur
et al., 2017). This increased awareness positively influences teachers’ behaviors in the classroom, leading to improved quality and
efficiency in teaching.
Therefore, it is crucial for school administrators to offer extensive and comprehensive professional development opportunities and
incentives to teachers operating at the mid-to-high level of classroom quality. These incentives, honors, and professional development
opportunities can serve as motivation for teachers to continuously enhance their ICT capacity, bolster their confidence in technology
adoption, and effectively contribute to the overall improvement of teaching standards. Furthermore, we actively build communities of
middle- and high-level teachers to provide a platform for professional development and experience sharing so as to give full play to the
radiation effect of promotion. High- and middle-level teachers grow together in the community, share best practices in the application

6. Limitations and future prospects
Firstly, this study is cross-sectional data, which can objectively reflect the relationship between variables, but it can not provide a
vertical and developmental perspective. Therefore, longitudinal design should be considered in future research to better understand
the development context of teachers’ ICT capacity.
Secondly, this study only discusses the influence of teachers’ ICT capacity on classroom teaching quality and does not involve the
evolution of information technology. We recommend that future research include the vertical technological evolution process,
particularly in the context of the current era of artificial intelligence. This will allow for tracking the impact of the evolution of
teachers’ ICT capacity on the quality of classroom instruction.
Thirdly, while Shanghai, as the only region in mainland China consistently participating in the TALIS project, offers valuable data
for international comparisons, its highly developed economy and leading education system make it unique within both China and the
Asia-Pacific region. Therefore, caution is necessary when extrapolating these findings to other regions, particularly less advantaged
areas within China and the broader Asia-Pacific. Research has shown a significant “digital divide” within China, both in terms of ICT
infrastructure—such as disparities in network bandwidth and multimedia equipment between urban and rural schools—and at the
software and human capital levels(Guo & Wan, 2022). Teachers in disadvantaged areas often lack adequate, high-quality digital
literacy training and face greater challenges integrating technology into their teaching practices. Furthermore, substantial differences
exist in students’ access to digital resources, such as personal computers and stable internet, which directly impact the feasibility of
ICT-based teaching models. Given these regional disparities, the findings may represent an “upper bound” scenario in a relatively
resource-rich environment. Future research should use nationally representative, stratified sampling or comparative case studies
across multiple regions to test the applicability and variability of the proposed model under different socioeconomic and technological
conditions. This would provide a foundation for more differentiated and equitable national policies on educational informatization.
Finally, some studies have indicated that primary school science teachers possess significantly higher technical knowledge, situational knowledge of technical teaching content, and TPACK compared to math teachers (Nelson et al., 2019). However, our existing
research has not thoroughly explored how the ICT capacity of teachers across different disciplines impact classroom teaching quality.
Therefore, future analyses should incorporate teachers from various disciplines and examine how their ICT capacity influences
classroom teaching quality from the perspective of these different fields. In addition, this study found that while teachers’ self-efficacy
significantly mediated the relationship between ICT capacity and teaching quality, the effect size was small in practical terms. This
suggests that, although enhancing self-efficacy is a viable route to indirectly improve teaching quality, its impact is supplementary. The
primary mechanism driving teaching quality lies in the direct influence of teachers’ ICT capacity itself. As Preacher and Kelley (2011)
emphasized, effect size interpretations should be contextualized within the research setting, rather than relying on fixed benchmarks.
In educational practice, this finding highlights the importance of prioritizing resource allocation when designing professional
development programs aimed at improving teachers’ technological skills. Interventions focused solely on psychological reinforcement
to “boost confidence” may yield limited outcomes. More effective strategies should emphasize hands-on, skill-based training targeting
pathways with larger effect sizes, complemented by supportive environments that reinforce teachers’ self-efficacy. Future research
could explore other potential mediating or moderating variables to better understand the complex mechanisms through which ICT
capacity impacts instructional quality.
