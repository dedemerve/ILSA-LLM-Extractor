ABSTRACT

KEYWORDS

This paper uses data from PISA 2018 in Germany to explore how the students‘chronotype (known as larks and owls) and their academic achievement is related to truancy. We used an adapted version of the Munich
Chronotype Questionnaire (MCTQ) to measure the students’ chronotype.
The results demonstrate a correlation between the students’ social jet lag,
student achievement, and truancy. Finally, we discuss the potential inclusion of chronotypes as a measure in large-scale international student
assessments.

Introduction
In 2017, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to three researchers “for their
discoveries of molecular mechanisms controlling the circadian rhythm” (The Nobel Assembly at
Karolinska Institutet [Nobelf€
orsamlingen], 2017). Jeffrey C. Hall, Michael Rosbash, and Michael
W. Young shed light into the phenomenon that is widely known as our internal, biological clock
that helps us adapt to the regular rhythm of our daily lives. Early risers, often referred to as larks,
and late sleepers, also known as owls, differ in their preferences of how they would structure their
day according to their inner clock if they were completely free of societal conventions, institutions, and norms. This classification system distinguishing owls from larks is known as chronotypes (Roenneberg et al., 2003). According to the Nobel Assembly, the laureates showed that “our
inner clock adapts our physiology to the dramatically different phases of the day. The clock regulates critical functions such as behavior, hormone levels, sleep, body temperature, and metabolism. Our well-being is affected when there is a temporary mismatch between our external
environment and this internal biological clock, for example when we travel across several time
zones and experience “jet lag”.” (The Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet Nobelf€
orsamlingen,
2017). While it has long been well-known that humans have a biological clock, which helps our
body to prepare for the different phases of the day—usually referred to as circadian rhythm
(Latin: circa, approximately and dies, day)—it was only recently discovered that this circadian
rhythm is genetically regulated.
In contrast to this, the German school system as well as many others has been adapted to the
societal structure of the day with regard to working hours in institutions, firms and other workplaces. In the last third of the 19th century, the German school system increasingly proved to be
inadequate for current social and economic developments. So it was further developed and

modernized in line with the process of industrialization (van Ackeren et al., 2015). The published
literature scaffolds the finding that adolescents in high school suffer from a certain circadian misalignment, i.e. undersleeping on weekdays and oversleeping on weekends (D�ıaz-Morales &
Escribano, 2015). It is in that sense obvious that school starting quite early during the day
matches the circadian rhythm of “larks” better than “owls”. While we know that students who
tend to skip school achieve lower in assessments like the Programme for International Student
Assessment (PISA, S€alzer & Heine, 2016) little is known about how students who are physically
present at school perform due to their chronotype. How much potential do we lose by suboptimal
performance time windows during a school day that goes back to a mismatch of chronotype and
scheduled tasks at school? Students who struggle with such a mismatch are often called “socially
jetlagged” (Roenneberg et al., 2003; Wittmann et al., 2006), since their inner biological clock does
not match the social clock according to which school days begin. As especially high schools usually impose early schedules, adolescents are likely to suffer from permanent social jet lag even
more than children in primary school. This paper uses data from PISA 2018 in Germany, where
we had the unique opportunity to collect data on both the students’ chronotype, especially their
resulting social jet lag, and their self-reported truancy during the PISA test, which is mandatory
for all schools and students drawn into the sample. Our guiding question is to what extent truancy can be predicted by both student achievement and their social jet lag, beyond relevant control variables. Given that PISA is a standardized student assessment, the data at hand provide
both standardized proficiency measures and grades received at school. From an ecosystemic perspective (cf. Bronfenbrenner, 2009), students with different circadian preferences are confronted
with a school system and fixed daily schedules, so the resulting individual social jet lag can be
seen as an emergence from a disposition-environment interaction (e.g. Ahnert, 2014; Kray, 2019;
Sameroff, 2009). We use stepwise linear regression analyses as a method to elaborate an answer
to our question.

Theory and State of Research
Sufficient and restful sleep is a central factor for human health (Adan et al., 2006; Fabbian et al.,
2016), learning ability (Keller et al., 2015) and overall human development from childhood to
adolescence (Randler et al., 2017). In this regard, the study of human sleep preferences has generated a considerable body of literature and, in sum, suggests that there are individual differences
in the preferred timing of sleep and active wakefulness in humans (Adan et al., 2012; Aschoff,
1965; Horne & Ostberg, 1977; Kerkhof, 1985). Such individual differences constitute a circadian
typology affecting our biological and psychological functioning (e.g. Adan et al., 2006;
Roenneberg & Merrow, 2016; Roenneberg et al., 2003). The term chronotype refers to biologically
determined (Adan et al., 2012; Roenneberg & Merrow, 2002), interindividual differences in the
preferred time of sleep and active waking phases in humans (e.g. Roenneberg, 2012a, 2012b;
Roenneberg et al., 2003; Wittmann et al., 2006). However, social settings (e.g. school and job)
provide fixed daily schedules for sleep and wake phases that can interfere with such individual
rhythms (Touitou, 2013; Wittmann et al., 2006). As an overarching tendency, the socially prescribed schedules tend to prefer the so-called “larks” (morning types) to the “owls” (evening
types), which in turn, specifically in the current school system, leads to educational outcomes
such as students’ science grade point average and language grade point average being negatively
associated with eveningness (e.g. Preckel et al., 2013; Scherrer & Preckel, 2021). In particular
Wittmann et al. (2006) introduced the concept of “social jet lag” in connection with such interference between socially predetermined and individual, biologically determined, circadian rhythms
(see also Roenneberg, 2012a, 2012b).
Adolescent truancy has been repeatedly found to be related to student socio-economic status,
student achievement and, as a reason for missing school, to having overslept or being tired when

in school (Hochadel et al., 2014; Pasch et al., 2010; S€alzer & Heine, 2016). In our study, we take
into account that truancy may comprise both late arrival for school and missing of lessons up to
whole school days and is thus a construct with numerous degrees of prevalence (Reid, 2003;
Wilson et al., 2008). Although truancy has been shown to be prevalent across all social classes,
there often is a negative correlation of socio-economic status and truancy (Bos et al., 1992;
Vaughn et al., 2013). The same applies to student achievement, truants can be found across all
achievement levels, but there is a tendency to low achievement (Henry & Huizinga, 2007).
Students’ socio-economic status (SES) has been shown to play a central role in the associations
between sleep quality, health, fitness, and resulting cognitive performance. For example, Moore
et al. (2002) show that the effects of income on mental and physical health are mediated by sleep
quality, with sleep quantity being related to measures of health, but not directly related to SES.
Further, Buckhalt (2011) shows that children from low SES families are typically more likely to
sleep poorly, which has negative effects on their cognitive functioning as well as academic performance, resulting in an “achievement gap between groups of children differing in SES” (see
Buckhalt, 2011, p. 1).
In a recent paper, S€alzer and Heine (2016) could show that students’ performance in PISA is
further linked to their self-reported truancy. Skipping classes means a probably lower score in the
corresponding PISA test domain (S€alzer & Heine, 2016). What they could not show, however, is
how a more differentiated analyses of how skipped lessons (arriving late in the morning, skipping
any lesson or skipping several lessons up to whole schooldays) may be associated with the students’ chronotype and social jet lag (S€alzer & Heine, 2016) as one possible reason for missing
parts of lessons or school days. The concept of social jet lag provides a plausible and promising
framework to be policy-relevant in terms of scaffolding student learning processes (Roenneberg
et al., 2003; Wittmann et al., 2006). The more teachers and policy makers know about optimal
and suboptimal performance time slots during the day, the better the various school days can be
organized and adapted to the students’ needs (Escribano & D�ıaz-Morales, 2014; Roeser et al.,
2013). This does not necessarily mean a later school start in general, but may involve considerations of flexible periods in the beginning and the end of a school day.

Methods
Using data from the German PISA 2018 sample, we specify two stepwise multiple regression
models to predict students’ truancy by three blocks of predictor variables. One model is specified
for Gymnasium schools, the second for all other school types at the secondary level I and II.
School type is used as a grouping variable with regard to the German federal school system being
a two-tier system: all 16 federal states have Gymnasium schools, but they differ in terms of the
other secondary school types that exist alongside them. In some cases, there is just one additional
school type, in others two or three. Conceptually, these school forms differ from state to state,
which is why differentiation is not meaningful here. We base this approach on the established
reporting on PISA (Prenzel et al., 2013).
Stepwise Linear Regression Predicting Truancy
We build up our linear regression models in three steps. The variables used are described in
detail below. First, we introduce the students’ socio-economic background, second their social jet
lag as two unmodifiable student characteristics. In the third step, we include measures of student
achievement, represented by standardized proficiency scales (PISA reading score, cognitive ability
test) and the grades in German. All three blocks are well-established predictors in large-scale
assessments with the specificity that in our analyses we predict truancy and not student
achievement.

Data base
In PISA, student competencies in the domains of reading, mathematics and science are measured
using standardized tests (OECD, 2019a). To complement these cognitive indicators, background
questionnaires are used to capture context variables such as motivational orientations and student
attitudes as well as views of their parents and teachers (Kuger et al., 2017). PISA has been conducted every three years since the first round in 2000, each focusing on one of the three domains.
The 2018 PISA study covered reading literacy as the major domain for the third time. A total of
79 countries participated in PISA 2018, of which 37 were OECD countries (OECD, 2020a).
Worldwide, a total of around 600.000 students in nearly 22.000 schools participated. In Germany,
the sample comprised n ¼ 5451 students in 223 schools (Reiss et al., 2019). As a special in the
German administration of PISA 2018, a number of national extra questions on students’ circadian
rhythms have been added to the international standard questionnaire. These variables refer to
both individually preferred circadian rhythms and fixed circadian times by the school (see
Table 1) which we used to construct social jet lag.
Dependent Variable
Measures of Student Truancy
Student truancy has repeatedly been shown to be related to student achievement (Henry &
Huizinga, 2007; S€alzer et al., 2012; Vaughn et al., 2013). Furthermore, students who tend to play
truant also have a tendency to skip items in questionnaires that are meant to capture truant
behavior (S€alzer & Heine, 2016). The PISA student questionnaire collects information on the students’ truant behavior as follows: In the past two full school weeks, how often did you … skip a
whole day of school? … skip single lessons? … arrive late for school? These three items are
merged to represent truant student behavior in a formative index. Accordingly, the truancy index
is the sum score of these items. Taking into account the stratified sampling structure of the data,
as well as all ten plausible values from the PISA reading domain, all data analyses have been carried out by means of the R package BIFIEsurvey (Robitzsch & Oberwimmer, 2022) for the free
statistical environment R (R Core Team, 2023).

Social jet lag
The role of the students’ chronotype in our study is best operationalized by their social jet lag.
Social jet lag is constructed as follows: First, a total of 29 questions has been used to capture the
students’ individual circadian rhythms (Heine, 2022). The single items assessing general circadian
rhythms and specifically the variables derived from it, such as sleep habits on school days versus
non-school days (weekends), are based on a version of the “Munich ChronoType Questionnaire”
(MCTQ, 2003b) adapted for PISA (see Heine, 2022). From the time variables thus obtained,
derived variables are calculated which are needed for the analysis of the circadian typology (cf.
Roenneberg et al., 2004; Roenneberg et al., 2003). Central to the present analyses are first the two
derived variables sleep midpoint on free and school days respectively. Among previous studies of
circadian typology, sleep midpoint has been shown to be a favorable sleep phase reference point
for melatonin release and is therefore useful for identifying different chronotypes (e.g., (Benoit
et al., 1981; Roenneberg & Merrow, 2016; Roenneberg et al., 2003; Terman et al., 2001). Midsleep time is calculated using sleep duration, which is defined for free and school days as simple
time differences between the wake-up time and the start (ONSET) of sleep (see Table 1). The
times for the middle of sleep on free and school days are calculated by adding half the sleep duration to the time of the respective sleep start.
According to Roenneberg et al. (2004) the central reference point is the midpoint of sleep on
free days, assuming that this represents a (natural) sleep habit without the disruptive influence of
social commitments during school hours. In the last step, a derived measure for social jet lag is
calculated for the present study from the two sleep mid times.
Thus, social jet lag (SJL) operationalized according to Wittmann et al. (2006) is defined as the
(absolute) difference between mid sleep time on days off “mid sleep free” (MSF) and school days
‘mid sleep school’ (MSS):
CHRONOSJL ¼ j MSF – MSS j
Behind this lies the assumption that when individuals are free to decide when to get up in the
morning, their rhythm of getting up and going to bed matches their chronotype, while on school
or work days, they have to adjust to the socially defined schedules (Heine, 2022; Roenneberg
et al., 2004). For the interpretation of the findings, it is therefore important to keep in mind that
the more positive (i. e. bigger) the numerical values on the CHRONOSJL variable are, the more
severe is the social jet lag.
PISA Reading literacy
In PISA 2018, around half of the test items comprised the students’ reading literacy, while the
domains of mathematics and science were covered by around 25 percent of the items each
(OECD, 2019a, 2020b). Based on the Item Response Theory, students were assigned an estimated
score combining their reading ability and the item difficulty of the reading items (Heine & Reiss,
2019).

Students’ German Grade
Within the sampled schools, one teacher was designated as the school coordinator. This person
was asked to provide a range of information for the selected students, including their German
grade. The grading scales at different schools and in different federal states in Germany have a
different grading system. As a target reference, we used the most common grading system (1–6)
in German-speaking school systems and skipping any intermediate levels by integer rounding to
a uniform 6 point (ordinal) grade scale. In this scheme, 1 is the best grade (“very good”), while 6
is the lowest (“fail”).
Students’ Figural Intelligence
In order to address a regular point of criticism toward large-scale assessments in education (the
one-dimensional conceptualization of the domain-specific abilities, cf. Brunner, 2008), a measure
of the students’ cognitive abilities was included in PISA 2018 in Germany. According to Brunner
(2008), the relationship between domain-specific competencies and individual student characteristics changes depending on the underlying model of the cognitive abilities. Therefore, in order to
complement the domain-specific assessment of cognitive competencies, the German subsample
was administered the so-called Berliner Test zur Erfassung fluider und kristalliner Intelligenz (Engl.
Berlin test capturing fluid and crystalline intelligence, BEFKI 8–10; Wilhelm et al., 2014).
Specifically, against the backdrop of limited time resources in the student background questionnaire, the assessment of intelligence is limited to the BEFKI sub-scale “Figurale Intelligenz” (Engl.
figural intelligence), which has been proven to capture the general factor of intelligence quite well,
independent from knowledge and social or cultural specificities (Wilhelm et al., 2014).
Two-Group Strategy for Data Analysis according to School Type
We conducted a two-group analysis splitting up our student sample according to their school
type. With regard to the federal structure of the German school system, we find one secondary
school type prevalent in all 16 federal states (Gymnasium) and one to three secondary school
types besides the Gymnasium. Taking into account the diversity of this non-Gymnasium school
types and in order not to fragment the sample, we put these school types together as one group
called non-Gymnasium schools. This group includes schools that use within-school streaming,
but also between-school tracking (Chmielewski et al., 2013). As PISA data regularly show, the
variance of student achievement is distributed so that for Gymnasiums, only a very small amount
of the variance lies between schools (e. g. PISA 2015: 2%, see S€alzer et al., 2016), whereas for the
non-Gymnasium schools, around 21% of the variance lies between schools. All in all, the variance
partition between schools in PISA 2015 was at 44% in Germany (S€alzer et al., 2016).

Regression analysis Predicting Student Truancy
The PISA 2018 student sample in Germany has a mean truancy score of 4.33 points (SE .04).
The three items included in the truancy index on tardiness, truancy of individual lessons, and
truancy of entire school days were each coded with the values 1 (never) to 4 (five times and
more frequently). This results in a range of values from 3 (never) to 12 (five times and more frequently) on the index. The mean value thus tends to be low and indicates that there is a prevalence greater than zero in one of the three items on average.
Table 3 represents the results of the stepwise linear regression analyses predicting truancy by
indicators of socio-economic background, social jet lag and achievement. It differentiates two
groups of school types, a distinction relevant for the German school system as well as for other
multi-tier school systems. Students in Germany are to a very wide extent sorted into different secondary school tracks as early as at age 10, after four years of elementary schooling due to their
prior achievement (Chmielewski et al., 2013). The variance in student achievement between
schools is about as high as the overall variance within schools (see, for example, S€alzer et al.,
2016). Notably, Gymnasium schools have very low between-school variance (S€alzer et al., 2016),
while the overall within-school variance can be as high as 47 percent (e.g. S€alzer et al., 2016). For
both groups of schools, the Gymnasiums and the non-Gymnasiums, the students’ socio-economic
background is weakly correlated with truancy. The amount of correlation is low, but this is consistent with previous findings (S€alzer, 2010; Vaughn et al., 2013; Veenstra et al., 2010). In the
second step, a difference between the two groups of school emerges when the students’ social jet
lag is added as a predictor: While in non-Gymnasium schools, both the socioeconomic background and the social jet lag are related to truancy, we find that this is only the case for social jet
lag in Gymnasium schools. In view of the permanently pronounced social gradient in Germany
(OECD, 2019b; Stanat et al., 2022), these results have to be interpreted knowing that the variance
in ESCS in Gymnasium schools is considerably lower than in the other group of schools: This is
due to the fact that prior achievement is decisive for the school type the students are assigned to
after primary school and since achievement is indicated in the grades received in primary school,
ESCS and prior achievement are confounded (Baumert et al., 2006; Baumert et al., 2003; Dumont
et al., 2014; Maaz et al., 2010; Unger et al., 2020). In both groups of schools, Gymnasium and
non-Gymnasium, the students’ social jet lag predicts truancy. This correlation is not surprising,
as it lies in the nature of the social jet lag construct: When school or work starts so early that the
individual chronotype is incompatible with it, missing school or work hours either through tardiness or avoidance are an effect of the lack of fit or also a coping strategy.

pattern for both groups of schools, Gymnasium and non-Gymnasium: Two of the predictors are
significantly associated with truancy, social jet lag and the PISA Reading score. In both cases, the

Discussion
This paper focused on the question to what extent adolescent truancy is predicted by the students’ social jet lag, their socio-economic background and achievement. We used stepwise linear
regression analyses, predicting the students’ truancy in three models. All three concepts, socioeconomic background, social jet lag and achievement resulted as significant predictors of truancy.
In line with the current state of research, the key findings are that the students’ socio-economic
background plays a predictive role in the heterogeneous non-Gymnasium schools, as long as no
other student characteristics are included in the regression analysis. As stated in the results section, the variance of the students’ social background is known to be reduced in Gymnasium
schools due to the selection processes into this school type, which in Germany is closely related
to the students’ socio-economic background (Dumont et al., 2014; Kang & Cogan, 2022; Reiss
et al., 2019). In addition, truancy has repeatedly been shown to be prevalent across all socio-economic strata in society (Dunkake, 2009; S€alzer et al., 2012; Vaughn et al., 2013). Thus, our findings confirm the expectations stated in the theoretical part of this article on the role of student
socio-economic background.
The predictor variable of central interest to our study, social jet lag, is the second characteristic
that significantly predicts the extent to which adolescents miss school in both groups of school
type, Gymnasium and non-Gymnasium. This indicates that school start collides with the typical
individual ideal wake-up time of adolescents in Germany and probably most other educational
systems, since the distribution of chronotypes across the life-span has been found to be a crosscultural phenomenon and adolescents tend to be owls rather than larks (Escribano & D�ıazMorales, 2014; Roenneberg et al., 2003). According to previous findings, student achievement is
significantly correlated with truancy. However, we find that only the PISA Reading score and not
the cognitive ability (BEFKI) or the grades in German predict the students‘truancy in our analyses. This is surprising, since in earlier studies, school grades have been shown to be negatively
correlated with truancy (Bos et al., 1992; Henry & Huizinga, 2007; S€alzer et al., 2012; Vaughn
et al., 2013). One plausible explanation may be that we limited the grades to the grade in
German since it matches Reading as the major domain in PISA 2018.
Given that student truancy has been shown to be differentially associated with achievement in
the PISA test (S€alzer & Heine, 2016) across school types, this finding is consistent with earlier
studies indicating that students attending a Gymnasium have a weaker association of low achievement and truancy. This may be because they are better able to balance the dose of truancy that is
on the one hand just sufficient not to impair their performance and on the other hand is likely
to reduce the extent of social jet lag by means of such a self-selected later start of school. In this
sense, students’ truancy behavior can be viewed as a desperate attempt, within the narrow confines of socially predetermined routines, to mitigate the negative effects of an unfavorable fit
between their individual circadian preferences and predetermined social schedules.
Interestingly, since in contrast to the state of research, our results do not show the expected
significant association of social jet lag and the indicator of student general cognitive performance
(BEFKI-score) in our regression analyses (see Table 3) apart from the scores for academic
achievement – the PISA reading score (Preckel et al., 2011; Roeser et al., 2013; Scherrer &
Preckel, 2021). However, on the basis of the pure bivariate correlations (cf. Table 2), a consistent
picture emerges, for example, with the findings from the meta-analysis by Preckel et al. (2011).
While Preckel et al. (2011) quantify the correlation between individuals’ eveningness and

cognitive ability with r ¼ .08, our analyses show a comparable bivariate correlation between social
jet lag and the measure of fluid intelligence of r ¼ −.1. The different sign is here due to the fact
that eveningness is equivalent with a higher social jet lag. Similarly, the negative relationship
(r ¼ −.14; Preckel et al., 2011) between indicators of academic achievement and eveningnes found
by Preckel et al. (2011) is more or less replicated in our analyses. We find a bivariate correlation
of r ¼ .09 between social jet lag and school grades (if higher in value then lower in meaning) in
the subject German. Although somehow different in amount, the findings from our analyses are
thus consistent in terms of the pure pattern of the associations found with the findings of Preckel
et al. according to which eveningness or higher social jet lag is generally associated with lower
academic performance and, albeit if slightly positively asotiated, with intelligence.

International Comparative Perspectives
With regard to an international comparison, our study offers a number of connecting points.
Germany has a multi-tier school system that sorts its students as early as at age 10 into different
secondary school types, according to their prior achievement (van Ophuysen, 2009). Student truancy and its association to their socio-economic status, performance in the PISA Reading test
and social jet lag varies across school types (S€alzer & Heine, 2016), so it is worth a look how this
association reveals in single-tier school systems or systems that sort their students at a later point
than Germany.
Overall, chronotypes are a general phenomenon and can be universally found. Social jet lag as
a consequence resulting from a mismatch of eveningness and system-related early institutional
schedules is therefore to be seen as a typical student characteristic in adolescence (Roenneberg,
2012a, 2012b). According to our findings, international large-scale student assessments may offer
a sound data base for identifying potential effects of the chronotype like we found them for
Germany in this study. Settings like PISA or similar assessments provide a well-established environment for studying differences in such relationships across countries and educational systems.
Strengths And Limitations
Our findings advance research on adolescent truancy as well as the effects of individual circadian
preferences in several respects.
First, our analyses were based on a large sample (n ¼ 5451 students) that was representative of
the German population of 15-year-old students. Second, the data stem from a mandatory international large-scale assessment using standardized tests to capture student achievement as well as
well-established contextual questionnaires as instruments. Using a national addition to the student
questionnaire to assess individual circadian preferences, a so far unique data set containing information on students’ social jet lag was obtained. Third, we investigated the interplay of some
highly relevant variables known to be associated to student achievement (ESCS, social jet lag and
truancy). So far, most previous studies have focused on the combinations of truancy and achievement or social jet lag and achievement (D�ıaz-Morales & Escribano, 2015), but not the interplay
of all three. To our knowledge, this is the first study to combine all three concepts.
However, a number of limitations warrant discussion. First, our results showed that the proportion of explained variance in our regression models were rather low. This small proportion of
explained variance can be explained relatively easily by the fact that essentially all students in our
sample were affected by social jet lag (albeit to varying degrees).
On the other hand, our models use an intentionally and theory-based limited set of variables
according to prior research on student achievement and truancy, so we cannot rule out that there
are other aspects of students’ cognitive abilities that should be included in further analyses (omitted variable bias, see Wilms et al., 2021).

Second, the design and data base of the PISA study does not allow causal analyses
(Goldstein, 2017). Hence, the regression analyses are to be read as correlational rather than
directional. This recommendation is based on two issues. On the one hand, we cannot rule
out the possibility that any external confounding variables that were not controlled in our
analysis may have influenced the results presented in this article. Especially reverse causality
is a common problem in cross-sectional studies like this one. On the other hand, an extensive
body of research showed that the biological clock in humans is hardly modifiable (Adan
et al., 2012; Roenneberg & Merrow, 2002). Hence, we included social jet lag as a predictor
variable alongside socio-economic background and achievement to see how they are interrelated with truancy.
Third, the data we used exclusively rely on student self-reports and test results. With regard to
truancy as a norm-violating behavior subject to social desirability (Grimm, 2010; Nederhof,
1985), we are facing the dilemma that on the one hand, only students themselves know when
they were truant and on the other hand, they might be hesitant to respond truthfully to the corresponding items in a questionnaire. So, with regard to the reliability of data on truancy, sources
might usefully be complementedaby other approaches such as external observers or teacher
reports.
Fourth, more studies are needed before our results can be generalized. For one, our
results apply specifically to 15-year-old students in Germany. Previous research has shown
major age effects on social jet lag, which evolves and varies over the lifespan (Preckel
et al., 2011; Roenneberg et al., 2004; Roenneberg et al., 2003; Roeser et al., 2013). Similar
observations have been made for truancy, it increases with age until the end of compulsory schooling (Vaughn et al., 2013; Veenstra et al., 2010). Future studies should therefore
analyze the link between student truancy, achievement and social jet lag across a wider
age range. It is also possible that cultural characteristics and differences affect the results
of such studies. Cross-cultural studies are therefore necessary to test the generalizability of
the correlations found in our study.
To conclude, this article revealed critical associations between adolescent truancy, student
achievement, socio-economic background and social jet lag in school. Most importantly, when we
know students tend to skip school, we have reason to assume that they might suffer from social
jet lag. In order to eliminate this social jet lag completely, the start of school would on average
ideally have to be shifted later by a remarkable amount of 2 h and 49 min, according to our findings for the German PISA students. Although this is not quite realistic in many modern societies,
a smaller degree of shifting the start of school or flexible periods at the beginning and the end of
a school day may be considered.
The request for taking into account the biological clock of students in sum, the call for greater
consideration of students’ biological clocks in educational planning and instructional organization
is a recurring one. However, these aspects can only be implemented to a very limited extent as
individual measures in social contexts. Therefore, the topic of everyday organization in societies
would have to be thought about and discussed more comprehensively, for which this article provided another argument.
