A B S T R A C T
Technology integration is an important aspect of modern classrooms. However, research is scarce that empiri­
cally addresses the question of how technology can be integrated into classroom settings in ways that support 
high-quality teaching. This study uses lesson plans of student teachers to examine how the integration of digital 
technologies into teaching processes aligns with theoretical principles of technology integration and dimensions 
of instructional quality. We analysed 130 anonymized lesson plans from teacher training seminars in Germany, 
identifying 568 instances of technology integration. Each instance was coded using two frameworks: the SAMR 
model (Substitution, Augmentation, Modification, Redefinition) and dimensions of instructional quality 
(cognitive activation, student support, classroom management). Most technology integrations remained at lower 
SAMR levels, such as substitution or minor enhancement. Higher SAMR levels were more likely to be associated 
with dimensions of instructional quality, particularly student support. By combining different theoretical per­
spectives, this study provides knowledge on how student teachers integrate technology into their lessons. The 
findings call for more systematic support for meaningful and theoretically grounded planning practices in teacher 
training.
Systematic lesson planning is a core component of teachers' profes­
sional competence and is associated with dimensions of instructional 
quality (K¨onig et al., 2020; Kunter et al., 2013; Stürmer et al., 2015). 
Lesson plans offer a structured representation of planned instruction, 
making visible how student teachers translate pedagogical intentions 
into instructional design (Backfisch et al., 2020; K¨onig et al., 2024; Liu, 
2016). In modern classrooms, an important question is how student 
teachers conceptualise and plan meaningful technology integration into 
their lesson plans (Henning et al., 2006; Tondeur et al., 2012).
An established model that describes the functional level of technol­
ogy integration into classrooms is the Substitution Augmentation 
Modification Redefinition (SAMR) model (Puentedura, 2006). The 
model distinguishes between four hierarchically ordered lev­
els—substitution, augmentation, modification, and redefinition. The 
model is highly valuable, as it describes different levels of technology 
integration in detail. However, the model does not consider whether and 
how these levels of integration contribute to effective and good in­
struction in class, here indicated by levels of instructional quality. While 
the SAMR model is often described as a hierarchical progression from 
substitution to redefinition (Puentedura, 2006), this hierarchy does not 
necessarily correspond to pedagogical relevance or instructional quality. 
Higher levels of technological transformation do not automatically 
imply more effective or meaningful teaching. Therefore, in the present 
study, the SAMR model is applied as a categorical rather than evaluative 
framework, distinguishing functional types of technology use without 
assuming a linear improvement in instructional quality. This analytical 
perspective allows for a more nuanced interpretation of technology 
integration that focuses on pedagogical purpose rather than hierarchical 
advancement.
Instructional quality here is defined in terms of three dimen­
sions—emotional support, classroom organisation/management, and 
instructional support, which is also referred to as cognitive activation 
(Hamre & Pianta, 2010; Klieme et al., 2006; Praetorius et al., 2018). 
Research on dimensions of instructional quality only rarely explicitly 
addresses the role of digital technologies in teaching (Alp Christ et al., 
2024; Consoli et al., 2024). However, recent conceptual work (e.g., 
Lachner et al., 2024), emphasises that high-quality technology integra­
tion consists of both technology exploitation and dimensions of 
instructional quality. This dual focus is particularly relevant in the 
context of teacher education, where technology integration should be

1. Technology integration: theoretical perspectives
Technology integration in educational contexts is broadly under­
stood as the intentional planning and employment of digital elements to 
support instructional processes and align with pedagogical intentions 
(Lachner et al., 2024). However, the concept remains inconsistently 
defined and is frequently reduced to the mere presence or frequency of 
technology use in classrooms (Scherer et al., 2019; Voogt et al., 2013). 
This narrow interpretation risks overlooking how technology is inte­
grated into teaching and whether it contributes meaningfully to di­
mensions of instructional quality. To address this limitation, Lachner 
et al. (2024) proposed the Teachers' Professional Competence for 
Technology 
Integration 
(TPTI) 
model, 
which 
conceptualises 
high-quality technology integration through two interrelated compo­
nents: technology exploitation and instructional quality. This model 
underscores that effective integration requires not only technical 
implementation, but also alignment with and theoretical con­
ceptualisations of dimensions of instructional quality (Lachner et al., 
2024). In the model, technology exploitation refers to the extent to 
which technology integration transforms instructional practices 
(Lachner et al., 2024; Puentedura, 2006). A widely used framework for 
studying technology exploitation is the SAMR model (Puentedura, 
2006). The SAMR model distinguishes between four hierarchically or­
dered levels of technology exploitation: at the substitution level, tech­
nology merely replaces a traditional tool without functional change (e. 
g., using a digital worksheet instead of a printed one). Augmentation 
introduces functional improvement while maintaining the same 
instructional task (e.g., adding interactive feedback to the worksheet). 
At the modification level, technology allows for significant redesign of 
learning activities (e.g., using collaborative online platforms to revise 
student work in real time). Redefinition involves creating entirely new 
tasks that would not be possible without technology (e.g., students 
producing multimedia presentations and sharing them with external 
audiences).
While several theoretical frameworks describe technology integra­
tion in educational contexts, such as the TPACK framework (Koehler & 
Mishra, 2009) and the PICRAT model (Kimmons et al., 2020), the pre­
sent study focuses on the SAMR model (Puentedura, 2006). The SAMR 
model was selected because it provides a functional and observable 
description of how technology is used within instructional activities, 
making it particularly suitable for analysing lesson plans. In contrast to 
frameworks such as TPACK, which emphasise teachers' technological, 
pedagogical, and content knowledge, or PICRAT, which focuses on 
teachers' and students' interactions with technology, SAMR directly 
captures the pedagogical purpose of technology exploitation in planned 
instruction. This focus aligns with the aims of the current study, which 
examines how pre-service teachers integrate technology as part of their 
instructional design rather than their underlying knowledge or beliefs. 
While the SAMR model offers a helpful taxonomy for the technology 
exploitation, it focuses solely on technological transformation and does 
not provide criteria for evaluating pedagogical relevance or dimensions 
of instructional quality (K¨onig et al., 2024).
Instructional quality remains a foundational principle of effective 
teaching, regardless of whether technology is present (Consoli et al., 
2024). The TBD framework (Klieme et al., 2006; Praetorius et al., 2025) 
provides a well-established model for describing dimensions of 
instructional quality based on three dimensions: cognitive activation, 
student support, and classroom management. Cognitive activation refers 
to instructional strategies that stimulate students' higher-order thinking 
and conceptual understanding (e.g., through problem-solving tasks or 
open-ended questions) (Baumert et al., 2010). Student support captures 
the provision of emotional, motivational, and individual assistance to 
foster a positive learning climate (Wentzel & Skinner, 2022). Classroom 
management refers to the organisation of time, space, and behaviour to 
maintain a productive learning environment and minimise disruptions 
(Praetorius et al., 2018). Conceptually similarly, the internationally 
established Classroom Assessment Scoring System (CLASS) framework 
(Pianta & Hamre, 2009) conceptualises dimensions of instructional 
quality through the dimensions of emotional support, classroom orga­
nisation, and instructional support. Empirical studies have shown that 
these dimensions are closely linked to students' academic achievement 
and engagement (Allen et al., 2011, 2013). Despite differences in ter­
minology, both frameworks emphasise that effective teaching involves 
structured, emotionally supportive, and cognitively stimulating learning 
environments. Although originally developed outside digital contexts, 
both the TBD (Klieme et al., 2006; Kunter et al., 2007) and CLASS 
(Pianta & Hamre, 2009) frameworks have been applied in studies on 
technology-enhanced teaching (e.g., Allen et al., 2013; Consoli et al., 
2024), underlining their relevance for evaluating pedagogical aspects of 
planned technology integration.
Combining the SAMR model and theoretical conceptualisations of 
dimensions of instructional quality allows for a more comprehensive 
analysis of planned technology integration. While SAMR enables cate­
gorisation of the technology exploitation, frameworks regarding di­
mensions of instructional quality provide criteria for evaluating 
instructional quality aspects. To visually summarize the interrelations 
between these theoretical frameworks, Fig. 1 provides an overview of 
how the TPTI model (Lachner et al., 2024), the SAMR model 
(Puentedura, 2006), and the dimensions of instructional quality (Klieme 
et al., 2006; Praetorius et al., 2025) are conceptually linked within the 
analytical framework of this study.
1.1. Technology integration: empirical perspectives
Empirical research has documented a continuous increase in the 
integration of digital technologies in education over the past decade. In 
Germany, the International Computer and Information Literacy Study 
(ICILS) 2023 shows that 70 % of teachers now report that they integrate 
technology on a daily basis in their classrooms, compared to only 23.2 % 
in 2018 and 9.1 % in 2013 (Bos et al., 2014; Eickelmann et al., 2020, 
2024). Despite this trend, the pedagogical quality of technology inte­
gration remains insufficiently explored. The few existing studies indi­
cate that digital technologies are often integrated for teacher-centred 
purposes such as content presentation or administrative support, while 
more student-centred or cognitively activating integrations are less 
common (Eickelmann et al., 2024; Francom, 2020; Hamilton et al., 
2016; K¨onig et al., 2024).
Findings from scoping reviews confirm these patterns and show that 
technology integrations are present in most lesson plans, but it is often 
unclear how this integration supports dimensions of instructional 
quality and whether technology exploitation is systematically planned 
for this purpose (K¨onig et al., 2024). Findings from classroom research 
show that when technology integration is present, it is frequently 
disconnected from broader instructional goals or quality dimensions 
(Heitink et al., 2016). Other studies based on lesson planning or 
self-reports suggest similar patterns of limited pedagogical alignment

(Farjon et al., 2019; Schmid et al., 2021). However, technology inte­
gration can only contribute to student learning if it is pedagogically 
grounded. Integrating technologies at higher levels of the SAMR model 
(i.e., Modification and Redefinition) yields the potential for instruc­
tional transformation of lessons, but, without alignment to pedagogical 
principles, such integrations do not necessarily lead to improved 
learning outcomes (Consoli et al., 2024; Hamilton et al., 2016).
The TPTI model combines both conceptual approaches and defines 
high-quality technology integration as the intersection of technology 
exploitation and dimensions of instructional quality (Lachner et al., 
2024). Although the TPTI model (Lachner et al., 2024) has not been 
explicitly used in recent empirical studies, findings by Consoli et al. 
(2023, 2024) provide support for its assumptions. Their results show 
that when technology is used in ways that reflect dimensions of 
instructional quality—such as promoting cognitive activation or offer­
ing individualised student support—and leverages the functional po­
tential of digital technology (i.e., technology exploitation), it is 
associated with increased student behavioural engagement and the 
development of digital competencies. These findings illustrate how both 
components emphasised in the TPTI model (Lachner et al., 2024) 
contribute to effective technology integration.
Despite the existing conceptual background, empirical work has 
rarely tested when and under which conditions (planned) technology 
integration aligns with dimensions of instructional quality. The present 
study addresses this gap by analysing the levels of technology exploi­
tation and dimensions of instructional quality of technology integration 
using student teachers' lesson plans against the theoretical background 
of the SAMR model (Puentedura, 2006), frameworks of dimensions of 
instructional quality (Klieme et al., 2006; Kunter et al., 2007; Pianta & 
Hamre, 2009), and the TPTI model (Lachner et al., 2024).
1.2. Lesson planning and technology integration
Lesson planning is a central aspect of pedagogical reasoning and a 
key component of professional competence for teachers (K¨onig et al., 
2021). Lesson planning involves anticipating learning goals, organising 
content, and selecting instructional strategies aligned with students' 
needs (K¨onig et al., 2021). In this context, technology integration is not 
an add-on, but a deliberate design decision that shapes the structure of 
learning scenarios (Backfisch et al., 2020; Heitink et al., 2016).
Only few studies have examined how student teachers plan tech­
nology integration. Existing research often focuses on general digital 
competencies or attitudes rather than on the pedagogical rationale 
behind technology integration (Henning et al., 2006; Tondeur et al., 
2012). Lesson plans, however, offer a structured and authentic repre­
sentation of instructional planning and are therefore particularly well 
suited to investigate how student teachers conceptualise technology 
integration in relation to teaching goals and dimensions of instructional 
quality (Cevikbas et al., 2024; Sahin-Taskin, 2017). As K¨onig et al. 
(2020, 2024) demonstrate, written lesson plans allow for the recon­
struction of pedagogical reasoning and situation-specific planning de­
cisions, making them a valuable data source for analysing professional 
competence in teacher education. Although only few studies explicitly 
focus on technology integration in lesson planning, existing findings 
suggest that student teachers often integrate technology for surface-level 
purposes such as content presentation, with limited connection to di­
mensions of instructional quality (K¨onig et al., 2024; Schmid et al., 
2021). These findings, while not always centred on lesson planning, 
highlight the need to better understand how student teachers plan 
technology integration and whether it reflects deeper pedagogical 
reasoning.
At the same time, some studies indicate that the planning process 
itself can support the development of professional reasoning. This term 
refers to the ability of (student) teachers to make informed, theory-based 
instructional decisions that are sensitive to the specific teaching context 
(Heitink et al., 2016; K¨onig et al., 2021). Findings show that student 
teachers with higher levels of professional knowledge produce more 
coherent and instructionally grounded lesson plans (Backfisch et al., 
2020). Other research suggests that guided lesson planning tasks during 
teacher training can foster more meaningful technology integration 
(Henning et al., 2006; Liu, 2016).
Despite growing interest in technology integration, little is known 
about how student teachers plan for the pedagogically meaningful 
integration of digital technology in their lesson plans. Frameworks such 
as the SAMR model (Puentedura, 2006) and theoretical conceptualisa­
tions of dimensions of instructional quality (Klieme et al., 2006; Pianta 
& Hamre, 2009; Praetorius et al., 2025) allow for a differentiated 
analysis of planned technology integration. While the SAMR model 
provides a structure to classify the technology exploitation (Puentedura, 
2006), the theoretical conceptualisations of dimensions of instructional 
quality (Klieme et al., 2006; Kunter et al., 2007; Pianta & Hamre, 2009) 
offer established indicators for evaluating instructional quality (Klieme 
et al., 2006; Praetorius et al., 2018). Combined, these frameworks sup­
port a systematic examination of both the technical and pedagogical 
dimensions of technology integration in the lesson planning of student 
teachers (Consoli et al., 2023; Kramer et al., 2019). This integrated 
approach addresses a key limitation of previous work by linking func­
tional and instructional aspects of lesson planning (Backfisch et al.,

2. The present study
To explore how student teachers conceptualise and plan meaningful 
technology integration in the context of lesson planning, the present 
study examines how student teachers plan to integrate technologies into 
their lessons referring to the SAMR model and dimensions of instruc­
tional quality. Specifically, we analyse student teachers' lesson plans 
with the objective of identifying the level of technology exploitation 
using the SAMR model (Puentedura, 2006). Since the SAMR model fo­
cuses on the functional transformation of teaching through technology 
but does not consider theoretical conceptualisations of dimensions of 
instructional quality, we additionally apply dimensions of instructional 
quality (Klieme et al., 2006; Pianta & Hamre, 2009; Praetorius et al., 
2025) to examine whether the planned technology integrations support 
cognitive activation, student support, and classroom management. 
Specifically, we investigate the following research questions. 
RQ1: Which levels of the SAMR model are addressed by the planned 
technology integration of student teachers, visible in their lesson 
plans?
RQ2: To what extent do student teachers' technology integrations in 
their lesson plans reflect dimensions of instructional quality?
RQ3: To what extent do technology integrations based on the SAMR 
model relate to the dimensions of instructional quality?
3. Method
3.1. Participants and lesson plan scenario
To explore how digital technologies are integrated into planned 
teaching, lesson plans of 130 student teachers were analysed in this 
study. These lesson plans were developed as part of undergraduate 
teacher training seminars in the field of educational science, designed as 
a theory-practice course. The seminars focused on fostering motivation- 
enhancing teaching and also addressed the topic of technology inte­
gration and instructional quality. The seminars consisted of weekly 
sessions spread out over the course of a semester. The data used in the 
present study stemmed from different cohorts of student teachers who 
attended the courses in different terms within the time period 
2022–2024.
After the thematic input of the first few weeks, student teachers were 
tasked with developing a lesson aligned with the seminar topic. As part 
of this process, they were required to integrate technology into their 
planned teaching and consider theoretical conceptualisations of di­
mensions of instructional quality. The resulting lesson plans formed the 
corpus for this study.
Before compiling the data corpus, all lesson plans were anonymized 
by removing or redacting any details that could allow for the identifi­
cation of individual student teachers. This procedure was coordinated 
with the local data protection officer to ensure compliance with relevant 
regulations. Only after this step were the analyses conducted. As a result, 
no statements can be made regarding the student teachers' gender or age 
distribution.
To finalise preparation of the data corpus, instances of technology 
integration in each of the 130 lesson plans were identified, resulting in a 
total of N = 568 technology integrations. Most lesson plans were 
developed for grades 5 through 13. A total of 63 % (n = 82) of the lesson 
plans were developed for STEM subjects.
Although the lesson plans covered a wide range of grade levels 
(grades 5–13), age-related aspects were not explicitly considered in the 
coding process. This decision was based on the assumption that the 
dimensions of instructional quality (Klieme et al., 2006; Pianta & 
Hamre, 2009; Praetorius et al., 2018) and the SAMR model (Puentedura, 
2006) describe overarching pedagogical and technological principles of 
instruction that apply across educational levels. Since the coding 
focused on the pedagogical function of each technology integration 
rather than on student characteristics, age differences were not included 
as an analytical dimension.
The distribution of technology integrations varied widely, ranging 
from a minimum of 1 to a maximum of 14 technology integrations per 
lesson plan (M = 4.37, SD = 2.57). To analyse technology integrations 
with respect to technology exploitation and instructional quality in the 
lesson plans, we conducted a systematic qualitative coding of all iden­
tified instances.
3.1.1. Coding process
Building on the prepared corpus, we proceeded to analyse the 
identified instances of technology integration on two analytical di­
mensions: a) technology exploitation and
b) dimensions of instructional quality. To ensure the reliability and 
validity of our analysis, we followed the coding scheme development 
process outlined by Hugener et al., (2006). Based on this process, we 
constructed a preliminary coding scheme. To validate the preliminary 
coding scheme, three coders independently conducted trial coding on a 
randomly selected subset comprising 10 % of the identified technology 
integrations (n = 57). Interrater reliability indicates a high level of 
agreement among all raters with Fleiss' κ and Krippendorff's α = .88. 
Pairwise Cohen's κ ranged from 0.82 (Coders 1 and 2; Coders 2 and 3) to 
1.00 (Coders 1 and 3), and the average pairwise agreement was 93 %. 
These results reflect strong interrater consistency and are comparable to 
recommended thresholds for reliable qualitative coding (McHugh, 
2012).
Subsequently, the same three coders independently coded a 
randomly selected subset comprising additional 10 % (cumulative 20 %) 
of the identified technology integrations (N = 568). of the identified 
technology integrations (n = 568). Interrater reliability remained high 
with Fleiss' κ and Krippendorff's α = .85. Pairwise Cohen's κ ranged from 
0.80 (Coders 2 and 3) to 0.91 (Coders 1 and 3), and the average pairwise 
agreement across all coders was 92 %. These results further support the 
consistency of the coding scheme and indicate its applicability for sub­
sequent analyses across the complete corpus of lesson plans. This iter­
ative process ultimately resulted in a finalised coding scheme (see 
Tables A1 and A2 in the Appendix).
Finally, the remaining 80 % of the identified technology integrations 
(n = 454) were coded by one coder using the finalised coding scheme. 
This step ensured the complete coverage of the dataset while main­
taining consistency through the application of the validated coding 
scheme. The final coding scheme consisted of two components; each 
aligned with one of the analytical dimensions (technology exploitation 
and dimensions of instructional quality). These two subcoding schemes 
are described in the following sections.
All coding was performed using Microsoft Excel. Each identified 
instance of technology integration was entered as a separate case, and 
the corresponding SAMR and instructional quality codes were assigned 
within the spreadsheet. The coded dataset was subsequently exported to 
R for calculating interrater reliability indices and conducting statistical 
analyses.

To analyse the exploitation level of technology integrations, each 
technology integration was coded using a scheme based on the SAMR 
model (Puentedura, 2006) developed by Kramer et al. (2019; see 
Table A1 in Appendix). Each technology integration was coded from 1 
(substitution) to 4 (redefinition). The original coding scheme was 
adapted for the purpose of this study by incorporating illustrative

examples drawn from our data corpus, allowing for a consistent yet 
context-sensitive application. This ensured that the coding reflected 
both the theoretical structure of the SAMR model (Puentedura, 2006) 
and the specific characteristics of the lesson plans under analysis. It 
should be noted that the SAMR levels were coded based on how tech­

teacher's perspective as described in the lesson plans. Direct indications 
of students' active technology use were comparatively rare and thus not 
coded separately.
3.1.3. Dimensions of instructional quality
To analyse the instructional quality of technology integrations, we 
considered theoretical conceptualisations of dimensions of instructional 
quality (Klieme et al., 2006; Kunter et al., 2007; Pianta & Hamre, 2009). 
We developed a coding scheme to categorise the extent to which the 
technology integration served the purpose of enhancing the dimensions 
of instructional quality of the lesson – and thus, whether the technology 
integration served to either increase classroom management, support 
students, or foster cognitive activation (see Table A2 in Appendix). The 
coding scheme was operationalised using established indicators from 
Praetorius et al. (2018) and supplemented with illustrative examples 
drawn from our data corpus. This adaptation allowed for a theoretically 
grounded yet context-sensitive application of the framework to 
technology-enhanced instruction.
Based on the described coding schemes, each instance of technology 
integration could be systematically analysed.
4. Results
4.1. Analyses
To address the research questions, we conducted systematic analyses 
of the 568 technology integrations identified in the lesson plans. In the 
following, we outline the analytical approaches for each research 
question.
For RQ1, we analysed the level of technology exploitation using the 
SAMR model (Puentedura, 2006). Each instance of technology integra­
tion was coded into one of four hierarchical levels: substitution, 
augmentation, modification, or redefinition. The coding scheme (see 
Table A1 in the Appendix) was adapted from Kramer et al. (2019) and 
supplemented 
with 
examples 
from 
our 
corpus 
to 
ensure 
context-sensitive application. Descriptive statistics were used to report 
the frequency distribution of SAMR levels.
For RQ2, we examined how technology integrations aligned with 
dimensions of instructional quality: cognitive activation, student sup­
port, and classroom management (Klieme et al., 2006; Kunter et al., 
2007; Pianta & Hamre, 2009). Each instance was coded based on in­
dicators from Praetorius et al. (2018), with illustrative examples added 
from our data (see Table A2 in the Appendix). Frequencies were calcu­
lated to determine the prevalence of each dimension.
For RQ3, we investigated how the SAMR levels relate to dimensions 
of instructional quality. First, we calculated descriptive distributions 
across SAMR levels and dimensions of instructional quality. Next, we 
conducted Spearman's rank correlation analyses to explore associations, 
and Kruskal-Wallis tests with post-hoc Dunn-Bonferroni comparisons to 
assess group differences across SAMR levels. Given the non-normal 
distribution of the data and the ordinal structure of the independent 
variable, these non-parametric tests were chosen over traditional 
ANOVA or Chi-square procedures. Additionally, binary logistic regres­
sion models were computed to examine whether SAMR levels predicted 
the likelihood of addressing each instructional quality dimension. Odds 
ratios and McFadden's pseudo-R2 were used to interpret model effects 
and fit.
4.2. Research question 1: technology exploitation levels
To address the first research question (RQ1), we examined the extent 
to which the planned technology integrations in the student teachers' 
lesson plans could be categorized according to the four hierarchical 
levels of the SAMR model (Puentedura, 2006). The majority of tech­
nology integrations (N = 568) were coded at the lower SAMR levels: 
substitution (n = 347, 61 %) and augmentation (n = 154, 27 %). In 
contrast, considerably fewer instances reflected higher levels, with 63 
coded as modification (11 %) and only 4 lesson plans reaching the 
highest level of redefinition (0.7 %). Notably, all instances coded as 
Redefinition referred to the use of AI-based tools (e.g., ChatGPT or

4.2.1. SAMR level: substitution
An example of technology integration at the lowest SAMR level, 
namely substitution, was that one lesson plan included the use of a 
PowerPoint presentation to display the structure of the lesson via an 
interactive whiteboard. In this instance, the technology merely replaced 
an analogue medium such as a chalkboard, whiteboard, or flip chart 
without any functional improvement. Another example involved the 
presentation and discussion of an infographic. Whether the infographic 
was shown via an interactive whiteboard or in analogue form (e.g., in a 
textbook or handout) was pedagogically irrelevant, as the digital tech­
nology did not enhance the instructional function. These examples were 
classified as substitution because they involved no functional enhance­
ment and did not leverage the affordances of digital technology.
4.2.2. SAMR level: augmentation
In contrast, examples coded at the second SAMR level, namely 
augmentation, involved slight functional improvements compared to 
analogue alternatives. One student teacher planned to use short video 
clips via the interactive whiteboard to provide an introductory overview 
at the beginning of the lesson. Although the content could theoretically 
be conveyed through text or audio alone (e.g., in a textbook, podcast, or 
speech), the video format offered a modest enhancement by combining 
visual and auditory channels. Other student teachers integrated videos 
at the end of lesson phases as a summarizing element. In these cases, the 
added value lay in the multi-modal presentation and greater temporal 
flexibility. These examples all feature a slight functional enhancement 
through technology integration, without altering the core structure of 
the instructional task.
4.2.3. SAMR level: modification
Examples at the third SAMR level, namely modification, reflected a 
significant transformation of the instructional design. These cases were 
characterised by individualised, collaborative, and production-oriented 
learning activities. One student teacher, for instance, employed a digital 
feedback tool that allowed students to reflect on the structure and flow 
of a lesson or learning sequence directly before the lesson. Students 
submitted feedback via their own devices; results were presented to the 
class via the interactive whiteboard and discussed collectively. Students 
could prioritise, upvote, or downvote ideas, resulting in a collabora­
tively revised version of the lesson structure based on their preferences. 
This kind of task reconfiguration would not have been feasible in the 
same way using analogue tools. Another modification-level example was 
the use of a collaborative digital workspace, which allowed students to 
co-create content, such as headings for text sections or categorized lists 
of features and associations, in real time. Both examples involved a 
substantial redesign of the learning activity enabled by digital tools, 
meeting the criteria for modification in the SAMR model.
4.2.4. SAMR level: redefinition
Examples at the highest SAMR level, namely redefinition, demon­
strated how technology enabled instructional methods that would not be 
feasible without digital tools. In one case, student teachers used AI-based 
image generators to create original visual products through iterative 
prompt engineering. Students explored how changes in textual input 
influenced the generated image, engaging in a process-oriented and 
exploratory activity. Although analogue representations of the images 
could theoretically be printed and analysed, such an approach would not 
allow for real-time experimentation and interaction, central elements of 
the pedagogical value in this example. Another redefinition-level case 
involved using an AI image generator to design flyers based on a spec­
ified topic and structure. While similar outcomes could be achieved via 
analogue methods (e.g., collage from print media), this would not fully 
exploit the creative and iterative potential of the digital tool. Unlike 
static analogue processes, the AI tool enables real-time modifications 
based on prompt changes, allowing students to experiment with abstract 
concepts (e.g., mood, style, symbolism) and immediately observe how 
these influence the output—something that is neither feasible nor effi­
cient with analogue materials. What unites these examples is that they 
rely on the affordances of advanced digital tools to implement entirely 
new pedagogical strategies, fulfilling the criteria for redefinition in the 
SAMR framework.
4.3. Research question 2: dimensions of instructional quality
To address the second research question, we examined the extent to 
which the identified technology integrations reflected dimensions of 
instructional quality (Klieme et al., 2006; Kunter et al., 2007; Pianta & 
Hamre, 2009), namely cognitive activation, student support, and 
classroom management. Cognitive activation was the most frequently 
observed dimension, present in 163 cases (28.7 %). Student support was 
present in 107 cases (18.8 %), while classroom management was the 
least frequently addressed, appearing in only 100 cases (17.6 %).
4.3.1. Technology-enhanced cognitive activation
To further investigate the findings for RQ2, we first considered how 
the identified technology integrations reflected the dimension of 
cognitive activation. In line with our coding scheme, technology- 
enhanced cognitive activation refers to student engagement in higher- 
order thinking, knowledge construction, and meta-cognitive reflection 
(Alp Christ et al., 2024; Consoli et al., 2024; Praetorius et al., 2018).
One example of technology integration that fulfilled these criteria 
was the presentation of a short newspaper article via an interactive 
whiteboard at the beginning of a lesson. The student teacher initiated a 
discussion by asking the class what the article might have to do with the 
upcoming topic. Students' associations and ideas were collected and 
visualized collaboratively on the interactive whiteboard. This approach 
promoted the elicitation of students' prior knowledge and activated their 
reasoning through a structured discussion, thereby fostering discursive 
engagement and meta-cognitive reflection. Another example involved 
the use of an interactive digital map presented via the interactive 
whiteboard. The map featured color-coded elements that students were 
asked to interpret. Working on their own devices, students explored the 
meaning of the colour patterns and were encouraged to hypothesise as to 
how such information might be presented in a traditional analogue atlas. 
The digital format allowed for exploratory learning and conceptual 
engagement, enabling students to construct an understanding through 
discovery and comparative reasoning. Each instance exemplifies how 
technology integration can foster exploration, elicit student thinking, 
and promote content-related discourse, core aspects of cognitive 
activation.
4.3.2. Technology-enhanced student support
The second dimension of instructional quality reflected in our data 
corpus was student support. According to our coding scheme, 
technology-enhanced student support fosters students' competence, 
autonomy, and social relatedness by enabling differentiated and indi­
vidualised learning experiences (Alp Christ et al., 2024; Consoli et al., 
2024; Praetorius et al., 2018).
One example illustrating this dimension was the use of a digital 
learning station, which student teachers integrated as an individualised 
access point for learning tasks. Students would access the learning sta­
tion via their own devices and select from a variety of tasks tailored to 
different levels of prior knowledge and thematic interest. Tasks could be 
completed in any order and at the students' own pace. The student 
teacher planned to provide support throughout the process, responding 
to content-related and organisational questions. In this case, student 
support was realised through the digital structure, which offered au­
tonomy, choice, and created a personalised learning environment.

individual learning diagnoses. The quiz, designed by the student

4.3.3. Technology-enhanced classroom management
The final instructional quality dimension addressed in this analysis 
was classroom management. According to our coding scheme, 
technology-enhanced classroom management supports the establish­
ment of a structured and disruption-free learning environment by rein­
forcing clear rules, routines, and efficient instructional practices (Alp 
Christ et al., 2024; Consoli et al., 2024; Praetorius et al., 2018).
One example illustrating this dimension was the use of a slide-based 
routine presented via an interactive whiteboard at the beginning of the 
lesson. In the lesson plan, the student teacher included an introductory 
slide combining a timer for organisational tasks (e.g., preparing mate­
rials), an overview of the lesson structure, and a visual reminder of 
behavioural expectations (e.g., symbols representing “raise hand to 
speak” and “stay on task”). This digitally supported entry phase was 
intended to help students orient themselves quickly, reduce transition 
time, and establish clarity regarding the lesson flow. While this case 
relied on a custom-designed slide to structure the lesson entry phase, 
another example demonstrated how a dedicated classroom management 
tool can serve similar functions in a more systematic way: the integra­
tion of a classroom screen, a digital classroom management technology 
presented via the interactive whiteboard. The technology includes 
functions such as timers, visual task instructions, traffic light symbols, 
and a clear display of rules and expectations. In this example, the student 
teacher used the classroom screen to maintain an overview of the lesson 
agenda, provide visual cues for transitions, and display working in­
structions. These functionalities can help to reinforce classroom rou­
tines, reduce misunderstandings, and maintain student focus, thereby 
supporting the establishment of an organised and efficient learning 
environment. Across both examples, digital technologies were used to 
establish structure, provide clarity, and support instructional efficiency, 
thereby fulfilling the criteria for the classroom management dimension 
of instructional quality.
4.4. Research question 3: relations between SAMR levels and dimensions 
of instructional quality
To address the third research question, we examined how technology 
integrations at different SAMR levels, namely substitution, augmenta­
tion, modification, and redefinition, relate to dimensions of instructional 
quality, namely cognitive activation, student support, and classroom 
management (Klieme et al., 2006; Kunter et al., 2007; Pianta & Hamre, 
2009). Table 1 illustrates how often each dimension of instructional 
quality was addressed across the four SAMR levels.
Overall, technology integrations were predominantly situated at the 
lower SAMR levels of substitution and augmentation, indicating limited 
transformative integration of technology in the lesson plans. Cognitive 
activation was most frequently addressed at the substitution level and 
showed a decreasing trend as the SAMR level increased. This may reflect 
the fact that cognitively demanding tasks are often embedded in tradi­
tional formats that are digitally represented without functional trans­
formation. Student support was most commonly addressed at the 
augmentation level, while classroom management was primarily present 
at the substitution level.
To statistically examine these differences, we conducted a series of 
non-parametric and regression-based analyses. A Spearman's rank cor­
relation analysis showed significant, though weak to moderate, positive 
associations between SAMR levels and the three dimensions of instruc­
tional quality (cognitive activation: r = 0.17, p < .001; student support: 
r = 0.37, p < .001; classroom management: r = 0.10, p < .05) (Cohen, 
2013).
Kruskal-Wallis tests indicated significant differences in the distribu­
tion of SAMR levels across the three dimensions of instructional quality: 
cognitive activation, χ2 (3) = 23.64, p < .001, η2 = 0.04, ε2 = 0.04; 
student support, χ2 (3) = 78.98, p < .001, η2 = 0.14, ε2 = 0.13; and 
classroom management, χ2 (3) = 10.17, p = .017, η2 = 0.01, ε2 = 0.01. 
We chose the Kruskal-Wallis test over a Chi-square test because it is 
more appropriate for ordinal data and does not require minimum ex­
pected frequencies per cell. Several cells in the distribution (e.g., 
redefinition cases) had very low counts, violating Chi-square assump­
tions. While the effect sizes for cognitive activation and classroom 
management were small, the effect size for student support was 
comparatively large (Cohen, 2013; Tomczak & Tomczak, 2014), indi­
cating a more substantial association between SAMR level and student 
support.
Post-hoc comparisons using Dunn-Bonferroni tests revealed signifi­
cant differences between SAMR levels for the three dimensions of 
instructional quality. For cognitive activation, significant differences 
emerged between substitution (level 1) and modification (level 3) (Z =
−4.38, p < .001) and between augmentation (level 2) and modification 
(level 3) (Z = −3.09, p = .019). For student support, all comparisons 
between substitution (level 1) and higher levels were significant: sub­
stitution (level 1) vs. augmentation (Level 2) (Z = −5.75, p < .001), 
substitution (level 1) vs. modification (level 3) (Z = −7.68, p < .001), 
and augmentation (level 2) vs. modification (level 3) (Z = −3.30, p =
.002). A significant difference was also observed for classroom man­
agement between substitution and modification (levels 1 and 3) (Z =
−3.16, p = .002). These post-hoc comparisons indicate that most sig­
nificant differences in dimensions of instructional quality occurred be­
tween lower and higher SAMR levels, particularly between substitution 
and modification (levels 1 and 3). This means that cognitive activation 
was more often applied when technology was integrated at the lowest 
SAMR level, substitution (level 1), compared to integrations at the 
modification level (level 3). In contrast, student support was most 
frequently applied at the augmentation level (level 2), with fewer cases 
at the substitution (level 1) and modification levels (level 3). For 
classroom management, the number of applications decreased with 
higher SAMR levels, with only one case coded at the redefinition level 
(level 4).
To assess whether SAMR levels predicted the likelihood of addressing 
dimensions of instructional quality, we conducted binary logistic

.050, OR = 9.70). For student support, all higher SAMR levels showed

5. Discussion
The present study analysed lesson plans created by student teachers 
during university-based teacher training seminars based on theoretical 
conceptualisations of dimensions of instructional quality (Klieme et al., 
2006; Pianta & Hamre, 2009; Praetorius et al., 2025). This allowed us to 
examine both the functional level of technology integration and its 
alignment with dimensions of instructional quality. The findings show 
that most technology integrations remained at lower SAMR levels, such 
as substitution or augmentation. Less than one-third of technology in­
tegrations reflected indications of dimensions of instructional quality, 
with cognitive activation being the most frequent. Statistical analyses 
further revealed that higher SAMR levels were positively associated with 
dimensions of instructional quality, particularly student support.
Research question 1 was concerned with how the levels of the SAMR 
model (Puentedura, 2006) were reflected in student teachers' lesson 
plans. The findings show that although digital technologies were 
frequently integrated into the lesson plans, their use remained primarily 
at the level of substitution or minor functional enhancement. Instances 
of technology integrations that substantially transformed or redefined 
instructional practices were rare. This replicates previous findings sug­
gesting that student teachers often adopt conservative planning strate­
gies that focus on familiar, low-complexity tools (Henning et al., 2006; 
K¨onig et al., 2024; Liu, 2016). This trend may stem from limited expo­
sure to pedagogically rich technology integrations during teacher 
training (Tondeur et al., 2012). This dominance of substitution-level 
integrations may because for reflection, because it suggests that tech­
nology is often integrated to replicate traditional practices rather than to 
enhance or transform them. Such patterns risk reinforcing existing 
instructional routines instead of leveraging the pedagogical affordances 
of technology. Moreover, research indicates that higher levels of tech­
nology integration—such as modification and redefinition—are more 
likely to support student-centred learning, foster engagement, and 
enable cognitively activating tasks (Hamilton et al., 2016). When tech­
nology is used merely as a digital substitute, these instructional poten­
tials remain largely untapped. From a teacher training perspective, these 
findings call for a stronger implementation of knowledge on how tech­
nology can be used to meaningfully enhance instruction—particularly at 
higher levels of complexity. This includes supporting student teachers in 
planning for technology integration that goes beyond substitution and 
enables instructional redesign or redefinition. Teacher education pro­
grammes should therefore provide structured opportunities to explore, 
practice, and reflect on such forms of integration in order to foster 
innovation-oriented professional reasoning and better prepare student 
teachers for the demands of digitally enriched learning environments.
Research Question 2 examined whether planned technology in­
tegrations addressed theoretical conceptualisations of dimensions of 
instructional quality (Klieme et al., 2006; Kunter et al., 2007; Pianta & 
Hamre, 2009). Results show that cognitive activation was the most 
frequently addressed dimension, followed by student support and 
classroom management.
This finding is particularly noteworthy given that cognitive activa­
tion appeared most often at the lowest SAMR level, substitution. While 
substitution represents the least transformative form of technology use 
(Puentedura, 2006), this suggests that cognitive activation does not 
necessarily depend on the technological sophistication of an instruc­
tional activity. Rather, it may arise from the pedagogical design of the 
task itself. Even simple substitutions, such as replacing printed work­
sheets with interactive digital materials, can promote reasoning, anal­
ysis, and reflection when the instructional focus targets higher-order 
thinking processes (Klieme et al., 2006; Praetorius et al., 2018). In the 
context of student teachers, this pattern may also reflect that 
low-threshold forms of technology use provide manageable entry points 
for creating cognitively demanding tasks without overburdening 
instructional design skills (K¨onig et al., 2024; Lachner et al., 2024). This 
interpretation aligns with the view that effective technology integration 
depends less on the level of technological transformation and more on its 
pedagogical alignment with learning goals.
This pattern may also indicate how pre-service teachers conceptu­
alise the pedagogical role of technology integration. Rather than 
viewing technology as a transformative element in itself, they may 
perceive it primarily as a tool to facilitate cognitively demanding ac­
tivities. In this sense, technology serves an instrumental function that 
supports, rather than defines, instructional quality. Such an interpreta­
tion would align with previous research suggesting that student teachers 
often transfer traditional cognitively challenging tasks (e.g., text anal­
ysis or problem solving) into digital environments without fundamen­
tally altering their instructional design (K¨onig et al., 2024; Lachner

However, across all three dimensions, fewer than one-third of the

are in line with prior research suggesting that many (student) teachers

technologies with high-quality instruction (Cevikbas et al., 2024; Farjon

technology demonstrates openness and awareness, it does not neces­
sarily indicate instructional quality. As K¨onig et al. (2021) and Henning

such as teacher-led questioning or text-based analysis, which are then 
digitally represented without leveraging the transformative potential of 
technology.
Research Question 3 explored how levels of technology exploitation 
related to theoretical conceptualisations of dimensions of instructional 
quality (Klieme et al., 2006; Pianta & Hamre, 2009; Praetorius et al., 
2025). Statistical analyses revealed small to moderate positive associa­
tions between higher SAMR levels and the dimensions of cognitive 
activation and student support.
These findings can be theoretically explained by considering how 
advanced levels of technology integration transform the cognitive and 
socioemotional demands of instruction. On the modification and 
redefinition levels of the SAMR model (Puentedura, 2006), digital 
technologies are integrated not only as tools for substitution but as 
means to design more complex, open-ended learning activities. Such 
tasks require students to engage in reasoning, problem-solving, and the 
transfer of knowledge, thereby increasing opportunities for cognitive 
activation (Klieme et al., 2006; Praetorius et al., 2018). At the same 
time, these cognitively demanding activities heighten the need for 
pedagogical guidance and socioemotional scaffolding to help students 
manage these challenges effectively. This may explain the positive as­
sociation with the dimension of student support (Pianta & Hamre, 
2009). Consequently, higher levels of technology exploitation appear to 
foster instruction that is both cognitively activating and socially sup­
portive. This resonates with conceptual models like TPTI (Lachner et al., 
2024) and empirical research emphasising that pedagogically mean­
ingful integration tends to co-occur with more advanced technology 
integration (Consoli et al., 2024). However, the SAMR level alone is not 
a reliable predictor for dimensions of instructional quality, as it focuses 
on functional transformation without considering the pedagogical intent 
or instructional context. As critics have pointed out, even technology 
integration at higher SAMR levels can lack instructional value if it is not 
meaningfully aligned with learning goals (Blundell et al., 2022; Ham­
ilton et al., 2016).
The findings of this study must also be interpreted considering the 
national and institutional context. The lesson plans were collected from 
pre-service teachers enrolled in German teacher education programs, 
which are characterised by a strong theoretical orientation and struc­
tured progression toward practical teaching phases (K¨onig et al., 2020, 
2021; Stürmer et al., 2015). This framework may have influenced how 
student teachers conceptualise technology integration, emphasising 
pedagogical appropriateness and alignment with instructional goals 
over experimentation or innovation. Consequently, the results should be 
understood within the cultural and educational context of German 
teacher training. Future research could examine whether similar pat­
terns emerge in other educational systems that differ in their approaches 
to digitalisation, teacher autonomy, or practicum-based training.
Taken together, the results of the present study underscore the need 
for teacher training programmes to provide more targeted support for 
planning technology-rich lessons that align with pedagogical principles. 
Models like TPTI (Lachner et al., 2024) may offer helpful guidance for 
linking digital technology with core aspects of instructional quality.
6. Implications
These findings have direct implications for the design of teacher 
training curricula. To foster meaningful technology integration, pro­
grammes should go beyond teaching general digital competencies and 
instead embed structured opportunities for student teachers to plan, 
justify, and reflect on technology integration in relation to instructional 
quality. This could include scaffolded lesson planning tasks, model- 
based feedback using frameworks like TPTI (Lachner et al., 2024), and 
explicit discussions on how technology integration can support cogni­
tive activation, student support, and classroom management.
Finally, the study contributes to a growing body of work suggesting 
that lesson plans offer valuable insights into preservice teachers' 
instructional reasoning for technology integration (Janssen et al., 2019; 
K¨onig et al., 2020). While they do not capture enacted teaching, they 
reveal how digital technologies are conceptualised during the planning 
phase—a critical step in professional development. Future research 
should explore how these planned intentions unfold in practice and how 
the planning process can be further scaffolded to promote more trans­
formative and instructionally grounded integrations of technology.
6.1. Limitations and future research
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the 
findings of the present study. First, the data are based on written lesson 
plans submitted in the context of teacher training seminars. As such, 
they capture intended instructional decisions rather than actual class­
room practices. While lesson plans offer insight into pedagogical 
reasoning (Backfisch et al., 2020), they do not provide evidence of 
enacted teaching or student learning outcomes. Future studies could 
supplement planning data with classroom observations or video ana­
lyses to examine how planned technology integrations are implemented 
in real teaching contexts.
Second, the study relies on qualitative coding of lesson plan content, 
which, despite high interrater reliability, is inherently interpretive. It is 
possible that some nuances of instructional intent were not fully 
captured in the coding process. Follow-up studies could employ mixed- 
method approaches, including interviews with student teachers, to 
triangulate findings and gain deeper insight into their reasoning behind 
technology integration.
Third, the study sample was drawn from a specific set of university 
seminars in Germany, limiting the generalisability of the results. 
Although the sample included multiple cohorts across different semes­
ters, the findings may not reflect lesson planning practices in other in­
stitutions or educational systems. Future research could investigate 
similar questions in different national or institutional contexts to assess 
the broader applicability of the findings.
Finally, while the SAMR model (Puentedura, 2006) and TBD 
framework (Klieme et al., 2006; Kunter et al., 2007) offer complemen­
tary perspectives on technology exploitation and instructional quality, 
they do not capture all relevant aspects of technology integration. Other 
models, such as the Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge

cesses, offering a more nuanced view of professional competence. 
However, recent research also highlights a persistent gap between pre­
service teachers' declarative knowledge about technology integration 
and their ability to apply this knowledge in authentic instructional 
contexts—a phenomenon referred to as the TPACK-Practice Gap 
(Schmid et al., 2021). This gap underscores the importance of not only 
fostering integrated knowledge structures but also providing opportu­

routines, or professional development formats influence the quality of 
both planned and enacted technology integration.
7. Conclusion
The present study contributes to a deeper understanding of how 
student teachers plan for technology integration and how these planned 
integrations align with theoretical conceptualisations of dimensions of 
instructional quality (Klieme et al., 2006; Kunter et al., 2007; Pianta & 
Hamre, 2009). By combining the SAMR model (Puentedura, 2006) and 
dimensions of instructional quality (Klieme et al., 2006; Kunter et al., 
2007; Pianta & Hamre, 2009), the study offers an integrated analytical 
approach that captures both the functional and pedagogical dimensions 
of planned technology integration.
The findings demonstrate that while digital technologies are 
frequently integrated in lesson plans, most instances remain at lower 
levels of technology exploitation, such as substitution or augmentation. 
Moreover, dimensions of instructional quality—particularly student 
support and cognitive activation—are more often associated with higher 
levels of technology exploitation, though effect sizes remain modest. 
These results underscore the need for teacher training to move beyond 
promoting general digital competence toward fostering pedagogically 
meaningful technology integration in lesson design.
Overall, lesson plans serve as a valuable medium for assessing how 
student teachers conceptualise technology integration. By making 
instructional intentions visible, they provide an entry point for evalu­
ating and supporting the development of professional competence in 
technology-enhanced teaching. Future work should examine how these 
intentions translate into classroom practices and how lesson planning 
can be used more systematically as a developmental tool in teacher 
education.

• Integration of an interactive whiteboard that served as a collaborative surface for students to contribute ideas. In this lesson,

students to see each other's entries as they appeared, enabling them to avoid duplication and build on peers' contributions.

• Collaborative use of an online brainstorming tool in which students jointly developed a thematic map. In this task, students 
were not only required to generate relevant content but also to establish connections or contrasts between different elements

• Integration of an AI-based image generator. Students were first asked to create their own illustrations based on a short story 
and then to generate images using the AI tool based on the same text. The two sets of images were then compared with

4)
• Integration of an AI-enhanced brainstorming platform to collaboratively collect associations in response to a prompt on 
current challenges facing the German welfare state. Each student submitted contributions via a personal device. The system 
automatically categorized entries and visualized them in a dynamic word cloud, which was continuously updated and 
displayed on both student devices and the interactive whiteboard. What distinguished this integration from similar cases at 
the modification level was the extended range of digital functionalities: students could enrich the discussion with external 
materials such as links to websites, scanned textbook excerpts, and other media. A timeline view allowed learners to track 
the evolution of the collective knowledge base. Additionally, the system offered the option to automatically generate a 
podcast summarizing the brainstorming results, with the tone, structure, and content shaped by the prompt used.

repository, intended as a resource for exam preparation.

“need feedback,” “done”), and the student teacher could respond directly through comments.
