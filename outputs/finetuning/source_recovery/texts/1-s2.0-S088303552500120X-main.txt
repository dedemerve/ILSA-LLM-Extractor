In response to the growing number of immigrants in South Korea, there has been an increasing
emphasis on fostering inclusive attitudes toward immigrants (IATI) among students. Drawing on
the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals, the South Korean government and educational leaders have adopted and implemented Global Citizenship Education (GCE) as a strategic
approach to address this issue. This study investigates the interplay between GCE, IATI, and
intergroup contact by providing empirical evidence on these relationships. Utilizing data from the
2018 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) and applying school-fixed effects,
the study finds a positive association between GCE and IATI. Furthermore, this relationship is
stronger in the presence of intergroup contact within school settings, suggesting that GCE mitigates the negative association between school-based intergroup contact and IATI. However, no
significant interaction is observed between GCE, IATI, and intergroup contact in community
contexts. These findings suggest that school leaders should ensure that students have access to
GCE opportunities not only to cultivate inclusive attitudes but also to perceive intergroup contact
as a constructive experience in shaping such attitudes. This study provides both theoretical and
practical implications for promoting positive intercultural attitudes, contributing to creating inclusive environments for immigrant students and supporting the development of all students as
global citizens. Although the study focuses on South Korea, its findings hold broader relevance for
educational leaders and policymakers in other countries experiencing rising immigration, aligning with global efforts to advance intercultural understanding.

1. Introduction
South Korea (hereafter Korea) has long been perceived as a predominantly homogeneous society in terms of race/ethnicity, language, and culture (Choi & Lee, 2020; Lee & Misco, 2014). This cultural uniformity limited students’ educational opportunities to
understand and respect people from other countries as equal members of their nation (Hong, 2010). The pervasive “one blood, one
nation” myth significantly influenced students’ perceptions and attitudes toward people from other countries (Y. Song & Freedman,
2022). However, globalization has brought about an increase in the immigrant population in schools and communities. Notably, the
number of students with immigrant backgrounds surged from around 47,000 in 2012 to 193,814 in 2024, comprising approximately

3.8 % of the student population (Statistics Korea, 2025). While this influx has fostered opportunities for intergroup interactions, such
contact does not always lead to transformative experiences promoting inclusive attitudes toward immigrants (IATI), which are defined
as the belief that immigrants should have equal rights and opportunities as all other citizens, regardless of their origin or ethnic/racial
background (Esses et al., 1998). In fact, students often display negative attitudes and discriminatory behavior toward immigrants,
hindering immigrant students’ adaptation to new environments (J. H. Kim et al., 2022).
In response to this issue, the Korean government and educational leaders have adopted global citizenship education (GCE) as a
central policy strategy. GCE is a set of learning experiences that aim to cultivate the knowledge, skills, values, and attitudes essential
for fostering a more inclusive and peaceful global society amid a diverse population. It addresses three key conceptual dimensions:
cognitive, socio-emotional, and behavioral development (UNESCO, 2015). These objectives are closely aligned with the challenges
posed by the increasing diversity in Korea. A significant milestone in this effort was the endorsement of the Incheon Declaration for
Education 2030 at the UNESCO World Education Forum in 2015. This declaration emphasized the promotion of GCE to enhance the
understanding and appreciation of cultural diversity (UNESCO, 2016). Following this, the Ministry of Education integrated GCE into
the national curriculum and teacher education programs (Cho & Mosselson, 2018; Pak & Lee, 2018). Given Korea’s highly centralized
education system, GCE has been widely and quickly incorporated into school systems, offering educational experiences aimed at
fostering an understanding and appreciation of diverse cultures (Kim & Kwon, 2023).
With the increased attention to GCE and the surge in immigration worldwide, research has made significant contributions to our
understanding of GCE’s role, providing evidence across different regions (Choi, 2024; Goren & Yemini, 2017). However, previous
literature has overlooked the interplay between GCE and different types of intergroup contact and failed to address certain methodological issues (e.g., school-level heterogeneity), along with a lack of contextual relevance. Moreover, focusing on the Korean
context could offer unique and global insights to educators, policymakers, and researchers in other regions. This is because Korea has
positioned itself as a leader in GCE to achieve the UN’s Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 4.7, but has also faced criticism due to a
narrow approach to GCE and challenges resulting from an increase in immigrants (Y. Kim, 2019).
Addressing these gaps, this study investigates whether GCE effectively fosters the development of IATI among students, specifically
examining the interaction of GCE with intergroup contact, measured by contact with individuals from other countries in the school and
community. This intersection will enhance our understanding of how GCE facilitates positive experiences from intergroup contact,
helping students reduce negative attitudes and cultivate a more inclusive mindset toward immigrants. The study aims to provide
empirical evidence on the relationship between GCE and IATI and the potential of GCE to assist students in navigating a changing
environment where interactions with immigrants are becoming increasingly commonplace. As the proposed 2030 timeline for SDGs
approaches, the need for evidence of GCE’s effectiveness in student development is increasingly pertinent.
To achieve this objective, the study uses data from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2018. The
assessment began to include GCE and global competence in 2018, recognizing the need to prepare students for a globalized and
multicultural world. It should be noted that the OECD-PISA framework of global competence has received significant criticism due to
its capitalistic and Western-centric ideology, and the lack of critical perspectives (Engel et al., 2019). In line with these critiques, this
study does not aim to assess the entire scope of global competence using PISA 2018 data but focuses on students’ attitudes toward
immigrants and their rights, given the rapid increase in immigrants and prevalent discrimination against them in Korea. As suggested
by Engel and colleagues (2019), there is room to explore issues surrounding immigration using this framework. Therefore, this study
takes advantage of this assessment to answer the following key questions:
(1) To what extent does GCE enhance the development of IATI among students?
(2) Does GCE moderate the relationship between intergroup contact in the school and IATI?
(3) Does GCE moderate the relationship between intergroup contact in the community and IATI?
2. Literature review
2.1. GCE in Korean context
GCE aims to reframe the educational paradigm by providing a new perspective on what citizenship means and what attributes
students need to interact respectfully and peacefully with individuals from diverse backgrounds (Tarozzi & Inguaggiato, 2018).
UNESCO views GCE as a comprehensive approach rather than a single subject, promoting shared agendas across various subjects (e.g.,
human rights education and education for international understanding) to foster values and attitudes toward people from different
backgrounds (UNESCO, 2014). The definition, typologies, and outcomes of GCE have been discussed in research from various perspectives (see Chiba et al., 2021; Goren & Yemini, 2017; Oxley & Morris, 2013). Debate also exists regarding the approaches to GCE
and global citizenship, particularly regarding its neoliberal, nationalistic, and/or Western-focused aspects (Pashby, 2011; Sun &
Buripakdi, 2024; Wolhuter & Niemczyk, 2023). This study primarily focuses on elucidating the meaning and context of GCE in Korea,
aligning it with the discourse in the literature, as it has been applied in various ways across different contexts.
The phrase “one blood, one nation” was not merely a slogan in Korea but was deeply embedded in educational policies and national
curricula across various subjects (S. Kim et al., 2010). This curriculum, along with textbooks, aimed to instill pride in students about
the homogeneous image of the nation, thereby serving as a unifying force within society. Students rarely had meaningful opportunities
to learn about different perspectives, cultures, and global issues, exhibiting a lack of understanding of individuals different from
themselves. However, the advent of globalization led the government and educational leaders to adopt policies aimed at transitioning
from a nation-state to a global leader and to embrace immigration (J. Song, 2016). Despite this rapid demographic shift, the education

system and national curriculum remained entrenched in the myth of “one blood, one nation.” As a result, in 2007, the UN Committee
on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination urged the Korean government to adapt the education system to the ethnically diverse
context, promoting understanding and respect for individuals from other countries.
In an effort to overcome the perception of an ethnically homogeneous society and cultivate students as global leaders, educational
leaders in Korea have been dedicated to introducing and advancing GCE in recent years, aspiring to take a proactive stance on GCE
discourse within the global community (Cho & Mosselson, 2018). The impetus for adopting GCE was sparked by the Global Education
First Initiative in 2012, led by Ban Ki-moon, the former UN Secretary-General and former Korean Minister of Foreign Affairs and Trade.
In 2015, Korea hosted the UNESCO World Education Forum, where world leaders endorsed the proposition that integrated GCE within
the SDGs. This event garnered significant attention to GCE from policymakers, educators, and the public in Korea (Choi & Kim, 2020).
The Ministry of Education has integrated GCE principles into the national curriculum to equip students with values and attitudes
essential for evolving into global citizens who appreciate diverse cultures (Ministry of Education, 2015). GCE has been extensively
implemented within schools and districts, encompassing a diverse array of subjects and extracurricular activities (UNESCO, 2014),
facilitated by Korea’s highly centralized and top-down education system (Pak & Lee, 2018). For instance, the national curriculum
clearly emphasizes the value of being a global citizen and the importance of developing competencies and attitudes such as empathy
and tolerance through the discovery of others and an understanding of different cultures (UNESCO, 2015).
Despite the widespread implementation of GCE in schools with the goal of fostering global citizens, concerns have been raised
about its limited scope (Choi & Kim, 2020; Pak & Lee, 2018). Researchers argue that government-led GCE implementation is heavily
rooted in neoliberal ideology. Within a neoliberal framework, GCE is often viewed as a tool for equipping students with the necessary
skills, knowledge, and attitudes to succeed and become global leaders in the global labor market (Y. Kim, 2019; Marshall, 2011). This
perspective tends to neglect GCE’s potential as a critical approach that challenges social injustice and inequality in global structures
(Choi & Kim, 2020). In the Korean education context, GCE has been aggressively adopted as another form of capital to differentiate
learners from their peers rather than as a bridge between individuals from different backgrounds and cultures. This education fever of
GCE aligns with the highly competitive education system, where acquiring new knowledge and experiences is primarily seen as a
pathway for social mobility and stratification (Seth, 2002).
Although GCE in Korea has faced criticism for its narrow focus (Choi & Kim, 2020), this drawback does not necessarily imply that
students cannot not benefit from GCE in the development of global citizenship. Research from other regions indicates that students
internalize values such as equality and mutual respect through GCE, arguing that more research is needed in different contexts to assess
its applicability (Goren & Yemini, 2017; MacKenzie et al., 2016). While addressing the criticisms it has received is important, more
empirical evidence with a critical lens and a rigorous analytical approach is needed to examine whether GCE in Korea effectively
facilitates the development of students. GCE may still provide students with opportunities to reflect on their attitudes toward immigrants and their interactions with them. The ability to coexist harmoniously with individuals from different countries is an essential
attitude in today’s global society and workplace. Despite the neoliberal underpinnings of government-led GCE initiatives, their
application and implementation at the school level can differ based on local educators and contexts, underscoring the diverse values of
GCE (Y. Kim & Kwon, 2023; Pak & Lee, 2018).
Despite its drawbacks and criticisms, empirical evidence regarding the effectiveness of GCE on IATI provides practical implications
that can inform discussions among educators, school leaders, and policymakers about the significance of GCE. This evidence can guide
decision-making on whether it is worthwhile to continue/reduce or expand relevant policies and practices. It will also facilitate datainformed discourse on whether the positive outcomes outweigh the drawbacks, ultimately refining the approach while acknowledging
criticisms. Additionally, this will be instrumental in enhancing educators’ efficacy in integrating GCE into their schools and classrooms
by alleviating uncertainty about the implementation of top-down policies and its effectiveness in preparing students to respect diversity (Dunn et al., 2013; Thoonen et al., 2011). Therefore, it is essential to empirically investigate whether GCE helps students
develop IATI, preparing them to live and learn harmoniously with immigrants and fostering an inclusive and respectful environment
for all. Furthermore, since student development involves the interplay of classroom learning with daily experiences and relationships
(Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006), it is important to investigate the intersection of GCE with other student experiences, such as
intergroup contact, to gain a deeper understanding of its role in fostering more inclusive attitudes.
2.2. Intergroup contact with learning experiences
Since Allport’s (1954) work, intergroup contact theory has served as a foundational framework for understanding the role of
intergroup contact in shaping attitudes toward outgroup members (Wilson-Daily et al., 2018). Its main assumption is that individuals
can reduce prejudice and anxiety through intergroup contact and develop positive attitudes toward outgroup members under optimal
conditions, including equal status, common goals, intergroup cooperation, and institutional support (Pettigrew & Tropp, 2008). This
theory has been widely tested and replicated in different contexts with adjustments and refinements based on accumulated empirical
evidence. Research shows that the optimal conditions contribute to facilitating positive outcomes of intergroup contact, but they are
not always necessary to produce a positive effect, suggesting the complexity of the interplay between intergroup contact and attitudes
(Pettigrew & Tropp, 2008).
The theory has also faced criticism for its overly idealistic and utopian concepts of intergroup interactions. Pettigrew (2016)
observed that “the ever-increasing list of “necessary” conditions rapidly excluded the majority of the world’s intergroup situations and
rendered the theory trivial” (p. 13). Such criticism highlights the need for a more pragmatic approach that acknowledges the
complexity and variability of real-world intergroup interactions, which are not designed by an experiment but occur naturally in
students’ daily lives. Scholars have called for further studies centered on real-life experiences, considering that most interactions

between different groups occur in everyday situations, including schools and communities (Dixon et al., 2005). However, a substantial
gap remains in investigating how intergroup contact—real-life experiences rather than research-designed conditions—interacts with
school-based learning experiences (i.e., GCE in this study; Lee, 2024). This gap hinders practical insights into how schools can
effectively encourage students to benefit from intergroup contact. By shifting the focus from under optimal conditions to with learning
experiences, we can better understand the dynamics of intergroup contact and develop more applicable and effective educational
strategies for fostering positive intergroup relations among students.
Intergroup contact in schools and communities, such as in classrooms, cafeterias, and supermarkets, is likely interpreted and
perceived through the lens of students’ learning experiences (Yip et al., 2019). The subsequent reactions to intergroup contact may be
shaped by what students learn because the interaction is situated in a reciprocal relationship with their everyday activities (Rogoff,
2003). Research indicates that students can benefit from intergroup contact (i.e., with friends or at school) in the development of
positive attitudes toward members of different social and cultural groups when coupled with learning experiences about diverse
cultures (Choi, 2024) or with high-quality positive interactions (Árnadóttir et al., 2022). These studies enhance our understanding that
students may require additional support to view intergroup contact as a bridge-building opportunity between immigrant and
non-immigrant groups.
The literature makes significant contributions to our understanding of the role of intergroup contact in shaping students’ attitudes
toward other groups and the conditions under which it produces more positive outcomes. However, several issues remain underexplored, including how intergroup contact interacts with different educational experiences and whether different types of intergroup
contact (e.g., at school and in the community) function similarly when combined with GCE. Additionally, due to the nature of secondary data or small-scale analyses, analytical issues (e.g., heterogeneity of schools) are not adequately addressed, potentially leading
to biased estimates. By addressing these empirical gaps, the current study aims to bridge the gap between theoretical models and
practical applications, ensuring that the insights gained from research are relevant and useful for school leaders to address students’
learning experiences within a regular school setting.
3. Methods
3.1. Data and analytic sample
This study draws on data from PISA 2018, organized by the OECD, which includes all variables used in this study. This data encompasses various information on students’ educational experience across 79 participant countries. The data enables the provision of
representative estimates for 15-year-old students through a two-stage stratified sampling method, while also accounting for potential
confounders by including a range of background information. In Korea, the survey included 6650 15-year-old students across 188
schools. Six students with immigrant backgrounds and 74 non-respondents on this item were excluded from the analysis, resulting in
an analytic sample of 6570 students. This small portion of immigrant samples may be because they are systematically excluded from
participation in the assessment.
The data contains missing values ranging from 0 % (gender) to 3 % (intergroup contact). The percentage of complete cases is
approximately 93 %. This study addresses missing data by employing multiple imputation techniques. While seven imputed datasets
would be sufficient, this study created 50 imputed datasets and pooled the results to produce less biased estimates (Graham, 2009). The
imputation was performed using the Blimp software, which is designed to account for nested data structures when imputing data
(Enders et al., 2018).
PISA 2018 employed a two-stage stratified sampling procedure to represent the target population. Approximately 150 schools were
chosen using a probability proportional to size sampling method, and around 42 15-year-old students were selected with equal
probability from each chosen school to represent nationally representative samples of the target population. Weights were provided to
account for potential biases due to unequal selection probabilities. Consistent with the recommendations in the PISA technical report,
this study applied the student weight to accurately represent the target population.
3.2. Measures
3.2.1. GCE
Students were asked in a survey about their engagement in various types of GCE within the school setting (yes or no). Using four
binary items, four dummy variables were created to denote the number of types of GCE engaged in school, ranging from one to four (e.
g., learn about different cultures). The reference category consisted of students not exposed to any types of GCE used in this study.
Dummy variables were favored over a composite score as the effect of GCE may not follow a linear pattern with the increase in the
number of types.
3.2.2. IATI
A composite scale of IATI, devised by PISA 2018, was included in the analysis. Four items were used to ask students about the extent
of their belief in immigrants’ rights, opportunities, and customs. The following question was used: “People are increasingly moving
from one country to another. How much do you agree with the following statements about immigrants?” The examples items included
were: “Immigrant children should have the same opportunities for education” and “Immigrants should have the same opportunities to
continue their own customs and lifestyle.” The response format was a 4-point Likert scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree; a
higher score indicated more inclusive attitudes. The internal consistency was tested, and the value of Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient was

3.2.3. Intergroup contact
Two binary variables were used to denote whether students had contact with individuals from other countries in the school and
community, respectively (0 = no; 1 = yes). This variable did not contain more detailed information on the nature of the interactions,
such as the frequency or quality. Despite this limitation, it allowed for capturing actual contact while the literature predominantly
relied on the proportion of immigrants as a proxy for intergroup contact, which does not assure actual contact (Burgess & Platt, 2021).
In addition to the main variables, control variables were included at the student level (e.g., sex, SES, and media use) to account for
potential confounders based on previous literature (Hjerm et al., 2018). Table 1 provides detailed information on the variables.
3.3. Analytic approach
This study used OLS regression with school-fixed effects while employing cluster-robust standard errors at the school level. This
approach accounts for the nested data structure and controls for any variability attributed to the school level (Huang, 2016). Model 1
included all predictors except for an interaction term between GCE and intergroup contact. Model 2 added the interaction terms while
keeping the previous settings consistent. The final model was expressed as:
Yij = γ 00 + γ 10 ICij + γ20 GCEij + γ30 STij + γ 40 ICij × GCEij + εij + πs
Where Yij is the standardized outcome of student i in school j; ICij is two dummy variables indicating whether student i in school j
has intergroup contact in the school or community, respectively; GCEij is four dummy variables indicating the number of types of GCE
to which students are exposed at school; ICij × GCEij is a series of interaction terms between each GCE and intergroup contact; STij is a
vector of student characteristics; εij are residual error terms; and πs is a school-fixed effect.
The dependent variable and SES were standardized to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation (SD) of 1 prior to analysis, allowing the
parameter estimates to be interpreted as standardized regression coefficients. The remaining continuous variables and dummy variables used in this study were not standardized, as their original scales provide a more intuitive interpretation.
4. Results

Table 2). The results of the main variables revealed that 15 % of students did not engage in any type of GCE. In contrast, 16 %, 14 %, 17
%, and 37 % were exposed to one, two, three, and four types of GCE, respectively, indicating varied GCE learning experiences among
students. Regarding intergroup contact, 37 % and 42 % of respondents reported having contact in their school and community,
respectively.

types. As expected, the effect of GCE did not follow a linear pattern with an increase in the number of types. Specifically, compared to

Next, with respect to intergroup contact in the community, this study found no significant association with students’ IATI, despite a
positive direction being indicated (0.087 SD, p > .05). The location of the 95 % CI included the null effect, with a range of [− 0.072,
0.247]. Further examination revealed that there were no significant interaction effects between the number of GCE types and intergroup contact in the community in predicting IATI, with the trends being negative. The 95 % CIs of these interaction terms did not
exclude the null effect, and their range was relatively large, from [− 0.288, 0.099] to [− 0.228, 0.148]. As depicted in Fig. 2, it is
difficult to discern a specific pattern in the interactions or a significant difference between the contact groups depending on the number
of GCE types. These findings contrast with those related to intergroup contact at school.
5. Discussion and implications
Given Korea’s emerging status as a destination for immigrants, students are being encouraged to foster IATI. This is not only for the
acceptance of immigrants, but also for the students’ development as global citizens who appreciate the values of diversity and advocate
for equality and social justice, irrespective of national origins. Despite criticism of the narrow focus of GCE, the Korean government and
educational leaders have embraced it as a key policy agenda. This study aimed to investigate whether GCE encourages students to
develop IATI and if it helps students benefit from interactions with individuals from other countries in school and community settings.
This investigation has both theoretical and practical implications for the development of IATI.
This study finds that GCE could assist students in enhancing their attitudes toward immigrants in a more inclusive manner. The
findings suggest that GCE offers students an opportunity to reflect on their attitudes toward immigrants and their rights, which are
essential for engaging with individuals from other countries. By learning about diverse cultures and global issues, students are likely to
regard immigrants as “co-responsible people [and] as full partners” (Apple, 2014, p. 295, emphasis in original). This attitude is
beneficial for all students, both immigrant and non-immigrant. It may reduce the discrimination and bullying that immigrant students
often face and enable them to learn in an inclusive school environment. Given that inclusive school climate shaped by peers’ attitudes
toward immigrants enhances immigrant students’ sense of school belonging (Choi et al., 2025) and academic performance (Murrar
et al., 2020), this study suggests that school leaders can promote the academic success of immigrant students by ensuring that students
have various learning opportunities for GCE at school. Cultivating IATI is also important for all students, as they are expected to learn
and live alongside individuals from diverse cultures (Ladson-Billings, 2021). Due to globalization, students are increasingly likely to
transcend national boundaries and encounter international migrants in various contexts, including study, work, and travel. The

It’s noteworthy that the estimates of this study came from within-school analyses, using school-fixed effects. In other words, even
within the same school, students exhibit varying attitudes toward immigrants and interpret intergroup contact differently depending
on exposure to the number of different types of GCE. GCE in Korea has faced criticism for its lack of critical aspects and its limited view
of global competence and citizenship (Y. Kim, 2019). Teachers and principals often report that they lack sufficient experience to
accommodate student diversity in their preparation programs, particularly given the short history of immigration (Mo & Lim, 2013).
However, with the widespread implementation of GCE, professional development opportunities have been made provided to educators, helping them cultivate the skills and attitudes necessary to deliver instruction and manage schools in a more culturally
responsive and socially just manner (Choi & Lee, 2020). The results of this study further support the notion that educators play a role in
ensuring their teaching practices related to GCE are effective in fostering more inclusive attitudes toward immigrants among students.
Given the significant role of teachers in fostering this area, ongoing school-based support—such as professional development and
leadership practices—should be a key focus to ensure the effectiveness of GCE.
School leaders may offer students educational opportunities that immerse them in diverse cultures and perspectives through
different activities, such as debates, role plays, and collaborative projects on global issues (UNESCO, 2014). By doing so, schools can
help students cultivate inclusive attitudes that respect different cultures, people, and their rights, and bridge the gap between
immigrant and non-immigrant students. Meanwhile, it is essential for educators, including principals and teachers, to continue
enhancing their skills and knowledge to effectively convey the values and ideas of GCE and achieve positive outcomes (Wu & Li, 2023).
This is particularly important in the context of the global anti-diversity, equity, and inclusion movement, including in Korea, which
often discourages teachers and principals from addressing issues embedded in GCE (Kerkhoffa & Cloud, 2020). In this context, school
leaders need to not only adopt and arrange GCE for student learning but also act as buffers to ensure that educators feel safe to address
issues of diversity and immigration from external pressures (Kohansal, 2015).
This study presents somewhat mixed results regarding the interaction between GCE and intergroup contact in relation to IATI.
When it comes to intergroup contact at school, GCE can counteract the negative relationship between contact and IATI, but only when
students engage with all four types of GCE. This suggests that exposure to different types of GCE enables students to mitigate their
biases and stereotypes against immigrants, while encouraging them to benefit from intergroup interactions in enhancing their inclusive attitudes. Notably, this study detected the significant effect size of the interaction between GCE and intergroup contact at
school compared to that of cross-national analysis (Choi, 2024). This might be because students in Korea find contact with people from
other countries at school a relatively new experience and GCE effectively guides students in interpreting such new and uncertain
experiences positively.
In line with the literature (Choi, 2024; Tawagi & Mak, 2015), this study contributes to intergroup contact theory by demonstrating
the necessity of learning experiences, such as understanding diverse cultures, perspectives, and global issues, for students to cultivate
IATI. We often anticipate that “racially diverse educational settings are effective in reducing prejudice” (Wells et al., 2016, p. 9).

However, as the results indicate, intergroup contact or racial/ethnic diversity alone may not suffice. When accompanied by various
types of GCE, it can transform into an opportunity rather than a threat or conflict, allowing students to reflect on diversity and immigrants’ human rights as universal values. Diversity itself may not provide an opportunity to grow as global citizens, but students
need such educational experiences to reap the benefits of diversity. The study shows that GCE can be conducive for students to develop
IATI in a diverse school setting, extending beyond the narrow focus of the optimal conditions for intergroup contact toward broader
experiences (Dixon et al., 2005; Pettigrew, 2016). This study contributes to filling this gap by demonstrating that GCE can serve as a
gateway to positive intergroup outcomes since GCE follows a school-wide approach that does not necessarily occur within contact
situations (Goren & Yemini, 2017).
On the other hand, this study does not find significant interaction between GCE and intergroup contact in the community on
students’ IATI. Due to the nature of quantitative analysis, it is not possible to provide conclusive answers to explain this mechanism.
However, it is plausible that students find it challenging to contextualize community-based intergroup interactions with school experiences in GCE because they occur outside the school environment and due to the randomness of such interactions compared to
contact at school. If this scenario holds true, it suggests important implications for practice at school. Educational leaders and policymakers might consider integrating community-based intergroup contact into the GCE curriculum more intentionally, creating
structured opportunities for students to engage with diverse groups beyond the classrooms. By fostering connections between schoolbased learning and real-world interactions, schools can support students to contextualize their experiences and develop more inclusive
attitudes. Additionally, professional development for teachers and principals could emphasize strategies to facilitate discussions that
bridge these two environments, helping students make meaningful connections between their GCE experiences and community interactions. These approaches could ensure that students benefit from GCE in developing IATI while extending its application beyond
the school environment to a broader community context.
The implications of this study may extend to countries around the world, particularly in light of global issues such as xenophobia,
racism, and hate crimes (Elias et al., 2021; Freitag & Hofstetter, 2022). The findings may also be relevant to other Asian nations, such
as Japan and Taiwan, which have implemented strict immigration policies while facing challenges associated with an increase in
immigrants (Lan & Kim, 2025). Furthermore, the implications of this study are not confined to countries with high immigrant populations, as students are increasingly likely to engage with individuals from diverse backgrounds through study-abroad programs,
internships, and global workplaces in today’s global society.
5.1. Limitations and future directions
While this study makes significant contributions, it also has limitations that could inform future research. Firstly, due to the crosssectional nature of the data, this study can only provide correlational evidence on the relationships of interest, rather than causal
inference. The self-selection of respondents into GCE and intergroup contact may prevent the study from producing causal estimates.
Future research could provide more rigorous evidence on the effect of GCE on IATI and its interaction with intergroup contact by
utilizing experimental research designs and/or longitudinal data. Secondly, the measurement of the key variables may not perfectly
represent all their aspects. For instance, the GCE items might not cover every learning activity related to GCE, especially considering
that GCE encompasses a wide range of topics, such as critical reflection on the inequality and status quo of global structures. Moreover,
the use of dichotomous variables for intergroup contact might not capture nuanced details about the interaction. Future quantitative or
qualitative work should collect more detailed information to better understand the dynamics of the relationship between GCE,
intergroup contact, and IATI. Lastly, future research should investigate why intergroup contact in school and community interacts with
GCE in shaping IATI in different ways. Although this study provides potential explanations, further research is needed to produce more
conclusive evidence.
