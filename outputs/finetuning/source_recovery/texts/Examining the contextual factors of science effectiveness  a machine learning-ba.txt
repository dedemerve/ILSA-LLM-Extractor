ABSTRACT

ARTICLE HISTORY

This research intended to identify key contextual factors that
synergistically inﬂuence high- and low-performing students’ science
outcomes by drawing upon a dynamic model of educational
eﬀectiveness. The dataset, the Programme for International Student
Assessment (PISA) 2015, consisted of 79,963 science scores for
secondary students (49,924 high performers at proﬁciency Level 6
and 30,039 low performers at proﬁciency Levels 1a and 1b) from 53
countries/economies along with students’ and school principals’
responses to the PISA questionnaires. By applying a support vector
machine (SVM) and SVM-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE)
sequentially, this study successfully (a) identiﬁed 30 key factors of
the total 127 contextual factors at the school, classroom, and
student levels that synergistically diﬀerentiate high and low
achievers and (b) provided evidence to support the validity of the
dynamic model of educational eﬀectiveness by recognizing the
multidimensionality of the contextual factors.

Introduction
Educational eﬀectiveness research (EER) suggests the potential power of schools and education in changing students’ lives (Creemers & Kyriakides, 2008). Consensus has been
reached that educational eﬀectiveness encompasses not only the achievement level
obtained by individual students, classes, and schools (quality) but also the equitable distribution of learning outcomes among students with various background characteristics
(equity) (Kyriakides, Creemers, & Charalambous, 2019). It suggests that society (including
national/state agencies and schools/teachers) shoulders the main responsibility of providing extra learning opportunities and guidance for socially disadvantaged groups of students (Creemers & Kyriakides, 2008). EER, therefore, requires theoretical frameworks to
be expanded by including factors associated with both the quality and equity dimensions
of eﬀectiveness. The dynamic model of educational eﬀectiveness is proposed as such a
theoretical model (Creemers & Kyriakides, 2008).

The dynamic model of educational eﬀectiveness
The dynamic model of educational eﬀectiveness views schooling as a dynamic process in
which institutions can adapt to changing circumstances to achieve eﬀectiveness and

equity (Creemers & Kyriakides, 2010a). This model oﬀers an evidence-based, theory-driven
approach to school improvement by capturing the multilevel structure of factors shaping
school eﬀectiveness. Four levels of factors have been identiﬁed to inﬂuence students’ outcomes, namely, the context, school, classroom, and student levels. Each eﬀective factor is
viewed as a multidimensional construct that can be measured in terms of ﬁve dimensions:
frequency, focus, stage, quality, and diﬀerentiation (Creemer & Kyriakides, 2008).
A series of studies have provided support for the validity of the dynamic model (Caro
et al., 2016; Creemers & Kyriakides, 2009, 2010a, 2015; Kyriakides & Creemers, 2008;
Vanlaar et al., 2016). However, most research has been contextualized within countries,
leaving the generalizability of their research ﬁndings in question (Caro et al., 2016). Moreover, researchers have long realized the necessity of investigating characteristics associated with high and low achievement separately across countries, since (a) factors
associated with students’ high and low performance could be diﬀerent with possible
overlaps, and (b) there may exist national diﬀerences that could be interpreted with
respect to diﬀerences in educational systems, policies, and practices (Gilleece et al.,
2010). Therefore, this study intended to examine the validity of the dynamic model by
incorporating cross-national data to shed light on the issue of educational equity by identifying factors associated with top and low performers.
Recently, a number of international educational assessments such as the Programme for
International Student Assessment (PISA) and the Trends in International Mathematics and
Science Study (TIMSS) have evaluated the academic performance of students from
diﬀerent educational backgrounds, and their research results serve as indicators of the
eﬀectiveness status (Kyriakides, Creemers, & Charalambous, 2019). PISA, an inﬂuential international assessment of educational debates and policies, was adopted in this current study.
The domain subjects (science, mathematics, and reading) are examined by PISA following a
triennial cycle. PISA is reported to be a splendid source for testing the dynamic model
because it provides a large number of questions in the student and school questionnaires
related to teaching strategies and the instructional context in the classroom that align well
with the conceptual framework of educational eﬀectiveness research (Caro et al., 2016).
Even if a group of educational eﬀectiveness researchers have based their investigation
on the PISA dataset, the potential of the PISA dataset for the development of EER has
not yet been fully explored (e.g., Caro et al., 2016; Kyriakides, Creemers, & Charalambous,
2019; Nash, 2003; Thorpe, 2006). In this regard, this study intended to use the PISA 2015
dataset to provide evidence for the future development conceptual framework of EER.
Because classroom-level factors are given the most emphasis in the dynamic model to
reﬂect their great signiﬁcance in predicting educational outcomes (Kyriakides, Charalambous, Creemers, Antoniou, et al., 2019), this study prioritized classroom-level factors by
focusing on teacher behaviors that had a direct impact on instructional quality.

Classroom-level factors
Classroom happenings and instructions are considered more signiﬁcant in explaining
educational outcomes than school- or context-level factors (Kyriakides, Charalambous,
Creemers, Antoniou, et al., 2019). Empirical evidence on the classroom disciplinary
climate has suggested that (a) high achievers beneﬁted greatly from the classroom disciplinary climate, while the low and medium achievers did not (Ning, 2019), and (b) that the

disciplinary climate ranked among the most predictive factors in the students’ superior
performance in science (J. Chen et al., 2019).
In addition to the classroom environment, EER has attached great importance to the
impact of instructional strategies ranging from more traditional (i.e., direct and explicit
teaching or teacher-directed instruction) to more constructivist paradigms characterized
by student-oriented instruction, cooperative learning activities, and adaptions to students’ needs (Caro et al., 2016). Each of the teaching paradigms has accumulated a
great number of supportive ﬁndings and arguments, leaving the eﬀectiveness of both
somewhat inconclusive (Connor et al., 2004). As pointed out by Caro et al. (2016), it is
more realistic to acknowledge that the eﬀects of instructional strategies are related to
a variety of factors, such as student, classroom, and school characteristics.
Regarding students’ performance levels, there exists a general trend that low-achieving students beneﬁt more from the traditional paradigm than the top ones, while their
top-performing cohorts achieve more learning gains from the constructivism paradigm
than the low ones (Caro et al., 2016; Connor et al., 2004). It may be reasonable to
assume that low-achieving students lack motivation as well as the self-regulatory and
higher order thinking skills that are required for student-oriented instruction (Caro
et al., 2016; Connor et al., 2004).

School-level factors
It is commonly recognized that school matters importantly not only in improving students’ academic performance but also in promoting economic and social equity (e.g.,
Chudgar & Luschei, 2009; Jennings et al., 2015; Rivkin et al., 2005). School-level factors
are expected to have both direct eﬀects and indirect inﬂuences on student achievement.
In other words, embedded in schools, the functions of classes and teaching practices are
largely inﬂuenced by school-level factors. They could inﬂuence the teaching-learning situation through two types of policies, namely, teaching policies and the policy on creating a
learning environment at school (Creemers & Kyriakides, 2010b).
School-based interventions, as explored in a plethora of investigations, are extremely
important in promoting educational eﬀectiveness (e.g., Kyriakides, Charalambous, Creemers, & Dimosthenous, 2019; Kyriakides, Creemers, & Charalambous, 2019). Echoing
this viewpoint, concern is raised about the eﬀects of the dynamic approach to school
improvement, a program based on the dynamic model of school eﬀectiveness that provides schools with a series of developments. The results show that these school-level
factors, including the learning environment, school policy for teaching, and school evaluation, have a great impact on both the quality and equity in schools from socially disadvantaged areas (Kyriakides, Charalambous, Creemers, & Dimosthenous, 2019; Kyriakides,
Creemers, & Charalambous, 2019).
In addition, school climate is predicted to be one of the most signiﬁcant factors of
school eﬀectiveness because the main function schools provide is teaching (Linnakyla
et al., 2004). Moreover, EER has shown that schools with high eﬀectiveness could
promptly satisfy the educational needs of both teachers and students and could be
more ﬂexible in making systematic changes of the school’s internal mechanism to
achieve educational goals better under uncertain conditions (Harris, 2001). It is also proposed that attention should be paid to other dimensions of school-level factors rather

than the frequency of the interventions, which has been used preponderantly in previous
EER studies (Teddlie & Reynolds, 2000).

Student-level factors
The eﬀects of student-level factors on educational eﬀectiveness can never be diminished,
since students have the ﬁnal say in terms of how much time and energy they devote to
their academic work (Kyriakides, 2008). An interactive eﬀect has been identiﬁed between
student achievement and a long list of student characteristics, including but not restricted
to socioeconomic status (SES), gender, motivation, and expectations (Creemers & Kyriakides, 2010a).
Generally, students with low parental SES have a lower probability of being a high
achiever (Ma et al., 2016; Sirin, 2005) because low SES not only means fewer opportunities
to learn (Schmidt et al., 2015) but also negatively inﬂuences students’ mindsets, causing
ﬁxed beliefs about learning abilities (Destin et al., 2019). However, the association
between SES and academic achievement has been somewhat inconclusive, given that
diﬀerent combinations of variables are used as indicators of SES (O’Connell, 2019).
Gender inequality in educational outcomes has also been intensively discussed. Male students, as the advantaged group in mathematics and science, enjoy higher ratios in the
top-performing end (Gilleece et al., 2010).
Interaction between measures of teaching quality and measures of other student
characteristics has also been demonstrated. For example, motivation to learn has been
found to be signiﬁcantly related to academic achievement (e.g., Ainley & Ainley, 2011;
Buehl & Alexander, 2005). Expectations have also been identiﬁed as strong predictors
of career success in early adulthood, regardless of gender (Feliciano & Rumbaut, 2005).
Despite the extensive list of factors discussed, student-level factors associated with academic outcomes are not restricted to the list.
Overall, EER has gradually uncovered the multidimensionality of educational eﬀectiveness and built a repertoire of related factors. Nevertheless, there are still gaps in EER that
need to be ﬁlled. First, EER was subjected to criticism due to a lack of cross-cultural perspectives (Teddlie & Reynolds, 2000). For instance, most current scholarly works take
national samples with restricted amounts of variability, only to ﬁnd a series of countryspeciﬁc eﬀectiveness factors that cannot be applied to other cultures or educational
systems. In contrast, cross-cultural studies could help to unveil the curvilinear relations
and signiﬁcant interactions between eﬀectiveness factors. Researchers have thus called
for the international educational dataset to further support the validity of eﬀectiveness
models (Creemers, 2006). PISA 2015, a cross-national survey where 72 countries and economies converged, can be eﬃcient for both theory-based and data-driven research testing
the quality dimension of the dynamic model.
Second, few diﬀerential studies on students with diﬀerent proﬁciency levels have simultaneously incorporated a great number of factors at diﬀerent levels. A series of studies
using conventional methods have meticulously discussed eﬀectiveness factors at the individual level (e.g., Kyriakides, Creemers, & Charalambous, 2019; Ortega et al., 2018;
Sammons et al., 2013), yet leaving the synergistic eﬀects of a group of variables unresolved. The innate complexities of the dynamic model indicate that it is not a single
factor but a group of factors that collectively promote eﬀective teaching (Creemers &

Kyriakides, 2008; Reynolds et al., 2014). In addition, it is argued that studies taking multilevel factors into account can be less susceptible to the threat of Type I error, aggregation
bias, and undetected heterogeneity of regression (Aitkin & Longford, 1986; de Leeuw &
Kreft, 1986; Goldstein, 2003; Raudenbush & Bryk, 1986, 1988). The provision of a more
comprehensive picture of the eﬀectiveness factors to better support the dynamic
model is thus needed. Currently, machine learning algorithms have been utilized in
many studies to examine students’ science performance (e.g., Filiz, & Öz, 2019; Kilic
Depren, 2018; Liu & Whitford, 2011; She et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2012). Speciﬁcally, as one
of the top machine learning algorithms, the support vector machine (SVM), which
excels in identifying the combined eﬀects of high-order factors, has been successfully
used in the evaluation of students’ academic performance in the PISA context (e.g.,
J. Chen et al., 2019, 2021; Gorostiaga & Rojo-Álvarez, 2016). Thus, a dearth of examination
of a plethora of eﬀectiveness variables and the application of machine learning algorithms to the EER research ﬁeld have attracted great research interest. Therefore, this
study not only utilized the variable-abundant international educational dataset of PISA
2015 to compensate for the lack of cross-cultural perspectives in the current studies of
EER but also introduced a novel machine learning algorithm of the SVM to identify the
eﬀects of a large number of factors/variables contributing to science eﬀectiveness.

Research questions
This study employed a machine learning approach, that is, the SVM, by selecting the
optimal set of key factors/features/variables from a total of 127 contextual factors/variables to explore the synergistic (combined/collective) impact of the optimal feature set
(key features) on high- and low-performing achievers of science literacy. Therefore,
three research questions were discussed:
(1) Can the contextual factors of EER be identiﬁed to diﬀerentiate high performers of
science literacy from low performers through the machine learning algorithm of
the SVM?
(2) What are the key features of science eﬀectiveness that synergistically aﬀect students’
high and low performance in secondary science education?
(3) Does the identiﬁcation of these key features support the multidimensionality of the
dynamic model of educational eﬀectiveness?

Methods
Data
The sample was taken from the authoritative global educational dataset PISA 2015 with
72 participating countries/economies (https://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/2015database/).
In this research, 53 countries/regions were selected due to the missing and void values
in the data of the 19 remaining countries/regions that were caused by technical error.
Overall, 79,963 ﬁfteen-year-olds consisting of 44,217 males and 35,746 females were
selected from PISA 2015 (data available upon request from the authors), with 49,924
high performers successfully meeting the requirement of proﬁciency Level 6 with a

Variables
The dependent variable of the study was dichotomous: high performance with
science scores at Level 6 and low performance scores at Levels 1a and 1b. According
to PISA 2015, 10 plausible values (PVs) were used to show participants’ science performance; however, “there are no signiﬁcant diﬀerences between using one plausible
value or ﬁve plausible values, due to the large number of the samples” (OECD, 2009,
p. 48). Therefore, PV1SCIE (the ﬁrst plausible value 1 in Science) was randomly
selected to represent each student’s science score, which is a fair and accurate
method for estimating their real science literacy (J. Chen et al., 2019; Gorostiaga &
Rojo-Álvarez, 2016).
The independent factors/variables extracted from the student questionnaire and
school questionnaire were analyzed at three levels, namely, at the school, classroom,
and student levels. Some variables were directly extracted from the original questions,
while others were derived variables that were developed by PISA 2015 from a combination of diﬀerent questions. The PISA 2015 technical report (OECD, 2016) provided
the reliability measure for the scale scores and the detailed information needed for the
extraction and construction of each variable, which are also provided in Table 2. The
coeﬃcient ranges between 0 and 1, with higher values indicating more reliable data
(0.9 for excellent, 0.8 for good, and 0.7 for acceptable reliability). It is noted that most
of the scale reliabilities (Cronbach’s alpha coeﬃcients) of variables in student and
school questionnaires are above 0.7, indicating a remarkable reliability of the variables
(OECD, 2016).

Statistical models
Reasons for choosing the SVM approach
The commonly used conventional methods within EER in terms of detecting eﬀectiveness
factors include multilevel modeling (e.g., Kelcey & Shen, 2016; Kyriakides, Creemers, &
Charalambous, 2019; Thorpe, 2006), structural equation modeling (SEM; e.g., Sammons
et al., 2013), and growth curve modeling (e.g., Verachtert et al., 2009). These classic and
powerful analytical methods have been shown to ﬁt well with the multilevel setting of
the learning environment and the complex nature of the dynamic model (i.e., students
embedded in classes within schools), having yielded substantial achievements for the
development of EER. In addition, it has been noted that multilevel analysis can be
eﬃcient for EER (e.g., Creemers et al., 2010; Kyriakides & Luyten, 2009; Mugendawala &
Muijs, 2020; van Hek et al., 2018) because of its strong ability to decode the latent complexity of hierarchical data with high accuracy of estimation (Cools et al., 2009; Hill &
Rowe, 1996; Snijders & Bosker, 2012). For instance, multiple regression models (MLRs)
are frequently adopted in EER to evaluate the impacts of explanatory variables on students’ performance. (Fariña et al., 2015; Snijders & Bosker, 2012), in which linear relationships among a number of factors in multiple sets of data can be analyzed intuitively and
promptly (Xiao et al., 2019). However, in regard to large sample sizes and multiple features, the performance of MLRs is negatively inﬂuenced, especially considering their
inability to deal with high-order interaction eﬀects and the problem of multicollinearity
(Strobl et al., 2009). In addition, data in real situations often consist of multiple data

Despite the numerous merits that classical statistical methods are endowed with, the
support vector machine (SVM) is more eﬀective for problems with large sample sizes and
feature sizes with high-order dimensions. Moreover, the combination of the SVM with
recursive feature elimination (RFE) could enable the simultaneous discovery of multiple
features that collectively determine classiﬁcation, and its superiority has been well established in PISA-based studies (e.g., J. Chen et al., 2019, 2021; Gorostiaga & Rojo-Álvarez,
2016). As an eﬀective machine learning algorithm for classiﬁcation, the SVM has recently
garnered massive popularity in academia and beyond due to its high performance in precisely recognizing underlying patterns from a myriad of variables without strong preconceptions (Larose & Larose, 2014), which is ranked among the top machine learning
methods in statistics due to its originality and practicality (Dosenbach et al., 2010).
Being robust and accurate in classiﬁcation, the SVM excels by its stable predictive performance (e.g., Shafri & Ramle 2009; Shao & Lunetta, 2012), high reproducibility (e.g.,
Chang & Lin, 2011; Fan et al., 2005), and strong ability to detect eﬀects collectively triggered by diﬀerent features (e.g., Dosenbach et al., 2010). Hence, considering the
complex nature of the dynamic model and the advantage of incorporating multilevel variables, the SVM was selected in this study as the combination of theory-based and datadriven techniques.
Admittedly, there are also some recognized drawbacks when utilizing the SVM. It is not
an optimal method when dealing with a small sample size containing low variable dimensions because it takes a long time to deal with the data and produce the ﬁnal results;
additionally, the noise of the data can also exert negative eﬀects on its eﬃciency and performance (Dosenbach et al., 2010; Jing et al., 2015). Furthermore, considering the transparency of methods, the computational process of the SVM is within sight but beyond
reach to the users (X. Wei & Li, 2010); however, this concern may be alleviated, as this
“black box” may become a “glass box” with the development of interpretable artiﬁcial
intelligence (Holzinger, 2018). Since the advantages of the SVM outweighed its disadvantages, this study applies the SVM to simultaneously analyze 127 contextual factors
extracted from the PISA 2015 questionnaires and to further identify the key school eﬀectiveness features that diﬀerentiate top performers from their low-performing cohorts; this
identiﬁcation can be accomplished by examining the three-dimensional factors in the
PISA dataset, namely, student-level, classroom-level, and school-level factors.

SVM modeling and evaluation
The SVM, as a classic epitome and indispensable statistical tool, is an eﬃcient and ﬂexible
machine learning model of binary classiﬁcation. This function is realized by creating a
multidimensional hyperplane when mapping input data to a feature space with kernel
functions, thus optimally dividing the data into two categories of descriptors (Cortes &
Vapnik, 1995; Y. Wei et al., 2018). Basically, the SVM classiﬁer aims to produce the hyperplane that possesses the maximum margin. For an intuitive understanding of this algorithm, please see an example in Appendix 1.
The classiﬁcation ability of the SVM model can be exhibited through the scores of
various commonly used indicators, including the measurements for its accuracy (ACC),
sensitivity (SEN), speciﬁcity (SPE), F measure, and area under the receiver operating
characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). Below are the formulas for producing these indicators
along with some brief clariﬁcations of their functions and roles, in which TP (true positives)

ACC is the metric that is most widely used to evaluate the classiﬁcation performance of

extent of balance (Huang & Ling, 2005). The combination of the two metrics with other
indicators is used in this research to provide a multilevel and fair assessment of the
SVM model.

SVM-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE)
To identify the major factors of school eﬀectiveness that can distinguish between the two
cohorts of performers, SVM-RFE is adopted in this study because it exhibits more resistance to data overﬁtting than other feature selection techniques (e.g., Saeys et al., 2007;
Y. Wei et al., 2018). The main idea of the RFE algorithm is to use a base model (the
SVM speciﬁcally) for multiround training. After each round of training, the feature is
scored according to the coeﬃcient of each feature, the feature with the lowest score is
removed, and then a new feature set is constructed with the remaining features for the
next round of training until all the features are removed.
The operation of SVM-RFE includes the following: First, it removes the factors that contribute least to the results iteratively. Second, the process continues until all of the factors

31

are carried oﬀ. Finally, the model produces a list of factors that take sequence according to
their relative importance to the results. After these procedures, several additional SVM
models are developed based on the sequence, the performance of which can then be
compared with the original factor set with all factors. In this investigation, SVM-RFE
was used to score the 127 eﬀectiveness factors and to rank them according to their
inﬂuence on the classiﬁcation of high and low performers. The basic working process
of SVM-RFE is provided in Figure 1.

Cross-validation
As a practical approach that facilitates the predictive performance of the model, crossvalidation allows for evaluating the generalization ability of machine learning algorithms
to data sets. K-fold cross-validation is the main method used for identifying the ﬁnest
hyperparameters to ensure the accurate performance of the SVM model (Li et al.,
2013). In particular, the ﬁvefold cross-validation used in this study has proven to be
eﬀective when predicting the results (James et al., 2013) and has been commonly used
as empirical heuristics (Mou et al., 2014). This validation involves randomly dividing the
data into ﬁve groups (folds) of subsets. Four folds of data (also called the “training
folds”) are randomly selected to produce a model for prediction, which is then validated
on the remaining fold of data (also called the “test fold”).

is, three-step data preprocessing and three-step data processing.

The three-step data preprocessing, which is indispensable for the next processing,
starts from data pruning/cleaning, followed by data imputation and data normalization.
In the ﬁrst step, missing and invalid data from students’ and schools’ questionnaires
were discarded. The second step entailed the Python programming language Version
3.7.0 (https://www.python.org/) to impute the data once deleted with the nearest-neighbor mean value (Gorostiaga & Rojo-Álvarez, 2016). In the third step, ordinal variables were
converted into dummy variables, and the centering and variance scaling principle comes
in handy to normalize the data.
With the assistance of Python 3.7.0, the data were then analyzed in three steps. First,
the SVM classiﬁer was utilized to process the dataset with 127 factors, to verify whether
the SVM was able to diﬀerentiate high- and low-performing students and to tune the parameter that could best satisfy the classiﬁcation need. Second, SVM-RFE was performed to
reorder the 127 contextual factors according to their weights in the ﬁnal classiﬁcation
endeavor. Finally, a ﬁvefold cross-validation (Mou et al., 2014) was adopted, with four sections randomly acting as the training data and the remaining section being evaluated to
yield the most suitable model, thus evaluating all the SVM classiﬁcation models and determining the optimal model for this study.

Discussion
The current study contributed to the ongoing discussion on educational eﬀectiveness by
adopting the dynamic model of educational eﬀectiveness and a machine learning technique of the SVM. The dynamic model served as a theoretical foundation for selecting
eﬀective factors associated with educational equity, while the key factors that diﬀerentiate top performers from low achievers were identiﬁed through the SVM, which was supposedly a relatively accurate prediction model. The identiﬁcation of the top 30 features
that synergistically diﬀerentiate the two groups of students provided evidence to
support the validity of the dynamic model of educational eﬀectiveness, given that the
key features were distributed across three diﬀerent levels speciﬁed in the dynamic
model of educational eﬀectiveness, namely, the school, classroom, and the student levels.

Quantity of instruction: student truancy
One feature nested under the quantity of teaching is the extent to which student learning
is hindered by “student truancy” (SC061Q01TA); this particular factor stands out as the top
school-level factor that predicts high or low performance. Student truancy not only leads
to less academic instruction but is also closely related to low levels of school responsiveness to student academic issues and insuﬃcient disciplinary responses to truancy (Appleton et al., 2008; Flannery et al., 2012). As conﬁrmed by previous research, students from
schools with higher unexcused absence rates underperformed compared with students
from schools with a lower proportion of truant students (Kearney, 2008). However, it
remains to be clariﬁed whether truancy causes low achievement or low achievement
causes truancy (Mills et al., 2019).
School resources: science-speciﬁc resources and the internet
Among various school resources, the analysis underlined the inﬂuences of “sciencespeciﬁc resources” (SCIERES) and “computers connected to internet” (RATCMP2).
Science-speciﬁc resources “have been found to actively promote, develop, and sustain”
students’ science interest (Archer et al., 2015, p. 924). The frequency of using computers
connected to the internet has also been found to be positively associated with students’
academic performance (X. Chen & Hu, 2020; Xiao & Hu, 2019; Xiao et al., 2019). Students
attending schools with fewer resources are usually from families with lower incomes.
Teacher collaboration
Teachers engaging in collaboration tend to employ more innovative pedagogies and
demonstrate a higher level of job satisfaction and self-eﬃcacy (European Commission,
2014). Even though our result consolidates the strong relationship between teacher collaboration and students’ academic achievement, whether certain types of teacher collaboration, which is a multidimensional concept, have a greater impact on students’ learning
cannot be deduced from the current analysis (Hu et al., 2020).
Partnership of school with community, parents, and experts
Many researchers view community involvement (SC063Q07NA) as an important factor
because (a) resources solely from schools and families may not serve academic achievement suﬃciently and (b) community involvement contributes to improvements in enrollment, the maintenance of school facilities, and the learning environment (Essuman, 2019).
Future studies adopting a qualitative methodology may provide more in-depth discussion concerning the eﬀectiveness of diﬀerent approaches to community involvement.
Parents’ involvement (SC063Q03NA) has been found to contribute to increased attendance, fewer disciplinary issues, and increased achievement (Epstein, 2001; González &
Jackson, 2013). Drawing upon Epstein’s (2001) parental involvement framework, González
and Jackson (2013) found that diﬀerent types of school eﬀorts to engage parents

inﬂuenced students’ achievement diﬀerently. Researchers can also advance research on
the diﬀerential predictive power of various types of school eﬀorts to engage parents
by employing a machine learning technique.
In some countries, school improvement also occurs by taking suggestions from experts
(SC037Q09TA), including inspectors, policy makers, educational consultants, or researchers (Reezigt & Creemers, 2005). However, in centralized educational systems, such as Portugal and Italy, teachers tend to perceive external experts as unimportant or even invasive
(Reezigt & Creemers, 2005). The current ﬁnding urges schools to create a favorable culture
for consultation with experts, especially schools contextualized in more centralized educational systems.

Values in favor of learning
Regarding strategies that schools use to encourage students to develop positive attitudes
toward learning, measures in promoting equity (SC040Q17NA) and considerations of students’ feedback (SC037Q07TA) rank among the key features that distinguish top- and lowperforming students. It is reasonable to assume that schools that exert greater eﬀorts in
promoting equity may more eﬀectively improve the academic outcome of deprived
groups (Kyriakides, Charalambous, Creemers, & Dimosthenous, 2019). The role of students’ feedback in school improvement has long been underrepresented, and our
research ﬁndings draw special attention to it.
Classroom-level factors
The top predictive features at the classroom level belong to four factors categorized as
teachers’ instructional behaviors (“structuring”, “modeling”, “questioning”, “application”)
and one factor nested under the classroom as learning environment (perceived teacher
fairness).

Teachers’ instructional behaviors
According to the dynamic model, the factors related to teachers’ instructional behaviors
do not “refer only to one approach of teaching such as structured or direct teaching or to
approaches associated with constructivism” (Creemers & Kyriakides, 2009, p. 296). The
ﬁndings of this study also suggest that a combination of teacher-directed and studentoriented instruction is associated with the categorization of top and low performance,
contrasting drastically with the widely accepted assumption that student-oriented
instruction beneﬁts top performers more than low performers, while teacher-directed
instructions are more appropriate for low achievers than top performers (Hu, 2014).
The results uncovered the very speciﬁc instructional strategies that predict students’
achievement levels by suggesting that teachers of top-performing students deliberately
adapt the instructional structure to students’ levels more frequently (“structuring”, i.e.,
ST107Q03NA); furthermore, these teachers devote a larger amount of instructional time
to the following: (a) explaining how a school science idea can be applied to a number
of diﬀerent phenomena and the relevance of broad science concepts to daily lives (“modeling”, i.e., ST098Q09TA and ST098Q06TA); (b) posing questions to involve students in
drawing conclusions from an experiment they have conducted (“questioning”, i.e.,
ST098Q05TA); (c) providing feedback (i.e., PERFEED); and (d) engaging students in

J. HU ET AL.

designing, discussing, and conducting investigations (“application”, i.e., ST098Q07TA,
ST098Q08NA, and ST098Q10NA).

The classroom as a learning environment: teacher fairness
Two dimensions of perceived teacher fairness, distributive (i.e., fairness of grades) and
interactional injustice (i.e., fairness of students’ communication with teachers), were
probed in PISA 2015. Diﬀerent from most previous research suggesting a signiﬁcant negative relationship between perceived teacher unfairness and students’ learning outcomes
(e.g., Molinari et al., 2013), F. Chen and Cui (2020) identiﬁed little contribution of perceived
teacher unfairness to science achievement, given its small eﬀect size. The current ﬁndings,
nevertheless, re-emphasize the importance of perceived teacher fairness and suggest that
more in-depth investigations are needed, particularly to help diﬀerentiate between distributive and interactional injustice, for example.

Student-level factors
At the student level, gender, SES, expectations, and subject motivation were identiﬁed as
key factors predicting academic achievement.

Gender
Consistent with previous research, the current study also ranked gender among the
factors that are strongly associated with educational inequality. This result, on the basis
of students’ science performance, unveils the severity of the gender gap in education,
since the gender gap in science is actually the smallest (J. Chen et al., 2019). Since stereotypes are responsible for gender diﬀerences in beliefs about academic capability, interest
in subjects, and academic performance (Reardon et al., 2019), the result further underlined
the necessity of a wide rejection of gender stereotypes.
SES
The results identiﬁed home possessions (HOMEPOS), home wealth possessions (WEALTH),
index of economic, social and cultural status (ESCS), highest education of parents
(HISCED), cultural possessions (CULTPOSS), mother’s occupation (BMMIJ1), and immigration status (IMMIG) as factors that clearly distinguish high and low performance. Since
cognitive ability and noncognitive characteristics (e.g., conscientiousness, perseverance,
and emotional stability) associated with educational success are heritable (Plomin &
Deary, 2015; Turkheimer et al., 2014), high-SES parents possessing these characteristics
are more likely to pass these characteristics to their next generation than low-SES
parents. Similar to previous research that draws special attention to mothers when examining SES (J. Chen et al., 2019), this study also identiﬁed mother’s occupation as a stronger
factor in predicting high and low performance compared with father’s occupation. The
analysis also suggests that immigrant students have a consistent advantage over their
native-born peers in educational outcomes, lending support to the “immigrant optimism”
hypothesis (Kao & Tienda, 1995), which states that immigrant parents hold high expectations for their children and tend to communicate higher aspirations to their children.

Expectations
Expectations, including occupational expectations (BSMJ) and educational expectations
(ST111Q01TA) in the PISA context, are viewed as psychological resources that support
an individual’s decision for further schooling (Feliciano & Rumbaut, 2005). In addition
to further verifying the predictive power of occupational expectations, the current
result ranked educational expectations at the top of all features, indicating an underestimation of the importance of educational eﬀectiveness in EER.
Subject motivation
Among these science-related noncognitive outcomes, enjoyment of science (JOYSCIE)
and epistemological beliefs (EPIST) emerged as leading factors that distinguish high performers and low performers. The predictive power of both factors has been well supported in the previous literature (e.g., Ainley & Ainley, 2011; Buehl & Alexander, 2005).
It is worth noting that the impacts of diﬀerent dimensions of epistemological beliefs
tend to diﬀer (Mason et al., 2013), which can be addressed in future endeavors.

Conclusion
In conclusion, the present study utilized the large-scale and variable-rich international
educational dataset – PISA 2015 – and a machine learning algorithm – SVM – to identify
the synergistic eﬀects of a group of eﬀectiveness factors, oﬀering new perspectives on
the development of the theoretical framework for educational researchers, policy
makers, and educational practitioners. For educational researchers, the current research
contributes to the development of the well-recognized, widely used, and far-reaching
dynamic model of educational eﬀectiveness. This is the ﬁrst investigation to identify
the collective eﬀects of an array of pronounced EER variables, thus promoting the
renewal and modiﬁcation of the dynamic model of educational eﬀectiveness (Creemers,
& Kyriakides, 2008; Kyriakides, 2008). In addition, a few previously underestimated or
underrepresented EER variables, including but not limited to students’ expectations
and students’ feedback, have been brought special attention in this research. For
policy makers, the optimal set of eﬀectiveness factors provides guidance for the
improvement of educational systems, indicating that the top design of education
should not only be inclusive but also attach great importance to the complex dynamics
of the educational environment. Educational practitioners, principals, and teachers
might use the results as a tool to monitor the process of eﬀectiveness improvement
and promote students’ academic performance. For instance, this study highlighted
the importance of the combination of both teacher-directed and student-oriented
instruction in enhancing students’ performance, which provides clues for the future
practice of classroom instruction.

Research limitations
The ﬁndings of this research need to be understood with the consideration of a few
caveats. First, even though the dynamic model of educational eﬀectiveness postulates
that each eﬀective factor is a multidimensional construct that can be measured
through frequency, focus, stage, quality, and diﬀerentiation (Creemers & Kyriakides,

42

J. HU ET AL.

2008), the design of the PISA questionnaires only provides measures of the frequency
for many eﬀective factors, thus slightly weakening the explanatory power of the
results.
Second, this study only centers on the dimension of quality rather than the dimension
of equity of the dynamic model, so the results cannot evaluate the model from a comprehensive perspective. Diﬀerent from the quality dimension, the equity dimension in EER
refers to the compensatory power of schools to narrow the performance gaps between
students (Strand, 2010). It is suggested that these two seemingly contradictory but inherently symbiotic dimensions should be evaluated simultaneously and combined with the
dynamic model to provide more reliable results (Kyriakides, Creemers, & Charalambous,
2019). Future research should thus target speciﬁed school groupings (e.g., students
from socioeconomically disadvantaged homes or students with special needs) and
make comparisons between students’ performances within schools for deeper knowledge
of eﬀectiveness factors.
Third, the secondary analysis is haunted by the nature of the PISA dataset, which
draws upon cross-sectional evidence within a speciﬁc period of time and responses
based on an individual’s experience and memory (Agirdag & Vanlaar, 2018). Being
cross-sectional, the PISA data can only evaluate students’ short-term learning outcomes,
so students’ overall development cannot be measured in the long run (Agirdag &
Vanlaar, 2018). In addition, similar to other large-scale international educational assessments, the PISA dataset is a collection of self-reported responses rather than real observations (Soh, 2014). Future research is suggested to make eﬀorts to adopt more reliable
data collected through longitudinal studies while maintaining the magnitude of the
dataset.
Fourth, although the match between the dynamic model and the PISA dataset has
been discussed in a series of previous studies, it is still insuﬃcient to achieve the
point-to-point correspondence between the variables in the dynamic model and
the PISA questionnaires. In the future, a large-scale assessment aiming for educational eﬀectiveness should be conducted to provide more tailored and reliable
variables for EER.
Fifth, the machine learning technique employed in the current research was unable to
establish causal relationships between eﬀective factors and students’ academic outcomes
not only because of the nature of the PISA data but also because of the innate limitation
of the method. The SVM can only evaluate the diﬀerentiation degree through the optimization of the feature set, which meant that this analysis could only provide classiﬁcation
and synergetic eﬀect conclusions, while assumptions about cause and eﬀect could not be
detected in this study. In future research, the clear visualization of the optimal feature set
produced by the SVM allows for a further deep investigation into the impact directions
and causal eﬀects by working with other statistical tools, such as a structural equation
model and multilevel path analysis (e.g., whether the use of science-speciﬁc resources
is mediated by students’ subject motivation or whether the eﬀects of teacher-directed
instruction are mediated by gender).

watermelon based on its edibility using three descriptive features

Suppose that there is a data set of eight watermelons with the three descriptive features of
“Texture”, “Color” and “Sound”. Each sample can be described by these three features. The
texture of the watermelon (i.e., “evident” or “obscure”), the rind color (i.e., “dark-green” or “jetblack”) and the sound it makes when we knock the watermelon (i.e., “clear” or “dull”). In the
support vector machine (SVM), each subject should be labeled to indicate its class (i.e., if the watermelon is edible, it is labeled “1”; if the watermelon goes bad, it is labeled “2”). In addition, the
descriptions of the three features were coded as “1” and “2”, respectively. Therefore, Table A1 provides a comprehensive illustration of the classes of each studied subject and detailed descriptions of
these subjects based on the two features. In Table A2, the classes of each subject and the coded
descriptions of these 10 subjects based on three features are shown.

(1, 1, 2), 2 (1, 1, 1), etc. Then, the SVM projects all subjects (vectors) into a mathematical space of N
dimensions (N is deﬁned by the total number of features – in this case, N = 3). Because it is a threedimensional problem, all eight subjects could be projected into three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates.
