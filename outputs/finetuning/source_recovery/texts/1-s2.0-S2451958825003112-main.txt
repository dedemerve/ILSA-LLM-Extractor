This study employs machine learning approaches to examine how socio-demographic, student-related, and
school-related variables predict the computational thinking (CT) performance of 5211 Taiwanese eighth graders
in the ICILS 2023 study (Fraillon, 2024). It further aims to identify the key predictors of Taiwanese students’ CT
scores in this international evaluation project. The study used seven trained models: Multinomial Logistic
Regression, Random Forest, AdaBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM, Gradient Boosting classifier, and Stacking Ensemble
to identify and rank the variables that affect CT scores. The CT performance score was used as a binary variable
with two classes: below and above average score. Findings showed that XGBoost and Stacking Ensemble performed best when classifying below and average CT scores respectively in terms of precision, recall and F1 score.
In addition, among the variables, student-related variables had the highest impact on students’ CT skills followed
by school-related and socio-demographic. Among student-related variables, CT disposition was the most significant variable followed by ICT self-efficacy and academic multitasking. Further, among school-related factor,
learning special applications in class had significant impact followed by a low impact of socio-demographic
variables such as home literacy and parents’ education. This study offers practical implications for educators,
policymakers, and curriculum designers by underscoring the role of CT disposition and recommending targeted
support for enhancing students’ digital self-efficacy. Additionally, the study shows the potential of ML for
creating adaptive learning environments and guiding data-informed decisions in educational policy and practice.

1. Introduction
As suggested by Wing (2006), Computational Thinking (CT) implies
students should learn to “think like a computer scientist”. Recently,
Ezeamuzie & Leung (2022) found that CT has been used to solve problems in different fields, not just computer science. CT is important in
education as it helps students to become better problem solvers, which is
the core of learning (Gagné, 1985). Educators agree that
problem-solving is one of the valuable skills of life (Jonassen, 2000).
Wing (2006) argued that CT may be considered as a crucial skill similar
to basic skills like reading writing and arithmetic skills. Although, some
may disagree with the idea, there may be several positives associated
with learning CT. Multiple studies have shown that studying CT helps
improve communication, social skills, confidence, self-management,
and collaboration (Denner et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2024). In addition,
learning CT can also positively influence mathematical thinking,

reasoning, creativity, and language skills (Scherer et al., 2019).
CT has become an important topic in education, especially among k12 students (Kong & Lai, 2023). Research has shown that CT supports
the development of both personal and interpersonal skills such as
self-regulation, communication, social interaction, confidence and
collaboration (Denner et al., 2019; Popat & Starkey, 2019). In addition,
CT is also positively associated with cognitive processes such as mathematical thinking, language literacy, reasoning, creativity, and metacognition (Scherer et al., 2019). These advantages have led to the
integration of CT into major curricular reforms. For example, in the
United States’ Next Generation Science Standards (National Research
Council, 2013) and in multiple curriculums across Europe (Palop et al.,
2025). Further, CT has also gained recognition in international assessment frameworks such as International Student Assessment (PISA) and
the International Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS).

These developments not only show that CT is now a central topic in

2022). At the school and pedagogical category, studies have pointed to
the importance of classroom environments (Jha et al., 2024; Noh & Lee,

into the curriculum (Hsu et al., 2018; Lye & Koh, 2014; Scherer et al.,

highlighted the growing role of ML in analyzing such predictors,
showing how advanced models are able to capture complex interaction
among variables and support data-driven educational policy (Ersozlu
et al., 2024; Sanusi et al., 2023).
Recently, prior studies also reported that CT disposition (a studentrelated factor) was a strong predictor of students’ CT performance
scores (Lee et al., 2023a; Tsai et al., 2022a). The core components of CT
disposition such as decomposition, abstraction, algorithmic thinking,
evaluation and generalization (Tsai et al., 2021, 2022b) are linked to the
factors explored in this study. For instance, students with strong
self-efficacy and CT dispositions are more capable of applying decomposition and abstraction when approaching complex problems (Lee
et al., 2023b). Likewise, school environments that promote ICT integration and digital literacy can strengthen students’ algorithmic
thinking and evaluation skills (Lee et al., 2023a). Although, the correlation between CTDS and CT performance has been confirmed in prior
research, it has not been examined with other variables together at a
time.
In summary, most studies examine only one category of variables at a
time. Also, the majority rely on traditional regression approaches, which
may not capture the complex and non-linear relationships among multiple predictors (Alhassan et al., 2020). To address these gaps, the present study uses supervised machine learning to systematically examine
the combined effects of socio-demographic, student, and school-related
factors. By applying ML models to the large-scale ICILS 2023 dataset, the
study aims to more comprehensively predict students’ CT outcomes by
linking core CT constructs with different categories of variables.

Since then, several frameworks have been proposed to guide CT
education, including those by Barr & Stephenson (2011), Csizmadia
et al. (2015), Brennan & Resnick (2012), Selby & Woollard (2013),
Weintrop et al. (2016), and Shute et al. (2017). While these models differ

challenges for comparing research and assessment (Tsai et al., 2021;

framed CT as “conceptualizing, not programming” (p. 35), many studies
continue to operationalize CT through programming tasks (Ezeamuzie &
Leung, 2022; Lye & Koh, 2014). To reduce ambiguity, this study adopts
the definition from the ICILS large-scale framework: CT as “an individual’s ability to recognize aspects of real-world problems that are
appropriate for computational formulation and to evaluate and develop
algorithmic solutions to those problems so that the solutions could be
operationalized with a computer” (Fraillon et al., 2019, p. 28). This
definition aligns with Wing’s (2006) and Tsai’s et al. (2021, 2022a)
conceptual framework of CT and provides the theoretical foundation for
the present analysis.
2.2. Theoretical framework
The current study is based on the ICILS-2024 contextual framework
as shown in Fig. 1 (Fraillon, 2024). The framework explains how
learning outcomes (especially in CT) is influenced by multiple variables
across four levels: individual, school, home, and the wider community.
Fig. 1 illustrates how these different levels work together. The individual
level focuses on how a students’ personal characteristics and behaviour
affect CT performance. The school/classroom level includes factors like
the design of learning environments and the availability of technology at
school. The home environment (third level) focuses on family-related
aspects such as parent’s education and home support. Finally, the
fourth level, the wider community, refers to how broader factors like
national education policies and a region’s access to technology.
Although these levels are defined differently, they are closely connected to each other. For instance, the use of technology can impact
learning at home, at school, or across the wider community. Each level
comprises of two types of factors: indirect influence on learning (antecedents) and direct influence (processes). The study explored the impact
of student-related (individual-level), school-related (school-level), and
socio-demographic (home environment) characteristics on CT
development.
2.3. Machine learning approaches

2. Related work

Machine Learning (ML) techniques have received a lot of attention in
recent years because it can unveil the relationship between the different
academic and personal input factors, and CT outcomes. According to
Srinivas (2020), ML models can learn on their own by using past experiences from training data, helping the system to make accurate predictions based on previous examples. Ghahramani (2015) pointed out
that ML models can identify patterns in training data and use that
knowledge to make predictions.
There are three main categories of ML: supervised, unsupervised and
reinforcement learning. In supervised learning, model is trained using
labelled data with known input and outputs while in unsupervised
learning there is unlabelled data to find hidden patterns or similarities.
Reinforcement learning implies learning from experience, where system
improved through trial and error, based on feedback.
The current study focused on supervised learning approach. Logistic
regression is widely used in education because it provides better results
for linear datasets (Allen et al., 2008; Martin et al., 2005; Ranasinghe
et al., 2017). However, in recent times, other ML algorithms have outperformed logistic regression when working on educational datasets.

2.1. Computational thinking
According to Wing (2006), CT can be viewed as thinking like a
computer scientist to help solve problems in other areas beyond computer science. This idea has been seen as a starting point for research on
CT (Grover & Pea, 2013; Shute et al., 2017). Earlier scholars such as
Papert (1980) emphasized procedural thinking, i.e., how children learn
by thinking about problems in a structured way, while Knuth (1974)
argued that writing computer-like solutions helps students understand
problems more clearly. Similarly, Alan Perlis, the first Turing Award
winner, promoted programming for all students as part of a
well-rounded education (Guzdial, 2008). These early contributions did
not use the term CT but already highlighted its role in problem-solving
(Bull et al., 2020). Wing (2006) and Tsai et al. (2021, 2022b) later
broadened CT to include practices such as problem decomposition,
abstraction, algorithm thinking, evaluation, and generalization.
Although some researchers argued this definition was too general
(Mannila et al., 2014), others considered it a useful foundation for

The study used common evaluation scores such as accuracy, precision, recall, AUC (Area Under the Curve), and the F1 score to measure
the comparative predictive performance of ML models. Accuracy is
calculated by dividing the count of correct predictions with the total
predictions. Next, Precision is calculated by dividing true positive predictions with the total positive predictions made. Further, Recall is
calculated by dividing true positive predictions with the sum of true
positives and false negatives. Finally, F1 score contains a mix of precision and recall, which can be helpful when the data is imbalanced. The
F1 score can be calculated using (2 x precision x recall)/(precision +
recall). During the predictions, the true positive rate changes with the
false positive rate resulting in a curve. The AUC is the area under the
curve that shows how good is the overall model.

ICILS 2023 International Student Questionnaire (Fraillon, 2024)
originally included 132 variables aimed to collect students’ demographic backgrounds and to assess students’ information and computer technology (ICT) using and learning behaviours at homes and

3.2.3. Computational thinking disposition scale (CTDS)
A total of 15 items selected from the original CTDS (Tsai et al., 2021)
were embedded into a part of the National Student Questionnaire for all
Taiwanese students in ICILS 2023. This scale assessed students’
computational thinking dispositions in terms of decomposition,
abstraction, algorithmic thinking, evaluation and generalization dispositions when solving problems in a computer programming context. The
original 19-items CTDS have been reported with a reliability of Cronbach alpha of .91 (Tsai et al., 2021). In this study, an overall reliability
.93 was obtained for the 15 selected terms assessed in the sample of
ICILS 2023. The scale was only taken by Taiwanese students as part of
ICILS questionnaire. Beyond internal consistency, the CTDS has
demonstrated convergent validity with measures of problem-solving
and self-efficacy in previous Taiwanese studies (Tsai et al., 2019,
2022a). The Cronbach’s alpha of the CTDS questionnaire used in this
study is .934. This evidence strengthens its use as a reliable predictor of
CT performance. Since CTDS scores are significantly correlated with
computer programming self-efficacy (Tsai et al., 2019, 2022a), this
study included the CTDS overall averaged score as a feature for the
modelling.

3. Methodology
3.1. Sample and data source
The sample of this study came from the 5211 Taiwanese students
who participated in the International Computer and Information Literacy Study 2023; ICILS 2023) project hosted by the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) (Fraillon,
2024). A two-stage sampling process was employed in this study: 169
junior high schools were first selected through stratified random sampling from over 2000 schools in Taiwan, followed by a cluster sampling
at the class level within each selected school. All participants were
informed with a consent form provided by the IEA for participating the
study. The final dataset cleaned and pre-processed by the IEA was
anonymous. With an average age of 14.3, all participants were 8th
graders and have all learned basic computer literacy course beginning in
their 7th grade under the new national curriculum standard beginning
in 2020 in Taiwan. This curriculum explicitly integrates CT within a
blocked-based programming framework. Although there was no
requirement for elementary schools regarding CT skills in Taiwan,
CT-related activities were implemented as a form of school-based
experimental curricula, especially emphasized in the 5th and 6th
grades. These activities aimed to support students in applying computer
technologies to solve real-world problems and were aligned with
broader educational goals to foster 21st century skills.

3.3. Procedures
All data from the CT test and the questionnaire were collected online
in Taiwan for the ICILS 2023 project. First, each student took about 30
min to finish an online survey, including all items of the IEA International Student Questionnaire and the 15 items of the CTDS as the national items required for all Taiwanese students. After a 15–20 min
break, a CT performance test was administered to all participants.
Beginning with a 10-min video of the system tutorial, the CT test was
composed by two CT modules and each module contained a series of
tasks. All the tasks were shown and allowed students to test or to
respond in a limited of time via an online interactive system provided by
the IEA (Fraillon, 2024). Each CT module was allowed to be finished no
more than 25 min. Therefore, it took around 60 min for an in-tact class to
finish the CT test in a computer lab at school. All of the data were
collected smoothly in Taiwan for ICILS 2023. Most of the CT test items
were evaluated automatically via an online evaluation system. Only
three critical response items were evaluated by a group of 5–6 national
domain experts using the evaluation criteria provided by IEA. Since the
study was performed globally, the CT test scores were standardized
using Rasch item response theory, with international mean score of 483
and a standard deviation (SD) of 112 (Fraillon, 2024). The Taiwanese
students’ data had mean average score of 548 and a standard deviation
of 110. Most students (around 99.5 %) had scores between 200 and 800.

3.4. Variables and the prediction model
To facilitate binary classification, students were grouped into two
categories based on their standardized CT scores. Students were grouped
into two levels based on CT scores: below average (less than 541- around
52.8 %) and above average (more than 541- around 47.2 %). This
threshold was decided by the mean of the first plausible value (PV) of the
students’ CT scores provided by the international database from ICILS
2023; Fraillon, 2024), in which five PVs were provided. The below
average CT scores were labelled 0, while above average was labelled 1.
The original ICILS dataset included over 600 attributes spanning performance data, CTDS performance, system logs, sampling weightings,
and questionnaire responses.1 To arrive at the final set of predictors, a
multi-stage filtering process was applied. First, overlapping or highly
correlated items were aggregated into derived indices based on IEA
guidelines (e.g., CTDS, S_HOMLIT). Second, variables with excessive
missingness or with little theoretical connection to CT performance were
removed (e.g., country identifiers not relevant to Taiwan). Third, features were screened for multicollinearity using the Variance Inflation
Factor (VIF), ensuring that retained variables provided unique information. Finally, in consultation with prior literature and the study’s
research questions, 18 features were selected across three categories:
socio-demographic, student-related, and school-related.

cognitive mechanism to behavioural mechanism.
In practical sense, students with a high CT disposition have a habit to
think systematically similar to a computer scientist when they encounter
complex/unfamiliar problems. These students will most likely decompose large problems into smaller subproblems for simplification, find
generalization across problems, evaluate, and refine their solutions
iteratively. In contrast, students with low CT disposition cannot think
systematically when they find unfamiliar or complex problems or tasks
that appear abstract or time consuming. These differences highlight why
CT disposition functions as a powerful predictor of CT performance.
Basically, it reflects not just knowledge but the mindset and problemsolving habits that drive effective engagement with computational
tasks. To put simply, a strong CT disposition added with persistence and
confidence can improve students’ engagement with CT tasks, fostering
CT skills development. Also, self-efficacy related to general (e.g., using
Excel to calculate data) and specialist applications (e.g., programming in
a block-based environment) were significant predictors of CT, indicating
that students’ confidence in using technology plays a crucial role in their
ability to apply CT skills effectively. In addition, academic multitasking
was also a significant predictor of CT. However, it is found that multitasking was negatively associated with Taiwanese CT performance scores
(Fraillon, 2024) suggesting that student’s attention might be distracted
when they study with ICT. The negative relationship between academic
multitasking and Taiwanese students’ CT performance is not found in all
countries (Fraillon, 2024) suggesting plausible cultural differences may
be worth exploring in future studies.
Next, among school-related variables, SPECLASS was a significant
predictor of CT. This finding is consistent with the literature as studies
have found positive effects of structured ICT integration and targeted
application-based learning on students’ CT skills development (Fraillon
et al., 2020, p. 297). The school environment, particularly the curriculum focusing on specialized digital tools, appears to support CT learning
more effectively when such tools are directly embedded into instruction.
However, the frequency of using ICT in Taiwanese schools was found
significantly lower than the international average, suggesting some
other factors may have facilitated students’ CT skills development in
schools or outside schools. For example, the educational research resources provided by the National Science and Technological Council in
Taiwan, the funding from industrial companies and non-profit organizations etc. may also play a role in shaping students’ CT learning
motivation and achievement.
Further, among socio-demographic variables, home literacy index and
parent highest education were significant predictors with relative low
impact. These findings suggest that students with more literate home
environments and parents with higher educational levels are likely to
perform better in CT assessments, which is consistent with previous
research on the socio-culture dimensions of learning (Fraillon et al.,
2020, p. 297). The findings of ICILS 2018 and 2023 had similar findings
regarding the socio-economic-status’s (SES’s) effects on CT performance
(Fraillon, 2024). Other factors such as gender and Internet access at

home didn’t significantly affect Taiwanese students’ CT performance
which indicates a potential narrowing of the digital divide in Taiwan.
The plausible diminish of the gender difference is in line with the prior
literature concerning the digital learning and education in Taiwan (Tsai
& Tsai, 2010; Tsai et al., 2015, 2019; Lee et al., 2023b).
5.3. Implications of the study
The findings achieved from this study have practical implications for
educators and policymakers. If we can identify the key predictors of
students CT performance early in academic journey, educational institutions can design targeted intervention to support learners who may
be at risk of underachievement. Such early identification is crucial to
teach CT, which is an essential 21st-century skill, just like reading,
writing, and arithmetic. Moreover, the successful application of ML
approaches in this context highlights the potential for integrating predictive analytics into educational decision making.
Apart from pedagogical implications, the study has wider implications for designing school curriculum, training teachers, and improving
education technology development. Firstly, the strong effect of studentrelated factors such as CT disposition and digital self-efficacy shows that
school must have more CT-focused programs. These programs can help
students feel more confident and interest in CT by using hands-on and
inquiry-based learning activities. Second, schools should design curriculum that encourages teachers to use more specialized applications
during classroom teaching. Thirdly, although socio-demographic factors
like parents’ education and home-literacy had less impact, they were
still important. This suggests that government should work towards
giving all students equal access to learning resources at home. Lastly, the
current study shows that ML can be useful not just for predictive analytics but also for creating future leaning environments that adjust to
each student’s needs. Future smart systems could analyse students’ realtime behaviour data to give personalized help. These systems could help
identify students that need early support, especially those in rural areas
with less support, and could also help in designing national policies for
teaching CT in schools.
6. Conclusion
This study explored how socio-demographic, student-related, and
school-related factors influence Taiwanese eighth graders’ CT performance using the ICILS 2023 dataset and several machine learning
models. Among all models, XGBoost and Stacking Ensemble performed
best, showing that advanced ensemble methods can better capture the
complex patterns in educational data. The study makes two main contributions. First, it found that student-level factors, especially CTDS,
were the strongest predictors of CT performance. This means that how
students think and approach problems (such as their ability to plan,
decompose, and persist) plays a bigger role than their background or
school environment. Second, by using ML models on a large-scale international dataset, this study shows that data-driven methods can help
identify important factors influencing CT such as CTDS. This suggests
that a cognitive variable can contribute to behavioural performance,
implying a connection between cognitive and the behavioural mechanism in CT development. Future studies may further explore the interaction effect of the variables on students’ CT performance. These
insights can help teachers and policymakers design programs that better
support students’ CT learning.
7. Limitations and future work
The study has few limitations. First, the data used in this study was
collected from grade-8 Taiwanese students that completed the ICILS2023 questionnaire. The results may not be consistent with the students from countries. Future studies can explore more diverse datasets
with different countries to see if the findings are consistent across

different countries. Also, considering diverse dataset for different grade
and age-groups can help to examine if the findings are consistent across
the same. Second, the study used socio-demographic, student, and
school-related factors to predict CT. However, there may be other variables related to teacher support, or students’ learning habits, that are
not included in the analysis. Future studies could also include those
variables to get a deeper understanding of what influences CT
improvement. Third, the binary classification method simplifies CT
performance into “below-average” and “above-average”, which may
miss some details about students’ actual CT skills. Future studies can use
multi-class classification by classifying CT scores into multiple levels as
defined by the ICILS. Finally, an important point is that the predictors in
the current study may not act independently but interact with one
another. For instance, CTDS may mediate or moderate the effects of ICT
self-efficacy on students’ CT performance scores. Future studies could
employ hierarchical SEM or explainable AI models to understand the
interaction effect among the predictors. In addition, the study focused
more on prediction (using ML models) than on explaining why certain
students perform better. Future studies can use explainable AI methods
to explain how ML models classify and make decisions.

A.2. Random Forest
The Random Forest algorithm is an ensemble method that combines the predictions of multiple decision trees to identify patterns and make
accurate predictions. Each decision tree in the model consists of several layers made up of nodes, where the data is split based on certain conditions. At
every node, the data is divided into two branches depending on a specific rule, and this process continues through the tree until it reaches a final
decision or class label (Rodriguez et al., 2006). In the context of this study, the algorithm continues splitting until it predicts the CT score (below or
above average) of a student. The key criterion used to decide how the data is split at each node is called information gain (Equation (2)), which
measures how much uncertainty (or entropy) is reduced after a split. Entropy, defined in Equation (3), represents the level of disorder or randomness
in the data. A lower entropy and higher information gain indicate better feature selection at each step of the tree. As a result, the model becomes more
efficient at making predictions (Bradley & Rajendran, 2021). Each tree in the forest makes its own prediction for the GPA class. The final predicted CT
class is determined by majority voting i.e., class with the most votes across all trees.

A.3. Gradient Boosting Classifier
Gradient Boosting Method (GBM) is an ensemble learning technique similar to Random Forests, but it builds decision trees in a sequential manner
rather than in parallel. The boosting process involves training multiple trees one after another, where each new tree focuses on correcting the mistakes
made by the previous one (Friedman, 2002). Specifically, after the first tree is built, more weight is assigned to the instances that were misclassified,
allowing the next tree to better handle those difficult cases. In this way, each subsequent tree is a refined or “boosted” version of the previous one.
10

N.K. Jha and M.-J. Tsai

Computers in Human Behavior Reports 21 (2026) 100896

Gradient boosting uses the gradient of a loss function to guide the improvement of the model. A common form of the loss function is expressed as y =
ax + b + e, where y is the model’s output, a and b are coefficients, and e is the error term. This function helps evaluate how well the model fits the data,
and minimizing this error across iterations leads to a more accurate predictive model.
A.4. AdaBoost
The Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), is an ensemble learning technique that combines several weak learners (e.g., simple decision trees) into a single
strong predictive model (Hussain & Zaidi, 2024). The main idea behind AdaBoost is to train a sequence of models, where each new model focuses more
on the instances that were misclassified by the previous ones. At the start, all training samples are assigned equal weights. After the first weak learner is
trained, the weights of the misclassified samples are increased so that the next model can pay more attention to them. This process continues for a set
number of rounds, with each learner contributing to the final model based on its accuracy. More accurate models have higher influence, while less
accurate ones contribute less. The final output is a weighted majority vote (for classification) or a weighted sum (for regression) of the predictions from
all the weak learners (Hussain & Zaidi, 2024). As a result, AdaBoost adaptively improves performance by emphasizing harder-to-classify examples
with each iteration, leading to a more robust and accurate final model.
A.5. LightGBM
LightGBM (Light Gradient Boosting Machine) is a highly efficient and fast implementation of gradient boosting decision trees, specifically designed
to handle large datasets and high-dimensional features (Qiuqian et al., 2025). Like other boosting methods, LightGBM builds an ensemble of decision
trees sequentially, where each new tree corrects the errors made by the previous ones. However, it differs in the way it constructs these trees. Instead of
growing trees level-wise (as in traditional methods), LightGBM grows tree leaf-wise. At each step, it finds the leaf with the highest loss and splits it,
which helps in reducing the loss more efficiently (Qiuqian et al., 2025). This approach allows LightGBM to achieve better accuracy than traditional
boosting methods, especially on large datasets.
A.6. XGBoost
Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is a widely used and powerful machine learning algorithm that builds an ensemble of decision trees to make
accurate predictions (Asselman et al., 2023). Like other boosting techniques, XGBoost creates trees one after another (level-wise), where each new tree
focuses on correcting the mistakes made by the previous trees. This step-by-step process helps improve the overall performance of the model. What
makes XGBoost stand out is its ability to combine speed and accuracy. It uses second-order derivatives (i.e., both gradients and Hessians) to better
understand the direction and magnitude of the error while optimizing the model. This leads to faster and more accurate training. XGBoost also includes several advanced features such as regularization to prevent overfitting, missing value handling, and parallel processing, which allows it to work
efficiently even on large datasets. Additionally, it supports tree pruning and sparsity-aware algorithms to make training even more effective (Asselman
et al., 2023). Overall, XGBoost is a reliable choice when high performance is needed for tasks like classification, regression, or ranking. Its robust
design and proven success in many machine learning competitions make it one of the most trusted tools among data scientists and researchers.
A.7. Stacking Ensemble
Stacking is a machine learning technique that combines multiple models to build a stronger predictive model, especially when it is unclear which
individual algorithm would perform best (Kurz et al., 2020). Instead of relying on a single model, stacking brings together several different learning
algorithms, each capable of capturing unique patterns in the data (Kurz et al., 2020; Sikora, 2015). These individual models are referred to as base
learners. To implement stacking, the dataset is typically divided into K folds. In each iteration, K − 1 folds are used to train the base models, and the
remaining fold is used to generate predictions. This procedure is repeated so that each fold serves as the validation set once. The intermediate predictions produced by all base learners are then used to form a new dataset, which is used to train a second-level model, often called the meta-learner.
The meta-learner is responsible for making the final prediction based on the outputs of the base models. This two-layered structure allows stacking to
take advantage of the strengths of various models and improve overall accuracy (Parvin et al., 2015; Sikora, 2015).
