Teachers’ work conditions impact their wellbeing. However, it is currently not well-known what role
school location plays in these associations. The current study used a multigroup structural equation
model to identify similarities and differences in the associations between job demands/resources, selfefﬁcacy, and teacher wellbeing in rural, regional, and metropolitan locations in Australia. Workload
and student behavior stress (negatively) and teacher collaboration (positively) were predictive of teacher
wellbeing in all locations. In contrast, professional development irrelevance had unique associations with
wellbeing for rural teachers. The ﬁndings suggest the importance of considering what demands/resources are most salient for teachers in speciﬁc contexts.

1. Introduction
Teacher wellbeing is of increasing international interest (Craig,
2017; Granziera et al., 2021) because it is associated with important educational outcomes, including teacher turnover intentions
(Madigan & Kim, 2021) and student academic achievement (Arens
& Morin, 2016). A growing list of international studies on teacher
wellbeing have demonstrated that teachers’ job demands and resources and their personal resources, such as self-efﬁcacy, are
broadly predictive of their wellbeing (Granziera et al., 2021). Yet, it
is currently not known whether the associations between job demands/resources, personal resources, and teacher wellbeing vary

location as a covariate control (e.g., Collie et al., 2021) and thus

Several empirical studies have demonstrated that teacher selfefﬁcacy is negatively predicted by job demands and positively
predicted by job resources (Aldridge & Fraser, 2016; Skaalvik &

JDRT also speciﬁes two interaction processes between job demands and resources. The ﬁrst is the boosting process, in which
higher levels of demands strengthen the relations between resources and positive work outcomes (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017).
The second is the buffering process, in which higher levels of resources weaken the relations between demands and negative work
outcomes (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). These two processes highlight the importance for teachers to have a balance between their
job demands and resources.
Recent JDRT developments have suggested that workers' wider
social environments may inﬂuence the associations between job
demands/resources and wellbeing (Demerouti & Bakker, 2022). For
example, within the COVID-19 pandemic the boundaries between
home and work became blurred for many workers, which may have
increased the associations between workers’ home demands, job
demands, and their wellbeing, and also made job resources more
imperative to manage these demands (Demerouti & Bakker, 2022).
A similar pattern may be present for teachers working in rural
settings. These teachers can experience overlapping challenges
within their professional and personal lives (Downes & Roberts,
2018), including professional and personal isolation and feeling

efﬁcacy, and wellbeing across three location categories in Australia:
rural (i.e., small towns with populations 15,000 people), regional
(i.e., towns with populations ¼ 15,001e100,000 people), and
metropolitan (i.e., cities with populations 100,001 people). We
were interested in whether the associations for rural teachers are
similar or different to teachers in regional and metropolitan locations due to limited research on rural teachers (Biddle & Azano,
2016; Downes et al., 2021; Hascher & Waber, 2021; Roberts et al.,
2022). This is important to examine because teachers in rural and
remote locations are considered to experience unique challenges
(Downes & Roberts, 2018), higher demands (Drummond & Halsey,
2013), and more severe stafﬁng issues (McKenzie et al., 2014).
Four job demands (workload stress, student behavior stress,
profession development needs, professional development irrelevance) and two job resources (teacher collaboration, pedagogical
autonomy) were examined because of their potential salience for
teachers in rural schools (Downes & Roberts, 2018). The job demands/resources were examined as predictors of self-efﬁcacy and,
in turn, all factors were examined as predictors of two types of

1.1. Theoretical framework
JDRT (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017) offers a robust model through
which to understand factors associated with teacher wellbeing
(Granziera et al., 2021). JDRT (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017) classiﬁes
physical, psychological, organizational, and social factors at work as
either job demands that hinder workers’ optimal functioning or job
resources that support this (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). Whereas
job demands are more strongly predictive of negative work outcomes, such as strain and turnover intentions, job resources are
more strongly predictive of positive work outcomes, such as job
satisfaction and engagement (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017).
JDRT also stipulates that workers have personal resources that
support optimal functioning at work. Personal resources are

1.2. Salient teacher job demands and resources

Self-efﬁcacy is a salient personal resource for teachers (Granziera
et al., 2021) that describes a person's perceived ability to be successful within a domain (Bandura, 2001). Teacher self-efﬁcacy is
often modelled to encompass three dimensions (Tschannen-Moran
& Hoy, 2001; Zee & Koomen, 2016). These include self-efﬁcacy for
classroom management (i.e., the capability to manage and support
student behavior), self-efﬁcacy for student engagement (i.e., the
capability to support student engagement), and self-efﬁcacy for
instructional strategies (i.e., the capability to differentiate pedagogy
to support student learning).
Job demands generally have negative associations with selfefﬁcacy (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2010, 2016, 2019). Workload and
student behavior stress are typically associated with lower selfefﬁcacy (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2010, 2019), likely because they
represent challenges that inhibit teachers from successfully fulﬁlling their roles. However, some research shows workload stress
can positively predict self-efﬁcacy (Klassen & Chiu, 2010; Klassen
et al., 2013), potentially because workload stress can be motivating (to a certain extent) when individuals value their work
(Schmitt et al., 2015). PD-needs also negatively predict self-efﬁcacy

Although researchers appear not to have examined PD-irrelevance
as a predictor of self-efﬁcacy, “barriers to PD” (a broader construct
that incorporates PD-irrelevance) is negatively associated with selfefﬁcacy (Renbarger & Davis, 2019), likely because these barriers
inhibit optimal engagement with PD.
In contrast, job resources generally have positive associations
with self-efﬁcacy (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2010, 2019). Speciﬁcally,
teacher collaboration has a positive association with self-efﬁcacy
(Collie et al., 2012; Y. Liu et al., 2021), likely because collaboration
can support efﬁcient practices. Pedagogical autonomy is also
associated with greater self-efﬁcacy (Vangrieken et al., 2017), likely
because it enables teachers to use pedagogies that they ﬁnd
effective.
Few studies have investigated whether teachers' job demands
boost the association between job resources and self-efﬁcacy, and
these results have been inconclusive. This research gap may exist
because, as mentioned, researchers often position personal resources differently within the JDRT model (Schaufeli & Taris, 2014;
Taris et al., 2017). In a study of therapists in schools (not teachers),
Gibson et al. (2009) found personal accomplishment was experienced at higher levels when therapists experienced both high supervisor support and high job demands. This demonstrates that
JDRT's boosting process can be predictive of workers' self-perceived
capabilities. In contrast, Devos et al.’s (2012) ﬁndings on novice
teachers were not consistent with JDRT's boosting process, as
collaboration was only positively linked to self-efﬁcacy when
novice teachers experienced fewer difﬁculties at work. This indicates that some resources may not be relevant for managing
certain demands. Given these mixed ﬁndings, more research is
needed to explore JDRT's boosting process for self-efﬁcacy.

demands/resources and self-efﬁcacy differ for teachers in rural
(compared to regional and metropolitan) locations. These associations may be impacted by the professional/personal isolation that
rural teachers may face (Downes & Roberts, 2018). For example, the

1.3. Self-efﬁcacy and the predictive role of resources/demands

The current study focused on six job demands/resources that
predict teacher wellbeing for teachers generally (Granziera et al.,
2021; Renbarger & Davis, 2019; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2018; Smet,
2022), yet which may be particularly salient for teachers working
in rural settings (Downes & Roberts, 2018). First, we introduce two
stress-related job demands. Both workload and student behavior
can be signiﬁcant sources of teacher stress (Collie, 2022; Klassen &
Chiu, 2010; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2018). Workload stress refers to the
pressure teachers experience from their workload, including stress
from lesson preparation, teaching multiple classes, and marking
(Collie & Mansﬁeld, 2022). Student behavior stress refers to the
stress teachers experience from negative student interactions,
including disruptive classroom behavior and verbal abuse (Collie &
Mansﬁeld, 2022).
The next two job demands relate to teachers' professional
development (PD). PD is compulsory for teachers in Australia
(Australian Institute for Teaching and School Leadership [AITSL],
2018) and in many other countries (Asih et al., 2022). PD-needs
refers to teachers' perceived need for PD related to teaching their
subject areas. Teachers with more urgent PD-needs may experience
work difﬁculties, especially if these needs are not met (Smet, 2022).
On the other hand, PD-irrelevance refers to teachers' judgements of
whether irrelevant PD reduces their participation in PD. Irrelevant
PD can be demotivating and an inefﬁcient use of teachers’ time
(Bayar, 2014; Damianidou, 2021).
Two job resources were investigated in this study: teacher
collaboration and pedagogical autonomy. Both teacher collaboration (Duyar et al., 2013) and pedagogical autonomy (Vangrieken
et al., 2017) are associated with teachers’ effective functioning at
work. Teacher collaboration refers to the degree to which teachers
collaborate, including sharing teaching materials and discussing
student learning (Collie, 2021). Pedagogical autonomy refers to the
control that teachers have over their classroom practices
(Vangrieken et al., 2017), such as teaching methods and discipline
procedures.
The aforementioned job demands/resources were chosen
because of their potential salience for teachers in rural locations
(Downes & Roberts, 2018). Teachers in more remote locations have
an increased likelihood of teaching school subjects that are beyond
their expertise (Weldon, 2016), which may create greater workloads and PD-needs. Moreover, many schools in more remote areas
struggle with attracting and retaining experienced staff
(Productivity Commission, 2022), which can lead teachers to take
on school leadership positions earlier in their careers (Graham
et al., 2015; Handal et al., 2013) and implicates their workload
stress. Student behavior is also typically more challenging in underserved schools (Collie, 2021), which disproportionately fall
within more remote locations (Echazarra & Radinger, 2019;
Infrastructure Australia, 2019). Schools in more remote locations
can also face logistical challenges, including small staff numbers
and professional isolation (Downes & Roberts, 2018), which may
impact teachers’ ability to access PD and collaborate with experienced teachers (Jenkins et al., 2015; Lyons, 2009). Finally, some
teachers in Australia report that the national curriculum is less
pertinent to the unique needs of schools and communities in more
remote locations (Haynes & Miller, 2016; Lock et al., 2012;
Papatraianou et al., 2018), which may implicate perceptions of PD3

JDRT's boosting and buffering processes are also implicated in

to teacher wellbeing both directly and via interactions.
Empirical research is needed to identify whether associations
between job demands/resources and self-efﬁcacy to teacher wellbeing vary for teachers in rural (compared to regional and metropolitan) locations (Downes et al., 2021). The isolation and high
community visibility of teachers in more remote locations (Lassig
et al., 2015; Miller et al., 2006) may impact the salience of workload and student behavior stress to teacher wellbeing because of
overlap between teachers’ professional and personal lives (Downes
& Roberts, 2018). The salience of PD-needs and PD-irrelevance to
teacher wellbeing also may vary because issues with PD are less
easily remedied in more remote locations where fewer PD providers exist (Jenkins et al., 2015). The salience of teacher collaboration and pedagogical autonomy to teacher wellbeing may vary
because professional isolation (Downes & Roberts, 2018; Lyons,
2009) and curriculum irrelevance (Haynes & Miller, 2016; Lock
et al., 2012; Papatraianou et al., 2018) are reported teacher challenges in more remote locations. Professional autonomy (which
encompasses pedagogical autonomy) is also perceived as a key
beneﬁt for staying in more remote schools (Lock et al., 2012).
Finally, without the support of more experienced colleagues
(Jenkins et al., 2015; Lyons, 2009), the salience of self-efﬁcacy to
teacher wellbeing may vary.

1.4. Teacher wellbeing and the predictive role of resources/demands
and self-efﬁcacy
Teacher wellbeing represents teachers' healthy and adaptive
functioning at work (Granziera et al., 2021). It is a multidimensional
construct that is often operationalized in different ways (Collie &
Perry, 2019; Hascher & Waber, 2021). For the current study, we
operationalized teacher wellbeing via strain and job satisfaction to
align our research design with JDRT's propositions for positive and
negative work outcomes (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). Strain refers
to the negative psychological and physical result of stressors (Bliese
et al., 2017). Job satisfaction refers to teachers' affective reactions
and contentment with their current school job (Skaalvik & Skaalvik,
2011). Together, these two factors encompass positive and negative
aspects of teacher wellbeing, representing whether teachers
experience work illbeing and whether they are satisﬁed in their
roles.

(Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2017c, 2018). Workload and student behavior

2. Study overview
The current study aims to examine the extent and manner in
which job demands (workload stress, student behavior stress, PDneeds, PD-irrelevance) and job resources (teacher collaboration,
pedagogical autonomy) are associated with teacher self-efﬁcacy
and, in turn, strain and job satisfaction within three location
groups (rural, regional, metropolitan), and to identify whether
there are any differences in these associations between the rural
and the other two groups. We examined potential buffering and
boosting processes between the job demands/resources in relation
to self-efﬁcacy and the teacher wellbeing outcomes, and indirect
associations from the demands/resources to the teacher wellbeing
outcomes via self-efﬁcacy.

5. Discussion

5.2. Differences between locations

The current study examined the associations between teachers'
job demands/resources, self-efﬁcacy, and wellbeing across rural,
regional, and metropolitan locations. Our hypotheses (H1e4) were
based on JDRT's principles (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017) and were
broadly supported. The bivariate correlations between the job demands/resources and self-efﬁcacy, strain, and job satisfaction were
all in the expected directions, supporting JDRT's principles. The
multigroup SEM further demonstrated which job demands/resources were most salient for each location group (after controlling
for shared variance), which identiﬁed both similarities and differences between the rural and the other two groups (RQ1e2).

This study identiﬁed several differences in the associations between the location groups. We concentrate here on ﬁndings where
the rural group differed from the other two groups because of the
current study's focus and because ﬁndings were broadly similar
between the regional and metropolitan group associations. First,
several factors were found to predict self-efﬁcacy for the regional
and metropolitan groups, but not for the rural group. All job demands/resources predicted self-efﬁcacy for the metropolitan
group, and all but pedagogical autonomy for the regional group.
However, self-efﬁcacy for the rural group was only predicted by
student behavior stress (negatively) and pedagogical autonomy
(positively). Moreover, Wald tests added further weight to several
of these differences (workload stress [signiﬁcantly different between the rural and regional groups], PD-needs [rural and metropolitan], PD-irrelevance [rural and regional], and teacher
collaboration [rural and regional]).
The above results suggest that efforts to improve teacher selfefﬁcacy in rural schools may be best focused on supporting student behavior stress and pedagogical autonomy. Student behavior
stress may be particularly salient in rural locations because student
behavior can be more challenging in underserved schools (Collie,
2021). On the other hand, pedagogical autonomy may be pertinent
for rural teachers' self-efﬁcacy because it enables teachers to use
pedagogical approaches that are effective for their unique contexts.
Overall, the results reveal the importance of considering what demands/resources are most salient in teachers’ working contexts.

5.1. Similarities across locations
There were several similarities in the associations between the
job demand/resources, self-efﬁcacy, and teacher wellbeing outcomes across the location groups. First, student behavior stress was
the only consistent predictor of all three dependent variables: selfefﬁcacy (negative association), strain (positive), and job satisfaction (negative). Second, workload stress was a positive predictor of
strain. Third, teacher collaboration was a positive predictor of job
satisfaction. These ﬁndings are consistent with JDRT's principles
regarding the associations between job demands (maladaptive), job
sources (adaptive), and work outcomes (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017).
The current ﬁndings suggest that workload stress, student
behavior stress, and teacher collaboration play some similar roles in
teachers' functioning and wellbeing, no matter where teachers
work. These ﬁndings are novel and important because there has

importance of ensuring that rural teachers have access to effective
and relevant PD (Jenkins et al., 2015). The current ﬁndings also
provide empirical evidence for the salient challenges/opportunities
experienced by rural teachers (Downes & Roberts, 2018). The
ﬁndings reiterate the importance of ensuring that rural teachers
have support for workload stress (Halsey, 2018b), student behavior
management (Lock et al., 2012), pedagogical autonomy (Lock et al.,
2012; Papatraianou et al., 2018), and effective collaboration (Jenkins
et al., 2015; Lyons, 2009) to support self-efﬁcacy and teacher
wellbeing.

5.4. Implications for practice
The current results demonstrate that workload stress, student
behavior stress, and teacher collaboration have some consistent
associations with self-efﬁcacy and teacher wellbeing across all locations. This suggests that all school and system leaders may wish
to consider how these job demands/resources can be supported.
For workload stress, mentoring can be an effective way to support early career teachers (Kutsyuruba et al., 2019). In Australia,
there also have been calls to reduce teachers' face-to-face teaching
hours to help teachers manage their workloads (Gallop et al., 2021).
For student behavior stress, research indicates the importance for
teachers to build positive teacher-student relationships to foster
student engagement (Pianta et al., 2012; Pino-James et al., 2019).
Autonomy-supportive teaching styles may help build these positive
relationships, as well as support students’ autonomous motivation
to learn (Reeve et al., 2019, 2020). For effective teacher collaboration, teachers should be convinced of the beneﬁts of collaborating
and be given time to collaborate (Vangrieken et al., 2015). Quality
teacher roundsdwhich involve collegial observations and discussionsdhave demonstrated promise as an effective and structured form of collaboration (Gore et al., 2017; Gore & Rickards,
2021). Such practices may help support self-efﬁcacy and teacher
wellbeing in all schools.
Support of the other job demands/resources may be important
for teachers in speciﬁc contexts. For the PD-related demands,
research suggests the importance for schools to listen to their staff's
speciﬁc PD interests and needs to help ensure PD is effective and
relevant (Damianidou, 2021). For pedagogical autonomy, it may be
important for schools to provide teachers with time to implement
agency in their work (Gallop et al., 2021). For rural teachers, this
may mean providing time to adapt and contextualize national
curriculum topics to be more relevant to their students (Haynes &
Miller, 2016; Lock et al., 2012; Papatraianou et al., 2018). Such

5.5. Limitations
Data availability
There are some limitations to consider for the current study.
First, this study relied on self-reports from teachers, alongside covariate measures from school principals. As such, the substantive
factors represent teachers' perspectives of their work conditions
and experiences, and are not objective measures from an external
source. Second, the TALIS data provide access to teachers from
geographic locations that are difﬁcult to sample from and thus
provide an important snapshot of teachers' experiences in these
different locations. Although the data collection included implicit
stratiﬁcation based on location, very remote and very small schools
were excluded from the OECD's Australian population sample.
Therefore, the current study's rural group does not represent the
most remote schools in Australia. It is recommended that more
research is needed that uses robust sampling methods to investigate representative samples of teachers across very remote locations. Third, the school characteristic covariates were measured at
the school-level but were modelled at the teacher-level in the
current study. We conducted single-level modelling given the
number of rural schools was not appropriate for multilevel
modelling. Future studies exploring multigroup, multilevel models
are important. Fourth, the rural group was substantially smaller
than the metropolitan group, which may have affected the significance level of some associations. To account for this, we compared
ﬁndings across locations using both effect sizes and Wald tests.
Finally, the current study relied on cross-sectional data, and
therefore causality between the constructs cannot be conﬁrmed.
We relied on theory to justify the direction of the regression associations (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). Nonetheless, longitudinal
research is an important future avenue.

5.6. Conclusion
The aim of the current study was to examine similarities and
differences in the associations between teachers’ job demands/resources, self-efﬁcacy, and wellbeing across rural, regional, metropolitan locations in Australia. Results suggest that teachers may be
better supported through careful consideration of which job demands/resources are most salient in their context. Student behavior
stress was found to be the only job demand to consistently predict
self-efﬁcacy, strain, and job satisfaction across all locations. Additionally, workload stress (maladaptive), teacher collaboration
(adaptive), and self-efﬁcacy (maladaptive) had consistent associations with teacher wellbeing across the locations. Several differences between the rural and the other groups also were found in
the predictors of self-efﬁcacy and teacher wellbeing. Of note, PDirrelevance uniquely predicted strain for rural teachers, yet this
association was buffered by the resource of collaboration. Taken
together, the ﬁndings suggest the beneﬁts for schools/school systems to consider location when identifying how to best support
self-efﬁcacy and teacher wellbeing.
