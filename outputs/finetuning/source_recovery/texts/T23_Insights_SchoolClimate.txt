Introduction
Students’ school experiences, their academic achievement, and social-emotional health can be 
affected by the complex social system of their school’s environment and the roles, relationships, 
and influence of relevant stakeholders. While greater attention is being given to measures of 
students’ emotional development in schools, the dynamics of the school’s social systems are 
not comprehensively covered by existing student-level measures.1 In addition, there is room 
for improvement regarding how to meaningfully aggregate intrapersonal attributes reported at 
the individual level to systems-level information and how this might inform evaluations of school 
quality. 
This TIMSS Insights report describes a new framework – school climate health – for defining 
similarities of school social systems internationally, operationalized through the items in the TIMSS 
2023 context questionnaires. School climate health integrates reports from different groups 
of stakeholders and posits that school climate and school well-being are dynamic constructs 
embedded in social systems shaped by the values and roles of those stakeholders. The contextual 
data available through TIMSS 2023 can be used to derive patterns of well-being and school 
climate, which allow exploring systems-level questions about the quality of national education 
systems. Data from the TIMSS 2023 Context Questionnaires provide collections of important 
indicators believed to be beneficial to school environments as agreed upon by the countries 
participating in the study. This report builds upon explorations of different facets of well-being 
in other IEA studies, including the first PIRLS 2021 Insights report, which examines indicators of 
this construct at a student level. 
The report begins with an overview of the concept of school climate health and how aspects 
of this construct are represented in the TIMSS 2023 Context Questionnaires. The report then 
presents the results of a multilevel latent class analysis (MLCA) that explores patterns among 
indicators from the context questionnaires to define clusters representing different profiles of 
school climate health in Grade 4. Clusters are further investigated by relating them to average 
socioeconomic status within schools. Averages of school-level mathematics and science 
achievement by cluster are also presented for each country. The report concludes with key 
takeaways and suggestions for future research related to school climate health in TIMSS.

Conceptualizing School Climate Health
A school’s environment reflects a combination of interrelated, multilevel factors that influence 
the actions of those involved, rather than simply its physical or administrative structures. School 
climate health addresses this complexity by combining relevant scales related to perceptions 
of the school environment using methodologies that can capture complex structures of 
interdependencies. Thus, school climate health is construed as the quality of the relationships 
among school participants, the environment that those relationships create, and the 
satisfaction with the schooling experience of all those involved. The resulting profiles of 
school climate health simultaneously capture the state of key environmental factors supportive of 
student well-being and learning such as student relationships, teacher performance, and school 
safety. 
Existing Literature
The definition of school climate health resulted from an extensive literature review of existing 
frameworks of well-being (e.g. subjective,2,3 psychological,4 and positive psychology5) and school 
climate,6,7 with special attention to approaches aimed at multidimensional measurement and 
cross-cultural comparison.8 Some frameworks suggest the experiences of multiple stakeholders 
must be considered to create school environments that support student well-being.9 Literature 
on non-academic outcomes and large-scale educational evaluations both share a call to embrace 
systems thinking or complex systems theory as a lens to capture the complexity of education 
at large.10,11 Through this lens, non-academic outcomes like well-being12 are understood as 
emerging from a system of social actors that work to either sustain or inhibit a positive school 
climate.13 It is essential to understand systems as a combination of interrelated parts that operate 
holistically within the boundaries of their context.14,15 
Every school can be considered as a social network that operates based on how the 
person-level mechanisms are supported—a complex social system knowable by the roles 
and mechanisms that are executed within it.16 A system in a state of well-being supports both 
individuals and the collective towards efficiently pursuing the shared goals of the system. Schools 
with healthy school climates could simultaneously support academic achievement and personal 
and social development. 
Happy Schools Framework and Sustainable Development Goals
Emerging international initiatives, such as UNESCO’s Happy Schools Framework, define 
frameworks for non-academic facets of schooling but have not been empirically tested. The 
Happy Schools project endeavors to implement sustainable school systems that support student 
academic success as well as well-being, grounded in a whole-school approach to “prioritize 
happiness and well-being” for “better quality” schooling.17 This three-part framework comprises 
several facets that will “promote happiness” by way of well-being and holistically supporting 
student development. The framework seeks to extend measures of quality within schools, beyond 
the more academically orientated objectives articulated in objectives from the United Nations 
Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [SDG 4.1 and SDG 4.7 (Exhibit 1.1)].

Operationalizing School Climate Health
School climate health exists at the intersection of major themes from literature on well-being and 
school climate, the Happy Schools Framework, and the constructs measured in the TIMSS 2023 
Grade 4 Context Questionnaires. The TIMSS context questionnaires gather information on factors 
of educational environments that are thought to relate to mathematics and science achievement 
internationally. At the fourth grade, questionnaires are distributed to sampled students, their 
teacher(s), school principal, and parents or guardians. 
Together, results from the literature review, criteria identified in the Happy Schools 
Framework, and content of the TIMSS 2023 context questionnaires come together to shape the 
framework of school climate health as depicted in Exhibit 1.2.

International Profiles in School Climate Health
A school climate health profile was established for each school with students participating in 
TIMSS 2023. The profile was based on the 22 indicator variables in Exhibit 1.3, which were 
aggregated to the school level for analyses using a multilevel latent class analysis (MLCA).18 Fifty-
eight countries were included in the analysis.a,b MLCA is an established procedure for exploring 
classroom- and school-level climate groupings based on self-reported questionnaire data.19,20 
In the multilevel structure of the TIMSS 2023 data, Level 1 represents schools and Level 2 
represents countries. Individual-level response data from teachers, students, and parents were 
aggregated to the school level for the analyses.c 
Multilevel Latent Class Analysis
MLCA allows for multidimensional profiles of schools and countries based on shared patterns of 
responses to the TIMSS context questionnaire scales and items relating to school climate health. 
At the school level (Level 1), variation in student, teacher, parent, and principal responses form 
distinct school climate health profiles. Such profiles illuminate how the different members of a 
school social system may report different experiences of school climate. At the country level 
(Level 2), countries are grouped based on similarities in the distribution of their schools across 
these profiles. 
Latent class analysis and model selection requires different iterations of estimated models 
to be compared for best fit. While there are metrics for comparison of model fit, the process of 
modeling also involves judgments of utility. For instance, more complex models—i.e., models 
with more clusters—may have better model fit indices, but more parsimonious models could 
hold greater meaning in interpretation. This balance guided the modeling approach to support 
meaningful interpretation of the relationship between responses to discrete scales and the 
ultimate evaluation of school climate health. Estimation with group-level (country) classes follows 
a similar approach where countries are assigned to classes based on the extent to which a group 
of countries has similar proportions of each cluster, according to the schools assigned to the 
extracted clusters. 
The following section focuses on qualitatively describing the clusters identified in the MCLA, 
along with the percentages of schools assigned to each cluster internationally. Detailed results 
concerning the country classes are not presented in this report; however, further details about 
how these may be interpreted are available elsewhere.21 Finally, cluster membership is related to 
socioeconomic status, as well as average achievement in mathematics and science, for Grade 4 
students within each country. Further information about analysis procedures, statistical models, 
and relevant fit statistics is available in Appendix C: Technical Details.
a	 See the TIMSS 2023 International Results for additional information about country participation in TIMSS 2023. 
b	 Four countries (Australia, England, the Netherlands, and the United States) did not administer the Home Questionnaire in TIMSS 2023 and do not 
have any data for Parents Satisfied with School.
c	 TIMSS randomly samples schools within countries and classrooms within schools to obtain a satisfactory representative sample of the population of 
students in each country. Aggregating to the school level allows for this analysis to incorporate data from all respondent types but does necessitate

school.

Characterizing the School Climate Health Clusters
Four interpretable clusters were identified based on the results of the MCLA. Brief descriptions of 
these clusters are presented below, along with the percentage of schools internationally assigned 
to each cluster based on the model results. These brief descriptions are followed by more detailed 
explanations of what distinguishes each individual cluster in terms of responses to the school 
climate health indicator variables.
There are several things to keep in mind when interpreting the cluster definitions presented 
in the following section. All 22 variables included in the analysis were recoded as ordinal variables 
with three categories: High (2), Medium (1), and Low (0). These categories should only be 
interpreted relative to each other, as they were defined to create relatively equal response regions 
per variable. They are not quantitative measures but reflect a relative ordering of the sets of 
responses. See Appendix C for further details about this procedure. 
Additionally, cluster descriptions follow the analysis in that they are reflective of reports of 
school-level attributes and the basis for cluster definitions is based on variable means relative to 
the other clusters. Cluster definitions include references to having the highest or lowest mean 
school scores compared to the other clusters, and for some variables may compare the proportion 
of respondents in schools in those clusters that give “High” or “Low” endorsements.
Cluster 1: Experiencing Difficulties
Respondents in these schools tend to report (on average) challenges in 
maintaining healthy relationships between students and teachers (disruptive 
classrooms), lack of supportive environments (low academic emphasis and 
more frequent bullying), and low stakeholder satisfaction (low confidence in 
mathematics and science; low teacher and parent satisfaction).
30% of schools internationally were classified as Experiencing Difficulties.
Cluster 2: System Misalignment
Different respondent types in these schools give different reports; while 
principal reports are largely positive, other respondents give mixed reports. On 
average, there are some healthy relationships based on reports of order within 
the school, but these are not evident in reports related to classroom practices. 
Principals and teachers tend to report that schools are well-resourced learning environments, 
yet students report lack of belonging. Positive reports of stakeholder satisfaction come only from 
principals, whereas students, teachers, and parents report on average low confidence and low 
satisfaction.
26% of schools internationally were classified as having System Misalignment.

Cluster 3: Healthy Social System
Respondents in these schools give largely positive reports. On average, 
reports indicate that healthy relationships exist between students and 
teachers, schools are sustaining high sense of student well-being with few 
limitations to resources and teaching, and stakeholders across respondent 
types (student, principal, parent, teacher) report high levels of satisfaction. 
22% of schools internationally were classified as having a Healthy Social System.
Cluster 4: Generational Incongruence
The degree of positivity reported in these schools varies consistently between 
students and the adults involved in their education. Relationships in the school 
as reported by teachers and principals, on average, are positive based on 
teacher practices and the state of discipline; students report operating in 
classrooms that are disruptive with less clarity from their teachers. Reports on the environment 
in the school are similarly split: principals and teachers are largely positive while students feel 
much less belonging and experience bullying. Student reports are much less positive about 
their confidence or affinity for mathematics and science whereas all the adults report high 
satisfaction.
21% of schools internationally were classified as having Generational Incongruence.
Exhibits 2.1 and 2.2 show the percentages of schools in each recoded response 
category by cluster, internationally. For most indicators, the “High” category corresponds 
to the most “positive” outcome— for example, a high score on Disruptive Lessons, 
Mathematics corresponds to students reporting little disorder in the classroom. The 
exceptions to this pattern are Principal’s Report, Teacher Absenteeism and Engaging 
Teaching Practices, where the “Low” category corresponds to less frequent teacher 
absences or more frequent use of the engaging practices.

Not all variables contributed equally to distinguishing among the four clusters. In more fully 
describing each cluster, the most discriminating variables are used for interpretation. Among the 
22 indicators, the four most discriminating variables are school averages for four scales measuring 
student perceptions of classroom functioning and attitudes towards mathematics and science. 
Instructional Clarity, Mathematics and Science reflect school averages of student reports of their 
perceptions of the teacher’s clarity in mathematics classes and science classes, respectively. 
Students Like Mathematics and Science reflect, on average, how much students in the school 
report liking mathematics and science, respectively. Following these four indicators, students’ 
reports of disorderly behavior in mathematics and science lessons are the most discriminating 
(Disruptive Lessons, Mathematics and Science, respectively), along with students’ reports on 
school belonging (Student Sense of Belonging). The only parent-reported variable (Parents 
Satisfied with School) is also relatively well-discriminating, along with Teacher’s Report, School 
Discipline. Details about the discrimination of all individual variables are available in Appendix C.

Cluster 1: Experiencing Difficulties 
 
Based on the self-reports of students, parents, principals, and teachers, schools Experiencing 
Difficulties (Cluster 1) struggle to maintain school climate health. This finding across all 
respondent types differentiates Cluster 1 from the remaining clusters. Cluster 1 has the lowest 
overall mean scores for the majority of student indicators. Students reported greater frequency 
of Student Bullying in these schools, on average, compared to schools in all other clusters. They 
also reported more frequently experiencing disruption in their mathematics and science lessons 
(Disruptive Lessons, Mathematics and Science, respectively) and arriving at school feeling tired 
or hungry (Students Arrive Hungry/Tired). Students’ reports are also the lowest, on average, for 
Students Confident, Mathematics and Science. 
Even when they do not show the lowest mean score compared to other clusters, Cluster 1 
schools’ scores remain relatively low on the remaining student level variables: Student Sense 
of Belonging, Instructional Clarity, Mathematics and Science, Students Like Mathematics and 
Science, and their parents’ level of satisfaction (Parents Satisfied with School). For Student Sense 
of Belonging, Instructional Clarity, Mathematics and Instructional Clarity, Science, roughly half of 
schools (48%, 50%, and 48%, respectively) have means recoded as “Low” scores. Although less 
than half of schools in this cluster have “Low” recoded means for Students Like Mathematics 
and Students Like Science, there are also less than one quarter or schools with “High” recoded 
means. Parent responses (Parents Satisfied with School) at the school level for Cluster 1 are more 
evenly distributed across the recoded response categories, but still, on average, more than twice

Schools that are experiencing difficulties are navigating apparent dysfunction in maintaining 
a healthy and productive school climate. These schools are struggling to sustain any of the 
three features of a healthy school social system (healthy relationships, supportive environments, 
stakeholder satisfaction). Students reported not feeling a strong connection to their school and 
experiencing bullying and teachers and principals reported that there is not a strong emphasis 
on academics and that there are problems with discipline and disorder. The most positive reports 
came from parents, who indicated that, on average, they were somewhat satisfied with the schools. 
Cluster 2: System Misalignment
Reports on the school climate health indicators show contradictions in schools experiencing 
System Misalignment (Cluster 2). Schools in this cluster have students who reported 
experiencing the least bullying (i.e., the highest average for Student Bullying), as well as the 
fewest classroom disruptions (i.e., the highest averages for Disruptive Lessons, Mathematics and 
Science). Principals, on average, reported to the lowest level of limitations on instruction due to 
resource shortages (Principal’s Report, Limited Resources). 
However, Cluster 2 schools have the lowest mean scores of any cluster on other student 
indicators, including Student Sense of Belonging, Students Like Mathematics and Science, and 
Instructional Clarity, Mathematics and Science. Parents of students in Cluster 2 schools also 
reported the least satisfaction with their child’s school (Parents Satisfied with School) across all 
clusters on average. Teachers reported the lowest Teacher Job Satisfaction across all clusters. 
Teachers in these schools, on average, reported employing engaging teaching practices at lower 
levels compared to other clusters (Engaging Teacher Practices). 
Different stakeholders report differing perceptions of schools experiencing system 
misalignment, leading to mixed findings regarding the school climate health areas of healthy 
relationships, supportive environments, and stakeholder satisfaction. Principals and teachers 
reported that schools in these clusters are well-resourced learning environments, characterized by 
sufficient resources and students’ readiness for instruction. While students reported little bullying 
or classroom disruption in these schools, the extent of an overall positive evaluation is undercut 
by reports of low sense of belonging and instructional clarity. Dissatisfaction is also evident in 
reports of teachers burdened with non-instructional tasks, students who do not like mathematics 
and science, and parents who expressed relatively negative perceptions of their child’s school.

Cluster 3: Healthy Social System
Schools experiencing a Healthy Social System (Cluster 3) have the highest levels of school 
climate health among the four clusters, with respondents who, on average, reported that the 
school system is functioning well. Schools in Cluster 3 have the most positive average reports 
for nine of the 22 indicator variables. Cluster 3 schools have the highest means for the following 
student indicators: Instructional Clarity, Mathematics and Science; Students Like Mathematics 
and Science; Students Confident, Mathematics and Science; Student Sense of Belonging; and 
Students Arrive Hungry/Tired. On average in Cluster 3 schools, teachers also report being the 
least burdened by non-instructional tasks (Teacher’s Report, Non-Instruction Responsibilities). 
Even for indicators where Cluster 3 schools do not have the most positive mean score, 
many schools in this cluster have high percentages of schools with average responses in the 
“High” recoded category. More than half of Cluster 3 schools had mean responses for Disruptive 
Lessons, Mathematics and Science in the “High” category (57% and 55%, respectively). Nearly 
half of these schools also had mean responses in the “High” category for Parents Satisfied with 
School (49%). Nearly half of schools in this cluster have teachers reporting in the “High” category 
for Teacher Job Satisfaction (48%). 
There is some indication that even in these schools there is room for improvement. More than 
one-third of schools have principals that reported in the “Low” category for Principal’s Report, 
Disorder (37%), indicating a greater prevalence of disciplinary problems. The two academic 
emphasis indicators (Principal’s Report, Academic Emphasis and Teacher’s Report, Academic 
Emphasis) have means that are noticeably lower than the highest means for these indicators in 
Cluster 4 schools (Generational Incongruence). 
Reports across stakeholders suggest that schools with healthy social systems show positive 
school climate health in the three areas of healthy relationships, supportive environments, and 
stakeholder satisfaction. Relationships in the classroom, both among students and between 
students and their teachers, are positive and teachers employ engaging teaching practices. The 
school environment is positive with high sense of belonging among students and students ready 
to learn. All groups of stakeholders reported high (if not the highest) levels of satisfaction in these 
schools on average. 
Cluster 4: Generational Incongruence 
 
Indicators of school climate health in schools with Generational Incongruence (Cluster 4) are 
judged differently by students compared to the schools’ adult stakeholders. In comparison with 
Cluster 3 (Healthy Social System), where most schools report in the “High” response category 
for most student indicators, student responses in Cluster 4 schools are less positive (although 
often still more positive than Clusters 1 or 2). However, many of the teacher, parent, and principal 
indicators in Cluster 4 have the highest means of any cluster. Schools in Cluster 4 have the 
most positive reports on average for Parents Satisfied with School, for both Principal’s Report, 
Academic Emphasis and Teacher’s Report, Academic Emphasis, as well as for both Principal’s 
Report, Disorder and Teacher’s Report, School Discipline, and Teacher Job Satisfaction. 
Some disconnect is implied by teachers who reported that they are, on average, using 
engaging teaching practices more often than teachers in schools in other clusters (Engaging 
Teaching Practices), whereas students in these schools reported, on average, more moderate

levels of instructional clarity (Instructional Clarity, Mathematics and Science) and classroom 
disruption (Disruptive Lessons, Mathematics and Science). For Students Like Mathematics and 
Science, far fewer Cluster 4 schools have means in the “High” category compared to Cluster 3 
(28% vs. 88% and 39% vs. 84%, respectively). For Student Sense of Belonging, less than half of 
Cluster 4 schools have means in the “High” category (45%). 
While student reports, on average, were more moderate in Cluster 4, reports from adults 
were overwhelmingly positive. This difference is the defining characteristic of generational 
incongruence. Characterizing this cluster with respect to school climate health themes is difficult 
without separating students from the adults within the school systems. Teachers and principals 
in these schools evaluated the quality of the relationships within the schools as if they were 
operating in different spaces than the students. The teachers and principals report positively 
on their own practices and the state of discipline while average reports from students might 
suggest disengagement – they reported classroom experiences characterized by disruptions and 
less clarity from their teachers. Reports related to school environment follow a similar pattern. 
Principals and teachers in these schools reported high academic emphasis, teachers in these 
schools reported high job satisfaction and that schools are safe. Meanwhile, students reported 
less belonging and experiences of bullying. The generational split is also evident in reports related 
to stakeholder satisfaction. Student reports in Cluster 4 schools are much less positive about their 
confidence or affinity for mathematics and science whereas all groups of adult stakeholders gave 
more positive evaluations.

reported for those schools. In contrast, countries with, on average, lower Home Socioeconomic 
Status scale scores often have relatively high percentages of schools assigned to this cluster. In 
addition, only 7.6 percent of the variance in school-level socioeconomic status is explained by 
the clusters on average across countries.f This provides evidence that the school climate health 
clusters are not simply proxies for the socioeconomic composition of the school.
Among the relatively small number of countries with average Home Socioeconomic Status 
scores reported for all four clusters, there is some evidence that schools in the experiencing 
difficulties cluster may have, on average, students with lower socioeconomic status; however, 
there is little difference among the other three clusters. For example, in Belgium (Flemish), the 
confidence intervals calculated from the standard errors of the average socioeconomic status 
scale scores overlap for the system misalignment, healthy social system, and generational 
incongruence clusters, providing evidence that the averages are not significantly different from 
one another. Germany, Hungary, Italy, Kazakhstan, and Spain, which also have socioeconomic 
status scores reported in all four clusters, show the same pattern.
As it is true in all international comparisons of self-reports, students, teachers, and principals 
may show some cultural differences in how they interpret questions, and how they answer.23,24 
Hence, the proportions of schools assigned to clusters within countries may be somewhat affected 
by cultural differences in item interpretation or the prevalence of certain response tendencies 
within particular cultural contexts.25 For example, there is some evidence of cross-cultural 
variation in individuals’ proclivity for potential socially desirable responding.26 Further research 
can explore the extent to which these phenomena might have contributed to the proportions of 
schools assigned to clusters in individual countries.

Key Takeaways
•	The TIMSS 2023 context questionnaires include a great variety of variables 
related to school climate health and can be used to create profiles characterizing 
school environments based on reports of multiple groups of stakeholders. 
Response patterns on these variables distinctly separated schools into practically 
meaningful typologies of school climate health—with student variables being the 
most discriminating overall. For variables found to be influential in determining cluster 
assignment, intervention can be targeted to support those schools where community 
members report they are not flourishing.

•	The four clusters highlight the importance of accounting for reports from multiple 
stakeholders in evaluating the quality of school environments. Two of the clusters 
identified with the help of MLCA can be described by their overall low mean scores 
relative to schools in the sample (Cluster 1: Experiencing Difficulties) or their relatively 
high scores (Cluster 3: Healthy Social System) across all respondent types. Cluster 
2 (System Misalignment) contains schools where reports from different stakeholders 
do not align. Cluster 4 (Generational Incongruence) is similar, with the added pattern 
that the adults involved are, on average, more positive where students are muted in 
their satisfaction and evaluations of relationships and the school environment. This 
suggests that efforts to positively shape school climate should account for the 
varying experiences of different stakeholders within school systems rather than 
obtaining information from a single source (for example, from only students or 
only teachers).
•	Overall, there is a limited association between cluster membership and the 
socioeconomic status of students within schools. However, several countries whose 
students have, on average, higher socioeconomic status had relatively small numbers of 
schools in the healthy social system cluster. While schools assigned to the experiencing 
difficulties cluster showed on average somewhat lower socioeconomic status scores, 
there was little difference observed among the averages of the other three clusters. 
It is important to note that with, on average, less than eight percent of the variance in 
socioeconomic status explained by the four clusters, there is substantial variance in 
socioeconomic status levels also within each cluster.
•	There appears to be a relationship between cluster membership and achievement 
in mathematics and science, although this should be interpreted with some 
caution. Within most countries, schools assigned to the experiencing difficulties 
cluster had the lowest average achievement compared to schools assigned to other 
clusters. Further investigation is encouraged to clarify the variation in mathematics 
and science achievement across school climate health clusters. There is at least some 
evidence of a negative association between poor school climate health and school 
average mathematics and science achievement within schools; however, the association 
between positive school climate and increased achievement is less clear. Therefore, the 
relationship between cluster membership and achievement merits additional exploration 
in light of potential intervening factors within countries. Cluster proportions vary across 
countries, as expected and explicitly enabled by the flexibility of the analytic approach; 
however, it is noteworthy that many high-achieving countries had very few schools 
assigned to the healthy social system cluster and relatively more schools assigned to 
the system misalignment cluster. This should not be interpreted to imply that system 
misalignment necessarily creates better learning conditions, especially in light of the 
finding that the healthy social system cluster was associated with higher achievement 
at a higher rate across countries than other clusters. Researchers interested in 
disentangling complex relationships between school climate and academic achievement 
should focus on within-country relationships rather than on how a given country 
compares with high-achieving countries.

Future Directions
The results of the TIMSS Insights report suggest several areas for further investigation of school 
climate health and its relationship to student achievement. This report is an important step in 
acknowledging the complexity of measuring school climate and explicitly accounting for schools 
as complex social systems with different groups of stakeholders enacting varying roles. 
	 One area for future research involves validating the findings of this report by investigating 
how school climate health may manifest in lower-secondary schools using Grade 8 TIMSS 2023 
data. Even with the absence of parent satisfaction as a measure,g multidimensional profiles of 
Grade 8 schools based on multiple stakeholders (students, principals, teachers) could still be 
established with the same methodologies. Similarly, this study’s analytic approach could be 
replicated using results from the Progress in International Reading and Literacy Study (PIRLS) to 
see how the context of a different school subject might influence the profiles of Grade 4 schools 
described in this report or their relationship with achievement. 
	 Another avenue of future research is investigating potential confounding school 
characteristics and response patterns that may have influenced the results in this report. It is 
possible that variables other than those included in the model can explain the association between 
the schools assigned to the same school climate health cluster. This does not discredit the 
usefulness of a multidimensional, multi-participant construct like school climate health, but can 
add to the systems-level understanding of forces acting on school learning environments. This 
report includes an initial exploration of the relationship between school-level socioeconomic 
status and school climate health; this investigation could be extended or applied to other school 
characteristics.
Further confoundedness could also be identified in cultural differences in response patterns 
to self-report surveys. Some countries that have the highest proportions of their schools in healthy 
social systems may also be more likely, on average, to respond more positively to survey questions 
compared to other countries. It could also be possible that relatively disengaged respondents 
choose the most positive option for all items to which they respond. The extent to which these 
habits impact the magnitude of positive or negative responses may reduce discrimination in some 
variables and is worthy of further investigation.
	 Finally, these results suggest room for improvement in the measurement of factors related 
to school climate in the TIMSS questionnaires in future assessment cycles. Many of the most 
discriminating items in this report were at the student level, and most of the variables included 
were from them as well. At the teacher or principal level, more information could be collected 
about the interactions between teachers and principals, the collegial environment among teachers, 
and the congruence of how values are communicated from school leadership to teachers before 
being communicated to students.

Appendix C: Technical Details
Multilevel Latent Class Analysis 
Latent class analyses are statistical models that extract group similarities by identifying subgroups 
that share response patterns on indicator variables for the model.27 Schools in the same country 
are likely to provide more similar responses than schools across countries.28 In the context of 
international large-scale assessments such as TIMSS, where data are collected using complex 
sampling designs. involving nested structures (classrooms within schools, and schools within 
countries), a multilevel specification of latent class models is more appropriate.29 This is because 
schools within the same country are likely to exhibit more similar response patterns due to 
shared curricula, educational policies, and cultural factors, compared to schools across different 
countries.30 As such, multilevel extensions of latent structure models are well-suited for capturing 
these hierarchical dependencies.
In this report, Level 1 represents schools and Level 2 represents countries in the multilevel 
structure of the data. Individual-level response data from teachers, students, and parents is 
aggregated up to the school level for our analysis. Ultimately each school climate health latent 
cluster in the final model was described based on variation in the indicator variables from the 
TIMSS 2023 Context Questionnaires. Compared to a latent class analysis performed on Level-1 
units sampled randomly from a population, MLCA also groups Level-2 units based on similarities 
in the Level-1 characteristics within. The MLCA model is based on a probabilistic model that 
allows the log-odds of Level-1 cluster proportions to vary across countries. The clusters identified 
by this methodology are mutually exclusive. Just as no school acts as a representative of multiple 
countries, the schools at Level 1 are assumed to be members of just one of the clusters that are 
identified in the analysis. The steps to finalize the model are to determine the number of Level-2 
classes and then re-estimate the Level-1 clusters under that restriction.

distribution form. Instead, it specifies an additional latent class model at Level-2, where the Level-2 
units are clustered into a discrete number of latent classes, each associated with different Level-1 
class membership distributions. In doing so, distributional assumptions like normality are relaxed 
and computational burden is reduced. Therefore, this study adopts the non-parametric approach.

Hereafter, we refer to Level-1 latent classes as “clusters” and Level-2 latent classes as “classes.” 
The true assignment of observations (schools) to clusters is unknown but can be estimated by 
calculating the posterior probabilities of belonging to a cluster based on the response pattern 
observed for each school. Thus, for each school the probability is calculated that its true cluster 
X is a specific cluster t given that its response pattern (Y) is observed to be y:
For each school, the posterior probability of belonging to each cluster is calculated, and the 
assignment is determined using the proportional or modal method. Proportional assignment 
places schools sharing response patterns y into clusters proportional to the probabilities 
calculated for each cluster. In contrast, the modal approach assigns all schools sharing y to the 
single cluster with the highest posterior probability. This research employed the modal method as 
the number of indicators specified and the multilevel specification resulted in singular response

Model Selection Details
When comparing specified models to evaluated different numbers of clusters or classes 
estimated, there is no target value or indicator of sufficiency. Instead, criteria such as the Bayesian 
Information Criterion (BIC; Schwarz, 197836) can be used to compare models, with lower BIC 
values indicating improvement in fit. A turning point in BIC can signal that adding more clusters 
no longer meaningfully improves the model. The classification error is computed as a weighted 
average of the probability that each school was not assigned to its true cluster, approximated as 
one minus the maximum posterior probability averaged across all schools.37 Modeling decisions 
can also be supported by entropy-based measures of classification quality, such as entropy R2. 
With a range from 0 to 1, entropy R2 gives a summary of the qualities of the model to correctly 
assign cases to clusters. Values over 0.8 are often considered indicative of good classification 
accuracy.38 Different indicators may be more or less influential in deciding on final models. The

School Climate Health Clusters and Achievement
After schools were assigned to clusters based on the modal approach described earlier, the 
average mathematics and science achievement for each cluster within each country was 
estimated using the IEA IDB Analyzer (version 5.0.48).42 The mean achievement for each cluster

To ensure the reliability of the reported statistics, achievement estimates (mean and standard 
error) and the standard error of the percentage were not reported when fewer than five schools 
in a country were assigned to a given cluster. This threshold is based on the rationale that data 
from fewer than five schools are insufficient to produce stable estimates of variance and cluster-
level average achievement.
School Climate Health Clusters and School-Level Average 
HomeSocioeconomic Status
The analysis of school climate health clusters and school-level average home socioeconomic 
status follows an approach similar to that used for achievement. The home socioeconomic status 
variable was derived from the Home Socioeconomic Status scale in the Home Questionnaire, 
which was based on parents’ reports of the number of books and children’s books in the home, 
as well as their self-reported levels of education and occupation. The student-level scale scores 
were aggregated to the school level by computing the weighted mean of students’ scores within 
each school, representing the overall home background of students enrolled in that school. 
Using the IEA IDB Analyzer, the average school-level socioeconomic status and its standard 
error were then estimated for each cluster within each country. Consistent with the reporting rule 
applied to achievement data, socioeconomic status estimates (mean and standard error) and the 
standard error of the percentage were not reported when fewer than five schools in a country 
were assigned to a given cluster.
To quantify the proportion of variance in the school average home socioeconomic status 
explained by the clusters, weighted one-way analyses of variance were conducted separately 
for each country using the IEA IDB Analyzer. The resulting country-specific explained variance 
values were then averaged across countries, excluding countries where home socioeconomic 
status data were available for less than 50 percent of the students.
