This study examines the role of school leadership support in moderating the relationship between student socioeconomic status (SES) and academic achievement in mathematics and science at the 8th-grade level. Using
data from the 2019 Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) across 46 countries, metaanalytic regression models were employed to analyze country-specific results. While SES was found to have a
significant positive impact on achievement in both subjects, school leadership support exhibited a negative and
significant relationship with mathematics achievement and a negative but non-significant relationship with
science achievement. Contrary to expectations, no significant moderating effect of school leadership support on
SES-related achievement gaps was observed at the country-specific level. These findings highlight the variability
in the influence of leadership practices across diverse educational contexts and underscore the challenges of
leveraging leadership support to reduce inequities in achievement. The study emphasizes the need for further
research into the contextual factors shaping leadership effectiveness, advocating for tailored approaches that
address the unique sociocultural and educational dynamics of individual countries.

Introduction
Every child possesses the inherent right to a high-quality education—a fundamental principle underscored by both international
agreements and national education regulations. The Sustainable
Development Goals of the United Nations strongly advocate for member
states to "ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote
lifelong learning opportunities for all" (United Nations, 2015, 2017).
Simultaneously, the significance of breaking the intergenerational cycle
of poverty through universal access to high-quality education is
emphasized in the UN’s World Social Report, highlighting the role of
education in fostering inclusive development (United Nations, 2020;
UNESCO, 2020). Similarly, the OECD (2021) upholds that inclusive and
equitable education is not just a human right but essential for the full
realization of individual capabilities and active societal participation.
Despite these global mandates and the extensive consensus on the
importance of equitable education, significant challenges persist. Socioeconomic achievement gaps remain a stark reality, with students
from lower socioeconomic (SES) backgrounds consistently facing substantial educational disparities. These disparities manifest as lower

academic achievement, higher dropout rates, and a decreased likelihood
of college graduation compared to their higher-SES counterparts (Ballard Brief, 2022; Carnoy & Rothstein, 2013). The widening of this "opportunity gap" over time, particularly in the United States, highlights the
enduring and complex nature of educational inequalities (Reardon &
Portilla, 2016). Furthermore, international assessments like PISA have
repeatedly confirmed the strong association between a student’s SES and
their performance across key academic domains, further substantiating
the need for ongoing efforts to address these disparities (OECD, 2018;
Strello, 2023).
Research exploring the socioeconomic achievement gap has revealed
its profound implications—not only limiting individual potential but
also perpetuating broader economic and social inequities (Duncan &
Murnane, 2011). This issue not only calls for continuous academic
scrutiny but also demands practical interventions aimed at mitigating its
effects. While substantial research has been dedicated to examining the
roles of teachers, curricula, and educational environments in addressing
achievement gaps (Cabral-Gouveia et al., 2023), effective interventions
such as high-quality preschool, challenging curricula, and the roles of
effective teachers have been shown to make significant differences

School leadership
School leadership is often cited as a pivotal element in shaping the
educational environment and influencing student achievement. While
principals and school leaders are recognized for their significant roles in
managing school organization, developing staff capacity, and overseeing the teaching and learning programs (Neufeld, 2014), the
assumption that these contributions always translate into positive student outcomes merits further scrutiny. Research such as Harris & Jones
(2023) supports the notion that effective school leadership correlates
with improved staff and student engagement, which can ultimately
enhance student achievement. However, this relationship is not uniformly observed across different contexts, suggesting that the effectiveness of leadership practices might be contingent on specific
environmental or institutional conditions (Evans, 2022).
Moreover, while leaders are advised to build vision, set direction,
and improve working conditions to positively impact learning (Evans,
2022), the direct correlation between these actions and student performance is not always clear or consistently demonstrated. Leadership influence is often mediated by a range of factors, including but not limited
to the socio-economic background of the student body, the school’s
resource allocation, and local educational policies, which can diminish
or amplify the perceived impact of leadership actions (Cruickshank,
2017).
Furthermore, the assertion that school leaders indirectly influence
student outcomes through establishing school goals, setting high expectations, organizing classrooms, and promoting a positive and orderly
learning environment (Tedla & Kilango, 2022) needs to be critically
evaluated against empirical evidence. This perspective often overlooks
potential discrepancies between the leaders’ initiatives and actual
implementation on the ground, sometimes due to resistance from within
the school staff or from broader community factors.
As education systems continue to evolve to meet the needs of
contemporary society, the role of effective school leadership in
improving the efficiency and equity of schooling is more pronounced
(Pont et al., 2008). However, the effectiveness of school leadership in
achieving these goals is highly dependent on aligning leadership practices with targeted training and resources that address specific school
and community needs. Therefore, while school leadership is a critical
factor in creating a conducive school climate that fosters student
achievement and well-being, its impact is neither automatic nor isolated
from the broader educational ecosystem.
To ensure clarity, we explicitly define the terms "school leadership
support" and "instructional leadership" as they are used in this study.
While these terms are sometimes distinguished in the literature, this
manuscript uses them interchangeably with specific contextual
emphasis. "School leadership support" refers broadly to the overall
support provided by school leaders to create an environment conducive
to student achievement, encompassing aspects such as teacher wellbeing, resource allocation, and fostering a collaborative culture. On
the other hand, "instructional leadership" narrows the focus to leadership practices directly aimed at improving teaching and learning outcomes, such as setting academic goals, monitoring instruction, and
promoting teacher development. This interchangeability aligns with
broader discussions in the field but has been clarified in contexts where
distinguishing these terms is critical, such as when addressing policy

Theoretical framework
This study is grounded in Bourdieu’s theory of social reproduction,
which highlights how inequalities in resource distribution perpetuate
educational inequities. Specifically, the framework underscores the role
of socioeconomic status (SES) in shaping access to quality education and
the compounding effects of school leadership on student achievement.
Additionally, Leithwood et al.’s (2006) conceptualization of instructional leadership provides the basis for examining the specific practices
of school leaders in fostering equitable and effective educational environments. These theoretical perspectives inform the research questions,
guiding the investigation into how school leadership support interacts
with SES to influence student outcomes and addressing gaps in the
literature regarding cross-national and contextual variations in educational equity.
Literature review
Socioeconomic status (SES) inequality in achievement
The conceptualization of inequality in education encompasses a
range of terms and normative ideas, primarily focusing on disparities
stemming from a student’s social background, rather than their individual efforts or abilities (Chmielewski, 2019; Bower, 2013; Strietholt,
2014; Strietholt et al., 2019; Brighouse & Swift, 2008). This study narrows its focus to socioeconomic status (SES) while acknowledging other
influential determinants such as immigration background, gender, and
ethnicity (UNESCO, 2018). The relationship between family SES and
student performance is predominantly explained by disparities in access
to educational resources, suggesting that children from lower SES
backgrounds are systematically disadvantaged, perpetuating educational inequalities (Erylmaz et al., 2020; Bourdieu, 1986; Broer et al.,
2019; Coleman, 1988, 1990). While Bourdieu’s theory effectively
highlights the initial impact of economic resources, followed by cultural
and social capital, it may underrepresent the dynamic interplay of these
capitals in different cultural contexts. The broad acceptance of this
theory overlooks the potential variability in how cultural and social
capitals are valued across different societies (Bourdieu, 1986).
Despite numerous studies affirming the strong association between a
student’s SES background and their academic performance across
various subjects and countries (Hopfenbeck et al., 2018), international
assessments like PISA 2018, TIMSS 2019, and PIRLS 2016 show that the
degree to which SES influences academic achievement varies

In this study, the conceptualization of leadership is more closely
aligned with instructional leadership. Instructional leadership is a specific facet of school leadership that concentrates on activities directly
related to enhancing teaching and learning within the classroom.
Leaders who embrace instructional leadership take a hands-on approach
to shaping the curriculum, improving pedagogical methods, and
fostering a conducive learning environment (Goddard et al., 2021). This
involves collaborating with teachers to refine instructional strategies,
aligning curriculum with educational standards, and providing professional development opportunities (Hallinger and Murphy 1985). The
primary goal of instructional leadership is to elevate the quality of instruction and, consequently, enhance student achievement by focusing
on the core aspects of teaching and learning (Hallinger & Wang, 2015).
By encouraging professional development for teachers, promoting a
positive educational the environment, and obtaining external support,
instructional leadership might additionally be able to help eliminate
these kinds of problems. In this study, I named the concept "school
leadership support" aligning it with previous studies (Toropova et al.,
2021; Eryilmaz et al., 2023, 2024).
Overall, by making up for the lack of direction and encouragement
they receive from their families in the classroom, instructional leadership could turn out to be especially helpful for individual students from
low-SES individuals in schools.

The interplay between leadership support, SES and academic achievement
There are many studies that investigated the relationship between
different type of leadership and academic achievement (Leithwood et al,
2020; Tan, 2018; Tan et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2022). However, the
relationship between school leadership and the socioeconomic status
(SES) academic gap has been a focus of recent research. Studies have
highlighted the potential of instructional leadership provided by principals to moderate the relationship between school-level SES and student achievement, particularly in the areas of mathematics, science, and
reading (Tan, 2018). While stronger instructional leadership has been
associated with diminishing the strength of the relationship between
school-level SES and average student achievement, reducing the
achievement gap between students in high- and low-SES schools
(Ishimaru, & Galloway, 2014), the impact on reducing the disparity
between different SES groups within schools has been less conclusive
(Gumus et al. 2022).
For example, Gumus et al. (2022) explored the moderation effect of
instructional leadership on students’ reading, science and mathematics
achievement with low and high socioeconomic statuses, using
cross-national PISA 2015 data. Their findings underscore a noteworthy
moderation effect, revealing that instructional leadership significantly
moderates the relationship between school-level SES and student
achievement in math. Notably, the study observes that the moderation
effect for individual SES and instructional leadership is not deemed
significant for any subject, adding nuance to the overall impact of
instructional leadership across different dimensions of socioeconomic
status.
Smith and Gümüs (2022) investigated the interplay of socioeconomic status and school leadership in shaping within- and
between-school inequality in student achievement. Drawing on longitudinal data from a survey of 2151 Danish public-school teachers and
administrative records of student achievement for 5729 students, the
study employs multilevel linear regressions with 8th-grade achievement
in mathematics and reading as dependent variables. Their findings
reveal a noteworthy negative interaction between teacher-leader dialogue and school SES for mathematics achievement, suggesting that
engaging in conversations about student outcomes may serve to alleviate between-school inequality in achievement.
While these studies offer valuable insights into the impact of
instructional leadership/school leadership on SES-related achievement
disparities, our research explicitly extends this issue in each country
group. By systematically examining how school leadership support
might mitigate achievement gaps in mathematics and science across
diverse demographics. This study seeks to contribute a more comprehensive understanding of the nuanced dynamics shaping educational
disparities. Through this intentional extension of the inquiry, we aim to
inform educational practices and policies that are not only sensitive to
SES gaps but also addressing how school leadership role is fostering a
more inclusive and equitable learning environment.
Recent research has raised questions about the direct impact of
school leadership on student achievement. Shen and Wu (2024), in their
multivariate meta-analysis, highlight the variability in findings, noting
that while direct effects of principal leadership are often modest, significant indirect effects exist through pathways like teacher practices
and organizational health. Similarly, Grissom et al. (2021) synthesized
two decades of research, emphasizing that principals influence student
outcomes primarily by shaping teacher quality, school climate, and
instructional priorities rather than through direct interactions with
students. These studies reveal gaps in understanding how leadership
practices translate into measurable achievement gains, particularly in
diverse educational contexts. Addressing this gap, the current study
examines how school leadership support interacts with socioeconomic
factors to influence educational equity, providing a nuanced perspective

Method
Data
I analyzed data from the International Association for the Evaluation
of Educational Achievement’s (IEA) Trends in Mathematics and Science
Study (TIMSS) 2019. This investigation assesses the proficiency of 4th
and 8th-grade students in mathematics and science. More precisely, our
analysis centers on the TIMSS 2019 dataset for eighth-grade students,
concentrating on the mathematics and science assessment. Besides the
assessment of achievement, TIMSS also assesses several aspects of students’ home and school, such as home environment and resources,
school climate, and students’ attitudes towards learning.
TIMSS applies a two-stage cluster stratified sampling strategy. In the
first stage, schools are sampled with probability proportional to their
size (PPS) from the list of all schools in the population that contain
eligible students. The second sampling stage consists of selecting one (or
more) intact class from the target grade of each participating school
(Martin et al., 2020).
In the context of TIMSS (Trends in International Mathematics and
Science Study) data collection, the linkage between students and
teachers is established through a systematic process. Initially, a representative sample of schools is selected within participating countries,
ensuring a diverse representation of educational settings. Specific
classrooms within these selected schools are then chosen for participation, and the students in these classrooms undergo assessments in
mathematics and science. Concurrently, teachers of the participating
students are asked to complete detailed questionnaires, providing
valuable information on their background, qualifications, and teaching
practices. This linkage is facilitated through a unique identifier, often
assigned at the school level, enabling researchers to analyze the intricate
relationships between teacher characteristics, instructional methods,
and student achievement. Through this comprehensive approach, TIMSS
aims to offer insights into the factors influencing student performance
on an international scale, fostering a deeper understanding of educational systems worldwide (Yin and Fishbein, 2020).

Analytical strategy
First, to enhance the robustness and replicability of our analysis, we
provide detailed information on the data cleaning and preparation
processes used in this study. We utilized IDB Analyzer software to
analyze the TIMSS 2019 data and applied listwise deletion to handle
missing data. This method ensured that only complete cases were
included in the analysis, maintaining the consistency of the dataset.
Outliers were identified using statistical methods (z-scores), extreme
values were examined to determine if they were data entry errors or
legitimate cases, with errors corrected or removed as appropriate. To
ensure data quality and comparability across the 46 countries, we conducted consistency checks, standardized variables according to TIMSS
coding schemes, and compared key metrics to detect and address
anomalies. These procedures were implemented to uphold the integrity
of the dataset and enhance the validity and replicability of our findings.
Following the approach of integrative data analysis described by
Brunner et al. (2022) and Munk et al. (2024), this study was conducted
in two main stages. In the first stage, country-specific analyses were
performed independently for each participating nation. In the second
stage, these results were combined through meta-analytic techniques to
develop a comprehensive overview. All statistical procedures were
conducted using R software (Version 4.3.0; R Core Team, 2024). The

Meta-analytical integration of the analysis
To address the research questions in this study, a meta-analytic
approach was applied to synthesize country-level regression coefficients. The analysis focused on examining the relationship between
leadership support and student achievement in mathematics and science, as well as the moderating role of leadership support in the association between socioeconomic status (SES) and student achievement in
these subjects.
The integration of results across countries was conducted using
random-effects models, implemented through the metafor package in R
(Viechtbauer, 2010). Random-effects models were selected for two primary reasons. Firstly, they account for both within-country and
between-country variations, effectively capturing cultural and systemic
differences across nations (e.g., Heine et al., 2001; Levels & Dronkers,
2008). Secondly, these models facilitate the generalization of findings
beyond the specific dataset to a broader global context (Borenstein et al.,
2010; Brunner et al., 2022). The restricted maximum likelihood (REML)
method was used for model estimation, recognized for its efficiency and
minimal bias (Viechtbauer, 2005).
To evaluate heterogeneity across countries, Q-tests and I² statistics
were computed. Q-tests assessed whether there were significant variations in results across countries (Borenstein et al., 2009), while I² statistics quantified the proportion of variance attributable to
heterogeneity rather than sampling error (Borenstein et al., 2017).
Heterogeneity levels were categorized based on Higgins and Thompson’s (2002) criteria as low (I² < 40 %), moderate (30 %–60 %), substantial (50 %–90 %), or considerable (75 %–100 %). Forest plots,
created using the metafor and ggplot2 packages in R (Wickham, 2016),
were used to visually represent country-specific findings alongside the
overall estimates.

Discussion
This study aimed to examine the relationship between school leadership, and student mathematics and science achievement and possible
moderating effects based on student SES, across 46 countries using the
TIMSS 2019 dataset. Drawing inspiration from the pioneering work of
Gumus et al. (2022) and taking into account the insights provided by
Smith and Gumus (2022), I contribute to the field by using the more
recent TIMSS 2019 data to investigate the relationship between school
leadership support, teacher characteristics, some student characteristics
and mathematics and science achievement in a comparative manner.
This study updates those results by using more recent TIMSS 2019 data.
Overall, the findings of this study suggest that leadership support is not
significantly related to students’ mathematics and science achievement
across countries and this study did not find any evidence for moderation
effect. This section presents and discusses the main findings in line with
the research questions of the study.

This aligns with the literature, which confirms the school’s limited role

schools to improve overall student achievement might inadvertently
widen achievement gaps, particularly for low-SES and special education
students who may already lag behind their peers. These students are
often at risk of suffering from low expectations, which further exacerbates disparities.
A shift in the focus of instructional leadership from a traditional
school effectiveness perspective toward addressing individual student
needs may be necessary to mitigate these disparities. Integrating a social
justice leadership framework, as suggested by Gumus et al., 2022, could
serve as a promising approach. This shift would emphasize adopting
appropriate teaching practices, providing professional development
opportunities for teachers, and aligning instructional strategies with the
needs of disadvantaged students. Notably, such elements are often
missing in many conceptualizations of instructional leadership.
The interpretation of leadership practices may vary significantly
across countries, influenced by diverse sociocultural and political contexts (Eryilmaz et al., 2024; Hallinger, 2018). These variations underscore the limitations of a one-size-fits-all approach to educational
leadership (Bush, 2018). To address these complexities, this study conducted a country-level regression analysis to examine the impact of

The findings from the regression models revealed a significant positive impact of student socioeconomic status on their mathematics and
science achievement. This suggests that higher levels of SES contribute
to improved academic performance in both subjects. These results align
with previous research highlighting the pivotal role of SES in shaping
educational outcomes (Gumus et al., 2022; Smith and Gumus, 2022).
Such findings emphasize the critical need for educational practices and
policies to mitigate SES-related disparities, ensuring students from
disadvantaged backgrounds receive equitable support and opportunities
to succeed academically.
Regarding school leadership support, the results indicated a negative
and significant relationship with students’ mathematics achievement
and a negative but non-significant relationship with science achievement. This would be an unexpected finding when compared to earlier
studies emphasizing the positive impact of leadership on student outcomes (Louis et al., 2010; Sun & Leithwood, 2017). However, recent
studies conducted by Eryilmaz and Strietholt (2024), Gumus et al.
(2022), and Smith and Gumus (2022) found similar trends, lending
further credibility to these results. These findings suggest that specific
dimensions of leadership support may need to be scrutinized more
closely, as their impact on student outcomes is likely nuanced and
context dependent.
One explanation for these findings lies in the cross-sectional nature
of TIMSS data, which captures relationships at a single point in time.
Previous studies, such as those by Heck and Hallinger (2010), propose
that improvements in student achievement due to leadership support
accumulate gradually over time. This temporal dimension may not be
observable in cross-sectional datasets, which limits the ability to capture
the full impact of leadership on student outcomes. Additionally, it is

Future research should adopt mixed methods approaches that integrate both quantitative data from large-scale assessments and qualitative case studies to provide a more nuanced understanding of school
leadership’s role in educational contexts. Conducting in-depth qualitative research or country-specific case studies could uncover the

future studies could explore the interplay between these forms of leadership and other contextual factors, such as government policies or
community involvement, to better understand their impact on student

applicability of findings across diverse educational systems.
Future research endeavors should focus on expanding the empirical
evidence base, employing diverse methodologies, and considering
various contextual factors to enhance our understanding of the nuanced
relationship between school leadership, socioeconomic status, and student achievement.

This study employs cross-sectional data from the Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS), which enables us to
examine direct associations between students and their teachers and
establish links between teacher and student data. This approach offers a
significant advantage over PISA data, as TIMSS allows for a more direct
and contextualized analysis of the teacher-student relationship. However, it is important to note that cross-sectional studies, such as the one
used in this research, do not permit the establishment of causal relationships. Cross-sectional studies provide a snapshot of data at a single
point in time, making it impossible to determine the directionality of
relationships between variables. While the findings of this study offer
valuable insights into the associations between student achievement,
socioeconomic status, and leadership support, it is essential to exercise
caution when inferring causation from the observed relationships.
Future research employing longitudinal or experimental designs may be
necessary to establish causal links between the variables examined in
this study.
One limitation of this study is that the measurement of school
leadership support was based on only one item, as the TIMSS data
provides limited information about school leadership. This restricted
measurement may not fully capture the multifaceted nature of school
leadership support, potentially overlooking other critical dimensions
that could influence student achievement. As a result, the study’s
assessment of school leadership support may not comprehensively
represent the various ways in which leadership influences student outcomes, highlighting the need for more extensive measures of school
leadership support in future research to provide a more nuanced understanding of its impact on student achievement.
