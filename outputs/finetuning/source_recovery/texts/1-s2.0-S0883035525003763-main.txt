This study explored the mediating role of motivation towards science and the moderating role of
cultures in the relationship between parental support and adolescent science achievement. We
conducted a moderated mediation analysis of the 2015 Programme of International Student
Assessment data from Korea (n = 5581) and the United States (n = 5712). The results imply that
culture moderated the effects of parental support on students’ science motivation and achievement: the predictive effect of parental support on intrinsic motivation was stronger in Korea than
in the United States, and parental support significantly predicted higher science achievement in
Korea but not in the United States. Furthermore, adolescents’ motivation towards science
mediated the relationship between parental support and science achievement. The study sheds
light on different mechanisms underlying parental support, adolescents’ motivation towards
science, and science achievement. The findings also highlight the need to consider adolescents’
motivation towards science when explaining the effect of parental support on their science performance. Finally, the findings advance motivational theories, particularly in the science education field.

1. Introduction
Parents, as their children’s primary socializing agents, play an important role in adolescents’ motivation and cognitive development (Bornstein, 2019). Studies show that parents significantly impact students’ future science-related choices (García-Pérez et al.,
2020; Sjaastad, 2012). Parental involvement broadly encompasses how parents engage with, participate in, and actively support their
children’s educational experiences (Boonk et al., 2018; Kim, 2022). Parents shape a holistic experience of learning for their children,
and actively engaged parents often create supportive and warm environments that reinforce learning and instill a passion for education
(Diaconu-Gherasim et al., 2022). Thus, parental involvement establishes a collaborative partnership between home and school,
empowering students to thrive personally and academically.
Numerous studies have shown a positive relationship between parental involvement and academic achievement (e.g., Kim, 2022;

2. Literature review
2.1. Parental support and its effect on adolescents’ academic achievement
Parental support for adolescents’ learning, a specific aspect of overall parental involvement, is strongly characterized by autonomy
encouragement rather than control. Discussions of parental support often commence with the broader concept of parental involvement, which encompasses how parents engage with, accompany, and support their children in their learning experiences through
diverse practices. Parental support can be categorized into three main involvement types: behavioral involvement, personal
involvement, and cognitive/intellectual involvement (Grolnick & Slowiaczek, 1994; Sha et al., 2016). Depending on the context,
involvement may be school-based or home-based (Boonk et al., 2018; Epstein & Sanders, 2002; Kim et al., 2020). Home-based
involvement can be further categorized as academic-oriented (e.g., talking about academic issues) and non-academic-oriented (e.g.,
exhibiting interest in the adolescent’s life; Borgonovi & Montt, 2012). That is, parents can provide academic guidance and engage in
adolescents’ school activities, while also providing emotional support by being responsive and granting autonomy (Pinquart, 2016).
Previous literature indicated that the effects of different types of parental support on student learning depend on student developmental stages. The efficacy of parental support varies as well (Boonk et al., 2018). For example, participating in reading activities,
consistently engaging in learning at home, and sharing recreational pursuits demonstrate a positive correlation with young children’s
academic performance. However, as individuals transition into adolescence, the impact of tangible support may diminish as greater
emphasis is placed on emotional support. Boonk et al.’s (2018) review suggested that future research should refrain from broadly
conceptualizing parental involvement, and instead focus on elucidating the specific beneficial aspects of involvement types tailored to
particular age groups. Thus, our study targeted adolescents (15-year-olds) and scrutinized parental support in the emotional
dimension.
Previous meta-analyses (Boonk et al., 2018; Kim, 2022; Kim et al., 2020) indicated a generally positive relationship between
parental involvement, in its broadest sense, and students’ academic achievement. Nevertheless, the magnitude of correlations has
varied, ranging from small to medium (e.g., Castro et al., 2015; Fan & Chen, 2001; Jeynes, 2003, 2007). On the other hand, some
researchers have found either a negative correlation or no discernible relationship (Fan & Chen, 2001; Patall et al., 2008; Pinquart,
2016). Such conflicting findings suggest the need for a more detailed examination of the parental involvement dimensions and possible
mechanisms that mediate the relationship between parental support and their children’s academic achievement.
2.2. Mediating effects of adolescents’ learning motivation on the relationship between parental support and academic achievement
Adolescents’ motivation towards learning is a crucial psychological construct that facilitates their learning. Our study focuses on
three major motivational constructs—intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and self-efficacy. Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation
are key components of self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2009; Ryan & Deci, 2020), while self-efficacy is rooted in social
cognitive theory (Bandura, 1997). Self-determination theory reveals the importance of individuals’ autonomy and relatedness in
driving adolescents’ behavior and personal growth. Intrinsic motivation, driven by enjoyment and satisfaction in learning, correlates
positively with deep learning and academic achievement (Hidi, 2006; Vasquez et al., 2016). Intrinsic motivation directly corresponds
with the need for autonomy, reflecting an individual’s desire for self-directed action and control over their behaviors. Conversely,
extrinsic motivation (particularly, identified regulation) entails a desire to learn to achieve future goals, and fosters a sense of
relatedness through an activity’s alignment with external rewards and social expectations (Ryan & Deci, 2009; Taylor et al., 2014).
Meanwhile, social cognitive theory suggests that self-efficacy, one’s belief in one’s competency to achieve tasks, is the dominating
factor explaining individuals’ actions (Bandura, 1997). It is shaped by experiences and perceptions, extending to discipline-specific
domains (Bong & Skaalvik, 2003). Moreover, it is closely linked to the need for competence, and embodies an individual’s belief in
their capability to accomplish tasks. In general, research shows that self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation benefit adolescents’ academic achievement (Britner & Pajares, 2006; Taylor et al., 2014). The literature shows an inconclusive relationship between extrinsic
motivation and academic achievement (Liou, 2021), which can be attributed to the diverse dimensions of extrinsic motivation, ranging
from integrated autonomous motivation to external controlled motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2020). Within the diverse dimensions of
extrinsic motivation, some aspects stem from an individual’s perception of the importance or personal benefits of completing an
activity, while others are driven by external pressures or contingencies. A meta-analysis based on 344 samples composed of >220,000
participants showed that intrinsic motivation and the two forms of extrinsic motivation with the highest levels of autonomy are
positively related to academic achievement (Howard et al., 2021). In comparison, the two extrinsic regulation types with the least
autonomy had non-significant and even negative relationships with academic achievement. A series of international comparative
studies has shown that, after controlling for intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation is unrelated or even negatively related to students’ achievement (Karakolidis et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020; Ustun, 2024). Meanwhile, extrinsic motivation can predict science career

Students’ intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and self-efficacy are impacted by those people they are most closely involved
with (Bandura, 1997; Ryan & Deci, 2009; Ryan & Deci, 2020). In other words, parents, often as the closest individuals to students,
inevitably play a crucial role in shaping students’ motivation. For instance, parental support can make learning activities more
enjoyable for their children and encourage them to face academic challenges; such support can enhance their confidence in learning
(Gonzalez-DeHass et al., 2005; Sha et al., 2016). Considering the relationship between parental support and students’ achievement, as
well as the effect of parental support on their children’s motivation towards learning, it is evident that parental support not only has a
direct predictive effect on achievement, but also exerts an indirect effect through motivation towards learning. Therefore, motivation
towards learning can be considered a crucial mediator in the relationship between parental support and academic achievement.
In fact, researchers have documented the mediating role of adolescents’ achievement-related motivation in the relationship between parental support and educational achievement outcomes (Boonk et al., 2018; Kim, 2022; Kim et al., 2020). The review work by
Boonk et al. (2018) indicated that, in several reviewed studies, mediating variables (e.g., motivational constructs) help clarify the
relationship between parental involvement and student achievement. Moreover, different types of motivation towards learning science
may have differential effects on student achievement (Liou, 2021; Liou et al., 2019). Understanding how motivational constructs are
influenced by parental support makes it possible to grasp their effects on achievement. Thus, this study aimed to address the lack of
research into how adolescents’ motivation towards science mediates the relationship between parental support and science
achievement.
2.3. Cultural variations in the effect of parental support on students’ motivation and academic achievement
The effect of parental support on adolescents’ motivation towards learning varies across cultures. A meta-analysis by Kim et al.
(2020) showed a small overall effect of parental influence indicators on immigrant students’ academic motivation (r = 0.22). However,
all 14 analyzed studies were conducted in the United States and Europe. A U.S.-based study by Fan et al. (2012) highlighted distinctive
patterns among East Asian American parents compared to parents from other ethnic groups. Specifically, East Asian American parents
exhibited lower rates of parent-child communication and school involvement but higher educational expectations for their children.
These differences extend to the effects of parental support on students’ academic motivation, emphasizing the importance of
considering cultural differences in educational research and practice.
From a comprehensive perspective, the interplay between parental support, motivation, and achievement occurs within diverse
proximal environments (e.g., parents) and distal contexts (e.g., culture and country; Bronfenbrenner, 2009; Hofstede, 2001; Kim et al.,
2020). Hofstede’s (2001) cultural dimensions theory includes the individualism-collectivism dimension for comparing national cultures. This dimension refers to the degree to which individuals are expected to take responsibility for themselves or remain integrated
into groups. In collectivist cultures, interpersonal relationships, especially within families, are more closely bonded than in individualistic cultures.
Our study targeted two distinct countries, the United States and Korea, representing the two extreme ends of the individualismcollectivism dimension. According to Hofstede’s cultural dimension scores (Hofstede, 2015), which range from 0 to 100, the
United States scores highest for individualist culture (= 91), while Korea ranks among the lowest (= 18), embodying a collectivist
ethos. This cultural framework profoundly shapes parental attitudes towards education. The United States is widely characterized as a
nation founded on individualism, emphasizing the distinctiveness and autonomy of each person (Gregg, 2022). In contrast, American
society demonstrates a sustained commitment to individual agency and self-expression, in which personal rights and independence are
central cultural values (Markus & Kitayama, 2010). In Korea, the collectivist culture instills a heightened focus on meeting societal and
familial expectations, which drives parents to invest heavily in private tutoring to ensure their children’s academic success (Park et al.,
2011; Young-Kyun, 2011). While such support provides resources and opportunities for achievement, it may prioritize conformity and
external validation over fostering students’ intrinsic interests or personal learning goals. Consequently, parental support in collectivist
contexts may be more strongly oriented toward tangible outcomes such as examination performance, thereby enhancing academic
scores, but potentially at the expense of students’ autonomy and internal motivation.
Korean adolescents consistently rank at the top in science achievement in international large-scale international assessments, yet
their motivation towards learning science is significantly lower than that of students from other countries, including the United States
(Elliott et al., 2019; Mullis et al., 2020; Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development OECD, 2016). In comparison, adolescents in the United States exhibit much stronger motivation towards learning science, while their science achievement is much
lower than that of Koreans. The importance of motivation towards science learning lies in its role as an essential educational outcome
in its own right (Osborne et al., 2003). We speculate that cultural differences shape the varying effects of parental support from Korean
and American parents on adolescents’ learning outcomes. Specifically, while Korean adolescents often demonstrate excellent academic
performance, parental support in Korea does not appear to significantly enhance their motivation towards learning. On the other hand,
parental support in the United States is more closely linked to shaping positive motivation towards learning than to directly influencing
academic performance. Relatively few studies have examined cultural differences in the effects of parental support on adolescents’
motivation towards science and their science performance. Thus, this study examined this relationship in two culturally different
countries, Korea and the United States.

4. Method
4.1. Samples
The data came from the Korean and American adolescent samples of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
2015. The PISA is a tri-annual Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) assessment that measures the
knowledge of 15-year-olds by evaluating their science literacy, mathematics skills, reading knowledge, and ability to meet real-life
challenges (Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development OECD, 2016). Each PISA focuses on only one domain; the
2015 PISA focused on science.
The 15-year-old participants were selected via two-stage stratified sampling. The representative schools were chosen based on the
school size, and around 30 to 33 adolescents from each sampled school were surveyed (Organisation for Economic Cooperation and
Development OECD, 2017). Selected individuals took approximately 3 hours to complete the assessment and a context questionnaire.
A total of 5581 Korean adolescents and 5712 American adolescents were included in the sample of this study.
4.2. Measures
This study included eight manifest variables. Parental support was the antecedent variable, and adolescent science achievement
was the outcome variable. Three mediators were related to adolescents’ motivation towards science (science intrinsic motivation,
science extrinsic motivation, and science self-efficacy), while country served as a moderator. Finally, the two control variables for
science achievement were gender and adolescents’ economic, social, and cultural status. The item descriptions for each variable and
corresponding reliability coefficients are presented in Table 1.
Adolescents’ perceived parental support was measured using four items that gauged their agreement with statements regarding
their parents’ interests, support, and encouragement regarding school or academic-related issues (e.g., “My parents are interested in
my school activities”). As noted above, we conceptualized adolescents’ motivation towards science across three dimensions: intrinsic
motivation, extrinsic motivation, and self-efficacy. Science intrinsic motivation was quantified by five items reflecting adolescents’
enjoyment of learning science (e.g., “I generally have fun when I am learning 〈broad science〉 topics”). Science extrinsic motivation
assesses the perceived future benefits of learning science through four items (e.g., “Making an effort in my 〈school science〉 subject(s) is
worth it because this will help me in the work I want to do later on”). Science self-efficacy was evaluated through eight items measuring
adolescents’ confidence in their ability to perform science tasks (e.g., “Recognise the science question that underlies a newspaper
report on a health issue”). All items for these variables utilized a 4-point Likert scale.
The four variables, parental support and adolescents’ motivation towards science, exhibited measurement invariance in the two
countries. We examined measurement invariance using a hierarchical procedure testing configural, metric, and scalar levels.
Furthermore, measurement invariance was evaluated based on changes in the comparative fit index (ΔCFI) and the root mean square
error of approximation (ΔRMSEA), with ΔCFI not exceeding 0.010 and ΔRMSEA less than or equal to 0.010 considered acceptable
evidence of invariance (Chen, 2007; Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014). Table 2 shows that the data achieved metric invariance, which
constrained factor loadings to equality, and resulted in minimal changes in the fit indices (ΔCFI = 0.001, ΔRMSEA < 0.001), supporting equal factor loadings in Korea and the United States. These variables were formed via item response theory scaling

In the PISA, adolescents’ science literacy scores were adjusted to a mean of 500 and a standard deviation of 100 across participating
OECD countries. In PISA, scientific literacy is defined as the ability to use scientific knowledge to identify questions, acquire new
knowledge, and draw evidence-based conclusions in order to understand and make decisions about the world around us (Organisation
for Economic Cooperation and Development OECD, 2017). However, despite having clear assessment objectives, it lacks representative items (Le Hebel et al., 2017; Nentwig et al., 2009). Furthermore, it includes a focus on testing knowledge of science, including
content knowledge, procedural knowledge, and epistemic knowledge (Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development
OECD, 2017). Therefore, we used “science achievement” to represent the PISA term, “science literacy.”
Participants’ economic, social, and cultural status were measured using the indicators of parental education, highest parental
occupation, and home possessions. Gender and country were dummy-coded (i.e., 0 = female, 1 = male; 0 = United States, 1 = Korea).
The proportion of missing values was 1.28 % for parental support, 2.17 % for intrinsic motivation, 3.04 % for extrinsic motivation, 3.38
% for self-efficacy, and 0.95 % for economic, social, and cultural status. All other variables included in the study had no missing values.
4.3. Data analytic strategy
We conducted a moderated mediation analysis to answer the research questions. The use of moderated mediation analysis is
particularly advantageous in our study as it can test whether motivation towards science explains the effect of parental support on
science achievement, while also accounting for the influence of country. This approach provides deeper insights that would be
overlooked by simple mediation or moderation models alone. This analytical approach (Hayes, 2017; Preacher et al., 2007) emphasizes the value of mediation and moderation to uncover complex mechanisms for educational settings.
We utilized bootstrapping to test the indirect effect of parental support on science achievement through motivation towards science
in Korea and the United States. Bootstrapping provides robust estimates of standard errors and confidence intervals by repeatedly
sampling the data (Hayes, 2017). When the results showed significant differences, we conducted multi-group analysis utilizing simple
slope tests to compare the regression coefficient between the Korean and American samples (Hayes, 2017). We subtracted the mean
estimate of the American sample from that of the Korean sample. Thus, if the coefficient of the simple slope test was positive, the effect
was deemed larger in Korea. Additionally, we plotted interaction graphs to show the mechanisms underlying these moderations.
All analyses were conducted using Mplus 8.11 (Muthén & Muthén, 2024). We considered the complex nature of factors implemented in the PISA, such as the cluster and weights of the sampling design (Liou & Hung, 2015). Moreover, we conducted the analyses
separately for each of the 10 plausible values of science achievement. The results were then pooled using Rubin’s rules (Rubin, 1987),
which account for both within-imputation and between-imputation variance, to report the final estimates and their standard errors.
The maximum likelihood method was used as the estimator for bootstrapping (Beauducel & Herzberg, 2006). Given the low portion of
missing data (ranging from 0.95 % to 3.38 %), full information maximum likelihood estimation (FIML; Enders, 2010) implemented via
the robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimator was employed to handle missingness.

Parental support generally showed small but significant correlations with science achievement and motivation towards science. In
Korea, correlations were observed between parental support and both science achievement (r = 0.15, p < .001) and motivation towards science (r = 0.14 ~ 0.15, ps < 0.001). In the United States, parental support also correlated with science achievement (r = 0.07, p

5.2. Effects of parental support on motivation and science achievement in Korea and the United States
The moderated mediation analysis revealed the relationships among adolescents’ perceived parental support, motivation towards
science, and science achievement in Korea and the United States, as shown in Table 4. Parental support did not interact with country
for extrinsic motivation or self-efficacy. It exerted identical effects on extrinsic motivation (B = 0.12, SE = 0.01, β = 0.13, p < .001) and
self-efficacy (B = 0.14, SE = 0.02, β = 0.11, p < .001) in both countries. In other words, parental support positively predicted adolescents’ extrinsic motivation and self-efficacy.
Conversely, two statistically significant interaction terms were found between parental support and country. These results indicate
that country moderated the predictive effects of parental support on intrinsic motivation and science achievement. Specifically, the
effects of parental support on intrinsic motivation significantly varied between Korea and the United States (B = 0.05, SE = 0.03, β =
0.02, p = .039). Likewise, the relationship between parental support and science achievement significantly differed between Korean
and American students (B = 7.52, SE = 2.25, β = 0.03, p = .001). The results of the simple slope tests summarized in Table 5 further
elaborate the mechanisms in the two countries.
Regarding the predictive effect of parental support on intrinsic motivation, the average levels of parental support and intrinsic
motivation were higher in the United States than in Korea. However, the relationship was stronger in Korea (B = 0.19, SE = 0.02, β =
0.16, p < .001) than in the United States (B = 0.13, SE = 0.02, β = 0.12, p < .001). Fig. 2 shows the relationships between parental
support and intrinsic motivation in the two countries. It should be noted that the predictive effect of parental support on the three
motivational constructs appears minimal, with R² values ranging from 0.02 to 0.03.
The relationship between parental support and science achievement was significant in Korea (B = 3.73, SE = 1.49, β = 0.04, p =
.012) but not in the United States (B = − 1.92, SE = 1.67, β = − 0.02, p = .249). The results reveal a significant moderation effect of
parental support on science achievement and a notable difference between Korea and the United States. As plotted in Fig. 3, parental
support had a positive relationship with science achievement in Korea but a nonsignificant relationship in the United States.
5.3. Mediating effect of adolescents’ motivation towards science in the link between parental support and science achievement
The results reveal that all three motivational constructs toward science mediated the predictive effect of parental support on
adolescents’ science achievement in both Korea and the United States. Table 6 presents the estimated coefficients. Specifically, the
indirect effect of parental support on science achievement via intrinsic motivation was positive and statistically significant in Korea (B
= 4.35, SE = 0.53, β = 0.04, p < .001) and in the United States (B = 3.08, SE = 0.41, β = 0.03, p < .001). The indirect effect of parental
support on science achievement via self-efficacy was also positive and statistically significant in both Korea (B = 1.59, SE = 0.28, β =
0.02, p < .001) and the United States (B = 1.20, SE = 0.25, β = 0.01, p < .001). Conversely, extrinsic motivation negatively mediated
the relationship between parental support and science achievement in Korea (B = − 1.17, SE = 0.24, β = − 0.01, p < .001) and the
United States (B = − 1.02, SE = 0.21, β = − 0.01, p < .001).
Despite the significant relationships observed for all three indirect effects across both countries, a direct effect of parental support
on science achievement was found only in the Korean sample (B = 5.38, SE = 1.71, β = 0.05, p = .002) and not in the American sample
(B = − 2.14, SE = 1.64, β = − 0.02, p = .193). Consequently, a significant and positive total effect was observed only in Korea (B =
10.15, SE = 1.84, β = 0.10, p < .001) and not in the United States (B = 1.13, SE = 1.64, β = 0.01, p = .491).
6. Discussion
In many ways, parents are crucial agents who nurture their children during adolescence. They are also the main stakeholders in
adolescents’ learning processes and outcomes. The present study unpacks the complex relationship between parental support and
science achievement by revealing the important role of adolescents’ motivation towards science. The results show how parental
support operates on adolescents’ development of science achievement-related motivation and educational performance in Korea and
the United States. While adolescents’ science achievement might be a primary concern for many parents, the results indicate that
parents should focus more on nurturing adolescents’ motivation towards science to optimize their educational achievements. The
following sections address this study’s most noteworthy findings.

typically fosters close parent-student relationships, particularly in academic matters. This indicates that American parents, compared

represent the first attempt to disclose initial insights in a research area that currently lacks empirical cross-cultural studies, thereby
contributing valuable preliminary evidence to this field of study.
6.2. Effects of parental support on adolescents’ motivation towards science
Parental support has positive effects on students’ motivation (Gonzalez-DeHass et al., 2005; Grolnick & Slowiaczek, 1994; Kim
et al., 2020). Specifically, our results indicate that parental support enhances students’ intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and
self-efficacy in learning science. Parents’ support and care foster students’ curiosity and enjoyment in learning, which increases their
intrinsic motivation. This support also benefits adolescents’ confidence, enabling them to tackle difficult tasks and challenges with
improved assurance and determination (Gonzalez-DeHass et al., 2005). Furthermore, positive parental support may boost students’
academic and future aspirations, thereby increasing the appeal of external incentives. Lerner et al. (2022) also found that when parents
adopt an autonomy-supportive approach, students tend to worry less and have higher perceived competence, autonomous motivation,
and grades. In our study, we described parental support as nurturing and warm, and our findings demonstrate that such support
improves adolescents’ motivation towards science.
Nevertheless, our results show that the magnitude of parental support on different aspects of motivation is minimal. We speculate
that students’ motivation is typically shaped by multiple factors. Take self-efficacy as an example; students’ self-efficacy may be
influenced by various social agents, such as teachers, peers, and parents (Bandura, 1997). Receiving feedback and encouragement from
others is one important source of building self-efficacy. Through communication and interaction between parents and children,
parental support has positive effects on students’ motivation towards learning (Gonzalez-DeHass et al., 2005; Grolnick & Slowiaczek,
1994). Nevertheless, the multiplicity of influencing factors may constrain the magnitude of the effect exerted by each individual factor.
Our study also found significant differences between Korea and the United States concerning the effect of parental support on
intrinsic motivation and science achievement. Specifically, parental support had a stronger positive effect on intrinsic motivation in
Korea compared to the United States. Emotional support is not as strongly expressed by Korean parents, who often focus more on
practical aspects such as performance and economic support. Thus, Korean parents should consider enhancing their emotional support
to benefit their adolescent children’s intrinsic motivation. In the United States, the relationship is less pronounced, possibly because
American teenagers already have relatively high levels of intrinsic motivation, leaving less room for additional improvement through
parental support.
6.3. Mediating effects of adolescents’ motivation towards science on the relationship between parental support and science achievement in
Korea and the United States
According to our analysis, the predictive effect of parental support on adolescents’ science achievement was mediated by all three
motivational constructs in both Korea and the United States. The predictive effect of parental support on adolescents’ science
achievement disappeared for the American sample when the three types of motivational construct were considered. As shown in
Table 1, parental support has a significant zero-order correlation with science achievement (r = 0.07, p < .001). However, this direct
connection becomes statistically insignificant (B = − 2.14, SE = 1.64, CI = [− 5.35, 1.08], p = .193) when other factors are considered.
The relationship is fully captured by the mediated connection through motivation towards science in the United States. In Korea,
however, both the direct and indirect effects of the three motivational constructs, which combine to have a significant total effect, are
statistically significant. While the mediational mechanisms differ between the two countries, the results highlight the significant role of
adolescents’ motivation towards science in facilitating their science achievement.
Our analysis also indicated that the indirect pathway from parental support to science achievement, through both intrinsic
motivation and self-efficacy, was statistically significant. This indicates that parental support boosts adolescents’ motivation to learn
science and their belief in their capability to succeed in scientific endeavors, leading to better science achievement. Intrinsic motivation is the most autonomous form of motivation. Intrinsically motivated individuals perform tasks because these tasks lead to
enjoyment and a sense of satisfaction. However, self-determination theory conceptualizes individual extrinsic motivation into four
types of regulation, ordered along a continuum of relative autonomy: integrated, identified, introjected, and external regulations
(Ryan & Deci, 2009; Ryan & Deci, 2020). Performing activities for external incentives (e.g., getting a job) is considered the least

autonomous external regulation.
The study also found that extrinsic motivation has a negative mediating effect. In our mediated moderation model, after accounting
for intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy, the initially weak positive correlation between extrinsic motivation and science achievement
became negative. This shift reflects a suppression effect, indicating that intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy explain most of the
positive variance in achievement, while the unique contribution of extrinsic motivation may be negatively associated with performance. On the other hand, as discussed in the literature review, extrinsic motivation comprises both high- and low-autonomy forms
(Howard et al., 2021). While high-autonomy extrinsic motivation is positively related to academic achievement, low-autonomy forms
show weak or negative associations. As a result, the overall effect of extrinsic motivation on science achievement can appear negative,
reflecting the limited or detrimental impact of low-autonomy external regulation. Another explanation is that, for 15-year-olds,
external influences such as rewards or social expectations may weaken the effect of parental support on science achievement, thus
underscoring the necessity of accounting for these contextual factors. Our results regarding self-efficacy align with previous studies
(Ardura & Galán, 2019; Liou, 2021) showing that science self-efficacy positively predicts science performance.
Our mediation results show that adolescents’ motivation towards science connects parental support with student success. We found
that parental support directly influences motivation. Meanwhile, intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy positively predict science
achievement, whereas extrinsic motivation negatively predicts science achievement. The indirect effect shows that motivation towards
science is a significant mediator in the link between parental support and achievement. Consequently, the directional predictive effect
of parental support on students’ achievement varies depending on the student’s motivation towards science. That is, when mediated by
intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy, the predictive effect of parental support on science achievement is positive. However, when
mediated by extrinsic motivation, the predictive effect of parental support on science achievement is negative. Overall, our findings
highlight the importance of students’ motivation towards science in understanding the impact of parental support on their academic
performance.
6.4. Limitations and suggestions for future research
A few limitations of the current study must be noted. First, the operational definition of parental support in this study focuses on the
emotional dimension due to the limited set of items included in the PISA 2015 dataset. This fact constrained our ability to capture all
dimensions of parental support, such as behavioral, personal, and cognitive/intellectual involvement, as suggested in the literature (e.
g., Grolnick & Slowiaczek, 1994). It is recommended that future studies include a more comprehensive range of parental support
indicators.
Second, we acknowledge that due to the diversity within the United States, it may not be entirely accurate to treat it as a uniformly
individualistic society. For instance, Vandello & Cohen (1999) demonstrated that although the United States is typically classified as an
individualist country in cross-cultural research, there is considerable regional variation in individualism-collectivism orientations
across different states. Compared to Korea, the United States is considered more heterogeneous, both in terms of ethnic composition
and the proportion of immigrants (Fan et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2020). Therefore, future research may need to take this issue into
account and address it with more nuanced analysis.
Third, the data were fundamentally correlational, meaning that various confounding variables were not ruled out. Based on the
understanding of the mechanisms for guiding interventions (Elliott et al., 2019; Sha et al., 2016), follow-up research is needed to
confirm the causal link between parental support and science performance. Moreover, the main outcome variable of this study was
adolescents’ science achievement at one time point. Future research should examine the longitudinal effects of parental support on
other desired outcomes, such as major, career, and life-long learning in science fields.
Finally, this study only examined data obtained purely from the adolescents. A more thorough understanding of the relationship
between parental support and adolescents’ science achievement could be attained by considering parents’ perspectives. Reparaz &
Sotés-Elizalde (2019) found that parental-derived measures of parental support (e.g., communication and involvement in
education-related events) positively predicted students’ science achievement in Spain and Germany. Moreover, parental perceptions of
support were stronger than those of their children. While the United States does not have a parent-derived version of parental support
in the PISA 2015 data, the Korea data do. It is recommended that future research compare results vis-à-vis students’ perceptions of
parental support and their parents’ perceptions of the support they provide.
7. Conclusion
Overall, the findings of this study highlight the role of three motivational constructs in linking parental support to students’ science
achievement, while also underscoring cross-cultural differences between Korea and the United States. The results indicate that cultural
context moderates the effects of parental support on students’ science motivation and achievement. In particular, the positive association between parental support and intrinsic motivation was stronger among Korean students compared to their U.S. peers. Moreover, parental support significantly predicted science achievement in Korea, whereas this relationship was not evident in the United
States. The analysis further revealed that students’ motivation towards science served as a mediating pathway through which parental
support influenced science achievement. Together, these findings suggest culturally distinct mechanisms through which parental
support shapes adolescents’ motivation towards science and their academic success.
