Abstract
Equal treatment of groups and individuals is crucial for fair assessment and demand
unbiased scoring decisions. We examined algorithmic fairness focusing on demographic disparities between groups of different gender and language use based on
automatic scoring ( n = 38,722 text responses). We tested various combinations of
semantic representations and classification methods on responses to reading comprehension items from the 2015 German PISA assessment. Classifications from the
most accurate method, namely a Support Vector Machine trained with RoBERTa
embeddings, exhibited no discernible gender differences, but a minor significant
bias in the automatic scoring of students based on their language background. Specifically, students speaking mainly a foreign language at home received significantly
higher automatic scores than their actual performance warranted, thereby gaining
a relative advantage from the machine scoring system. Lower performing groups
with more incorrect responses tend to receive more correct scores because incorrect
responses are generally less likely to be recognized. Differences are particularly evident at the item level, where we identified several factors that promote algorithmic
unfairness such as scoring accuracy, student performance, linguistic diversity of text
responses, and the psychometrically determined item difficulty.
Keywords Algorithmic fairness · Automatic short answer grading · Scoring bias ·

The automation of human work is becoming increasingly integrated into all areas of
life. In the educational context, automatic systems for natural language processing
(NLP) are often involved in decision-making processes (Chen et al., 2020). Various
NLP-based methods have been developed, including systems for automatic essay
scoring (Ramesh & Sanampudi, 2022), short answer scoring (see Burrows et al.,
2015; Galhardi & Brancher, 2018; Haller et al., 2022), plagiarism detection (Foltýnek
et al., 2019; Naik et al., 2015), or automatic feedback in tutoring systems (Gombert et
al., 2024; Mousavinasab et al., 2021).
Thus, the European Commission has published a white paper containing a framework for the application of artificial intelligence (European Commission, 2020) and
passed the Artificial Intelligence Act 2024 (hereafter the EU AI Act) to implement the
regulations on a legal level. The Act categorizes AI applications into various levels of
risk, each accompanied by its own set of explicit requirements. For example, the use
of automatic systems in high-stakes assessment, where the results have a significant
impact on a student’s school/academic career, is classified as high-risk and requires
‘high quality of the datasets feeding the system to minimize risks and discriminatory
outcomes’ (Regulation [EU]2024/1689 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 June 2024 Laying down Harmonised Rules on Artificial Intelligence and
Amending Regulations, 2024). Moreover, transparency and accuracy of the systems’
decisions are considered critical to promote fairness.
International large-scale assessments can produce vast quantities of data but, at
present, text responses are scored manually by humans. The primary reason for this
is the high scoring accuracy required for international comparisons and the lack of
feasibility of automation in many languages. National policy decisions, particularly
in education, rely heavily on these results (Prenzel et al., 2015). While the scoring of
multiple-choice items is straightforward, the evaluation of open-ended responses still
requires human scorers. In theory, automatic scoring of text responses can be applied
to any assessment involving constructed responses and would be particularly valuable in international large-scale assessments. This could, for example, include the
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA; OECD, 2017), the Progress
in International Literacy Study (PIRLS; Mullis & Martin, 2019), or the International
Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS; Fraillon et al., 2019).
A key of scoring open-ended text responses is the linguistic complexity and the
requisite skills needed to read, interpret, and evaluate the texts with respect to a given
coding guide. Machine learning and natural language processing are continuously
improving automatic scoring results, making widespread use more attractive. Many
different systems and methods for the automatic or semi-automatic scoring of text
responses have already been developed for short answer scoring (Andersen et al.,
2023; Basu et al., 2013; Camus & Filighera, 2020; Horbach & Pinkal, 2018; Zehner
et al., 2016) and essays (e.g., Attali & Burstein, 2006; Burstein et al., 2004; Ludwig
et al., 2021; see Ramesh & Sanampudi, 2022).
Most machine learning classification research focuses on the machine learning
models’ classification accuracy as a metric for evaluating the model’s quality; fairness, interpretability, and computational efficiency are largely neglected (Ruder,
2022). Putting these methods into the educational sector requires considering all
relevant perspectives and a detailed analysis of the decision-making process. For

Theory
Fairness and Bias
In assessment, “a fair test does not advantage or disadvantage some individuals
because of characteristics irrelevant to the intended construct, […] including those
associated with race, ethnicity, gender, age, socioeconomic status, or linguistic or cultural background” (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014, p. 50).
When fair test results are systematically jeopardized by machine decisions, this issue
is referred to as algorithmic unfairness (Hellman, 2020). The term fairness describes
a broader concept (Loukina et al., 2019), while the term bias is more technical. Bias
is generally employed to describe systematic statistical differences and is often used
interchangeably with machine- or algorithmic bias. It is often associated with the
training of a machine learning model (Hellström et al., 2020) which result in different outcomes for individuals based on sensitive attributes such as gender and cultural
background. We use the term algorithmic bias to describe the difference between
machine and human scoring, and compare these across demographic groups as a fairness indicator, referred to as demographic disparity.
Biased Applications
Bias in automatic systems is ubiquitous and can be found in various applications, for
example image recognition systems (Schwemmer et al., 2020), health care (Obermeyer et al., 2019), or NLP applications—for example in word embeddings (Bolukbasi et al., 2016; Caliskan et al., 2017) or automatic transcription (Tatman, 2017).
Given the pervasive impact that decisions can make on individuals’ lives and their
potential to cause harm through errors or misjudgments, it is imperative to examine
algorithmic bias not only from an ethical perspective but also from a legal standpoint
(Ntoutsi et al., 2020).
In terms of potential impact, high requirements are set for the operational use of
automatic systems (Madnani & Cahill, 2018). Machine decisions should be accurate,
transparent, and fair (European Commission, 2020). The quality of the automatic
scoring systems is measured by the agreement between automatically generated
codes and human ratings, while the agreement is compared to inter-rater reliability.
Comparative evaluations of machine systems against human decision-making serve
as a means of quality control by establishing a benchmark for performance in outcomes of interest. Notably, human decisions should be considered with caution as a
gold standard since they can be biased (Zanga & De Gioannis, 2023).

Human Fairness
The obvious handicap of human raters is the limited capacity of certain cognitive
processes such as perception and memory (Bejar, 2012; Suto, 2012), combined with
a varying productivity and quality of ratings during longer sessions and between
rating shift conditions (Ling et al., 2014) or simply due to rating being monotonous
(Klein & El, 2003). In the context of schools, teachers’ grading practices are also
susceptible to unconscious discrimination, e.g. when gender (Hofer, 2015) or anticipated origin (Bonefeld & Dickhauser, 2018) is known or believed, which has been
demonstrated across several countries (Zanga & De Gioannis, 2023). In contrast,
machines do not involve scoring-irrelevant demographic information, if they are not
a part of the training process. They are unaffected by the duration of the operating
scoring time and consistently produce the same decision (provided that no stochastic
term is involved). These decisions hinge on the quality of the input data. The adage
“garbage in, garbage out” aptly illustrates the relationship between input data quality and a model’s accuracy, suggesting that subpar training data will result in poor
model decisions. This principle extends to bias as well: “Bias in, bias out” signifies
that a trained system might disadvantage a specific group if trained on biased data.
However, in the absence of better alternatives, human scoring remains crucial, not
only from an operational standpoint but also for the production of training data. Additionally, the setup, evaluation, and deployment of automatic scoring models require
human labor. Once the scoring model is deployed, the scoring process can benefit
from machine efficiency.
Algorithmic Fairness
Machine-based decisions in this context typically refer to a decision resulting from a
specific classifier, trained by machine learning on labeled input data and a statistical
model. The algorithm is intended to be optimized to produce the most accurate result
for new input data. For instance, a classification model could be trained to recognize patterns of correct/incorrect text responses (input) to determine whether a new
unseen response aligns with those patterns and return a score (output). As previously
highlighted, models and their results are susceptible to various bias sources, with
certain stages of machine learning and the engineering process being susceptible to
different biases (Hellström et al., 2020; Johnson et al., 2022; Mehrabi et al., 2021). To
gain a deeper understanding of potential vulnerabilities in the process, we describe a
machine learning pipeline for an automatic scoring task (Fig. 1), elucidating which
steps are particularly susceptible to introducing biases.

1. Data Collection: Data are systematically collected from a sample, depending
on the research question and the object of study. In educational assessment, the
concern may be to test domain knowledge, problem solving skills, or reading
literacy. To maintain quality, questionnaires and tests must satisfy criteria such
as objectivity, reliability, and validity. Failure to do so compromises the extent
to which constructs are adequately measured, leading to measurement bias (see
Millsap & Everson, 1993). The representativeness of the sample in terms of
demographic characteristics is a key criterion for generalizing results. Failing to
achieve representativeness can challenge machine learning algorithms. Unequal
representations can systematically discriminate against particular groups (representation bias). When group membership is the attribute to be classified, underrepresentation leads to a lower prior probability of predicting that group. Thus,
machine learning models tend to favor the major class in classification problems (Guo et al., 2008). Additionally, a model may learn to discriminate better
between instances in overrepresented groups due to a lack of variance in underrepresented groups.
2. Scoring/Labeling: Scoring data, such as text responses, involves assigning labels
to the collected observations. This is done by hand and involves human decision-making processes, which can be biased (annotation bias). To maximize
objectivity of the scoring process, scoring guidelines are used. These specify the
conditions under which a score is applied. For example, they define what information a text response must and, sometimes, must not contain to be considered
correct. Improperly scored data can compromise not only model accuracy (Northcutt et al., 2021) but also introduce bias, which is ultimately human-induced.
Biased scorings may occur when raters have access to demographic information
or anticipate those about the individual being scored, such as the student’s migration background (Bonefeld & Dickhauser, 2018) or gender (Hofer, 2015).
3. Preprocessing: Typically, data is normalized to remove irrelevant (e.g., linguistic) variance and optimize model learning. The choice of preprocessing steps
is tailored and affects subsequent steps, including the model learning process.
Preprocessing of diverse texts might prove beneficial. For instance, implementing spelling correction could reduce the impact of more frequent spelling errors
made by foreign language speakers, thereby enhancing the scoring process.
4. Feature Engineering: Features are extracted from the preprocessed data. The
model uses these features to distinguish between the scoring of instances; that is,
label A should be associated with different feature values than label B to ensure
accurate classification. Ideally, the features should capture the data’s information
as comprehensively as possible. If the features also distinguish between groups,
because the labels correlate with group-specific characteristics, it is possible that
individual groups could be unfairly associated with certain labels. For example,
if an automatic scoring system is trained with features based on text length, boys
might be disadvantaged because they tend to write shorter texts in reading comprehension tasks (see Zehner et al., 2018).
5. Model Selection: A machine learning model is chosen based on characteristics of
the data. The selected models can be probabilistic, deterministic, or an ensemble of the two, depending on the characteristics of the data. While probabilistic

13

International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education (2025) 35:3128–3165

models, such as Naive Bayes, take the (prior) probabilities of different features
as a basis for decision-making, deterministic models, such as decision trees, can
create hierarchical rules to infer certain labels in the face of certain features.
The models’ performance is contingent on the data, and they can yield biased
decisions, as illustrated by the example of underrepresentation discussed earlier.
Another point concerns the number of model-relevant parameters, which can be
defined manually or through hyperparameter tuning, and may differently influence the model training and test output.
6. Data Split: The overall data set is divided into training, testing, and, sometimes,
validation sets. Typically, 60 to 80% of the data are used to train the model, and
the remaining data are used to evaluate it. Testing the model independently of the
training data reduces the risk that the model does not generalize well to new data,
largely preventing mere memorization of the training data, known as overfitting.
If the data are not stratified with respect to the sensitive class when being split,
imbalances may result in the induction of a sampling bias.
7. Model Training: The model iteratively learns patterns in the features associated
with annotated labels. Models typically require predefined hyperparameters,
which influence the feature separation process, the learning process of the model,
and its outcomes. In the training phase, biases induced by the aforementioned
steps are solidified. For example, an existing representation bias can be amplified
or reduced through random data splitting, if groups are not represented equally in
the training- and test data by chance.
8. Model Evaluation: Finally, the model performance is evaluated using metrics,
like accuracy, F1 score, or Cohen’s κ (Cohen, 1960). When evaluating the model,
the agreement between the predicted scores and the human-labelled scores may
vary according to the demographics of the student who wrote the corresponding
text response. This discrepancy may be due to biases from earlier stages, such as
sampling bias, or from unique characteristics of the student’s group that are not
learned properly during the training process. As a result, the evaluation phase is
when the bias finally appears, if it has not already been detected (and corrected)
earlier through data screening or monitoring processes.
The dependence on data is pervasive throughout the whole model construction process (e.g., the effects of underrepresentation). In summary, the decisions made at
each step—how data are collected, what parameters are defined, and any biases that
may result (even if only unconsciously)—are associated with human decisions. Statistical models can be very complex and it is sometimes difficult, sometimes impossible, to reconstruct and understand the machine’s decision, especially when a large
number of interdependent parameters are involved (e.g., in deep learning). The best
possible transparency in the development of methods and models is therefore essential. Models should, thus, be designed with this in mind. For this reason, data should
be accurately collected and scored according to precise scoring guidelines, features
should relate to construct-relevant information, and potential confounders should be
considered.

few exceptions. Johnson et al. (2022) identified demographic disparities in scoring

More research on algorithmic fairness has been conducted in the eras of automatic

for which fairness has also been evaluated. For example, early versions of the e-rater

cases, texts from people of different nationalities were scored with different levels
of accuracy (Burstein et al., 2004; Burstein & Chodorow, 1999), for example, the
system gave higher scores to people of Chinese nationality (Bridgeman et al., 2009).
Group-Specific Characteristics
Considering socio-demographic information can increase a machine-learning model’s accuracy. It can reveal correlations with student performance and, therefore, the
impact of group-specific deviations in the label distribution. For example, there is
evidence that girls outperform boys on reading-comprehension tasks for their first
language (Hedges & Nowell, 1995; Hyde, 2005; White, 2007) and second language
(Payne & Lynn, 2011). Including gender information to train a model that predicts a
student’s performance might increase accuracy over using randomly generated outputs but would also lead to unfairness against boys, because the model associates
correct responses more strongly with girls than with boys due to a higher prior probability that a girl’s answer is correct.
Machine-based decisions should be made independently of sensitive attributes
such as gender, race, or religion. Therefore, information beyond the target criteria,
such as demographic details, should not be included as features in the training data.
Thus, fairness under unawareness (Chen et al., 2020; Kusner et al., 2017) can be
achieved by training a model that is blind to these sensitive attributes. This is particularly important in assessments with high stakes for individuals. Consequently,
only text should be included in the automatic scoring process to provide an objective
assessment.
However, complete fairness cannot be expected by simply omitting sensitive information in features. This is because demographic variables are related with (linguistic)
characteristics through proximal and distal relationships that may not be immediately
apparent. For instance, handwritten texts can be recognized as written by females or
males (Burr, 2002). Both humans (Burr, 2002) and machines (Argamon et al., 2003;
Siddiqi et al., 2014) are significantly better at recognizing gender in handwriting
than a random baseline, because boys and girls have different handwriting styles to
some extent. While female handwriting is described positively, male handwriting is
described negatively (Burr, 2002) and being less legible (Greifeneder et al., 2012),
while essays with less legible handwriting are graded lower, regardless of gender
(Greifeneder et al., 2012). Handwriting style (Spear, 1989) and writing competence
(Petersen, 2018) are perceived differently between girls and boys. Girls’ handwriting
is perceived as neat, while boys’ writing is perceived as untidy (Spear, 1989); boys’

These visual clues can be ignored in the context of computer-based assessments.
Texts can be evaluated independently of their handwriting, although it remains to be
seen whether there are group-specific linguistic features that may bias the machinelearning model, for example, if non-native speakers include more spelling mistakes in
their responses (see Flor et al., 2015). The automatic scoring process may be affected
if these group-specific characteristics, which are unrelated to the target construct, are
included in the text responses.
Gendered linguistic differences can be seen at the text and sentence level (Jones
& Myhill, 2007). For example, when responding to German PISA reading comprehension tasks, girls tend to write more task-relevant information than boys (Zehner
et al., 2018). At the word level, there is also a difference in the use of determiners,
pronouns, prepositions, and negations between girls and boys (Koppel et al., 2002).
Building on these gender differences, it is also important to consider the impact of
linguistic background, especially when the language of assessment is not the examinee’s first language, which brings additional challenges. The process of writing in a
second language (L2) is cognitively more demanding (Piolat et al., 2008), less effective (Silva, 1993), and less fluent (Palviainen et al., 2012; Silva, 1993), which may
manifest in certain linguistic characteristics of the written texts. For example, texts
written in a foreign language are characterized by different structures of argumentation and narration (Silva, 1993). Due to a lesser lexical knowledge (Malt & Sloman,
2003), L2 texts exhibit lower lexical diversity. Also, Steinig and Betzel (2013) found
that multilingual students in Germany produce more spelling errors. The number of
spelling errors is also related to socio-economic status (SES), while the type of spelling errors further depends on the specific native language (Cook, 1997).
Numerical Representation of Language and Text
Texts are of qualitative character, and analyzing them requires various qualitative
methods which can be considered “an iterative process […] making new significant
distinctions resulting from getting closer to the phenomenon studied” (Aspers &
Corte, 2019, p. 139). In order to process texts automatically, they must be represented
numerically. This can be accomplished through different methods, each with varying
levels of complexity. These methods are described in more detail below, in order of
complexity.
A very basic representation of responses is the number of words or characters that
the response contains, resulting in a distribution of response lengths for all given
labels (e.g., correct/incorrect). Adding another feature, such as the average word
length, results in each response being represented by two features, which can be
mapped into a 2-element vector. This rudimentary representation is of course superficial and does not capture any semantic information, hence the exploration of richer
representations such as n-grams and word or sentence embeddings.

n-grams
When words are considered as a sequence of letters or characters, a text can be
decomposed into its individual parts, which are called tokens (i.e. words and numbers). These can be combined to a sequence of compound token sequences, each of
which contains n of the original tokens. While unigrams ( n = 1) comprise individual tokens, bigrams ( n = 2) comprise a token sequence of two tokens and, thus,
contain more context. For example, the sentence ‘The dog barks’ can be represented
by three unigrams ([The], [dog], [barks]), by two bigrams ([The dog], [dog barks])
or one 3-gram ([The dog barks]). For appropriate choices of n, it becomes feasible
to extract all n-grams from a set of responses and designate them as features in the
feature space. Thus, n-grams can be represented as one-hot vectors, in which a binary
value x ∈ {0,1} is assigned to the feature, corresponding to whether an n-gram
occurs in a text or not, and all of the values combined form the feature vector.
n-grams do not inherently carry semantic information. If synonyms are used for the
same semantic concept, they will be assigned to different features, failing to recognize the semantic similarity between those words. However, considering the context
in which an n-gram occurs can contribute to uncovering latent semantic information
(Deerwester et al., 1990).
Embeddings
A word’s context is particularly considered in word embeddings, carrying semantic
information to represent words in a multidimensional vector. Measuring the cosine
distance between two word vectors can reveal their semantic similarity in numerical
form (Mikolov et al., 2013; Pennington et al., 2014). Word embeddings are trained
on a large corpus of text (e.g., all English Wikipedia articles). Under the assumption
that similar words occur in similar contexts, a word’s relation to its context is captured, using word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013), that examines a pre-defined window
of words around a target word, using a neural net which represents words as vectors.
Through iteration, a model can be trained on a large corpus to optimize the vector
representation of a word in relation to other words. Similar words, such as synonyms,
are therefore represented by similar vectors in the vector space, measurable by e.g.
cosine similarity. The resulting language model contains all words from the corpus
in the form of vectors, with the term embeddings used to denote those vectors that
incorporate contextual information.
Transformers are models that can more accurately represent context through selfattention mechanisms (Vaswani et al., 2017). They account for words with identical
spelling but different meanings depending on the context that they appear in. This
leads to a more precise vector representation of words and sentences (Devlin et al.,
2019; Liu et al., 2019).
Transformers do not operate strictly on a word-by-word basis. Their tokens, socalled subwords, are more granular, allowing the assignment of vectors to words
with spelling errors. If a word, which is typically represented as a single token, is
misspelled, it is tokenized into smaller units, considering syllables or individual characters rather than the entire word as a single token. Misspelled words can also be

considered through n-grams. This is not typically possible using word embeddings,
as those would require the word to be pre-trained in its misspelled form. Since corpora used for pre-training, such as Wikipedia texts, contain fewer misspelled words,
this issue is less common.
Group-specific Performance Indicator and Base Rates
To understand the performance of automatic scoring and examine its fairness, groupspecific performance differences that lead to varying score distributions are pivotal.
Thus, a group’s mean performance defines the prior probability of label assignment,
which is relevant for the model output and, hence, for model evaluation.
There is empirical evidence that some groups perform better than others in particular assessment domains. For example, it is consistently reported that girls outperform boys in reading comprehension tasks (Hedges & Nowell, 1995; Hyde, 2005;
White, 2007), which can be attributed to various reasons. For one, motivational factors might play an important role, in particular a relationship between enjoyment of
reading and performance in reading tasks (OECD, 2011). In the 2009 PISA study,
girls were found to enjoy reading more than boys in all OECD countries, although
both the level of enjoyment and the gender gap vary widely across OECD countries
(Artelt et al., 2010). Boys have been shown to perform better on text comprehension
tasks when they have a personal interest in the task (Oakhill & Petrides, 2007). Girls
demonstrate better verbal skills than boys (Petersen, 2018; Weiss et al., 2003) and
show higher literacy and writing performance (Petersen, 2018).
Gender differences in language skills are also evident in foreign language writing.
Girls tend to have superior second language skills compared to boys (Payne & Lynn,
2011), demonstrating better scores on second language reading comprehension tasks
and are more fluent in L2 writing (Arabic with L2 English), which is also mediated
by greater language proficiency compared to boys (Al-Saadi, 2020).
L2 language skills are particularly relevant in the context of immigration and
testing in a foreign language, where untrained language skills can be a source of
comprehension and writing difficulties. As a result, immigrant students score lower
on literacy tests than native students (Entorf & Minoiu, 2005; Lopez-Agudo et al.,
2021). Furthermore, socioeconomic status (SES) is a relevant predictor of reading
performance for students with migratory background (Ibáñez-Alfonso et al., 2021).
From an international perspective, it is also important to highlight that scores for
immigrant groups differ depending on the country to which they immigrate. Entorf
and Minoiu (2005) considered this fact in their study and found that the influence of
SES on reading literacy performance in PISA varies across countries and their associated immigration policies, with the largest effect found in Germany.
Groups of Interest
Considering the various linguistic differences, our study aims to investigate fairness
in automatic scoring of text responses between (a) boys and girls as well as (b) native
and non-native language speakers. In PISA, students’ native language is not assessed
directly. As a result, we rely on information about the main language spoken at home

as a proxy for the individual’s language use and acquired language skills. Although
this information does not provide details on language proficiency in the national language or in the language of the test, it is the best available indicator of these aspects.
A study by Entorf and Minoiu (2005) found that the language spoken at home plays
an important role in the reading achievement of immigrant students. In particular,
those who practice the national language at home may even outperform native students (Entorf & Minoiu, 2005).
Research Questions
Students’ background characteristics, such as gender or their native language, can be
associated with their text production outputs, as well as their performance in assessments. Since different conditions in training the model can bias the outputs from
automatic scoring models, there is a risk of unfair scoring due to group-specific linguistic characteristics which may confound the written texts and, thus, the automatic
scoring. We aim to test whether there is a group-specific bias in the automatic scoring
of responses between boys and girls as well as between students who speak German
or a foreign language at home by comparing differences between machine and human
scoring on a group level. We compared different automatic scoring methods by investigating fairness using group-specific fairness indicators. We adopted an exploratory
inductive approach by initially examining the data for potential bias or unfair outcomes. If conditions were identified as unfair, we scrutinized them in greater detail
to determine which conditions may favor bias. In summary, our research examines
two main areas: (1) whether there is variability in fairness across different methods
for relevant groups, and (2) pinpointing the factors that might explain any detected
variations in fairness.

Method
Data
We analyzed constructed text responses from the German reading literacy assessment
of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) conducted in the year
2015. The PISA study is administered triennially in schools to measure competencies in mathematical, scientific, and reading literacy. About a third of PISA items are
administered in constructed-response format (OECD, 2024, p.489). A prototypical
item may, for example, require students to read a short news article and to describe
the purpose of the text or to repeat a specific piece of information from the text.
Publicly released items can be found in OECD (2009). Alongside literacy estimates,
the study gathers various demographic information including gender, socioeconomic
status, and language, enabling comparisons between different groups, such as those
defined by gender or language usage. The assessment is administered to 15-yearold students at school, and each student is given a set of items to answer. From a
total of 43 reading literacy items, we selected 37 items with dichotomous scoring
(correct/incorrect) for suitability of the employed analysis method. More detailed

13

International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education (2025) 35:3128–3165

3139

information about the scoring process can be found in OECD (2017b). Focusing on
dichotomously scored items allows for a more accurate examination of potential false
positives and negatives at the item level, as the (low number of) scores of the minor
groups are less scattered across the confusion matrix. Considering the included 37
items and due to a booklet design with randomized assignments, a student can only
have responded to between one and 15 of these items. Other specific details, including tasks and text-responses, cannot be disclosed for reasons of confidentiality. Blank
responses are not included in the analyses. According to the OECD (2017b, p. 258),
the interrater agreement (proportion agreement) for reading literacy in Germany is

Measuring Automatic Scoring Performance
The performance of the scoring models was evaluated using various metrics. Automatic and human scoring are compared in a confusion matrix (Table 3), from which
individual metrics can be read and calculated. Fairness indicators can also be derived
directly from these individual metrics, which are defined below.
In our use case, with dichotomous scoring (correct/incorrect), all correct responses
can be considered as the positive class and all incorrect responses as the negative
class.
The accuracy of the model describes the relative proportion of correctly automatically scored responses, measured against the number of all scored responses, thus
ACC = (TP + TN)/(TP + TN + FP + FN), in a range of 0 ≤ ACC ≤ 1 where
accuracy ACC = 1 signifies a perfect correspondence with the human scoring.
Interpreting accuracy can become a challenge with an uneven distribution of the
target variable. Therefore, we also report the interrater agreement beyond chance
between human and machine scoring, κ , as defined by Cohen (1960). κ ranges
from − 1 to 1, with κ = 1 meaning perfect agreement, κ = 0 indicating random
chance agreement with respect to the class distribution, and κ < 0 signaling prob-

(see Landis & Koch, 1977).

Measuring Fairness
To ensure fairness, various metrics have been developed, depending on the context in
which they are applied (see Hellman, 2020; Loukina et al., 2019). Fair machine learning methods should produce group-independent outputs and generate false negatives
or false positives equally across groups (Hellman, 2020). This concept reflects group
fairness in an ML classification scenario and is also referred to as statistical (dis)parity. We used student performance metrics to calculate a fairness metric, which serves
as an indicator of whether groups are favored or disadvantaged in automatic scoring.
The process can be thought of as creating and comparing group-specific confusion
matrices. In order to achieve this, two types of metrics were calculated:
1. automatic scoring bias; that is, the difference or residual between automatic and
manual scoring.
2. a difference measure comparing the biases between demographic groups.
Automatic Scoring Bias
A common measure of fairness is demographic disparity (also known as statistic
disparity), which is based on the expectation that the relative proportion of positive outcomes should be equal between groups (Chen et al., 2019). A fair comparison assumes equal prior probabilities, which is not applicable in our case given the
observed performance differences between the groups. Therefore, we first calculate
group-specific scoring biases by accounting for each group’s performance, and then
we compare these biases across groups to determine demographic disparity.
The automatic scoring bias is operationalized by the mean difference D between
the proportion of correct responses as per automatic scoring Y and the proportion

the difference between them, so the disparity can only be interpreted by considering
the direction of the scoring biases of the groups being compared.
Procedure
Initially, various feature representations (n-grams, word and sentence embeddings)
and different machine learning methods for classification were compared across all
items for their scoring accuracy and fairness. The most accurate model was then
employed for further item-level analyses.
A deterministic method included in the study are Support Vector Machines (SVMs).
This approach involves identifying optimal hyperplanes that separate instances based
on their class while maximizing the margin of the planes (Cortes & Vapnik, 1995).
Using Radial Basis Function kernel and its universal approximation (Park & Sand-

13

International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education (2025) 35:3128–3165

3143

berg, 1991), SVM can separate instances that may not be linearly separable in the
original feature space.
A second machine learning method included here uses hierarchical rules to train
decision trees that can classify new data deterministically. Random forests extend
this concept by bootstrapping, which entails constructing multiple decision trees for
various subsamples of the training data. The final classification is probabilistically
based on the individual decisions of all the trees.
Given their common use to represent lexical features, n-grams were included in
the present study. There is no theoretical upper limit for n. To keep the number of
models manageable, we have only considered unigrams. We filtered the 300 mostfrequent unigrams for each item from the training dataset, and their occurrence was
one-hot encoded, representing each text response as a 300-dimensional vector. We
have limited the number of dimensions here to 300 in order to provide a similar
amount of information as the word embeddings. In contrast to this lexical approach,
a second set of features addressed response semantics. For this, tokens were mapped
into a 300-dimensional word vector using the pre-trained German word embedding
Wikipedia2vec (Yamada et al., 2020) with all matching tokens assigned corresponding vectors and aggregated into a response vector. Finally, as the third set of features, sentence embeddings were obtained from the base RoBERTa model (Liu et al.,
2019) by passing tokenized input through the pre-trained model (without finetuning),
extracting the last layer’s hidden states for each token, and performing mean pooling,
encoding the responses into in 768-dimensional response vectors.
In summary, we tested four different combinations of different language/response
representations and machine learning methods: (1) unigrams with a decision tree,
(2) unigrams with random forests, (3) word embeddings with SVM (RBF), and (4)
sentence embeddings with SVM (RBF).
The analyses were conducted in R (v. 4.3.1, R Core Team, 2020). Feature generation and embedding extraction were also performed in R using the reticulate package
(v. 1.42.0) in a Python environment (v. 3.11.12), utilizing the transformers (v. 4.34.1)
and torch (v. 2.6.0) libraries. The scoring models were trained using the e1071 package (v.1.7.16; Meyer et al., 2024).
Models were trained and tested via cross-fold validation for each item, with data
randomly split into five subsets, stratified by their representative score distribution.
Each of the models were then trained on data from four of these subsets and tested on
the remaining one; this process was conducted five times, with each iteration involving a different subset as the test data set. In this way, each response from the dataset
was automatically scored independently of the training data. All scores were then
used to calculate fairness metrics. This approach was applied to all machine learning methods and feature sets described in the previous section. Finally, results were
compared between the groups of interest; that is, boys vs. girls as well as native vs.
non-native speakers.
Non-parametric tests were used to assess statistical significance of group differences. Bootstrapping was performed to generate 10,000 random and representative
group affiliations, to create a random distribution of difference coefficients. This
means that the number of group assignments of a given sensitive attribute (gender
or language use) is equal and representative in each bootstrap. The resulting random

13

3144

International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education (2025) 35:3128–3165

distributions include whether and how often machine scores were correctly assigned
between groups. These distributions were compared to the coefficient derived from
the group of interest. If a measure fell outside the 2.5% percentile on either side, the
difference was considered significant (i.e., α = .05).

Discussion
We have shown that an automatic scoring system can be very accurate, but biases
can emerge due to inaccurate scoring systems, mainly at the item level, leading to
advantages for certain groups (RQ1). One of the main factors contributing to scoring
bias is the automated scoring methodology and its ability to detect correct and incorrect responses.
Inaccurate scoring systems tend to yield a higher number of false-positive classifications. Combined with the different average ability of certain student groups to
correctly answer items, this can lead to unfair scoring. That is because groups that

13

International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education (2025) 35:3128–3165

3151

generally provide more incorrect responses are disproportionally affected, receiving
a greater absolute number of false positive classifications. As a result, the lowerability group is overestimated more than the higher-ability group and thus receives
a minor advantage. If it were more difficult to detect correct answers, we anticipate
that the opposite effect on disparity would occur if there is an existing performance
gap between the groups.
No gender bias was found at the aggregate level across all items. However, a small
bias toward students speaking a foreign language at home was found, due to a higher
false-positive rate of the scoring system which overestimates their performance.
At the item level, this difference is particularly pronounced for certain items, with
minor biases revealed in language related groups. Due to substantial disparities and
systematic struggling to identify incorrect responses, there is a systematic overestimation of performance for weaker groups and students. Both groups are overestimated by the system, with students speaking a foreign language at home experiencing
greater overestimation than their counterparts speaking German at home. When
examining scoring biases between genders and language uses, the findings present a
less systematic pattern. Based on these results, we identified various factors contributing to scoring biases and (un-)fairness in automatic response scoring.
Fairness Factors (RQ2)
Scoring Accuracy
In general, models with higher scoring accuracy potentially contribute to fairer
scoring practices, as the increased accuracy minimizes the discrepancies between
manual and automatic scoring, leaving less room for scoring differences among various groups. This could, for example, be demonstrated within the models used in the
study. However, if responses contain group-specific linguistic features, bias could be
inadvertently introduced into machine decisions, particularly if the model overfits to
these linguistic characteristics. Therefore, while increased scoring accuracy generally tends toward a higher fairness, it can also heighten the risk of capturing and
perpetuating existing linguistic biases in the data (see Hofmann et al., 2024).
Linguistic Variance
Linguistic variance has a direct impact on scoring accuracy (Shin et al., 2024) and
may thereby contribute to scoring bias. Challenges arise when attempting to optimally separate correct from incorrect responses based on the distribution of information within a set of responses to a particular item.
The challenge to detect incorrect responses is more due to general linguistic properties between correct and incorrect responses. Incorrect responses tend to be more
heterogeneous and exhibit greater linguistic variance (see Shin et al., 2024) than

13

3152

International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education (2025) 35:3128–3165

correct responses, making them more difficult for the system to learn. In particular,
incorrect responses often contain partially correct semantic content, as students may
have identified some key elements but not enough to produce a fully correct response.
In addition, the proportion of correct information in incorrect responses varies. As a
result, incorrect responses are more heterogeneous than correct ones.
This is particularly the case for more difficult items, where the number of incorrect responses is higher than the number of correct responses. When partial credit or
multiple scoring schemes are applied, the requirement for class separation escalates,
potentially leading to a reduction in scoring accuracy and thus introducing a systematic scoring bias.
Thus, response length can be a significant variable; shorter responses show less
linguistic variance, a circumstance mainly attributable to students who are not
native speakers of the test language. Due to potential limitations in writing L2, their
responses may be simpler but also more variable in terms of grammar and spelling
(mistakes). The precise impact of these elements would need to be assessed under
more controlled conditions with balanced class distributions as imbalanced classes or
different prior probabilities could be another source of bias.
Unequal Distribution of Student Performance
We identified an imbalanced distribution of group performances as a critical factor
contributing to unfair automatically generated scores. This issue manifests particularly in the misclassification of incorrect responses due to an elevated false positive
rate. Consequently, in such cases, responses are predominantly scored as correct,
disproportionally benefiting students who demonstrate weaker performance and produce more incorrect responses. The automatic scoring process thus leads to a minor
systematic overestimation of performance and preferential treatment of lower-performing students and student groups.
Unequal Distribution of Item Design and Test Difficulty
Test items must exhibit varying levels of difficulty to accurately measure the
abilities of heterogeneous test takers. This directly impacts the distribution of
scores for training the scoring model: obviously, easier items receive more correct responses than incorrect ones. Optimal item difficulty distribution is essential for psychometric estimation but is not necessarily ideal for training a machine
learning model, as an imbalanced prior probability could affect classification. In
the context of binary classification (correct/incorrect), an optimal distribution
for machine learning models would be a 50% balance, corresponding to an item
correctly answered by half of the test-takers. Moreover, the item design may
contribute to potential false positives due to a ceiling effect, resulting in a lack of
linguistic variance within the smaller number of incorrect responses. This allows

13

International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education (2025) 35:3128–3165

3153

for better discrimination among correct responses, while there may be insufficient training instances for incorrect responses. The effect could be similarly
influenced by floor effects. Upon examining the bias between true and estimated
performance, it can be observed that this bias is related to item difficulty, albeit
only minimally affecting the disparity between groups. Therefore, the distribution of item difficulty presents a complex interplay of factors that must be carefully managed to ensure fair and accurate automated scoring.
Interaction of Factors
As evident from the descriptions, these characteristics (scoring accuracy, linguistic variance, different student performances, and test difficulties) are not standalone
but interact in a complex manner due to the intricate nature of the text data and the
machine learning models employed for automatic scoring. The interaction of various
factors, such as item and student characteristics, on linguistic variance was demonstrated, for example, by (Shin et al., 2024), who showed that items with higher
linguistic variance in the responses are associated with lower automatic scoring performance. In this study, item difficulty also played a crucial role in explaining linguistic variance (Shin et al., 2024).
Item difficulty dictates the content and complexity required for responses to be
deemed correct, influencing the linguistic homogeneity of all responses. Concurrently, difficulty modulates the distribution of scores, which is further contingent
on the performance of the students tackling these tasks. These factors shape the
accuracy with which patterns for correct and incorrect responses are represented
and recognized for automatic scoring. Some items often incorporate partial credits, thereby introducing multiple scoring schemes among which the model must
discriminate.
Another issue that arises is the small size of minority groups. If 1,000 responses
are collected from a simple item with a 90% correct response rate, and 10% of
the responses belong to a specific group with a representative proportion of correct answers, only 10 incorrect responses would come from the minority group
in the dataset. In this case, it would require a false positive rate of 10% (which
is considerable) to score a response from the group as a false positive. With
such a small number of responses, it becomes challenging to identify linguistic
patterns that could provide insights into the method, which, as demonstrated,
is also dependent on the item. These factors may interact due to differences in
group performance and item difficulty, which makes analyzing them even more
challenging.
Approaches to Reduce Bias
This section proposes a number of approaches for identifying sources of bias and
handling it accordingly. Essentially, the identified influencing factors form a multi-

13

3154

International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education (2025) 35:3128–3165

faceted landscape that requires a nuanced approach to model training and evaluation.
To defuse these interwoven sources of bias and promote fairer automatic scoring, it
may be advantageous to conduct sensitivity analyses to better understand the impact
of individual factors. For this, larger datasets may be needed to cover incorrectly
scored responses in minority groups by ensuring a minimum number of responses, or
alternatively, by generating synthetic text responses with specific linguistic features,
using generative language models (see Dai et al., 2023). Considering sensitive attributes during model learning may also increase fairness, especially in scenarios such
as Haeri and Zweig (2020) show for racial bias. Although this approach is contrary
to the fairness-through-ignorance approach presented earlier, the intentional use of
information about sensitive attributes is highly dependent on the data and method
used. Additionally, manipulation at the linguistic level can be tested through preprocessing (Ntoutsi et al., 2020) to normalize texts, for example, by adding automatic
spelling correction to the classifier pipeline.
Limitations
Even a reduction of the measured bias towards zero does not promise to prevent
unfair scoring. Perfect scoring agreement between the scores of humans and machines
would merely decimate the visible bias. There is a further possibility that human rater
biases will be learnt by the system, which is not measurable if they are trained and
evaluated on the scores of the same rater.
For the investigation of fairness, and specifically the extent of scoring differences
considering false positives and false negatives, only PISA items with dichotomous
scoring were examined. The impact of items with polytomous scoring remains
unclear. However, it is expected that the differences in estimated and actual performance will replicate, depending on the item and how well false responses can be
discriminated from partial credit responses and partial credit responses from correct responses. The datasets used, each containing over 900 responses per item, are
relatively large; however, examining the responses from minority groups such as
language speakers in conjunction with items of marginal difficulty poses challenges
due to a small resulting subset of responses. This is because there are comparatively
few responses to examine, and thus generalization is not feasible.
Language use could be measured only by the main language spoken at home,
which does not directly indicate the student’s native language. However, it can be
assumed that the level of language proficiency influences the text production (Cumming, 1989) and thus the automatic scoring. For international use, the impact on the
different test and national languages to be learnt should also be investigated, although
there may also be an effect between contrasting L1s and L2s (Lowie & Verspoor,
2008). As the results are rather general, it is also to be expected that the highest dis-

13

International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education (2025) 35:3128–3165

3155

parities will be found in countries with larger performance differences between native
and non-native speakers.

Conclusion
We demonstrated that the tested scoring models in this study are more accurate in
identifying correct responses than incorrect ones. This leads to a higher true positive
rate and a higher false positive rate. Individuals who answer more responses incorrectly over the course of the assessment are consequently assigned more correct scorings than their performance warrants.
It is debatable whether favoring one group automatically disadvantages another.
This question can assuredly be answered in the affirmative if direct comparisons
between groups are the focal point. In PISA studies, the major focus is on country
comparisons. Observing the effect that this has on demographic disparities, it can be
inferred that this effect might also apply at the country level. This suggests that countries exhibiting lower performance might benefit from such disparities. Additional
linguistic characteristics among languages also may play a significant role and must
continue to be thoroughly examined.
In the realm of individual evaluation, such bias would also impair the interpretability of test results, as from a psychometric standpoint, the validity of the instrument becomes compromised. By systematically favoring weaker performers, the
performance distribution among test-takers will skew, minimizing the performance
gap to stronger test-takers. This undermines the validity and interpretability of the
test results. Such issues should be scrutinized when integrating automated scoring
systems into high-stakes assessments and adaptive testing.
In addition, methods and their decision processes can vary in terms of their explainability (Burkart & Huber, 2021) and thus their acceptability (Schlippe et al., 2023).
Decisions derived from models with higher explainability are more understandable,
which is, among others, pivotal for accountability. Moreover, explainability is relevant for the identification of induced biases.
Revisiting the objectives, important insights have been gained concerning the
impact of automatic scoring, which pinpoint the significance of evaluating algorithmic fairness in assessments. The paper also highlights the differences between manual and automatic scoring and their respective influences. Concurrently, the findings
open up new avenues for further research on fairness to capture influences in a more
controlled manner and to test possible approaches for minimizing disparities.
