Examining student ICT use and learning outcomes: Evidence from Japanese
PISA data

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
ICT use
ICT competence
PISA 2018
ICT and education

The relationship between student ICT use and learning outcomes is being debated. Few studies on the topic
examined different forms of ICT use and heterogenous relationships with learning outcomes. This study addresses this gap by examining the association between different forms of ICT use and learning outcomes and by
exploring variations in associations based on student-perceived ICT competence and across the learning distribution. The Japanese sample of PISA 2018 data was analyzed employing a regression approach accounting for
the complexity of the PISA design and unconditional quantile regression. Results suggest that the variables
student ICT use outside school for schoolwork activities and at school in general are not statistically related to
reading and science achievements, but the former is weakly related to mathematics. The two forms of ICT use
improve learning among students with lower ICT competence and also show statistical significance for low- and
high-achieving students. Moreover, subject-related uses of ICT during lessons and outside lessons are positively
associated with the three measures of learning achievements investigated. The association remains consistent by
student ICT competence and across the learning distribution. In contrast, ICT use outside of school for leisure is
negatively associated with all three measures of learning achievements. The association remains negative by
student ICT competence and across the learning distribution. These findings suggest that education stakeholders
promoting student ICT use should be cautious by considering forms of ICT use, student ICT competence, and
heterogenous abilities.

1. Introduction
Information and communication technology (ICT) has been rapidly
growing worldwide, with more and more people and organizations
using it [1]. ICT has transformed how information is created, utilized,
and exchanged. The development and use of ICT in the education sector
also follow the general trend [2]. ICT has transformed the teaching and
learning process through, for example, ICT-based pedagogies and students’ increasing use of ICT in schools or at home. The break-out of the
Coronavirus pandemic has resulted in a learning loss, along with most of
the teaching and learning switched online, raising expectations in ICT to
support students’ learning in many countries.
Despite high expectations that ICT will support learning, empirical
evidence on its benefits is mixed [3,4,5]. While some studies argue that
student ICT use is associated with higher learning outcomes, others
show that the association is null or can even be negative [6,7]. Theoretically, the displacement hypothesis conceptualizes such a negative
association between student ICT use and learning outcomes. The hypothesis expects a negative association between ICT use and learning

outcomes because students may spend their time using ICT for leisure
activities instead of using the tool for learning [8].
In the debate, increasing literature suggests that the association between the use of ICT and students’ learning outcomes depends on some
other factors, including grade level, student background (e.g., gender,
attitudes towards ICT), and student academic performance [9,10]. These
studies note that there are interactions in the association between the
use of ICT and learning achievements, and these need to be accounted
for to understand how ICT influences learning outcomes [11]. From this
perspective, what is almost not known in the existing literature is how
different forms of ICT use are associated with learning outcomes and
how associations may differ depending on student ICT competence. This
knowledge is essential as it coincides with increased students’ use of ICT
in schools or at home (with the recently increasing use of ICT at home
relative to schools). Students may not have the necessary skills to
appropriately use ICT "by themselves" to support their learning. If so,
there is a need to identify those who may need to be provided with
essential ICT support. In Japan, this knowledge seems relevant because
the government recently adopted an ICT in education policy called

In light of this background, the objectives of this study are to
examine the association between different forms of ICT use and student
learning outcomes and to explore potential variations in these associations based on student ICT competence or across the learning distribution. Specifically, the paper answers the following research questions:
(1) To what extent are different forms of student ICT use associated with
students’ learning achievements? (2) How do associations vary
depending on students’ perceived ICT competence or across the learning
distribution? Based on the literature reviewed, we hypothesize that
student ICT use is positively associated with learning outcomes
regardless of its form (Hypothesis 1) and that associations vary
depending on the student’s perceived ICT competence or across the
learning distribution (Hypothesis 2). To answer the research questions,
we use Japanese students’ information from the organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2018 dataset.
Our study contributes to the literature in the following ways: first, it
shows how different forms of use of ICT within the school and home
settings influence three measures of learning achievements. It innovates
for ICT within school settings by taking advantage of two forms of student ICT use newly introduced from PISA 2018, i.e., "subject-related ICT
use during lessons" and "subject-related use of digital devices outside of
classroom lessons." Second, the study highlights heterogeneity in the
association between different forms of ICT use and learning outcomes by
students’ perceived ICT competence and across the learning distribution. This reveals changes in patterns in the association between ICT use
and student learning achievements. And third, it uses an analytical
approach with the package "intsvy," one of the packages suggested by
OECD when analyzing PISA data. This approach provides more robust
estimates by considering the complexity of the PISA design and by using
replicate weights and the rotated test forms with all plausible values of
dependent variables.

From the perspective of the negative effect, some studies suggest that
using ICT is not necessarily beneficial for learning. For example, Belo
et al. [18] have demonstrated that students’ use of ICT is negatively
associated with learning achievements. In fact, instead of using ICT for
activities that would benefit learning, students may use it to watch
entertaining videos or engage in activities on social media. From a
theoretical perspective, the displacement hypothesis expects such a
negative association because the time that should be spent on activities
that improve academic skills (e.g., reading for enjoyment) is rather spent
on leisure activities that do not improve them [8]. As children get
absorbed in leisure activities, their engagement in self-study is displaced. Moreover, Hu et al. [19] investigated 44 countries and showed
that students’ use of ICT for academic purposes negatively affects their
learning outcomes. Similar findings were also reported by Skryabin et al.
[20], who have shown that students’ use of ICT at school negatively
affects learning outcomes. Furthermore, Gorjón & Osés [21] investigated 22 OECD countries using PISA data and found an inverted
U-shaped association between students’ ICT use at school and mathematics achievements. This association suggests that the learning benefits
of ICT use at school are maximized to an optimum level. Subsequently,
learning decreases, sometimes severely. Karlsson [22] showed similar
findings for ICT use in school by using Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) data covering 32 countries. Specifically, the study found that students who use ICT in school monthly and
sometimes weekly show higher achievements, but those who use ICT in
school daily show lower achievements.
A third group of studies suggests that ICT use has no relationship
with learning outcomes. For example, Fairlie and Robinson [23] conducted a randomized control trial in selected Californian schools. They
provided evidence to rule out a positive or negative effect of ICT use on
test scores. Mixed results from previous studies may suggest that the
association between the use of ICT and students’ learning outcomes is
complex. ICT can be used by students with different characteristics, for
different purposes, and in different locations. As such, using ICT may not
affect students’ performance in itself. Rather, the relevance of the use
may explain whether ICT is beneficial or not [24,25]. A body of literature has examined this argument from the perspective of where children
use ICT (at home or school) and how that affects their academic performance. For instance, Agasisti et al. [6] investigated ICT use at home
for homework-related activities and found that this use is negatively
associated with students’ learning outcomes. They then suggested more
caution regarding using ICT as a tool for learning support outside the
school context. These findings seem to imply that the use of ICT in
schools would be beneficial for learning. However, many studies suggest
that its use at school has a null or even negative impact on students’
learning outcomes [26].
The association between ICT use and learning outcomes may be
moderated by other factors, highlighting that these factors must be
accounted for when investigating the relationship between ICT use and
educational outcomes [9,10,27]. In other words, a potential heterogeneity in the association between ICT use and learning outcomes must be
accounted for when investigating this association [4]. Empirical studies
seem to confirm that student ICT use has a differential effect on learning
achievements across ability groups, i.e., low-, average-, and
high-performing students [6,28]. Falck et al. [7] demonstrated that
students of different abilities may use ICT differently or for different
purposes. For example, for low-performing students, using ICT might
make it easier to adjust the learning difficulty or speed to their level.
They can also use ICT to repeat the learning materials at their convenience. On the other hand, we might expect high-performing students to
benefit more from ICT use because they are better equipped in terms of
autonomy to use the tool for learning. Different uses of ICT may imply
different effects; therefore, this study accounts for the potential heterogeneity by analyzing how the association between ICT use learning
outcomes may vary across student levels of perceived ICT competence

2. Literature review
Factors determining learning outcomes are usually investigated
within the framework of the educational production function. This
framework conceptualizes the relationship between input factors (e.g.,
student background, school, and community factors) and education
outputs (e.g., test scores). Student ICT use can be considered as an input
factor affecting learning outcomes [12]. Research suggests that the effect of ICT use on learning outcomes can be either positive or negative.
From the perspective of the positive effect, Lei et al. [10] have shown
that students can use ICT to enhance their learning process. Through
ICT, students can access more information on a wide range of educational topics than they would have using the traditional learning
approach. This can be a source of knowledge that will help them evaluate their ideas appropriately and identify or correct errors which might
hinder learning [13]. In addition, some studies suggest that students’ use
of ICT, mainly for fun, can have cognitive benefits for learning [14]. For
example, this literature supports the idea that video games enhance a
broad range of cognition, such as attention capabilities, pattern recognition, problem-solving skills, and strategic thinking [15]. In the gaming
industry, video games that serve learning purposes are often called
"serious games" [16]. Empirical studies have shown that students using

The ICT familiarity questionnaire devoted three questions to capture
how often students use ICT devices outside of school for leisure activities, outside of school for schoolwork activities, and for activities in
school. ICT use from this perspective refers to the frequency of ICT use in
different settings and for specific purposes. The items in each question
have five response categories ("Never or hardly ever," "Once or twice a
month," "Once or twice a week," "Almost every day," and "Every day").
The resulting index variables from the use of IRT with items of each
question are ENTUSE (use of ICT outside of school for leisure activities),
HOMESCH (use of ICT outside of school for schoolwork activities), and
USESCH (use of ICT at school in general). The wording and number of
items for each index variable are provided in the appendix in tables A7,
A8, and A9.
PISA 2018 included two new questions designed to measure students’ subject-related ICT use. The two questions feature items
measuring the time of students’ subject-related use of ICT during and
outside classroom lessons. Consequently, these variables capture the
time students use ICT devices for subject-related activities during or
outside lessons. Response categories from each related question are "No
time," "1–30 min a week", "31–60 min a week", and "More than 60 min a
week". The resulting variables from relevant items are ICTCLASS (subject-related use of ICT during lessons) and ICTOUTSIDE (subject-related
use of ICT outside of classroom lessons). The wording and number of
items used for each scale are provided in the appendix in tables A10 and
A11.
Another essential scale variable used in this study is student
perceived ICT competence. This index variable was used to investigate
potential variations in the association between ICT use and learning
outcomes depending on students’ ICT skills. Items used in the question
capturing this competence were of four-level Likert response categories
("Strongly disagree," "Disagree," "Agree," and "Strongly agree"). It might
be essential to note that the construct of competence in ICT use in the
context of PISA assessment is measured using students’ reports and not
actual testing. As a result, the expression "perceived competence" seems
to be the appropriate interpretation when approaching the variable. The
items used to create the ICT competence scale are given in the appendix
in Table A12.
In addition to the abovementioned variables, some variables were
used as control predictors to get more robust estimates. We controlled
for age, gender, family socioeconomic status (SES), attitudes toward
learning, and time devoted to learning. Family SES is a construct that
accounts for three dimensions which are home possessions, parental
education, and highest parental occupation. These dimensions are
computed indicators, and the family SES variable was derived from the
arithmetic means of the three indicators.
We also controlled for factors likely to influence students’ learning
outcomes at the school level. We controlled for class size, the type of
school (public versus private), the availability of ICT in the school, the
shortage of educational materials, and the classroom disciplinary
climate. All variables used in the study are presented in Table 1.

3. Data and methods
3.1. Data and variables
We used Japanese data from OECD PISA 2018 for this research. PISA
measures factors that affect the learning outcomes (reading, mathematics, and science) of 15-year-old students from OECD and non-OECD
countries. These factors include the characteristics of students and their
families, schools, principals, and teachers. In Japan, the number of
students surveyed is around 6100. In PISA 2018, a computer familiarity
questionnaire was offered as one of the five optional questionnaires to be
implemented by participating countries. This questionnaire captures
students’ ICT use and ICT competence.
PISA assesses reading, mathematics, and science; this study used
these three domains as dependent variables. According to the program,
the reading literacy variable measures students’ capacity to interact
with text for various purposes relevant to their daily lives and social
participation. The mathematical literacy variable measures students’
abilities to reason mathematically and use mathematical concepts and
procedures to explain or predict phenomena. Finally, the scientific literacy variable measures students’ ability to engage in reading discourse
about science and technology; this includes explaining phenomena
scientifically, designing and evaluating inquiry, and scientifically
interpreting data and evidence.
ICT use scales provided in PISA 2018 data were used as predictor
variables. The scales were constructed using item response theory (IRT)
with items designed to capture student ICT uses. The scales are designed
to capture latent constructs which are not easily measured using single
variables. PISA used Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient to check the internal
consistency of each scale within countries. When the minimum

This study revealed that ICT use influences learning outcomes positively or negatively, depending on its form. This influence is heterogeneous across forms of ICT use and depends on whether students are low,
medium, or high achievers. Our findings are consistent with previous
findings suggesting that the association between student ICT use and
learning outcomes may be either positive or negative, depending on the
nature of the use [12]. For instance, using ICT as educational software
will likely affect learning outcomes positively. In contrast, its misuse or
overuse will likely harm children through physical or psychological
problems [33]. As such, it is unsurprising that using ICT outside the
school for leisure is negatively associated with learning achievements.
Teenagers are likely unable to self-regulate their use of ICT for gaming or
social networking, for example, which will take much of the time they
are supposed to use for educational activities. Our findings seem to
empirically confirm the displacement theory related to technology use
and learning outcomes [4]. That theory conceptualizes that some forms
of entertainment displace time used for schoolwork activities through
ICT. Karlsson’s [22] findings also support the idea that the overuse of
ICT is negatively associated with learning achievements.
Our findings show that the cost of using ICT at home in terms of
distraction and displacement outweighs its educational benefits. However, this argument should be interpreted carefully because the
boundaries between ICT use for learning and gaming (leisure) are
becoming unclear. Gaming technology may support learning by making
learning complex concepts more exciting and interactive. Yet, ICT use
for learning is sometimes viewed as a leisure activity [16]. For example,
educational video games, often called "serious games," are increasingly
used to improve problem-solving and other skills.
The analysis showed that subject-related use of ICT in the classroom
(during and outside lessons) is positively associated with learning outcomes, with a consistent association regardless of student abilities (i.e.,
regardless of whether the student is a low or high achiever). This evidence may suggest that using ICT interacts with teaching strategies (e.g.,
during lessons) to improve learning outcomes. Barrow et al. [34] explain
the mechanism related to this through a model where ICT use increases
students’ learning time during a class period. In this model, a teacher
divides the class time between providing group and individual

using ICT outside of school for leisure is negatively related to all students’ learning achievements (reading, mathematics, and science), statistically significant at 1%.
The use of ICT at school in general was not statistically significant in
the mean analysis, but the UQR has revealed a statistical significance for
some student groups. Table 4 indicates that the variable is negatively
associated with students reading achievements at quantile 10, but the
association becomes positive for students at quantile 90. On the other
hand, Table 5 shows that the use of ICT at school in general is negatively
associated with mathematics achievement for students from quantile 10
to 30. Similarly, the association between the use of ICT at school in
general and science achievements is negative and statistically significant, but only for students on quantiles 10 and 20. These results suggest
that the use of ICT at school in general is negatively associated with lowachieving students’ mathematics and science achievements.
The UQR analysis results for subject-related ICT use during lessons
and subject-related ICT use outside lessons are also presented in Tables 4, 5, and 6 for reading, mathematics, and science, respectively. The
three tables indicate consistent associations between these two forms of
ICT use and learning outcomes. Specifically, the two forms of ICT use are
positively associated with the three measures of learning achievements
across the distribution, statistically significant at 1%. This is consistent
with the results of the mean analysis presented in Table 2. It suggests
that subject-related ICT use during lessons and subject-related ICT use
outside lessons benefit all students regardless of their abilities.

including heterogeneity by student ICT competence, aligns well with the
current increase in students’ ICT use at home and school. Additionally,
previous studies on the topic showed mixed findings. Furthermore, the

Interestingly, we found that ICT use for schoolwork outside of school
and its general use at school are associated with higher learning outcomes for students with lower ICT competence than those with higher
ICT competence. This finding is in line with previous studies. For
example, Saarinen et al. [29] have demonstrated in the context of
Finland that student ICT use for schoolwork outside the school is associated with higher learning outcomes for students with lower ICT
competence relative to their peers with higher ICT competence. Moreover, Sancho & Padilla [35] showed that students with higher ICT
competence might focus on the technical aspects of ICT when using it for
educational purposes, neglecting the potential of the technology to
improve learning through understanding and interest in topics studied.

The study has limitations that may be noted. First, the cross-sectional
design of PISA limits our findings to correlation and not causality. The
cross-sectional design did not allow to control for prior student
achievements to evaluate whether found associations change when prior
performance is considered. Second, it may be essential to note that the
variable ICT competence in this study is measured using student selfreported data and not actual testing of their ICT competencies. As
such, the variable may be a weak indicator of students’ real competence
in ICT. So, further research needs to measure students’ ICT competencies
by testing them to capture the construct in a better way. And third, from
a methodological perspective, there are limitations to note. One limitation is that even though the package used for the analysis handles the
complexity of the PISA, it may introduce bias due to handling missing
data using listwise deletion. Moreover, the package does not provide
other statistical tests to establish statistical significance beyond those
given in the regression. Future studies may replicate our study by using
different methods to test the external consistency of its findings.

In summary, our results indicate that subject-related uses of ICT,
during and outside classroom lessons, are the most efficient forms of ICT
use. They are expected to improve all students’ learning outcomes. ICT
use at school in general and outside the school for learning activities
may improve learning but mainly for less ICT-competent students. In
contrast, following general expectations, using ICT for leisure activities
is detrimental to learning outcomes, no matter the ICT competence of
students. These activities seem to take the time that is supposed to be
used for learning. These findings reinforce previous studies and show
that the association between student ICT use and learning outcomes may
be directly positive or negative, but ICT use can also indirectly influence
learning. If we want student ICT use to improve learning, exploring
various factors that may offset the potentially negative associations
seems relevant.
Conclusion
ICT’s rapid development and inclusion in education have led to its
increasing use by students in schools or at home. However, empirical
evidence on its learning benefits is mixed. In this paper, we examined
the association between different forms of ICT use and students’ learning
outcomes and explored variations in associations based on students’
perceived ICT competence. We also explored the potential heterogeneous effect of ICT use across the learning distribution. We used the
Japanese sample of PISA 2018 data that we analyzed employing an
approach that accounts for the complex nature of the program. Our
variables of interest account for a wide range of dimensions, and we also
controlled for a certain number of factors to have robust estimates.
Findings from the analysis suggest that student ICT use outside the
school for schoolwork activities and its general use at school are not
statistically related to reading and science scores. In contrast, although
statistically weak, the former is associated with mathematics achievement. This association shows statistical significance on the tails of the
distribution.
The analysis also revealed that students with lower perceived ICT
competence show learning gains regarding ICT use outside the school for
schoolwork activities and its general use at school. Furthermore, subjectrelated uses of ICT during lessons and outside lessons are positively
associated with the three measures of learning achievements, and the
association remains consistent by student ICT competence or across the
learning distribution. However, ICT use outside of school for leisure is
negatively associated with all three measures of learning achievements.
This association remains negative at all ICT competence levels and
across the whole learning distribution.
The policy implications of our findings are that promoting student
use of ICT for learning is encouraged, but caution is needed. Students
may use ICT for purposes other than learning, so they need a supervised
environment to use it appropriately, whether at school or home. In
mathematics, students should be guided on how to spend productive
time when using ICT to develop strategies for solving complex problems.
This can include skills on how to differentiate between pure entertainment ICT software or application and one that both entertains and educates. They can also be taught how to self-regulate when using ICT for
learning with specialized math ICT tools. In science, students should be
guided to use ICT efficiently for educational purposes through, for
example, mini-laboratory projects instead of letting them use the tool by
themselves. In reading or language learning, students can be taught how
to use ICT in the learning process to reduce their learning anxiety and
become autonomous learners.
