As digital distraction has become a critical issue in secondary education, it is important to explore
the heterogeneous patterns linking individual digital distraction to academic performance. We
analyzed data from 15-year-old students across 30 countries in the Programme for International
Student Assessment (PISA) in 2022. The main method we used included K-means for Ensemble
Clustering, XGBoost and SHAP analyses for classification and interpretation. Results identified
four distinct student profiles reflecting their susceptibility to digital distraction and mathematics
performance patterns. These profiles are predominantly represented across East Asian, European,
Latin American, Eastern European, and Middle Eastern education systems. The first two clusters
(optimal and susceptible profiles) confirm the expected negative relationship between distraction
and performance, while two unconventional profiles (resilient and invisible at-risk) demonstrate
exceptions to this trend. Key factors distinguishing these profiles include environmental factors
(peer influence and classroom discipline), internal factors (self-efficacy, ICT usage habits, and
emotions), and demographic characteristics. Based on these results, we propose targeted interventions for educators, institutions, and policymakers.

1. Introduction
With the widespread availability of internet connectivity, digital devices such as laptops, tablets, and smartphones have become
indispensable tools for educational contexts ranging from higher education to K-12 classrooms (Flanigan et al., 2023; Park et al.,
2024). These devices offer substantial benefits, such as promoting student motivation, increasing participation, and supporting
learning tasks (Rashid & Asghar, 2016; Tang et al., 2022; Wijaya et al., 2022). However, this connectivity introduces a pervasive global
challenge: digital distraction, which is defined as the diversion of attention from academic tasks to non-academic activities due to the
use of digital devices (Flanigan et al., 2022). Although the detrimental impact of digital distraction has been widely emphasized, a
considerable number of students continue to engage in off-task digital activities during class (Flanigan et al., 2022; Liao & Wu, 2022).
Importantly, research indicates that not all non-academic digital use is detrimental. For instance, brief digital engagement can help
regulate attention by providing short cognitive breaks; it may also support creative thinking by interrupting rigid thought patterns
(Kim et al., 2019; Ritter & Dijksterhuis, 2014). At the same time, academic performance may also reflect differences in digital behavior
patterns. For instance, high-achieving students often demonstrate stronger self-regulatory behaviors, allowing them to maintain task
focus (Bandura, 2001; Gaudreau et al., 2013; Zimmerman, 2002). In contrast, lower-achieving students may experience heightened

Despite the growing body of literature, some critical gaps remain. First, existing findings are primarily situated in higher education,
and relevant research in K-12 education remains insufficient (Park et al., 2024; Zhou & Deng, 2023). Given the developmental differences, secondary school students are likely to exhibit less mature self-regulation and greater sensitivity to environmental stimuli
compared to college students (Crone & Dahl, 2012). Therefore, greater scholarly attention to digital distraction in K-12 contexts is
warranted. Second, there is a lack of a comprehensive framework to explain the explanatory processes linking digital distraction to
academic performance. Traditional variable-centered approaches (e.g., examining whether distraction predicts poorer performance)
often rely on linear associations and average effects, which may overlook the heterogeneity and configurational patterns underlying
students’ digital distraction behaviors and academic outcomes.
Therefore, this study focuses on secondary school students' digital distraction in the classroom and its association with academic
performance, conceptualized as distinct profiles of distraction and performance. Rather than assuming a uniform linear relationship
between distraction and achievement, this study hypothesizes meaningful heterogeneity in students' digital distraction-performance
profiles, based on the integration of previous literature. Furthermore, guided by the Social Cognitive Theory, we develop an integrative framework to organize the factors associated with these profiles into three dimensions: internal, external, and demographic.
Methodologically, this study utilizes data from 30 countries and regions participating in the 2022 Programme for International Student
Assessment (PISA), where mathematics literacy served as the main assessment domain (OECD, 2023). To capture complex and
potentially non-linear relationships, this study adopts a person-centered analytical approach. We initially utilize data-driven clustering
techniques to identify student profiles based on their individual susceptibility to digital distraction and mathematics performance. We
then used supervised classification models to examine the key factors associated with profile membership. We aimed to identify
distinct profiles linking students’ digital distraction and academic performance, interpret them through the lens of Social Cognitive
Theory, and ultimately develop targeted interventions for secondary school students.
2. Literature review
2.1. Digital distraction in learning contexts
2.1.1. Conceptualization of digital distraction
The concept of “distraction” essentially refers to the process by which an individual's attention shifts from one object to another
(Hanin, 2021). When this kind of distraction is caused by digital devices, it is called “digital distraction.” In educational settings, digital
distraction is defined as the attention shifts from primary academic tasks to non-academic activities due to the existence or use of
digital devices (Flanigan et al., 2022). To clarify the scope of this study, we distinguish digital distraction from two related terms:
digital addiction and media multitasking.
Digital distraction and digital addiction are both problematic digital uses. However, digital addiction is a long-term psychological
dependence (Odaci, 2011), while distraction is a temporary shift in attention that occurs due to immediate environmental triggers
(Oraison et al., 2020). Most importantly, the two are intrinsically linked. If distraction occurs frequently and is reinforced by persistent
rewards, it may develop into a compulsive pattern and ultimately form a long-term addiction (Oraison et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2023).
Digital distraction is seen as an antecedent to more severe issues like digital addiction (Oraison et al., 2020; Tarafdar et al., 2019). As
for media multitasking, it is a neutral term used to describe the interactive behavior of simultaneously dealing with multiple forms of
media (Dontre, 2021; Zhou & Deng, 2023). In contrast, digital distraction is a more harmful form of multitasking, which emphasizes
the shift of attention from learning tasks to irrelevant digital activities (Deng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). After clarifying these
distinctions, digital distraction is regarded as a harmful phenomenon that may lead to more severe addictive behaviors.
2.1.2. Digital distraction in secondary education
The existing research on digital distraction mainly focuses on college students. However, since there are significant differences in
developmental stage and learning environment between college students and secondary school students, the existing findings have
limited applicability for secondary school students (Park et al., 2024). Students in secondary school are in a distinct psychological stage
compared to those in college. Their capacity for self-regulation is not as strong, and they exhibit more intense emotional responses to
environmental stimuli (Crone & Dahl, 2012). Consequently, they are more prone to impulsive behaviors, which also means they find it
harder to resist the temptations of the digital world. The educational environment in secondary schools also differs markedly from that
of higher education. University classrooms typically offer students greater autonomy and limited supervision. In contrast, secondary
schools are more teacher-led, and students are subject to stricter policies and closer teacher oversight (Flanigan & Titsworth, 2020; Kim
et al., 2019; OECD, 2023). However, as the importance of digital literacy grows, digital devices are being extended from university to
secondary school classrooms (OECD, 2023; Park et al., 2024). This creates a challenging environment, as secondary school students'
weaker self-control requires targeted classroom interventions to manage digital device use and minimize distraction. Accordingly, this
study focuses on secondary classrooms, examining students’ digital device use and digital distraction.
2.2. Digital distraction and academic performance
Following the definition of digital distraction in Section 2.1, we examine its complex interplay with academic performance.
Research has found that students with different levels of achievement exhibit distinct digital behavior patterns. However, digital

2.2.1. Academic performance and individual susceptibility to digital distraction
Numerous studies show that academic performance is closely related to students' digital usage behavior, including their susceptibility to digital distractions. High-performing students often show stronger self-regulation compared to their lower-performing
classmates (Zimmerman, 2002). Self-regulation is a multidimensional process involving planning, monitoring, and control of cognition. Learners with self-regulation could sustain attention and manage impulses when facing digital temptations (Deng et al., 2024).
Another characteristic of high-performing students is that they are more likely to use devices for academic purposes, such as reviewing
instructional materials or taking notes (Gaudreau et al., 2013). In contrast, lower-achieving students are more sensitive to distraction.
Due to a lack of cognitive control or self-regulatory capacity (Pekrun et al., 2010; Zimmerman, 2002), digital distraction is often seen
as an avoidance strategy for them. Especially when encountering academic boredom or negative emotion, they might choose to use
digital devices for emotional relief (Pekrun et al., 2010). Therefore, academic performance appears to be strongly associated with a
student's susceptibility to digital distraction.
2.2.2. The impact of digital distraction on academic performance
Using digital devices in the classroom can both support learning and introduce challenges, with digital distraction representing a
primary obstacle to students' academic engagement. Studies have indicated that digital device use during class has a detrimental effect
on both medium- and long-term academic performance (Liao & Wu, 2022; Wu & Cheng, 2018). The underlying mechanism is typically
attributed to frequent task switching, which imposes cognitive load. For students, this constant shifting of attention between academic
tasks and digital devices consumes the attentional resources that should have been used for concentrating on learning (Chen et al.,
2020; Dontre, 2021; Wu & Cheng, 2018). Notably, not only academic tasks, but also non-academic tasks show a negative impact on
students' learning. For example, using laptops for note-taking, Flanigan and Titsworth (2020) indicated this kind of note-taking can
compromise completeness and coherence of content. Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014) also found that it could lead to verbatim
transcription rather than conceptual engagement, negatively influencing comprehension and recall. Furthermore, Sana et al. (2013)
found that multitasking on laptops not only disrupts the user's learning but can also negatively affect the learning outcomes of nearby
peers.
However, appropriate usage of digital devices may support learning. For example, some instructional technology resources
designed for teaching and learning, like interactive e-books and multimedia micro-lessons, can support learning (Tang et al., 2022;
Wijaya et al., 2022). These digital tools could improve both learning satisfaction and outcomes by visualizing abstract concepts and
sustaining student engagement. Some non-teaching-oriented digital devices may also support individuals’ performance. For example,
Kim et al. (2019) discovered that frequent, but brief use of chat apps like KakaoTalk and web browsers was positively associated with
academic performance, while prolonged usage was negatively correlated. Similarly, Ritter and Dijksterhuis (2014) found that when
facing complex problem-solving and creative thinking tasks, individuals could break free from rigid thinking and activate the associative processing mechanism by appropriate, low-cognitive-load forms of distraction. Studies also suggest that purposeful social
media usage could enhance communication between instructors and students, thereby improving the learning experience (Dontre,
2021; Lambić, 2016).
Based on the review, we found a heterogeneous relationship between students' digital distraction in the classroom and their academic performance. This suggests that different patterns may exist, which could be influenced by key contextual factors. Therefore, it
is necessary to adopt a profile-based perspective to understand how students’ distraction interacts with performance and other related
characteristics. In the next Section 2.3, we introduce a theoretical framework to categorize the factors that drive these patterns.
2.3. Theoretical framework
2.3.1. The Social Cognitive Theory
To explore underlying factors driving different students' distraction-performance patterns, we adopt Social Cognitive Theory (SCT)
as our theoretical framework. The framework posits that human functioning is the product of triadic reciprocal determinism (Bandura,
1986, 2001). Behavioral, personal, and environmental influences are involved in the triadic reciprocal determinism, and these three
influences interact with each other in a continuous manner (Bandura, 1986, 2001; Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2019). Within this
framework, behavioral influences include observable actions such as activity choices, persistence, and learning-related behaviors;
personal influences encompass emotions, goals, and self-efficacy; environmental influences involve social modeling, feedback, and
instructional guidance. Drawing on this theory, we regard students’ distraction-performance patterns as the behavioral component,
while personal and environmental factors may shape different behavior patterns. The following content introduces how we constructed the internal and external factors.
Internal Factors (Personal Influences). We use the term “internal factors” to denote the personal influences in SCT. Personal
influences indicate students' internal cognitive and affective drivers of digital behavior, which could regulate human functioning
through reciprocal interactions (Bandura, 1997; Schunk & Usher, 2019). In studies related to digital distraction, there are two perspectives frequently used, namely the “rational” and the “irrational” (or automatic thinking) perspectives (Chen et al., 2020; Taneja
et al., 2015). Given that this classification is rather general in nature, we reconceptualize these internal factors as two more specific
dimensions, cognitive-motivational factors and affective-habitual dimensions. The first dimension focuses on cognitive and motivational attributes that reflect individuals’ regulation skills (Zimmerman, 2002). Self-efficacy, attitudes, and motivation are important
factors reflecting these attributes. Studies have found that students with these traits could align their actions with learning goals; they

also demonstrate greater persistence in learning tasks and are better equipped to resist the temptation of digital devices (Chen et al.,
2014; Schunk & Pajares, 2002, pp. 15–31). Another dimension, affective-habitual factors, emphasizes impulsive, unplanned, or
automatic thinking attributes, suggesting that distraction is not always the consequence of deep consideration (Chen et al., 2020).
Impulsiveness, emotions, and habits are primary drivers. Impulsive students tend to check devices without specific intent, and it
becomes habitual over time (Tarafdar et al., 2019). Furthermore, emotions may aggravate this automatic process. Device usage serves
as a coping mechanism for temporary relief to these negative emotions (Brady et al., 2022; Tarafdar et al., 2019). Therefore, we
construct self-efficacy, attitudes, and motivation as cognitive-motivational factors, and impulsiveness, emotion, and habit as
affective-habitual factors.
External Factors (Environmental Influences). We use the term “external factors” to refer to the environmental influences
defined in SCT. Environmental influences are defined as factors that can affect learners' personal processes and behavioral outcomes
(Schunk, 2012). To construct the external factors, this study refers to the source of distraction framework proposed by Deng et al.
(2024), which categorizes external factors into three dimensions: physical, social, and technological. The physical dimension focuses
on the classroom environment (Deng et al., 2024). For example, studies indicated that a disordered classroom and large class sizes
would lead to a higher frequency of distraction (Chen et al., 2014; Taneja et al., 2015). The social dimension focuses on interpersonal
relations within the classroom, such as the interaction between students and peers, and teachers (Deng et al., 2024). Sana et al. (2013)
indicated that peer distraction is closely linked to individual distraction through imitation. Teachers can stimulate students' interest.
They can also improve students' cognitive function. This directly enhances engagement (Batch et al., 2021; Flanigan et al., 2022).
Finally, the technological dimension refers to the digital infrastructure in students’ learning environment, such as the availability and
usage of digital devices (Deng et al., 2024). Although institutions integrate laptops and smartphones into classrooms to support
learning, these devices also create opportunities for off-task behavior (Dontre, 2021; Throuvala et al., 2021).
Demographic Factors. Beyond the internal and external factors specified in SCT, the distraction-performance patterns are
embedded within broader demographic and contextual conditions. As noted by Christanti et al. (2024), the digital divide has shifted
from disparities in access to usage to the outcomes derived from digital technology use. Within this framework, demographic factors
like gender, socioeconomic status (SES) background, and cultural background help explain the divide of students in regulating
distraction. As for gender, Baturay and Toker (2015) found that male students are more likely to check their digital devices during
class, whereas Halubanza et al. (2023) indicate that female students may experience greater distractions, particularly related to digital
notifications. SES further shapes students' access to different digital resources, which would affect their digital experience. For
example, students with greater family cultural capital usually have more exposure to cultural resources and opportunities for academic
support, thereby showing stronger academic engagement, higher persistence, and better academic outcomes (Chiu & Chow, 2010). In
this situation, the emergence of the divide is not only due to access to devices, but also because of the digital capabilities of the students
that facilitate learning rather than causing distraction (Christanti et al., 2024). At a broader level, the cultural background can influence the classroom environment, norms, and social expectations. For example, cultures that emphasize authority, discipline, and
collective responsibility may foster stronger external regulation, which interacts with students’ internal self-regulatory capacities, and
then prompts them to perform better (Chen et al., 2014; Han, 2022).
Within the SCT framework, demographic factors are a contextual background that shapes the reciprocal interactions between
internal, external factors, and behavioral outcomes. By incorporating gender, SES, and cultural background into the construct, this
study can capture the structural constraints related to students' distraction-performance profiles.

3. Methodology
3.1. Dataset and sample
This study utilized data from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022, which assesses 15-year-old students' competencies in reading, mathematics, and science, with mathematics as the major domain. Based on the proposed theoretical
framework in Fig. 1, we selected 30 predictors from the Student Questionnaire and the optional ICT (information and communications
technology) Familiarity Questionnaire to examine the factors associated with students’ digital distraction and academic performance
profiles.
To enhance cross-cultural generalizability, we included 30 education systems participating in PISA 2022. Specifically, the selection
of countries or regions was determined by data availability. Only countries and regions that administered the selected items were
included to ensure complete data on key predictors. The final dataset included 239,695 students drawn from 8826 schools across 30
participating education systems. Of these students, 118,297 were female, and 121,375 were male.
3.2. Measurement
In this study, aligned with our study constructs, we selected two clustering variables and a broad array of 30 predictors from the
PISA 2022 dataset. We draw on the rigorous psychometric validation procedures provided by the OECD (2024) to support the construct
validity of these measures across the participating education systems. Technical details for all measures, including item descriptions,
scaling methods, and reliability estimates, are provided in Table S1 in the supplementary material.
3.2.1. Clustering variables
Mathematical Performance. In PISA 2022, mathematics was the major domain of assessment. It is operationalized as mathematical literacy, defined as an individual's capacity to reason mathematically and to formulate, employ, and interpret mathematics to
solve problems in a variety of real-world contexts (OECD, 2024). Following OECD technical reports (OECD, 2024), student literacy is
not reported as a single raw score but as a set of Plausible Values (PVs). This approach addresses measurement error by providing a
probabilistic representation of a student's latent ability. Accordingly, we conducted analysis ten times, once for each plausible value
(PV1MATH-PV10MATH). Then, we combined the findings following OECD recommendations to provide reliable estimates of the
relationship between digital distraction and mathematics performance.
Digital Distraction during Mathematics Class. Digital distraction serves as a core construct in this study and can be measured
using item ST273Q06 from the PISA 2022 Student Questionnaire. This self-reported item states: “Students get distracted by using
digital resources (e.g., smartphones, websites, apps) during math lessons.” The original response format utilized a four-point Likert
scale ranging from 1 (Every lesson) to 4 (Never or almost never). We applied a reverse-coding procedure, inverting the scale so that 1
represents “Never or almost never” (low distraction) and 4 represents “Every lesson” (high distraction). This transformation allows for
a clear interpretation of the variable's direction. Higher values in the clustering analysis indicate higher levels of digital distraction.
Although multi-item scales are often preferred in psychological research, our use of this single-item (SI) indicator is justified by both
contextual relevance and methodological validity. ST273Q06 is the first PISA indicator designed to capture digital sources of
distraction in class, and it has not appeared in the previous cycles (OECD, 2024). There are studies supporting the use of SI measures
when the construct is concrete, unidimensional, and unambiguous to respondents (Allen et al., 2022; Diamantopoulos et al., 2012).
“Get distracted by using digital resources (e.g., smartphones, websites, apps) during math lessons” describes a specific, observable
behavior, thereby minimizing ambiguity and ensuring strong face validity.
However, this variable still requires further processing. As a component of the item set ST273, which focuses on the disciplinary
climate in mathematics classrooms, this item is characterized in the PISA 2022 Results (OECD, 2023) as capturing “the likelihood of
students becoming distracted by using digital devices in mathematics lessons.” Consequently, the item reflects both students'

susceptibility to digital distractions in class and their perception of distraction in the classroom. A critical challenge is that raw scores
conflate variance at two distinct levels. Level 1 is individual susceptibility to digital distraction (L1), which means the student's
subjective sensitivity to distraction, representing their deviation from the environment. Level 2 is the perception of distraction in the
environment (L2), which means the aggregated digital distraction phenomenon of the environment. Interpreting raw scores directly
could mistakenly attribute environment-level distraction to individual characteristics. To align with this study's focus on the individual
level, it is necessary to disentangle these levels to accurately profile students based on their personal propensity for distraction rather
than their environment. Thus, we applied Group-Mean Centering (GMC) to the data. The formula we calculated the relative deviation
for each student (L1) is:
Xcentered = Xij − Xj
Where Xij represents the raw reported distraction score of students i in school j, and Xj represents the aggregate mean score of all
students in school j (L2). Here, we employed a school-level approach rather than classroom level because we considered that class-level
identifiers were not available across all 30 participating education systems in the PISA dataset. As the primary sampling unit in PISA,
the school serves as a proxy for the shared learning environment. Therefore, we utilized the school as the L2 grouping unit. This
statistical transformation effectively removes between-school mean differences, retaining only the within-school variance. Consequently, the resulting variable serves as a measure of relative individual susceptibility, representing the extent to which a student's
distraction level deviates from that of their school peers. In Table S1 (supplementary material), we coded this individual-level variable
as DIGDIST_L1 and coded the school level as DIGDIST_L2.

3.3.1. Phase 1: data preprocessing and imputation
After selecting the target variables and restricting the sample to 30 participating education systems, we conducted a comprehensive
diagnosis of missing data for all variables (see Table 3). The proportion of missing values ranged from 0 to 54.20%, with an average
rate of 21.59%. Notably, variables with the highest missingness rates, such as ST309, correspond to the “within-construct matrix
sampling” design employed in PISA 2022 (OECD, 2024). In this design, students are administered random subsets of items to broaden
content coverage without imposing excessive burden. Consequently, the observed data gaps represent “planned missingness” rather
than student non-response. To statistically assess the missingness mechanism, Little's MCAR test was performed as a diagnostic
reference rather than a definitive test of the mechanism. The result (χ 2 (44,561) = 49,051.76, p < .001) may reflect the very large
statistical power associated with the sample size (N > 220,000), the primary justification for the missing data mechanism lies in the
study design. Unlike stochastic attrition, the missingness in PISA 2022 is systematic and conditional on the test booklet assigned to
each student, which is an observed design variable (OECD, 2024). Therefore, the data satisfy the Missing at Random (MAR)
assumption, where the probability of missingness depends on observed design variables rather than unobserved student traits. Based
on this transparent methodological rationale, we employed an iterative imputation approach grounded in the Multiple Imputation by
Chained Equations (MICE) framework. Specifically, we implemented the IterativeImputer algorithm utilizing LightGBM estimators.
This tree-based approach was selected for its superior ability to capture complex nonlinear interactions, which is particularly suitable
given the heterogeneous and nonlinear relationships expected in large-scale educational data.
Following this, selected variables were recoded, including reverse-coding where necessary, to ensure consistency with the direction
of the underlying constructs (see Table S1 in the supplementary material). Then, we applied GMC to the digital distraction to isolate
students’ individual susceptibility to digital distraction (DIGDIST_L1) and perceptions of environment (DIGDIST_L2), as detailed in
Section 3.2.1. Finally, we conducted Pearson correlation analyses to examine the preliminary associations among mathematics performance and digital distraction (including the raw digital distraction score, DIGDIST_L1, and DIGDIST_L2). Collectively, we obtained
a robust dataset suitable for subsequent ensemble clustering and classification.
3.3.2. Phase 2: ensemble clustering and profile identification
Given that PISA employs ten PVs to reflect measurement uncertainty, relying on a single K-Means run using an average score may
obscure data variability. To address this problem, we adopted a weighted cluster ensemble design, in which sampling weights were
incorporated at the clustering stage. This strategy enhances robustness to both measurement noise and random initialization, yielding
stable and reproducible student partitions across measurement uncertainty and random initialization (Khoudi et al., 2024). The
process consisted of three stages.
In the first stage, ten standardized datasets were constructed by pairing the digital distraction score (DIGDIST_L1) with each of the
ten mathematics PVs. For each dataset, clustering solutions ranging from k = 2 to k = 9 were evaluated using four internal validity
indices: the Elbow Method, Silhouette Coefficient, Calinski-Harabasz Index, and Davies-Bouldin Index. Synthesizing outcomes across
all datasets, the four-cluster solution (k = 4) demonstrated the most consistent balance between cohesion and separation.
In the second stage, with k fixed at 4, we analyzed each PV-based dataset using weighted K-Means. To ensure population representativeness, we used W_FSTUWT as sampling weights. A common challenge in ensemble clustering is the “label switching” problem,
where independent runs assign cluster labels arbitrarily. To address this problem, we employed the Hungarian Algorithm for alignment. This classical combinatorial optimization method solves assignment problems by finding the optimal one-to-one correspondence
between two sets of elements (Munkres, 1957). We used the partition from the first run (with PV1MATH as the plausible value) as the
reference and remapped the remaining nine partitions to maximize diagonal agreement in the resulting confusion matrix, thereby

3.3.3. Classification and feature interpretation
To delineate specific boundaries between student profiles, we decomposed the multi-class problem into pairwise binary classification tasks using a one-vs-one strategy (e.g., Cluster1 vs. Cluster 2). To address outliers and class imbalance, we combined
RobustScaler with Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE)-Tomek in a preprocessing pipeline. Hyperparameters of the
XGBoost classifier were then tuned using RandomizedSearchCV with 30 iterations under cross-validation, with the F1-score as the
optimization metric.
We assessed model performance using a stratified 5-fold cross-validation procedure. To rigorously prevent data leakage, a strict
pipeline was enforced within each fold. The process proceeded in three distinct steps: The preprocessing pipeline consisted of three key
steps. First, we performed independent scaling by fitting the RobustScaler exclusively on the training folds and then applying it to the
corresponding validation fold. Second, we implemented safe resampling, in which synthetic samples generated via SMOTE-Tomek
were restricted to the training partition to avoid contamination of the validation set. Third, we trained the XGBoost model on the
balanced training data and evaluated its performance on the original, imbalanced validation set. To convert probabilistic outputs into
binary labels, a fold-specific threshold maximizing the F1 score was dynamically determined to account for class imbalance and avoid
bias from an arbitrary fixed cutoff. Performance metrics, including Area Under the Curve (AUC), F1 score, Precision, and Recall, were
aggregated as means and standard deviations across folds.
In the third stage, a final XGBoost model was trained on the complete dataset using the optimized hyperparameters and the full
preprocessing pipeline for interpretability. Then, we utilized SHapley additive explanations (SHAP) to quantify the marginal contribution of each predictor. To ensure computational feasibility, SHAP values were computed on a representative stratified subsample of
2000 students. Consistent with the training phase, all predictor features were transformed using RobustScaler before SHAP calculation
to ensure methodological consistency.
To further examine the demographic composition of the profiles, we employed an adjusted standardized residuals approach. First, a
global chi-square test of independence was conducted to determine whether there was a statistically significant association between
profile membership and demographic variables (country and gender). This step provided the basis for subsequent residual analyses.
Second, conditional on a significant global test, we calculated adjusted standardized residuals to identify the specific sources of
distributional differences. This analysis allowed us to determine which countries or gender groups were significantly over- or underrepresented in each profile, based on adjusted residuals exceeding the conventional threshold (|z| > 1.96, p < .05).

5. Discussions
The main research questions in this study are: (1) identifying student profiles from math performance and digital distraction; (2)
finding key factors that distinguish these profiles; and (3) examining differences in demographic composition between profiles. In
Section 5.1, we describe the student profiles and their distinct distraction-performance patterns and contextual characteristics. Section
5.2 links these findings to the SCT framework. We focus on the role of individual agency in regulating behavior when interacting with
environmental factors. Section 5.3 presents practical implications. Section 5.4 discusses limitations and suggests directions for future
research.
5.1. Characterizing distinct student profiles
We analyzed 239,695 secondary school students across 30 countries, and our results show that secondary students’ susceptibility to
digital distraction is not uniformly associated with their mathematics performance. We identified four distinct distraction-performance
profiles, with key characteristics across internal, external, and demographic dimensions (see Fig. 10).
5.1.1. Clu_1: the optimal profile: alignment of high performance and supportive resources
Clu_1 is labeled as “optimal” profile. Students from this profile show lower susceptibility to digital distraction and excellent
mathematics performance. In this profile, male students are dominant, and most of them come from high SES backgrounds, with
considerable concentrations in East Asia and certain European regions. Compared with other profiles, a supportive environment and
strong internal resources are common characteristics of these students. All these external and internal factors show an optimal
reciprocal cycle within the SCT framework.
From the external perspective, they come from an environment with less peer influence and well-disciplined classrooms. Schunk
and DiBenedetto (2019), in their study related to motivation and SCT, indicated that the behavior of peers can be regarded as a
consistent model. When students observe successful peers, they tend to internalize the belief that they can also achieve the same
performance. In Clu_1, Hong Kong is the most representative region of this profile. Therefore, we infer that the imitative behavior of
students in Clu_1 aligns with external cultural norms. A study by Han (2022) indicated that, under the influence of Confucianism, East
Asian cultural norms emphasize diligence, classroom order, and collective responsibility. We could look back on students in Clu_1,
under the exemplary influence of outstanding peers, and through self-discipline within this cultural context, restrain themselves, a
behavioral pattern closely associated with their superior learning outcomes. (Chiu & Chow, 2010; Han, 2022). Many studies also
emphasize the importance of SES in resource provision. High family capital often provides students with better school resources and a
good learning environment (Assari et al., 2020; Tramonte & Willms, 2009), and these factors protect students from the interference of
adverse external environments.
Environmental advantages alone do not fully characterize Clu_1 students' academic success. They report higher self-efficacy in both
mathematics and digital competence. Self-efficacy can enhance students' learning experience in the classroom, and the way it works is
mutual, where high efficacy helps students sustain attention and get success in academic tasks, while this success further supports
efficacy (Bandura, 1997; Schunk & Pajares, 2002). The gender distribution of Clu_1 is also consistent with findings that males
frequently express better digital self-efficacy (Scherer & Siddiq, 2019). Deng et al. (2024) indicated that learners with self-regulation
could sustain attention and manage impulses when facing digital temptations. Another characteristic of high-performing students is
that they are more likely to use devices for academic purposes, such as reviewing instructional materials or taking notes (Gaudreau

for leisure activities, and notifications from social networks and apps are often turned off. Interestingly, their tendency to avoid digital

5.1.2. The susceptible profile: cumulative disadvantages in structure and agency
Clu_2 represents the susceptible profile and aligns most closely with previous findings linking higher digital distraction with low
academic achievement (Chen et al., 2020; Dontre, 2021), and it also illustrates a negative reciprocal cycle within SCT. Most of the
Clu_2 students come from lower SES backgrounds, are concentrated in Latin America, and are mainly female. Despite limited SES
resources, Clu_2 students report frequent weekday ICT use, which illustrates a usage-oriented digital divide in which access exists but
effective integration for learning is lacking (Lythreatis et al., 2022). The geographical distribution of this profile reflects regional
digital inequalities (Cai et al., 2016). For example, PISA 2018 results showed that fewer than half of students in Latin America achieved
basic skill levels (Agasisti et al., 2023), indicating that uneven resource distribution and structural inequalities remain widespread. The
predominance of female students may reflect a regional gender digital divide associated with stereotypes regarding female students’
abilities in mathematics and technology (Antonio & Tuffley, 2014). These structural disadvantages provide the background for the
unfavorable external conditions and internal limitations discussed below.
Compared with Clu_1, Clu_2 students are situated in learning environments with poorer classroom discipline and stronger negative
peer influences. Such environments may facilitate imitative digital behavior, potentially heightening susceptibility to distraction
(Chen et al., 2014). Peers' digital usage, combined with a disordered classroom climate, is closely coupled with students' distraction. As
indicated by Sana et al. (2013), multitasking on students' laptops not only impairs their own learning but also that of their classmates.
Chen et al. (2014) emphasized that in a large-scale class with poor discipline, students are more likely to participate in digital activities
anonymously. From an SCT perspective, peers’ behaviors can function as salient behavioral models (Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2019).
Therefore, when Clu_2 students are in a disordered classroom where classmates are frequently distracted, their own susceptibility to
digital distraction is likely to be heightened.
Unlike Clu_1, students in Clu_2 are primarily distinguished by insufficient self-efficacy, recognized as a critical internal buffer
against distraction as we discussed earlier. Beyond self-efficacy, this profile shows a distinct pattern of digital habits and emotional
response. Research suggests that students from lower SES backgrounds are more likely to adopt maladaptive coping strategies when
facing academic stress (Xu et al., 2021). This tendency is reflected in their keeping notifications active and their frequent engagement
in leisure-related ICT activities. This is consistent with the finding by Halubanza et al. (2023), who indicate that female students may
experience greater distractions, particularly related to digital notifications. It can be inferred that this repetitive behavior may serve as
an emotional buffer to alleviate mathematical stress. However, this coping mechanism likely incurs a high cognitive cost. As Wang and
Tchernev (2012) concluded, when students utilize multitasking to manage negative emotions, they prioritize immediate emotional
relief over cognitive efficiency. Over time, repeated ICT contact is likely associated with automated digital behaviors, which reinforce
their susceptibility to digital distraction (Soror et al., 2015).
5.1.3. Clu_3: the invisible at-risks profile: low achievement despite low distraction
Clu_3 is labeled as an “invisible at-risk” profile. This profile challenges the linear assumption that lower digital distraction is related
to better academic performance. Students in Clu_3 show relatively low susceptibility to digital distraction, yet this pattern does not
translate into academic success. Their demographic characteristics are similar to those observed in Clu_2, reflecting a convergence of
structural disadvantages. However, a comparison between these two profiles reveals an important difference. Clu_3 students’ learning
environments are not clearly destructive, suggesting that the observed performance gap is more closely related to internal limitations
rather than the external environment.
A lack of self-efficacy emerges as a central characteristic associated with limited academic success in Clu_3. They exhibit low selfefficacy in both mathematics and digital competence. Existing research suggests that insufficient self-efficacy is often associated with
psychological withdrawal from learning tasks. Rather than active cognitive engagement, students may display ritualistic and passive
compliance in academic settings (Bandura, 1997; Zakariya, 2021). Low digital competence self-efficacy may act as a barrier to students’ ability to use ICT as a learning support. Consistent with the findings by Hatlevik et al. (2017), students from lower SES families
often lack the internalized confidence needed to apply ICT to higher-level learning activities. Similarly, Rohatgi et al. (2016) identified
ICT self-efficacy as a major factor distinguishing entertainment-oriented from educational use. The combination of lower SES backgrounds and limited ICT self-efficacy of Clu_3 students aligns with these perspectives. Correspondingly, their usage remains basic and
is largely oriented toward entertainment.
The observed digital habits of Clu_3 further support this interpretation. Despite the relatively quiet learning environments, these
students report frequent weekday leisure-related ICT use and are less likely to turn off notifications during class. Beyond usage patterns, lower self-efficacy is also linked to students’ academic anxiety. In detail, according to the control-value theory, when students
feel that they have limited control over their academic outcomes, anxiety is more likely to emerge (Pekrun et al., 2010). In Clu_3,
insufficient self-efficacy and the elevated level of anxiety reinforce this theoretical explanation. These characteristics indicate an
15

C. Li and F. Chen

ineffective device usage pattern, where digital technology is primarily treated as a passive entertainment tool rather than an active
resource for supporting learning.
Viewed through the triadic reciprocal perspective, Clu_3 students are embedded in a relatively favorable environment. However,
structural disadvantages interact with limited internal resources, likely limiting their ability to translate regulated digital use into
positive academic outcomes. This profile reflects a constrained form of agency within the SCT framework.
5.1.4. Clu_4: the resilient profile: high achievement amidst environmental deficits
Clu_4 is labeled as a “resilience” profile. Students in this group maintain excellent academic performance despite high susceptibility
to digital distraction, challenging the assumption that high distraction necessarily leads to low achievement. In terms of demographic
composition, Clu_4 is similar to Clu_1, but they do not consistently report highly supportive external learning environments as Clu_1
students. At the same time, although their external environments resemble the challenging conditions observed in Clu_2, Clu_4 students are characterized by robust internal resources that appear to support their resistance to these distractive contexts.
High self-efficacy emerges as a defining internal characteristic of Clu_4 students. This strong sense of efficacy coincides with an
effective cognitive filtering capacity. Such an internal state supports the ability to screen out irrelevant environmental stimuli and
maintain focus on academic tasks (Zimmerman, 2002). These findings are also consistent with the view that SES-related advantages
may serve a protective role against environmental distractions (Chiu & Chow, 2010). Although school environments may present
multiple digital triggers, the structured digital habits commonly established in high SES families may help foster a more protective
internalized environment. This internal structure likely supports students in managing external disruptions through instrumental
self-regulation (Nikken & Jansz, 2014).
In their habitual digital behavior, Clu_4 students demonstrate active self-regulation and instrumental use. They report less frequent
weekday ICT use for leisure and are more likely to consciously turn off social media notifications during class. Given that many
students struggle to resist immediate impulses when exposed to attractive digital stimuli (Xiang et al., 2020), these students reflect
self-disciplined and metacognitively informed usage habits. By anticipating potential distractions, Clu_4 students proactively disable
them, which may help minimize cognitive load and preserve attentional resources for learning (Daleiden et al., 2025).
Overall, Clu_4 exemplifies a profile of resilience. Resilience refers to the capacity to achieve positive educational outcomes despite
exposure to pressures or risk factors that threaten development (Masten, 2001). From the SCT perspective, individuals are not merely
shaped by their environments; rather, they actively select, regulate, and modify environmental influences through their own agency
(Bandura, 2001). Therefore, our interpretation of this group is that, despite objectively challenging environments and high sensitivity
to distraction, Clu_4 students demonstrate strong cognitive filtering abilities accompanied by high self-efficacy and positive
self-regulation. These internal resources seem to facilitate the separation of external disturbances from their academic performance.
5.2. Theoretical synthesis
We captured specific snapshots of four profiles in this study, which demonstrate that distraction-performance profiles reflect the
complex interplay between external and internal factors, aligning with the principle of triadic reciprocity.
Regarding internal processes, our findings highlight self-efficacy, habit, and emotion as three pivotal personal factors shaping
students' digital behavior. We integrate these elements through the lens of personal agency, referring to individuals' perceived capacity
to exert influence over important life events (Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2019). Theoretically, self-efficacy represents a core mechanism
through which agency operates, enabling students to initiate emotional regulation. Over time, such regulation may consolidate into
more disciplined digital habits. In this sense, agency is enacted through self-regulatory processes, including the regulation of emotion
and behavior, for example, goal setting and strategy adjustment (Bandura, 1977; Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2019; Zimmerman, 2000). In
our study, Clu_4 can be characterized as a high-agency profile. These students' lower math anxiety is likely mitigated by their active
self-regulation. They actively manage their digital devices to minimize distractions, creating conditions conducive to sustained and
focused learning. In contrast, students with weaker agency exhibit reduced self-regulatory capacity and are more vulnerable to
emotional overwhelm, and these internal limitations manifest differently across profiles. In Clu_3, students show lower self-efficacy
accompanied by psychological withdrawal from academic tasks. In contrast, Clu_2 students tend to use digital devices as a coping
strategy when experiencing academic anxiety. Thus, agency represents the capacity to integrate these elements into a defense against
distraction, whereas its absence is linked to susceptibility to digital distraction. Within this study, peer digital behavior and classroom
discipline function are key environmental factors that interact with students’ internal self-regulatory processes. Our observations from
Clu_4 further indicate that environmental constraints do not inevitably lead to negative outcomes, underscoring the critical role of
self-regulation in shaping academic responses to digital distractions. Self-regulation enables learners to regulate how they respond to
environmental conditions (Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2019; Zimmerman, 2000). Therefore, as the degree of digital integration has
increased, the capacity to self-regulate against potential distractors becomes increasingly critical for academic success in contemporary
technological contexts.
Our findings suggest that the relationship between digital distraction and academic achievement is deeply embedded in the broader
context of the digital divide. Specifically in the second-level digital divide (differences in skills and usage) and the third-level digital
divide (differences in the outcomes of internet use) (Scheerder et al., 2017; Wei et al., 2011). In detail, we found that structural inequalities in SES, regions with cultural backgrounds, and gender are integral to students' digital agency, playing a key role in whether
digital devices serve as tools for empowerment or sources of distraction. Furthermore, it contributes to a gap in digital agency, which is
related to whether students control technology or are controlled by it. In Clu_1 and Clu_4, male students are overrepresented, and these
profiles are characterized by more advantageous SES backgrounds and stronger self-regulatory capacities. Low-performing profiles,

Clu_2 and 3, on the other hand, female students dominate, struggling with the lack of self-regulatory capacities in an adverse SES.
These findings reveal the crucial role of these demographic characteristics in analyzing the phenomenon of students' digital distraction
with performance. Therefore, when emphasizing the agency and self-regulation in support of learning, it is essential to consider
whether students’ structural contexts truly provide opportunities for the development and cultivation of agency.
5.3. Implications
5.3.1. Implications for educational practice
The intervention that should be given first is resolving the external roots of distractions. When students observe their classmates
using smartphones or engaging with non-instructional websites and apps, they are more likely to imitate such behaviors and become
distracted (Chen et al., 2014). Thus, cutting the distraction source from other students' screens might be the most direct way to reduce
the distraction. Direct bans on devices and strict classroom rules might provoke resistance among adolescents. They can also limit
opportunities to develop self-regulation. Alternative physical methods, such as increasing the distance between the seats or reducing
class size, may help minimize distractions. Based on our findings, we propose that well-designed instructional practices and a relatively
engaging and relaxed classroom environment might be effective. As our findings indicate, susceptible students, such as those in Clu_2,
tend to adopt maladaptive coping strategies when facing academic stress (Xu et al., 2021). Clu_3 students also show a stronger sense of
anxiety in mathematics. The engaging and less stressful class could help students reduce their anxiety and engage in the courses, which
may in turn reduce their reliance on digital devices. Apart from reducing the use of devices passively, it is important to help Clu_2 and
Clu_3 students build strong self-regulation abilities and help them take the initiative in managing the use of devices. A key instructional
priority is cultivating students' confidence in digital competence. It can be started by assigning specific and practical tasks related to the
use of digital devices, such as collecting relevant information or using digital devices to complete assignments. The teacher's affirmation and immediate feedback can also help students build confidence in using ICT. This confidence can enhance students' sense of
control over digital devices, and they may be able to reduce inappropriate digital usage behaviors due to negative emotions. In
addition, it is also important for teachers to pass on skills related to ICT management, such as turning on do-not-disturb mode, tag
management, digital note management, etc. These implications are primarily targeted at lower-performing students. For those who
experience distraction but still maintain relatively strong performance, like students in Clu_4, different forms of support may be
needed. In addition to helping them solve the existing environmental problems objectively, it is also crucial to assist them in leveraging
their own strong cognitive resources and fostering their engagement in higher-level mathematics learning, so that these cognitive
resources can be fully utilized. For institutions and policymakers, the issue of inequality is the most crucial thing they need to consider.
Solving the accessibility of digital devices is insufficient, as we found that without effective guidance, access to ICT poses a risk of
increasing distraction. More resources should be allocated to the training of teachers' digital skills and the development of courses on
the use of technology. In addition to this, structural issues of social and economic inequality, as well as gender disparities in technology
usage, also need to be given special consideration in intervening measures related to distractions.
5.3.2. Implications for pedagogy research
This study offers several important implications for advancing research on digital distraction and academic performance. From a
methodological perspective, we adopted a person-centered approach to examine heterogeneity in students' susceptibility to digital
distraction and mathematics performance. The identification of distinct profiles reveals different patterns and associated characteristics across profiles. Accordingly, this approach is particularly suitable for uncovering latent and easily overlooked student populations. In terms of the theoretical framework, this study integrates prior research with Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) to examine
interactions among students' digital behavior and internal and external factors. The results, by confirming the principle of triadic
reciprocity, support the validity of this framework. This framework may serve as a useful reference for researchers seeking to
investigate students' digital distraction across different contextual settings. Finally, our study highlights the importance of considering
the multidimensional nature of the digital divide when examining digital distraction. The analysis shows that structural factors (e.g.,
gender, SES, and regional disparities) function as key determinants of second-level (usage) and third-level (outcome) digital divides.
Consequently, when researchers examine digital distraction in relation to students' self-regulation, it is essential to consider whether
students’ structural contexts genuinely provide opportunities for the development and cultivation of agency.
5.4. Limitations and future work
There are several limitations of this study that should be acknowledged. First, although the selected sample includes students from
30 countries and regions, the geographical coverage is uneven. Countries and regions with stronger representation are primarily
located in East Asia, Europe, Latin America, Eastern Europe, and the Middle East. Thus, some regions and cultural contexts remain
underrepresented. Future studies could extend this research to a broader range of education systems to improve generalizability.
Additionally, although the data were drawn from multiple countries, this study does not conduct systematic cross-national analyses or
cultural comparisons. Instead, we treated countries as contextual background factors. Future research could extend this work by
conducting within-country analyses. Second, we focus exclusively on mathematics performance, as mathematics was the major
domain assessed in PISA 2022. Digital distraction may operate differently across academic domains. Future research could examine
whether similar distraction-performance profiles emerge in reading, science, or across multiple subjects. Third, digital distraction was
measured using a single self-reported item. While this item has strong face validity and contextual relevance, it cannot capture the
multidimensional nature of distraction or distinguish between different sources (e.g., social media, academic multitasking). Future

research could consider multi-item measures or collect digital trace data to conduct an in-depth assessment. Finally, this study is based
on cross-sectional data, which precludes causal inference. Although the identified profiles reveal meaningful heterogeneity, they
represent snapshots rather than developmental trajectories. Longitudinal designs are needed to examine whether students transition
between profiles over time and to test the causal impact of targeted interventions on digital distraction and academic performance.
6. Conclusions
This study analyzes fifteen-year-old students from 239,695 participants across 30 countries in the PISA 2022 test. We reveal the
complex relationship between students’ susceptibility to digital distraction and academic performance, highlighting the roles of internal and external factors, as well as demographic characteristics. Specifically, we found four meaningful profiles. In addition to the
two profiles that reveal the traditional negative relationship between digital distraction and math performance, we notably identified a
resilient profile where students sustain strong academic performance despite unsupportive digital environments, and an invisible atrisk profile that experiences academic difficulties even in a relatively quiet environment. These four profiles provide empirical support
for the triadic reciprocity principle of SCT. They show that distraction-performance profiles reflect reciprocal interactions between
environmental resources (e.g., peer influence and classroom discipline) and personal resources (self-efficacy, digital habits, and
emotions). Within the SCT framework, we also propose that managing digital distraction is not merely a behavioral adjustment. It is a
manifestation of agency, enacted through self-regulation. Students in these four profiles differ in their ability to intentionally regulate
attention, learning strategies, and digital behaviors. Furthermore, we found that digital distraction reflects structural inequalities in
digital access and outcomes. It is not solely an individual shortcoming. Based on our findings, educators and policymakers should move
beyond uniform device restrictions. Interventions should focus on developing self-regulation and digital agency through profilespecific strategies. Future research could examine these profiles using longitudinal and cross-cultural designs to investigate how
digital learning environments can be structured to support diverse learners in technologized educational settings.
