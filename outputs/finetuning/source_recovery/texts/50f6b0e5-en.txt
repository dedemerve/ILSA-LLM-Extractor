Empowering schools’ improvement
Insights from PISA for Schools case studies

What the data tell us
• PISA for Schools is a school-level version of the Programme for International Student Assessment
(PISA), which measures how 15-year-olds perform in maths, reading and science. While PISA
enables countries to accurately compare education systems, PISA for Schools gives participating
schools confidential reports so they can benchmark their students’ performance against
comparable schools internationally.
• Around 40% of schools taking part in PISA for Schools have implemented targeted programmes
and interventions to support child well-being and improve equity, after their results identified areas
of concern.
• Around 30% of participating schools have taken deliberate steps to work with other schools and
to collaborate at the network, district and international levels to address challenges highlighted
in their results.
• Roughly 85% of participating schools say PISA for Schools acts as a real catalyst for change,
in areas such as curriculum design, pedagogical practices and professional development for
teachers.

For over two decades, the Programme for
International Student Assessment (PISA) has provided
valuable data to policymakers from governments
around the world, enabling them to benchmark their
education systems internationally and guide reforms
aimed at improving learning outcomes.
PISA for Schools, introduced in 2012, extends this
approach to the school level. It gives school leaders a
way to benchmark performance against international
standards, while offering practical insights into how
students learn, their motivation, and perceptions of
the school environment. Just as importantly, it puts
a spotlight on equity and inclusion by identifying
performance gaps across gender, socio-economic
background, and competency levels. It also explores
how students’ well-being, confidence, and resilience
influence academic outcomes and overall life
satisfaction.
So far, hundreds of schools from 18 countries have
taken part in PISA for Schools. Each receives an
individual, confidential report designed to prompt
reflection and discussion. These reports have served
as valuable tools for fostering professional dialogue

To better understand what participation leads to
in practice, PISA for Schools developed a Case
Studies Research Project in collaboration with
Oxford University. Drawing on survey responses and
multilingual in-depth interviews with school leaders
and teachers in participating countries, the project
examined how schools interpret their results and
use the data to inform decision-making and change,
based on priorities and resources. In parallel, the
research aimed to identify lessons from school-level
practices that could be translated into system-level
recommendations, supporting the scaling of effective,
data-informed approaches across education systems.
A total of 366 responses were collected across nine
countries, which voluntarily participated in the survey.
The data combined survey responses with multilingual
in-depth interviews. This analysis led to the
identification of four thematic categories that capture
the different ways schools use PISA for Schools data
and articulate the value of participation:

• Global benchmarking and comparison
• Data-driven insights for school improvement
• Student well-being and equity
• Collaboration and networking
These themes reflect why schools participate in PISA
for Schools and the practical ways they use the data.
Based on this analysis, ten case studies from six
countries were selected to illustrate how schools have
translated data into change (Ratner & Rusnak, 2025).
This edition of PISA in Focus highlights the diverse
ways schools have leveraged PISA for Schools data,
revealing common benefits and challenges across
contexts. By showing how schools move from
evidence to action, the case studies also demonstrate
how these lessons can inform policy and wider
system-level improvement.

What did we learn from the PISA
for Schools case studies?
Many education systems already draw on diverse
data sources, including PISA results, to strengthen
policy. PISA for Schools adds an important
dimension by showing how assessment data can be
meaningfully applied in real school contexts. In doing
so, it becomes not only a tool for local improvement,
but also a complementary source of evidence that
can enrich national policymaking and help bridge
the gap between system‑level expectations and
school‑level realities. Together, these experiences
offer guidance on how national systems can use
assessment results more effectively to shape smarter
policies and improve the overall quality of education.

This is where PISA for Schools can be most effective.
It offers internationally comparable data that allows
schools not only to benchmark their performance
against global standards, but also to reflect on
curriculum alignment, pedagogical practices, and
teacher professional development.
Evidence from case studies confirm the school-level
demand for this type of data: 90% of the schools
participating in the case-study research project
reported being drawn to PISA for Schools as a way
to better understand where they stand and to reflect
on the results. For many, the assessment served to
validate existing strategies; for others, it provided the
means to address performance gaps.
But the case studies also make one thing clear: data
alone is not sufficient to drive improvement. While
schools consistently valued the information provided
by PISA for Schools, many struggled to translate
results into concrete action without additional
support. Across contexts, data only became
actionable when schools were supported to interpret
it collectively and purposefully.

1. For reforms to be effective, schools
need credible data that informs
decision-making and supports targeted
interventions.
Data is at the heart of effective education reform,
but its impact depends on how well it is used. At the
international level, PISA has long been a source of
reliable evidence on student performance and equity,
helping governments identify effective strategies to
improve quality and fairness in education systems.
At the school level, leaders want the same thing as
policymakers: legitimate, credible data that helps
them make decisions. Used well, data can help

Education systems can play a critical role here by
creating the conditions for structured dialogue around
assessment data. When PISA for Schools results
are shared transparently and discussed collectively
- with school leaders, staff, teachers, parents, local
communities, and even students - data becomes
a trigger for coordinated, relevant action. The case
studies show that inclusive engagement strengthens
alignment, helps schools target the right needs, and
supports more sustained improvement.
Several examples illustrate this dynamic. In Case
Study 10, the school was excited about its PISA
for Schools results - but also surprised, particularly
due to their limited experience in analysing socioemotional skills data. The municipality stepped in
by providing training for school leadership, group
leaders, department coordinators, psychologists,
and social mediators. This support helped the school
build a plan focused on fostering personal, social
and community development, community well-being,
parental engagement and better use of students’
strengths.
Similarly in Case Study 2, a school engaged students
in interpreting well-being data. By positioning students
as active agents of change, the school enabled them
to lead initiatives such as organising a well-being day,
redesigning surveys, and integrating well-being into
the school’s core philosophy.
Lastly, Case Study 4 shows how a network of
18 schools conducted joint analysis sessions
with pedagogical teams, external experts and
school councils, including with parent and student
representatives. This collaborative process ensured
plans reflected collective insights and reinforced a
culture of evidence-based innovation.
Overall, these cases demonstrate that effective
school improvement can be achieved through
locally driven, evidence-informed practices.
PISA for Schools gives schools diagnostic tools
aligned with the PISA framework, enabling reflection
on curriculum design, pedagogical practices, and
equity. It also helps schools identify learning gaps,
prioritise resource management, and the monitoring
of progress over time, while fostering dialogue and
collaboration among key stakeholders.
By supporting the implementation of initiatives such
as PISA for Schools, policymakers enable schools
to access internationally benchmarked, school-

2. Schools need the capacity and
conditions to turn insights into
continuous improvement.
The case studies show that linking assessment results
to sustainable school development requires more than
data alone. Schools benefit most when assessment
insights come with constructive external feedback,
monitoring tools, and staff with the skills and time to
interpret and act on evidence.
Approximately 85% of schools participating in the
survey described PISA for Schools as a stimulus for
changes in curriculum design, pedagogical practices,
and professional development.
“The data does not tell us what to do, but it
opens a mirror. It makes us ask questions we
might not otherwise have asked.”
– School Deputy Director –
For instance, several schools used PISA for Schools
as a mechanism to redesign teaching and learning
practices in response to identified gaps - not only
to evaluate performance, but to drive meaningful
development. Case Study 5 shows how students
at a school performed well academically, but the
curriculum often lacked real-world relevance and
failed to sustain motivation, according to their PISA for
Schools results. In response, the school introduced
project-based learning, technology-enhanced
instruction, and interdisciplinary approaches that
connected classroom learning with contemporary
issues and student interests. It is a strong example of
how assessment results, used as constructive
feedback, can support meaningful curriculum
redesign and evidence‑based improvement.
Case Study 6 shows how international benchmarking
can act as an incentive for sustained, system-wide
improvement. After participating in PISA for Schools

in 2023, a group of schools used the results as
a baseline to launch a six-year plan focused on
strengthening mathematical reasoning, aligned with
the PISA 2022 framework.
Led by the local Department of Education, the
initiative plans a second PISA for Schools assessment
in 2026, when all students will have been taught
under the new methodology. This will enable the
region to track progress and evaluate the impact of
its reforms. The case demonstrates how PISA for
Schools goes beyond benchmarking to guide
strategic change over multiple years, fostering
evidence-based decision-making, replicable
innovation and alignment with PISA’s longterm objectives. It serves as both an external
evaluator and a tool for monitoring progress.
“Having our prestige endorsed by an external
entity has been invaluable.”
– Academic coordinator –
Professional development is another important lever
to drive school improvement. In today’s fast-evolving
educational landscape, continuous professional
learning is essential. TALIS 2024 data shows that
teachers who take part in targeted professional
development report higher job satisfaction and
motivation. This increased engagement translates into
better instructional practices and stronger teacherstudent relationships. It also supports more effective
use of innovative methodologies, ultimately improving
student learning outcomes and fostering a culture of
continuous improvement across the school.
PISA for Schools reinforces this by showing how
school-level data can be used to shape and improve
professional learning, encourage collaborative
reflection among teachers, and strengthen the use of
evidence in classroom practice.
Notably, professional development was central to
the transformation in Case Study 7. Using PISA for
Schools data, the school discovered that teacherstudent relationships were weaker than expected,
triggering a major change in priorities. To address this,
they launched a professional development strategy.
Teachers were trained in a character education
programme to strengthen their role as mentors and
role models. It also encouraged peer observation
and feedback - teachers began visiting each other’s
classrooms, inviting colleagues from other schools,
and even organising visits to learn from external

practices.
In Case Study 8, the school prioritised staff
professional development on tackling classroom
disruption and noise, which had been flagged in
the results. Within a year, 85% of the staff were
trained in a methodology emphasising collaboration,
communication, critical thinking, and creativity. This
approach not only improved students’ perceptions of
classroom disruption but also enhanced their overall
learning experience, which was the goal.
These efforts show that assessment should
not be treated as “just data”. Used well, it
can be a catalyst for building a culture of
openness and continuous learning. Support for
targeted professional development and school
improvement is essential to make this possible.

3. To maximise impact, schools
need environments that foster trust,
collaboration and engagement.
Nearly 30 % of schools in the case-study research
project reported enhanced collaboration due
to participating in PISA for Schools. This aligns
with other OECD evidence on school-to-school
collaboration. For instance, the OECD’s Schools+
Network brings together over 150 schools from
40 countries, forming a “network of networks” to
share practices, co-create solutions, and foster
innovation. This demonstrates that school systems
are increasingly valuing structured, cross-school
partnerships to advance teaching, learning, and
policy.
The OECD’s Education Policy Perspectives report
also shows that school partnerships can address
both well-being and digital learning, combining
expertise from schools, civil society organisations,
and community actors to co-develop interventions
that would be difficult to scale otherwise.
Case Study 3 provides a good example of this,
where a public school shared its results with its
school council, including parents and community
members. That openness sparked strong community
engagement: alumni returned as volunteer tutors,
and universities partnered with the school to offer
teacher training and student courses in areas like AI
and computational science, all aimed at addressing
performance gaps.

In Case Study 9, the PISA for Schools results in both
cognitive and socio-emotional domains helped the
school advance initiatives that promote collaboration,
engagement, and well-being within the school
community. As part of a school network, teachers
developed lesson plans for the network, enhancing
their knowledge in functional literacy and fostering
collaboration.
Additionally, a teacher introduced the “Map of
Empathy” to identify students who were struggling
emotionally. This involved class teachers, parents,
the school psychologist, and external experts
collaborating to design strategies that help students
calm down, engage more effectively, and create a
better learning environment. In parallel, the Conscious
Parent Project encouraged parents to become more
involved in school life by leading sessions where they
shared their professional and personal interests.
These cases highlight that collaboration is
not just about teacher-to-teacher exchange,
it is also about building school networks and
multi-stakeholder ecosystems. Shared data helps
create shared ownership. PISA for Schools provides
a common, credible evidence base that can bring
people together and turn assessment results into a
foundation for sustained, collective improvement.

Conclusions
The PISA for Schools case studies show how
comparative data can empower schools to drive
meaningful, lasting improvement. Across diverse
contexts, the evidence is consistent: when schools
gain access to legitimate and comparable data - and
the time and support to make sense of it together
- assessment becomes more than a measurement
exercise. It becomes a stimulus for reflection,
innovation, and real change.
The experiences of participating schools highlight
three interrelated lessons. First, data-informed
decision making is essential. Schools that
successfully turned results into action treated data
as common ground for discussion, planning, and
pedagogical redesign. PISA for Schools provides
diagnostic insights that enable leaders and teachers
to identify gaps, validate existing strategies, and
prioritise initiatives in curriculum, teaching practices,
and student support.

Second, sustained improvement requires an
ecosystem of support. Access to high-quality
data alone is insufficient. Schools benefit most when
assessment insights are combined with external
validation, reliable monitoring tools, and meaningful
professional development opportunities. The case
studies demonstrate that targeted professional
learning, peer collaboration, and structured feedback
can improve instructional practices, teacher-student
relationships, and curriculum design. Continuous
professional development ensures that advances are
maintained over time and embedded into the school’s
culture of learning.
Third, school improvement takes place in
environments characterised by trust and
collaboration. Networks of teachers, parents,
students, community partners, and local authorities
played a vital role in turning insights into targeted
interventions, particularly in areas related to equity
and student well-being. Collaborative processes
foster an understanding of challenges, encourage
co-creation of solutions, and amplify the impact of
school-level initiatives.
These findings have implications for school leaders,
who are responsible for turning data into action
in their own contexts. The insights from the case
studies show how leaders can use evidence to drive
continuous improvement and ensure that national
priorities translate into real change in classrooms.
At the same time, these findings offer valuable
lessons for policymakers and system leaders. Schoollevel data can provide a unique perspective into
how national policies are experienced in practice.
It helps reveal where reforms are working, where
resources are insufficient, and where inequities
persist. By examining patterns across hundreds of
individual school contexts, policymakers can design
more responsive strategies, improve curriculum
and assessment frameworks, strengthen teacher
development systems, and target support to the
schools and student groups who need it most.
“A student who leaves here returns as a
teacher, as a volunteer, as a member… They
help us continue the pursuit of quality.”
– Principal –

Overall, the case studies underline the value of
providing schools with high-quality, actionable data.
But schools also need enough autonomy to design
context-oriented responses, the leadership capacity
to engage multiple stakeholders, and the professional
development conditions that allow teachers, leaders
and support staff to act on evidence. At the same
time, autonomy must be balanced with clear
expectations and accountability mechanisms to
ensure equity, coherence, and trust.
Ultimately, the case studies demonstrate that real
transformations begin when schools move beyond
asking “How did we perform?” and instead ask “What
does this tell us about how we can improve?”. By
supporting schools through this process, education
systems can foster durable cultures of evidencebased practice that benefit learners and strengthen
system-level reform at the same time.

The bottom line
PISA for Schools shows that meaningful school improvement happens when high-quality data reaches
schools, is interpreted collectively, and is supported by professional development and autonomy. When
scaled, these insights reveal system-wide patterns that can help policymakers refine national strategies,
target resources, and align reforms with the realities of school life.
