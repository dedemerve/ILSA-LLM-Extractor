A B S T R A C T
This study summarizes the predictive effects of individual-level information and communication 
technology (ICT) and country-level factors on students’ learning outcomes, including cognitive, 
affective, and behavioral domains. Moreover, we synthesized how country-level factors shape the 
predictive effects of student-level ICT factors on learning outcomes. We systematically reviewed 
empirical studies that conducted secondary analyses of the Programme for International Student 
Assessment (PISA) data incorporating country-level factors into their analyses. The analysis 
showed that the most frequently studied topics in PISA-derived ICT research were student-level 
ICT attitude and ICT use, followed by country-level factors directly related to ICT. The associa­
tion between student-level ICT adoption and academic achievement varied depending on the 
educational setting, such as academic domains and instructional designs. Meanwhile, student 
attitude was consistently positively associated with learning outcomes. Country-level factors, 
such as national ICT access in education and human capital indicators, were generally positively 
associated with learning outcomes. Conversely, national ICT infrastructure and economic in­
dicators showed mixed or negative results. Furthermore, country-level factors moderated the 
relationship between student-level ICT factors and learning outcomes, exhibiting facilitating, 
buffering, diminishing, or exacerbating effects. This study highlights that the educational effect of 
ICT is highly context-dependent, meaning it requires pedagogically aligned integration within 
learning contexts and institutional support at the broader institutional and national contexts to 
ensure effective learning outcomes.
1. Introduction
As information and communication technology (ICT) has become integral to modern life, ICT literacy, the ability to utilize ICT, has 
emerged as a critical skill. Many countries have integrated ICT into education to foster students’ digital competence (OECD, 2016). 
Consequently, research interest in ICT’s impact on learning outcomes has grown (Hopfenbeck et al., 2018). Reflecting this, the Pro­
gramme for International Student Assessment (PISA), which significantly influences educational policy (Domínguez et al., 2012), has 
consistently included ICT-related assessments.
ICT’s effect on students’ learning outcomes has been widely expected, with research indicating inconsistent effects depending on 
context. For example, Scherer and Siddiq (2019) conducted a meta-analysis and found gender differences in ICT literacy, suggesting 
individual background matters. Additionally, Petko et al. (2017) analyzed cross-sectional data across 39 countries and reported

To better understand how ICT is associated with learning outcomes, this review systematically analyzed PISA-based studies. Many 
studies have shown that student-level ICT factors, including access, use, and attitude, predict students’ learning outcomes (Guzeller & 
Akin, 2014; Wang et al., 2023). Going beyond Odell et al. (2020), who focused on student-level ICT factors and academic outcomes in 
mathematics and science, this study expands the scope to include both student- and country-level factors across cognitive, affective, 
and behavioral outcomes. Several studies showed that national contexts also explained a substantial proportion of the variance in 
student outcomes (e.g., Deng & Sun, 2022; Loh et al., 2023; Park & Weng, 2020). By applying ecological systems theory, this study 
examines how national contexts not only predict student learning outcomes directly but also shape the relationship between 
student-level ICT factors and achievement. This study also identifies gaps in domains that remain underexplored, particularly affective 
and behavioral outcomes. For policymakers, the findings guide on when and in what contexts ICT use tends to be educationally 
effective.
2. Literature review
While PISA-based research on ICT in education has grown rapidly, findings remain inconsistent. In particular, the role of country- 
level factors and their interaction with student-level ICT factors have received limited attention. This review addresses this gap by 
adopting an ecological systems perspective. The literature is organized into four areas: ICT in PISA, the application of ecological 
systems theory, national contexts, and the synergistic effects between individual and contextual factors.
2.1. ICT in the PISA
The PISA, conducted by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) since 2000, has shaped global 
education policy by assessing the competencies of 15-year-olds in reading, mathematics, and science every three years (Domínguez 
et al., 2012; Owens, 2013). The PISA also offers optional instruments, such as collaborative problem-solving (CPS) and ICT familiarity 
questionnaires, the latter of which evaluates ICT access, use, and attitude (OECD, 2017; 2019). This questionnaire, which is revised 
regularly to reflect technological changes, was expanded in 2015. Appendix A outlines the evolution of ICT-related variables from 
2000 to 2022.
A growing body of research has utilized PISA data to investigate the relationship between students’ ICT access, use, and attitude, as 
well as their learning outcomes (Hopfenbeck et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2024). However, findings across studies remain inconsistent. In 
research on ICT in education, the associations between ICT-related variables and learning outcomes are commonly examined in terms 
of three key dimensions: access, use, and attitude.
ICT Access. ICT access refers to the availability of ICT resources and the Internet connectivity. While many countries now provide a 
basic ICT infrastructure in schools (OECD, 2020a), access alone does not guarantee educational benefits. It is a necessary but insuf­
ficient condition for effective ICT-based learning, as outcomes depend on environmental background. Studies using PISA data showed 
mixed findings. Positive associations emerged when access was accompanied by well-organized implementation in schools (Bozkus, 
2021; Jeong et al., 2024). However, other studies reported non-significant or negative effects, particularly when access was un­
structured at home (Gui et al., 2014; Kong et al., 2022; Tan & Hew, 2019).
ICT Use. ICT use refers to students’ engagement with ICT devices and resources. Its relationship with learning outcomes is highly 
context-dependent, varying by the purposes and extent of ICT usage. Not all ICT use is educationally beneficial, and its impact depends 
on how it is integrated into the learning process. For example, ICT use at school for leisure was negatively linked to reading perfor­
mance, whereas ICT use for digital reading showed positive associations (Kong et al., 2022; Sanfo, 2023). Moreover, the relationship 
tended to follow a non-linear pattern. PISA data from 2009 to 2018 indicated that moderate ICT use was correlated with the highest 
level of achievement, while excessive or minimal use led to lower outcomes (Borgonovi & Pokropek, 2021; Gubbels et al., 2020).
ICT Attitude. ICT attitude refers to a student’s perception of their own ability to use ICT, such as their competence and interest, 
along with their view on ICT related to social interactions. ICT attitude has been linked to learning outcomes in complex ways. Positive 
ICT self-efficacy was generally linked to academic success (Petko et al., 2017), but the extent and nature of students’ interest also 
matter. Excessive ICT interest was associated with lower outcomes, while moderate interest supported better reading performance 
(Gubbels et al., 2020). Meanwhile, using ICT mainly for social interaction showed negative links with learning. Frequent chatting or 
social communication through ICT correlated with lower academic performance (Goh et al., 2024; Kunina-Habenicht & Goldhammer, 
2020; Luyten, 2024).
2.2. Ecological systems theory
Ecological systems theory explains how environmental factors shape an individual’s learning through five nested, interacting 
systems: the microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, macrosystem, and chronosystem (Bronfenbrenner, 2000). The microsystem is the 
most immediate environment, involving direct interaction with family, friends and school. The mesosystem is the interaction between 
different microsystems, such as parent-teacher communication. The exosystem is the external environment influencing indirectly, such 
as a local government and mass media. The macrosystem includes broader cultural and policy contexts, such as political ideologies, 
and socioeconomic conditions. Finally, the chronosystem refers to the dimension of time, encompassing both individual life changes

With the growing integration of ICT into education, traditional models have struggled to account for digital learning. To bridge this 
gap, Johnson and Puplampu (2008) proposed the ecological techno-subsystem, addressing students’ ICT-based learning. For instance, 
student-level ICT factors such as ICT attitude, representing the microsystem, directly affect learning outcomes. While national policies 
and economic conditions, representing the macrosystem, shape individuals’ ICT access and use. Recent studies have also adopted this 
framework to investigate the predictive effects of country-level factors on students’ learning outcomes (e.g., Berger, 2019; Erdogdu & 
Erdogdu, 2025).
2.3. Country-level factors
Building on ecological systems theory, national contexts play a critical role in shaping how ICT contributes to student learning. 
Country-level factors can be grouped into two categories depending on whether they are directly (e.g., ICT infrastructure and 
governance) or indirectly related to ICT (e.g., economic and human capital indicators).
ICT infrastructure is often measured by the ICT Development Index (IDI), which reflects national levels of access, use, and digital 
skills (International Telecommunication Union, 2023). Higher IDI scores are associated with improved digital literacy and better 
economic outcomes (Gerpott & Ahmadi, 2015; Wilson et al., 2014). ICT governance refers to how governments promote digital 
integration through national strategies and policy frameworks. For instance, international initiatives, such as the OECD’s Going Digital 
provide guidelines for the effective use of ICT in education (OECD, 2020b), which many national governments adapt into their own 
educational policies. However, outdated or misaligned ICT policies may limit its effectiveness, emphasizing the importance of national 
ICT governance in shaping learning outcomes.
Economic indicators commonly include national wealth and measures of inequality (e.g., the Gini Index), both of which influence 
educational outcomes. While gross domestic product (GDP) per capita is often linked to learning outcomes, income inequality can also 
limit students’ access to ICT and its benefits (Cullen, 2003; Fuchs, 2009; McCreary et al., 2015). Meanwhile, human capital indicators, 
such as years of schooling and the Human Development Index (HDI), reflect a country’s educational and cultural foundation. These 
factors influence not only ICT access but also how effectively students engage with it while learning (Gimenez & Vargas-Montoya, 
2021).
2.4. Synergistic effects of country- and student-level factors on learning outcomes
Ecological systems theory highlights that individual learning is shaped by the dynamic interactions among multiple systems 
(Bronfenbrenner, 2000). This implies that the effects of student-level ICT factors, such as access, use, and attitude, can vary depending 
on national contexts. Moderation occurs when the impact of a predictor (e.g., ICT use) on an outcome (e.g., achievement) changes 
depending on a third variable (e.g., national ICT infrastructure). In this way, contextual effects may amplify, weaken, or even 
neutralize the role of ICT-related factors. For example, Tucker-Drob et al. (2014) found that scientific interest predicted achievement 
more strongly in wealthier countries than in poorer countries. Similarly, Chiu (2007) found that in countries with a high GDP, having 
more siblings had a stronger negative effect on science scores than in countries with a low GDP.
2.5. The present study
This systematic review synthesizes the relationships between student- and country-level ICT factors and student learning outcomes 
using PISA data. Furthermore, we synthesize how country-level factors shaped these relationships. Beyond providing descriptive 
characteristics, we also identify overarching research themes and explore the synergistic effects of national contexts and students’ ICT 
factors on learning outcomes. This study addresses the following research questions: 
• RQ1: What are the main research themes in studies examining the relationships between ICT-related factors and learning outcomes 
in studies using PISA data?
• RQ2: How are student-level ICT factors associated with learning outcomes?
• RQ3: How are country-level factors associated with learning outcomes?
• RQ4: How do country-level factors moderate the relationship between student-level ICT factors and learning outcomes?
3. Method
3.1. Literature search and screening process
A comprehensive literature search was conducted using the Web of Science database, a well-established platform for educational 
research, focusing on journals indexed in the Social Sciences Citation Index (SSCI) and the Emerging Sources Citation Index (ESCI). The 
literature search was conducted in four separate phases. The first and second searches, performed on September 6 and November 5, 
2024, targeted SSCI-indexed articles using the terms “PISA” or “Programme for International Student Assessment” and “ICT” or “Infor­
mation and Communication Technology.” The third search, conducted on January 23, 2025, also included ESCI-indexed journals and 
employed the same search terms used in the prior phases. A final SSCI search on the same day included additional terms such as 
“computer,” “digital,” “Internet,” and “media” to increase the literature search’s coverage.

Inclusion criteria were adapted from prior reviews based on international large-scale assessments (e.g., Myoung & Liou, 2022; 
Veleti´c et al., 2024) to ensure the credibility of our findings. We included peer-reviewed, English-language studies using PISA data, 
with both student- and country-level variables analyzed in the same statistical model. Specifically, we defined country-level variables 
as those representing contextual characteristics, such as national economic indices or country classifications (e.g., OECD membership). 
Studies were included if they incorporated these contextual variables either through multilevel modeling (two- or three-level analyses) 
or by explicitly including country-level indicators in single-level analyses. Studies that analyzed countries separately without including 
country-level factors were excluded. Only full-text articles were retained. Screening followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Sys­
tematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) standards (Page et al., 2021). After removing duplicates, titles, abstracts, and full texts 
were reviewed using Microsoft Excel. The first author conducted the initial selection, and the second author verified the eligibility of 
each study selected by the first author. Final inclusion was based on agreement, yielding 29 studies. Fig. 1 shows the search and 
screening process.
3.2. Coding procedure and data extraction
To synthesize findings, we created a coding sheet based on a prior systematic review (Veleti´c et al., 2024), which was refined 
through weekly meetings from June to July 2024. The final sheet had two categories: general article information and relationships 
between variables. The two authors independently coded the 29 studies. In cases of disagreement, they discussed the differences and 
reached a consensus. Appendix B presents the full coding sheet.
General article information covered authors, year, journal, first author’s country affiliation, PISA cycle, data source (PISA only or 
combined), number of countries, sample justification, questionnaire type, overarching research themes, and student learning outcomes 
(cognitive, affective, behavioral domains). Table 1 summarizes the overarching themes by individual, country, and other factors.
The second category covered the predictive effects of independent variables (student- and country-level factors) and the moder­
ation effects of country-level factors on the relationships between students-level factors and learning outcomes. Table 2 summarizes 
the types and nature of these relationships.

The results are presented in two sections, including general article characteristics and the relationships between variables. The first 
section covers publication year, PISA cycle, journal distribution, author’s country affiliations, questionnaire types, research themes, 
and learning outcomes. The second section outlines the associations between student- and country-level factors, and moderation ef­

4.2. Predictive effects of student-level factors on learning outcomes
4.2.1. ICT access
ICT Access at Home. ICT access at home was found to have a negative relationship with most learning outcomes in PISA cycles 
from 2009 to 2018 (Courtney et al., 2022; Erdogdu & Erdogdu, 2023; Hu et al., 2018; Lee & Wu, 2012). However, PISA 2018 data 
revealed an inverted U-shape relationship with reading achievement (Peng et al., 2023), while another study reported an inconclusive 
relationship with reading and mathematics achievement, but a positive association with science outcomes (Erdogdu, 2022a). For 
behavioral outcomes, engagement in online reading activies was positively associated with ICT access at home (Lee & Wu, 2012).
ICT Access at School. Earlier studies found no significant associations for reading achievement (Hu et al., 2018; Lee & Wu, 2012). 
However, a negative relationship emerged in PISA 2018 (Vargas-Montoya et al., 2023), with one study identifying an inverted 
U-shaped pattern (Peng et al., 2023). The outcomes were consistently negative in mathematics and science achievement (Courtney 
et al., 2022; Vargas-Montoya et al., 2023), while one study found non-significant associations (Erdogdu, 2022a). The association 
between affective outcomes and ICT interest was also negative (Erdogdu & Erdogdu, 2023). The association was non-significant for the 
behavioral outcome of online reading activities (Lee & Wu, 2012).
4.2.2. ICT use
ICT Use at Home for Leisure Purposes. Findings generally showed a positive relationship for reading achievement (Hu et al., 
2018; Hu & Yu, 2021; Skryabin et al., 2015). However, in PISA 2018, an inverted U-shape relationship was observed (Peng, 2023), 
while another study found inconclusive results (Jeong et al., 2024). For mathematics and science achievement, results indicated 
predominantly negative associations (Courtney et al., 2022; Skryabin et al., 2015), while some studies reported inconclusive (Jeong 
et al., 2024) or non-significant associations (Courtney et al., 2022; Erdogdu, 2022b; Hu et al., 2018; Skryabin et al., 2015). 
Furthermore, studies examining combined academic achievement across subjects found a negative relationship (Park & Weng, 2020), 
whereas research on CPS achievement provided inconclusive results (Wang et al., 2023). Erdogdu and Erdogdu (2023) found a positive 
association between affective learning outcomes and ICT interest in PISA 2018.

4.2.3. ICT attitude
The reviewed studies explored multiple aspects of ICT attitudes, including self-confidence with ICT, ICT interest, ICT competence, 
ICT autonomy, and ICT’s role in social interaction. ICT interest, competence, autonomy, and ICT as a topic in social interaction were 
first introduced in the 2015 PISA cycle. This section examines how these factors relate to cognitive, affective, and behavioral learning 
outcomes.
Self-confidence with ICT. The reviewed studies examined two aspects of self-confidence in ICT use, including general internet use 
and high-level programming tasks. Both were predominantly associated with positive learning outcomes. Self-confidence in general 
internet use was examined in PISA 2003 and 2006, with findings indicating positive relationships with mathematics and science 
achievement (Zhang & Liu, 2016). Self-confidence in high-level programming tasks, examined from PISA 2003 to 2009, also showed 
generally positive associations with cognitive outcomes (Courtney et al., 2022; Lee & Wu, 2012; Zhang & Liu, 2016). However, one 
study found a negative relationship between science achievement (Zhang & Liu, 2016). Additionally, Lee and Wu (2012) found a 
positive association with engagement in online reading.
ICT Interest. In PISA 2015, ICT interest was consistently positively associated with reading, mathematics, and science achievement 
(Courtney et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2018; Park & Weng, 2020). However, the findings were more complex for PISA 2018. While positive 
associations continued to dominate (Courtney et al., 2022; Fayda-Kinik & Cetin, 2025; Hu et al., 2023), several studies reported mixed 
or inconclusive results (Fayda-Kinik & Cetin, 2025; Ma & Qin, 2021). One study identified an inverted U-shape relationship with 
reading achievement (Peng et al., 2023).
ICT Competence. In PISA 2015, all reviewed studies reported positive relationships across reading, mathematics, and science 
achievement (Courtney et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2018; Park & Weng, 2020). In PISA 2018, while positive associations remained dominant 
(Courtney et al., 2022; Fayda-Kinik & Cetin, 2025; Jeong et al., 2024; Loh et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2023; Xiao & Hew, 2022), some 
studies reported inconclusive results (Fayda-Kinik & Cetin, 2025; Ma & Qin, 2021). Regarding affective outcomes, ICT competence was 
also positively associated with ICT interest (Erdogdu & Erdogdu, 2023) and various science-related attitudes, including enjoyment of 
science, interest in broad science topics, science self-efficacy, and epistemological beliefs (Areepattamannil & Santos, 2019).
ICT Autonomy. ICT autonomy was consistently found to have positive associations across all examined learning outcomes. In 
terms of cognitive outcomes, ICT autonomy was positively associated with reading, mathematics, and science achievement in all 
reviewed studies that examined this variable (Courtney et al., 2022; Fayda-Kinik & Cetin, 2025; Hu et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2023; Ma & 
Qin, 2021; Park & Weng, 2020; Peng et al., 2023; Xiao & Hew, 2022). Regarding affective outcomes, students who perceived greater 
autonomy in ICT use exhibited higher ICT interest (Erdogdu & Erdogdu, 2023) and more positive affective dispositions toward science 
(Areepattamannil & Santos, 2019).
ICT in Social Interaction. This category encompasses students’ attitudes toward ICT-based social media, the perceived value of 
social media, and ICT as a topic in social interactions. Studies on ICT-based social media attitudes showed positive associations with 
reading achievement (Hu & Yu, 2021), while the perceived value of social media was positively related to CPS achievement (Wang 
et al., 2023). In contrast, ICT as a topic in social interaction was consistently negatively associated with cognitive outcomes (Courtney 
et al., 2022; Fayda-Kinik & Cetin, 2025; Hu et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2023; Ma & Qin, 2021; Peng et al., 2023), except in one study that 
reported a positive association with reading achievement (Xiao & Hew, 2022). Regarding affective outcomes, a positive association 
was observed with ICT interest (Erdogdu & Erdogdu, 2023).
4.3. Predictive effects of country-level factors on learning outcomes
Country-level factors represent a broad environmental context associated with students’ learning outcomes, including ICT infra­
structure, ICT governance, and economic and human capital indicators. In our sample, many studies reported intraclass correlation 
coefficients (ICCs), showing that national contexts explained a substantial portion of the variance in students’ learning outcomes. The 
ICC represents the proportion of total variance in an outcome attributable to differences between countries, with higher values 
indicating stronger influences of country-level factors. Among the 29 studies included in our review, 26 employed multilevel analyses, 
and 17 of them reported ICCs for learning outcomes. The specific ICC values reported in these studies are summarized in Table C3 in 
Appendix. For instance, the cognitive outcomes, such as reading (ICC = 7.9 %–52.5 %) (Hu & Wang, 2022; Hu & Yu, 2021) and 
mathematics (ICC = 16.1 %–35.0 %) (Park & Weng, 2020; Skryabin et al., 2015) showed relatively high variance explained at the 
country level. However, affective outcomes (e.g., science-related attitudes: ICC = 3.0 %–9.0 %; ICT competence: ICC = 3.5 %) 
(Areepattamannil & Santos, 2019; Ma, 2021) and behavioral outcomes (e.g., ICT use: ICC = 7.2 %–9.8 %; digital skills: ICC = 4.4 %– 
7.7 %) (Ma, 2021; Zhong, 2011) showed smaller but meaningful contribution of national contexts.
4.3.1. ICT infrastructure
The ICT Development Index (IDI). The IDI was positively associated with reading and science achievement (Deng & Sun, 2022; 
Jeong et al., 2024; Skryabin et al., 2015), but was negatively associated with mathematics in one study (Jeong et al., 2024). Regarding 
affective outcomes, the IDI had an inverted U-shaped relationship with ICT competence (Jeong et al., 2024). In terms of behavioral 
outcomes, ICT use for academic purposes had a negative association, whereas ICT use for entertainment showed an inverted U-shaped 
pattern (Jeong et al., 2024).
The Penetration Rate of ICT. The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) measures the penetration rate of ICT to collect 
data on the percentage of households that have access to telecommunications. It showed no significant results for adolescents’ digital 
skills in PISA 2003. However, a negative association emerged when the ITU was examined using PISA 2006 and a combined dataset of 
2003 and 2006 (Zhong, 2011).

4.3.2. ICT governance
National ICT Access in Education. National ICT access in education refers to the extent to which digital resources are integrated 
into a country’s educational system. Loh et al. (2023) found that greater ICT access in national education was positively associated 
with mathematics achievement.
Government ICT Promotion. Government ICT promotion indicates how each country’s experts perceive how the national gov­
ernment supports ICT development. Loh et al. (2023) found that government ICT promotion showed no significant relationship with 
mathematics achievement.
4.3.3. Economic indicators
Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita. GDP per capita was the most frequently used economic indicator across the reviewed 
studies. Several studies reported positive associations between GDP and student cognitive outcomes in reading, mathematics, and 
science (Bhutoria & Aljabri, 2022; Courtney et al., 2022; Deng & Sun, 2022; Gimenez & Vargas-Montoya, 2021; Hu & Yu, 2021; Ma & 
Qin, 2021; Wang et al., 2023; Xiao & Hew, 2022). However, some studies found no significant relationships across various learning 
outcome types (Courtney et al., 2022; Drabowicz, 2014; Hu & Wang, 2022; Hu & Yu, 2021; Ma & Qin, 2021; Park & Weng, 2020; Peng 
et al., 2023; Skryabin et al., 2015; Zhang & Liu, 2016).
Gini Index. The Gini coefficient, an indicator of income inequality, was examined in relation to various learning outcomes. Park 
and Weng (2020) found a negative association between the Gini Index and overall academic achievement across subjects. Meanwhile, 
Ma (2021) reported no significant relationship between income inequality and affective and behavioral outcomes, including ICT 
competence and ICT use for academic purposes, both at home and at school.
4.3.4. Human capital indicators
Human capital indicators include the average years of schooling and the Human Development Index (HDI), which measures a 
country’s investment in education and overall human development. The findings consistently showed positive relationships between 
these indicators and cognitive learning outcomes across reading, mathematics, and science. Vargas-Montoya et al. (2023, 2024) re­
ported positive associations with the HDI. Similarly, Gimenez and Vargas-Montoya (2021) found positive associations with years of 
schooling, suggesting that countries with longer average schooling tend to reveal higher levels of student academic achievement.
4.4. Synergistic effects of the country-level moderator
Eight studies examined the moderating effects of country-level moderators on the relationship between student-level ICT factors 
and learning outcomes. These studies identified five distinctive types of moderation effects: facilitating, diminishing, buffering, 
exacerbating, and minimal effects. Facilitating effects refer to situations when the moderator strengthens a positive relationship. 
Diminishing effects indicate that the moderator weakens or reverses a positive relationship. Buffering effects indicate that the 
moderator reduces the strength of a negative relationship, functioning as a buffer. Exacerbating effects relate to cases when a negative 
relationship becomes even more negative due to the influence of the moderator. Lastly, minimal effects are relevant in situations when 
the moderator has little to no impact on the relationship. Fig. 5 illustrates four types of moderation effects (minimal effects are not 
included). Table 4 overviews the country-level moderators and their moderation effects on the relationship between student-level ICT 
factors and learning outcomes.

4.4.1. Facilitating effects
Three studies reported facilitating moderation effects by which country-level factors enhanced the positive association between 
student-level ICT variables and learning outcomes. Arpacı et al. (2021) found that in Latin American countries, higher levels of IDI 
strengthened the positive links between student-level ICT access, use, and attitudes and science achievement. Similarly, Deng and Sun 
(2022) observed that in countries with higher IDI scores, the relationship between students’ ICT access and competence and their 
reading achievement was stronger than in countries with lower IDI scores. Likewise, Loh et al. (2023) reported that in countries with 
greater national ICT access in education, the positive association between ICT autonomy and mathematics performance was 
strengthened compared to countries with low ICT access.
4.4.2. Diminishing effects
Four studies identified diminishing effects by which country-level moderators reduced or reversed the positive association between 
student-level ICT factors and learning outcomes. Arpacı et al. (2021) found that in Europe, higher IDI weakened the positive effects of 
ICT access and use on science achievement. Similarly, in the Asia-Pacific region, a high IDI reduced the predictive effect of ICT at­
titudes on science achievement. Bhutoria and Aljabri (2022) found that the moderation effect of IDI levels was characterized by an 
inverted U-shaped pattern. It suggests that the association between ICT use and academic achievement in reading, mathematics, and 
science peaked at moderate IDI levels but declined in both low- and high-IDI contexts. Zhong (2011) employed PISA 2003 data and 
found that high national ICT penetration weakened the positive effect of home ICT access on students’ digital skills. Furthermore, Park 
& Weng, 2020 reported that high economic inequality (i.e., high Gini Index scores) weakened the positive association between ICT 
autonomy and overall academic achievement.

4.4.3. Buffering effects
Buffering effects were found in three studies, indicating that higher country-level moderators buffered the negative predictive 
effect of ICT factors on learning outcomes. Vargas-Montoya et al. (2023) reported that in countries with high GNI per capita and HDI 
(representing greater economic capacity and stronger social development), the negative relationships between subject-related ICT use 
during lessons and achievement in reading, mathematics, and science were reduced. Similarly, Park and Weng (2020) found that in 
countries with high GDP per capita and low income inequality, the negative effects of ICT use at school for academic purposes and at 
home for leisure purposes on students’ overall achievement were reduced. Gimenez and Vargas-Montoya (2021) found that in 
countries with a high average education level (which reflects a well-educated population), the negative association between 
subject-related ICT use and academic achievement was mitigated or even reversed.
4.4.4. Exacerbating effects
One study identified an exacerbating effect, whereby a country-level factor made the negative association between ICT use and 
achievement even stronger. Park and Weng (2020) found that in countries with a high GDP per capita, the negative association be­
tween ICT use at home for leisure purposes and overall achievement became stronger.
4.4.5. Minimal effects
Four studies reported minimal effects, indicating that country-level moderators had no significant influence on the relationship 
between student-level ICT factors and learning outcomes. Arpacı et al. (2021) reported that IDI did not significantly influence students’ 
ICT attitudes concerning science achievement in Europe. Similarly, in Asia and the Pacific, IDI had minimal effects on the associations 
of ICT access and use with science achievement. Zhong (2011) found no significant moderating effect of national ICT penetration on 
the relationship between home ICT access and digital skills in the PISA 2006 dataset or the combined datasets of PISA 2003 and 2006. 
Similarly, Loh et al. (2023) found no moderating effect of government ICT promotion on the association between ICT autonomy and 
mathematics achievement. Further, Park and Weng (2020) found no significant results when examining several moderation effects of 
GDP and the Gini Index.
5. Discussion
This study systematically reviewed the relationships of student- and country-level factors with learning outcomes in PISA-based 
studies. Our analysis revealed that most studies examined cognitive learning outcomes, with limited research on affective and 
behavioral outcomes. Therefore, findings on affective and behavioral outcomes should be interpreted more cautiously. The findings 
suggest that, among the main research themes, individual focus, particularly ICT attitude, has reveived greater attention, whereas 
country focus has remained comparatively underexplored. ICT adoption showed mixed associations with learning outcomes, while ICT 
attitude exhibited generally positive associations. In addition to student-level factors, country-level factors were found to be significant 
predictors of learning outcomes. Several studies also found that country-level factors moderate the relationship between student-level 
ICT factors and learning outcomes. The following sections discuss the findings across different ICT factors, including ICT adoption, 
attitude, national contexts, and moderation effects for implications for educational policy.
5.1. Varied relationships between ICT adoption and academic achievement
The associations between ICT adoption, including access and use, and academic achievement were inconsistent across the reviewed 
studies. These inconsistencies cannot be explained by ICT availability or frequency of ICT use alone. Instead, they may stem from 
environmental and instructional factors, including the purpose of ICT use, teachers’ instructional capacity, the academic subject, 
students’ achievement level, and how well ICT is aligned with pedagogical strategies.
Our review examined ICT adoption in both home and school contexts, with varied patterns observed. While ICT use at home 
showed mixed associations depending on purposes, we focus primarily on school-based findings in this discussion section as they 
represent more direct targets for educational policy implications. Regarding school-based ICT adoption, our findings revealed that ICT 
access at school was generally negatively associated with achievement, suggesting that increased access alone does not improve 
learning outcomes. One reason may be usage purpose. For example, Erdogdu and Erdogdu (2015) analyzed large-scale Turkish data 
from 15-year-old students and found a positive association between ICT access at school and academic performance. However, they 
also noted that frequent non-academic use of ICT in schools potentially reduces its educational benefits. Another contributing factor is 
teachers’ capacity to integrate ICT meaningfully into their lessons. Namome and Moodley (2021) examined four African countries with 
Grade 8 and 9 students and found no significant association between ICT access at school and mathematics performance. They argued 
that teachers need to possess digital skills and pedagogical insight to utilize ICT effectively.
In terms of ICT use, our review found that ICT use in classroom lessons was predominantly associated with negative academic 
outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of specific settings where, for whom, and how ICT was used. Prior research supports 
this view. For instance, Carrillo et al. (2011), using a randomized trial in Ecuador, found that ICT improved mathematics but not 
language performance, attributing success to subject-specific software and teacher training. Moreover, Park et al. (2009), who used a 
quasi-experimental design with Grade 8 students in South Korea, reported that ICT was particularly effective for low-achieving stu­
dents in science than high-achieving students. This finding indicates that student performance levels determine ICT’s effectiveness. 
Additionally, a meta-analysis by Sung et al. (2016) found that ICT-integrated instruction was most effective when technology was 
aligned with pedagogical strategies. For example, mobile ICT devices were more effective in personalized, feedback-driven

instructional environments than in other environments. Additionally, the negative associations observed may reflect the remedial use 
of ICT, where technology is often employed to support students with special educational needs or lower-performing students (Gilleece 
& Eivers, 2018; Zhang et al., 2016). In such cases, the relationship between ICT use and lower achievement may not indicate causality 
but rather the targeting of ICT interventions toward students who already face academic challenges.
5.2. Predominantly positive relationships between ICT attitude and academic achievement
ICT attitudes generally showed positive associations with academic achievement across the reviewed studies, though associations 
varied depending on the functional orientation of attitudes (e.g., motivational vs. social), perceived academic relevance, and nature of 
ICT engagement. This section focuses on motivational attitudes (e.g., competence, confidence, interest) and social-oriented attitudes 
(e.g., attitude toward social media, ICT as a social topic).
Motivational attitudes, including ICT self-confidence, competence, general attitude, and interest, were generally positively asso­
ciated with academic performance. For example, Moos and Azevedo (2009) reported that ICT self-efficacy improved outcomes in 
computer-based learning, while Cos¸ar and ¨Ozdemir (2020) found a positive link between ICT attitude and programming achievement 
among Turkish 7th graders. However, Altun and Cakan (2006) reported no such relationship when general GPA was used as the 
outcome variable among undergraduates, suggesting that the influence of motivational ICT attitudes may decrease when ICT is 
perceived as irrelevant to the academic domain. These findings suggest that the academic benefits of motivational ICT attitudes depend 
on whether students perceive ICT as a meaningful learning tool. When students view ICT as closely aligned with academic tasks, 
motivational engagement with ICT appears to support better performance.
Mixed patterns were found regarding social-oriented attitudes, such as attitudes toward social media and ICT as a topic of social 
interaction. Kyalo and Origa (2024) found that Kenyan high school students with positive attitude toward using Facebook for learning 
achieved higher scores in programming due to their interactions with their peers and teachers on social media. In contrast, attitudes 
toward ICT as a topic of social interaction, such as casually chatting about digital devices with friends, were consistently negatively 
associated with academic outcomes across several reviewed studies. Large-scale studies from Spain and Germany (G´omez-Fern´andez & 
Mediavilla, 2018; Lezhnina & Kismih´ok, 2022) also showed negative links between superficial ICT-related social engagement and 
performance in mathematics and science. These findings indicate that the academic value of social ICT attitudes depends on whether 
students engage with ICT for purposeful, collaborative learning or superficial, informal interaction.
5.3. ICT and affective and behavioral learning outcomes
Beyond academic achievement, affective and behavioral outcomes offer critical insights into how students engage with and 
internalize learning. These non-cognitive outcomes influence students’ motivation, persistence, and long-term academic growth, 
making them essential for understanding the broader value of ICT in education.
Although many studies examined ICT use for instruction, its affective outcomes varied by implementation method and the 
pedagogical design. One study found that ICT use at school in general was negatively correlated with ICT interest, whereas ICT use 
during classroom lessons showed a positive association. This suggests that the method used to integrate ICT into instruction influences 
affective learning. However, Korucu and Gunduz (2011), who used data from Turkish vocational high schools, found that ICT-based 
instruction had no significant effect on students’ attitudes toward ICT. In contrast, Van Eck (2006) observed that math anxiety was 
reduced among U.S. middle school students when ICT was paired with contextual pedagogical support. These findings suggest that 
ICT’s affective impact depends on its pedagogical design and how students emotionally engage with the experience.
Regarding behavioral outcomes, although the number of studies examining these outcomes was limited, one study suggests the 
importance of academic engagement. Lee and Wu (2012) found that online reading engagement was positively associated with ICT 
access at home and positive ICT attitude, such as self-confidence. Beyond PISA-based studies, other research from diverse educational 
contexts provides the predictive effects of ICT in promoting behavioral engagement. LeJeune and Lemons (2021), who examined three 
U.S. elementary students with developmental disabilities, found that ICT-based instruction reduced challenging behaviors and 
improved academic engagement, supported by feedback and audio narration of ICT devices. Similarly, Chen et al. (2010), who 
examined large-scale U.S. undergraduate data, reported positive associations between academic ICT use and engagement in higher 
education. These results emphasize that behavioral engagement is shaped by how ICT tools are pedagogically designed and integrated 
into instructional practices.
5.4. Country-level factors and learning outcomes
ICT is not a neutral tool that brings the same benefits to all countries. Our review highlights the predictive role of national context, 
such as ICT infrastructure and economic and human capital indicators, in shaping educational outcomes. One study also suggested the 
potential role of ICT governance factors, though this requires further investigation. Understanding these country-level factors is 
essential for explaining students’ learning outcomes and recognizing ICT’s long-term educational implications. For example, Khan 
et al. (2022) noted that national ICT use, accompanied by well-organized institutional support from the government, is positively 
associated with social well-being.
Regarding ICT infrastructure, ICT penetration rates, which reflect nationwide ICT diffusion, were negatively associated with 
students’ digital skills. In contrast, ICT access in national education correlated positively with mathematics achievement, suggesting 
that availability alone is insufficient to enhance learning outcomes. Instead, integration into formal learning environments is key.

Similarly, Ganju et al. (2016) found that ICT diffusion must be supported by legal and institutional frameworks to promote effective 
adoption. However, implementing these frameworks faces considerable challenges, such as insufficient teacher training and unequal 
digital resource distribution between urban and rural areas (OECD, 2018; 2025). These disparities in practice can exacerbate existing 
educational inequalities, where advantaged students benefit more from ICT integration while disadvantaged groups fall further 
behind. Nevertheless, an international organization leads global initiatives to ensure universal access to digital learning through efforts 
like the AI Competency Frameworks for Students and the ICT Competency Framework for Teachers (UNESCO, 2018; 2024). These 
frameworks deliver structured guidelines for policymakers and instructors seeking to embed ICT effectively and inclusively within 
education systems.
Economic indicators, such as GDP per capita and GNI, showed inconsistent relationships with performance, indicating that wealth 
alone is not a reliable predictor. Human capital indicators, including HDI and years of schooling, were consistently positively linked to 
cognitive outcomes across various subjects. Yunis et al. (2012) emphasized that countries with mature ICT environments tend to show 
high levels of global competitiveness, particularly in innovation, technological readiness, and human capital development. These 
findings highlight the importance of providing ICT access in education and investing in broader human development.
5.5. Synergistic effects of national and student-level factors on learning outcomes
National contexts not only directly shape students’ learning outcomes but also moderate the relationship between students’ ICT 
adoption and attitude and their learning outcomes. For instance, cognitive outcomes, such as reading, showed relatively high country- 
level variances (ICCs = 7.9 %–52.5 %) (Hu & Wang, 2022; Hu & Yu, 2021), while affective and behavioral outcomes demonstrated 
smaller but meaningful contributions of national contexts. Across studies, ICC values varied considerably, suggesting that the extent to 
which learning outcomes are influenced by national contexts differs by outcome domains and research settings. Additionally, several 
studies showed that the educational effects of ICT use varied depending on the levels of country-level factors (e.g., Vargas-Montoya 
et al., 2023). Consistent with ecological systems theory, learning emerges from the interactions among multiple systems, meaning that 
education is not isolated but embedded within broader social, cultural, and institutional contexts. Economic and human capital in­
dicators often buffered the negative predictive effects of ICT and learning outcomes, while national ICT infrastructure sometimes 
diminished them. In contrast, ICT access in education facilitated positive learning outcomes. Some studies also found that regional 
cultural values, such as individualism versus collectivism, influenced ICT’s educational value (Arpacı et al., 2021; Kaba & Osei-Bryson, 
2013).
These findings carry clear policy implications. Our review highlights that ICT access alone is insufficient to enhance student 
learning outcomes. Instead, meaningful outcomes require pedagogical alignment, meaning that ICT must be purposefully integrated 
into instruction. One notable advantage of ICT-based learning is its capacity to support personalized instruction (Marienko et al., 
2020). ICT can produce significant educational benefits when used strategically to meet individual needs and supported by 
well-designed curricula and professionally trained teachers. This underscores the need for investment in infrastructure, professional 
development, and instructional design that is responsive to diverse learner needs.
These results support ecological systems theory by showing that learning is shaped by many interconnected systems. Future 
research should go beyond simply asking whether ICT is effective and instead explore when and where it works best depending on the 
specific kind of learning environment. Understanding the contexts that maximize ICT’s impact on different national and cultural 
contexts can facilitate the development of more effective and locally appropriate education policies.
5.6. Limitations
Despite its contributions, this review has several limitations. First, this paper focused on secondary data analyses while excluding 
articles critiquing the PISA and measurement issues and grey literature, such as book chapters and government-issued reports. Second, 
we only considered English-language peer-reviewed journals. Future studies could examine articles written in other languages, such as 
Chinese, Spanish, and German. Third, the review focused solely on PISA-based studies while omitting qualitative and mixed-methods 
research, whose inclusion could have provided richer contextual insights. Despite these limitations, this review offers a useful over­
view of key findings from PISA-derived ICT research to inform future studies.
