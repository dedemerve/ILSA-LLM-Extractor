Small-class effects on science achievement in secondary education
Ting Shen *, 1
Missouri University of Science and Technology

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Small classes
Science achievement
Propensity score analysis
PISA

Small classes may affect students’ science achievement, but research evidence for small-class effects on science
achievement in secondary education has been scarce. To fill this literature gap, this study examines small-class
effects on 15-year-old students’ science achievement using the Program for International Student Assessment
(PISA) 2015 data from 26 countries including 24 European countries plus U.S. and Canada. To reduce confounding factors and estimation bias, propensity score analysis was conducted to control significant differences
between students in small versus non-small classes on important observed covariates. Results indicate that the
effects of small classes on science achievement were statistically significant and negative in 9 countries (e.g.,
Germany) whereas in other 17 countries small-class effects were non-significant. Fixed-effects meta-analysis was
utilized to combine effect sizes, and findings reveal that the effects of small classes were significant and negative,
suggesting that students in non-small classes had higher science achievement, but the overall effect size was
small.

Class size policies have been highly debated because class size is one
of the key variables that can be easily manipulated to control spending
on education. Class size effects have been a long-term research interest
(Filges et al., 2018; Glass & Smith, 1979). As an important classroom
context, class size ( i.e., the number of students in a classroom) has
important implications for the nature and practices of teaching and
learning (Blatchford & Russell, 2020; Rice, 1999). Overall, small class
size has been enthusiastically supported by parents, teachers, and
practitioners who think larger classes adversely affect teaching and
learning, while policymakers and academics have different opinions
regarding the importance of class size (Blatchford, 2021).
Although there are surprisingly few theories to explain class size
effects, both qualitative and quantitative research on class size has been
conducted to build up more research evidence and provide unique
contributions to the development of class-size research. Using systematic observations and case studies, qualitative research sheds light on
potential mechanisms for effects of small classes; such research focuses
more on broad academic progress and outcomes such as classroom
management and critical thinking (Blatchford & Russell, 2020). However, quantitative analysis of class size effects still dominates the
research field as it provides a definitive way to quantify the magnitude
of class size effects on specific learning outcomes (typically academic

achievement), which can be more useful to inform policy and practice
(Blatchford, 2021). Quantitative researchers are essentially interested in
examining the correlation between class size and academic attainment.
As students and teachers are not randomly assigned to different sizes of
classrooms, there are confounding factors that may taint the true correlation between class size and achievement. Therefore, experimental or
quasi-experimental studies are advantageous than correlational studies
to reveal true class size effects (Akerhielm, 1995; Shen & Konstantopoulos, 2021).
Thus far, most class size studies measure class size effects on first
language and math while few studies examined class size effects on
science (Filges et al., 2018). However, science has been involved in
every aspect of our daily life and work, and science achievement is
crucial to students’ success in their academic career and job market. The
lack of success in providing a strong pipeline of science majors in higher
education and career path has been of a considerable concern (Duschl,
2008). One study found that young adolescents (e.g., 8th graders) were
more likely to earn a science degree in college if they had expected to
have a science major in high school (Tai et al., 2006). Arguably, science
knowledge is more likely to be taught to students in a classroom, so
learning environment matters as students’ science learning occurs during their interactions with peers and teachers in classes of different sizes.

Under the influence of the project STAR, class size reduction (CSR)
has been carried out in other states in the U.S. in the 1990 s. For
example, Wisconsin’s SAGE (Student Achievement Guarantee in Education) Program reduced class size from 25 to 15 students per classroom
and similar results were observed in the first two years’ experiment
compared with the Project STAR (Molnar et al., 1999). In California,
statewide CSR program was carried out in 1996, but it failed to achieve
expected results due to the adoption of mixed grades and the hire of a
great number of inexperienced new teachers (Sims, 2008). Florida also
carried out CSR to improve student’s early school experience, which,
however, had little effect on student achievement (Chingos, 2012).
In the last two decades, class-size experiments were rare due to
practical, ethical, and economic reasons. First, it is very expensive to
carry out class size experiments. Second, parents are not willing to
assign their children to non-small classes. Thus, quasi-experimental
studies were prevalent, in which researchers used econometric
methods such as the instrumental variables (IV) and regression discontinuity (RD) designs to yield large-scale causal evidence of class size
effects using large-scale data (e.g., the Trends for International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS), the Progress in International Reading
Literacy Study (PIRLS), and the Program for International Student
Assessment (PISA)). For example, Akerhielm (1995) used the IV method
in the National Education Longitudinal Study of 1988 (NELS:88) to
examine class size effects on 8th graders’ achievement in four subjects (i.
e., math, English, history, and science), and the results showed that
students in smaller classes had significantly higher achievement in science and history but not in math or English (Akerhielm, 1995). Utilizing
the IV method, Hoxby (2000) did not find significant class size effects on
math and reading achievement for 4th and 6th graders in Connecticut
(Hoxby, 2000). Nevertheless, other researchers found reducing class size
raised students’ math and reading test scores in Grades 3 and 5 in
Minnesota, but the effect sizes were very small (Cho et al., 2012).
In European countries, there are a few experimental studies of class
size effects. For example, in one recent study in France, Bressoux et al.
(2019) examined the contemporaneous and carryover effects of CSR on
literacy scores in a two-year experiment and found a significant positive
effect in Grade 1 but there was no carryover effect in Grade 2 (Bressoux
et al., 2019). Overall, research evidence for class size effects from
quasi-experimental studies in which researchers applied statistical
methods on regional or national administrative survey and assessment
data was not consistent. In the U.K., using the National Child Development Study (NCDS) and IV method, Iacovou (2002) found a significant
and sizable positive effect of small classes on reading achievement in
early years of school (Iacovou, 2002). In Cyprus, utilizing IV and RD
methods and TIMSS 2003 and 2007 data, class size effects on 4th and 8th
grade mathematics achievement were not significant (Konstantopoulos
& Shen, 2016). In Denmark, Krassel and Heinesen (2014) reported significant class size effects on students’ 10th grade GPA (Krassel & Heinesen, 2014). Similarly, Heinesen (2010) reported substantial positive
effects of CSR on French exams in Denmark (Heinesen, 2010). Konstantopoulos and Traynor (2014) found that small-class size effects on
reading achievement in 4th grade were positive but non-significant
using Greece data from PIRLS 2001 (Konstantopoulos & Traynor,
2014). In Norway, class size effects on mathematics were detected in 8th
through 10th grades (Bonesrønning, 2003), but other researchers did not
find significant class-size effects in Norway (Falch et al., 2017; Leuven
et al., 2008). In Sweden, Lindahl (2005) reported that being in small
classes resulted in higher scores in 6th grade math (Lindahl, 2005).
Similarly, Fredriksson et al. (2013) analyzed longitudinal data from
primary schools and found that being in small classes during the upper
grades of primary schools had positive effects on achievement in secondary schools (Fredriksson et al., 2013).

1. Literature review
The effect of class size reduction on student achievement has been
studied extensively in the past four decades (Glass & Smith, 1979; Shen
& Konstantopoulos, 2022). Research evidence for class size effects has
impacted class size policies and practices around the world especially in
the U.S. (Chetty et al., 2011; Nye et al., 1999) and European countries
(Shen & Konstantopoulos, 2021; Wößmann & West, 2006). Nevertheless, there is no consensus about class size effects on academic
achievement, and it is inconsistent about whether, when, and where
class size is important and in what contexts (Wößmann & West, 2006).
From the methods perspective, research design and statistical analysis are important in examining and quantifying class size effects
(Greenwald et al., 1996). Many prior studies used observational data to
examine linear and non-linear relationships between class size and
student learning achievement. However, correlational studies of class
size may suffer from estimation bias due to non-random selection of
students and teachers that possibly occur in forming classrooms in
observational data (Konstantopoulos & Shen, 2016). For example, students from rich families typically have higher academic achievement
and they are more likely to attend schools that have more resources to
operate small classes, whereas students from low-income families may
attend non-small classes in poor school districts. In this case, small class
is associated with higher achievement. In addition, low achieving students may be assigned to small classes for a remedial purpose, which
then yields a negative association between small class and achievement.
Similarly, spurious negative or positive associations between class size
and achievement could be due to students’ prior achievement as well as
other factors such as teacher seniority and quality. Therefore, randomized experimental and quasi-experimental studies are preferred over
observational studies to reveal true class size effects (Dobbelsteen et al.,
2002).
In this section, a literature review of prior studies that used
experimental/quasi-experimental methods and meta-analysis to
examine class size effects on academic achievement will be provided.
Class size research in the U.S. has a long history. Early meta-analysis of
class size studies indicated positive small-class effects (Glass & Smith,
1979). In the 1980 s, in the state of Tennessee in the U.S., there was one
single large-scale randomized experiment of class size, the Project STAR
(Student Teacher Achievement Ratio). In that four-year experiment from
kindergarten through third grade, a cohort of kindergarten students and
their teachers were randomly assigned to small classes (15 students on
average) versus regular classes (23 students on average) (Finn &
Achilles, 1990; Nye et al., 2000). Project STAR has yielded the most
noticeable evidence for class size effects on student achievement,
showing that students in small classes had significantly higher

In Japan, Akabayashi and Nakamura (2014) found that reducing class

(Akabayashi & Nakamura, 2014), but other researchers found that the
effects of CSR on academic test scores were not significant (Ito et al.,

Moreover, some researchers used large-scale data from multiple
countries to study class size effects. Using IV and TIMSS 1995 data,
Wößmann and West (2006) examined class size effects on math and
science in 11 European countries, and results showed that in math,
significant negative class size estimates were found in France and Iceland, and significant positive estimate in Portugal, whereas in science,
significant negative class size effects were found in Greece and Spain,
but in other 9 European countries, class size effects were not significant
(Wößmann & West, 2006). Using TIMSS 1995 from seven eastern European countries, Ammermuller and colleagues (2005) found class size
effects were not significant (Ammermüller et al., 2005). Li and Konstantopoulos (2016) analyzed TIMSS 2011 from 14 European countries
and found statistically significant class size effects on mathematics
achievement only in Romania and the Slovak Republic (Li & Konstantopoulos, 2016). Similarly, using PIRLS in 2001, 2006 and 2011,
class size effects on 4th grade reading achievement were found to be
significant only in Romania among 8 European countries (Shen &
Konstantopoulos, 2017). In addition, one recent study showed that small
classes increased students’ science scores in physics, chemistry, and
earth science in Romania in TIMSS 2003 data while results were not
significant in other 3 countries (i.e., Hungary, Lithuania, and Slovenia)
in TIMSS 2003, 2007 and 2011 (Shen & Konstantopoulos, 2021).
However, other researchers found that in three (i.e., Taiwan, Hong
Kong, Singapore) out of six Asian countries/regions, class size estimates
were significant and positive using the IV method and TIMSS 2007 data
(Hojo & Oshio, 2012). Similarly, using PISA data in 2003 from both the
United States and the United Kingdom, Denny and Oppedisano (2013)
concluded that increasing class size lead to improvement in math scores,
but the significant result existed only in the U.K. (Denny & Oppedisano,
2013).
Small classes signal education quality and facilitate an improved
environment for teaching and learning. However, research evidence for
class size effects on student achievement has been inconsistent (Akerhielm, 1995). Class size effects could be positive or negative effects, or
do not have effects on students’ science achievement. Based on the
literature of educational effectiveness research, multilevel dynamic
theoretical models have been developed to explain how student
achievement is affected by different factors at student, classroom,
school, and context levels from the economic, sociological, organizational, and psychological perspectives (Kyriakides et al., 2020). One
conceptual model proposed that in smaller classes: there must be fewer
discipline problems, teachers have a greater knowledge about students,
and teachers are more satisfied and enthusiastic; these three favorable
conditions enable teachers to provide more individualized instruction
and more content coverage at a more rapid rate, which are expected to
produce greater student achievement (Anderson, 2000). However, class
size is a classroom contextual variable that may have a direct or indirect
effect on student achievement depending on what teachers and students
do in the classroom that varies from one context to another.
There are three research hypotheses for small-class effects. The first
research hypothesis is that effects of small classes on achievement scores
are not statistically significant. In other words, small class effects are not
different from zero. This hypothesis is supported by the research evidence from both quasi-experimental and meta-analysis studies
(Hanushek, 1999; Hattie, 2005). One possible mechanism is student
factors (e.g., intelligence, motivation, and effort) and other school factors (e.g., teacher and teaching) tend to more important in affecting
students’ achievement than class size. The second research hypothesis is
that effects of small classes are statistically significant and positive,

which means that students in small classes have higher achievement
than those in non-small classes. The mechanism of small-class benefits is
that small classes reduce disciplinary management, empower more
effective teaching, and increase attention and individualized instruction, which may lead to enhanced student learning and achievement
(Blatchford & Russell, 2020). The most pronounced evidence is from the
Project STAR (Chetty et al., 2011; Konstantopoulos, 2008; Krueger &
Whitmore, 2001). The third hypothesis is that small class effect is significant and negative, which means that students in non-small classes
have higher achievement than those in small classes. One possible
reason is that non-small classes rather than small classes provide a better
environment for each individual student to have more interpersonal
interactions with other students for learning activities. This hypothesis is
also supported by prior empirical studies (Denny & Oppedisano, 2013;
Hojo & Oshio, 2012), and by one recent Campbell Collaboration
meta-analysis, which concluded that small classes have negative effects
on mathematics (Filges et al., 2018).
There are several contextual factors that may partly explain
considerable variations in class size effects on achievement (Leuven
et al., 2008). First, class size contexts vary considerably over time or
from one setting to another. Class size policies and practices are different
in eastern and western countries. For example, the average class size is
about 20 students in western countries whereas it is about 40 students in
East Asian countries in elementary grades (Blatchford et al., 2016).
Asian countries excluding Japan typically adopt small class teaching
whereas western countries implement CSR policies, and many European
countries establish maximum class size rules in primary and secondary
education (Galton et al., 2019; Li & Konstantopoulos, 2016). Second,
class size effects vary at different education levels (Nandrup, 2016).
Thus far, more class size studies were in elementary and secondary education whereas a few class size studies were on higher education
(Ake-Little et al., 2020; De Giorgi et al., 2012). Overall, class size effects
were more pronounced in elementary education, especially in early
grades (Nye et al., 1999, 2000). In addition, class size effects decrease as
grade goes up. For example, Slavin (1989) concluded that class size effects on achievement were small in Grades K-3, smaller in Grades 4–8,
and non-existent in Grades 9–12 (Slavin, 1989). Third, class size effects
vary across different subjects (e.g., math, reading, science) even in the
same country and grade level (De Paola et al., 2013). For example, in
Israel, in 4th grade, the positive small size effect was found only in
reading, but not in mathematics (Angrist & Lavy, 1999).
1.1. The present study
As prior class size studies have typically examined class size effects
on mathematics and reading (or language) in primary education,
empirical research evidence for class size effects on science achievement
in secondary education is surprisingly scarce. Therefore, it is unclear
whether small classes increase science achievement in secondary education, and whether the effects are consistent across different education
systems. This study aims to fill the literature gap by examining small
class effects on 15-year-old students’ science achievement using PISA
2015 data from 26 countries. The research question is whether there are
significant effects of small classes on science achievement in secondary
education. As the study utilizes propensity score analysis and metaanalysis on large-scale PISA data, so findings have both high internal
validity (i.e., causal inference) and external validity (i.e.,
generalizability).
PISA is one of the most successful pieces of work for Organization for
Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). Over the past two
decades, PISA has broadened its measurement scale and scope, covered
more countries and systems, and enhanced its explanatory power to
provide better education information worldwide (Sellar & Lingard,
2014). In many European countries, PISA findings have been used to
examine whether the results correspond to national expectations and
how the academic performance was compared with other education

With respect to the method, propensity score (PS) analysis has been
used to address important research questions in education (Hong &
Raudenbush, 2005). PS methods contain a group of strategies that utilize
PS to reduce systematic differences in observed variables between
different treatment and control groups and to increase estimation precision by reducing the effects of confounding in observational data (Guo
& Fraser, 2015). However, this statistical approach has rarely been
applied to examine class size effects except for one study that used PS
matching on Project STAR (Wilde & Hollister, 2007).
In this study, small class is defined as 20 or fewer students in a
classroom. This threshold is regarded as the standard recommended
cutoff point for American small classes (Finn, 2019). An early
meta-analytic review of about 77 studies suggested positive effects of
small classes on academic performance when class size is less than 20
students (Glass & Smith, 1979). Other studies also suggested that the
maximum number of students in small classes should not exceed 20
students as the effects are not clear until the number is reduced to lower
than at least 20 (Slavin, 1989). In Project STAR, two types of classes with
the average of 17 versus 23 students were compared, and the mid-point
between these two sizes is 20, so it may be an artefact of experimental
threshold to claim a non-linear effect of small class (Blatchford, 2021).
In practice, California introduced a class-size reduction program that
reduced class size in the early grades to 20 or fewer students. Although
there are other cut-off points (e.g., 17 or 15 students) that could be
defined as small classes, the threshold of 20 is more appropriate than
other smaller cut-off points because the average class sizes in secondary
education are typically larger than those in primary grades in most education systems worldwide except for very few countries (e.g., United
Kingdom) (Blatchford, 2021). In addition, fixed effects meta-analysis
was used to provide causal effects of small classes on secondary science achievement across these 26 countries.

Education systems in these 26 countries have differences rooted in
their national traditions and development over time, but there are some
structural similarities. First, United States, Canada, and United Kingdom
have decentralized education systems. In the United States, each of the
50 states runs its own education system. In Canada, each of the 13
provinces and territories is responsible for operating the education
system, but government standards are followed to ensure high quality of
public education across the country. In the United Kingdom, four
different education systems co-exist between England, Wales, Scotland,
and Northern Ireland. Among the European Union (EU) countries,
Germany and Switzerland’s education systems are decentralized, and
the members of the federal states are responsible for educational matters
whereas most other EU countries have centralized education systems.
For the latter, typically, the Ministry of Education or similar agency is
responsible for national education, but there are some variations in the
degree of autonomy (Hörner et al., 2007). For example, Greece has a
standardized education system, and the Greek Ministry of Education
establishes the same guidelines to ensure content coverage and classroom practices. In Hungary’s education system, National Core Curriculum issued by the government is responsible for setting the national
curricula for different levels of education. Austria has a long tradition of
centralized education system, but in recent years individual schools
have gained more autonomy in school governance. Similarly, Italy’s
school system used to be highly centralized, but it has gradually changed
towards some decentralization. In Lithuania, although schools operate
according to curricula approved by the Ministry of Education, schools
have flexibility to modify guidelines based on their own needs. In
Slovakia, the Ministry of Education regulates the national curricula, but
central regulation of curricula has been reduced to some degree and
schools have opportunities to monitor classroom instruction.
With respect to class size policy, there are no nationwide class size
rules in the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada. Among European countries, some countries have standardized class size caps for the
maximum number of students allowed in a classroom in primary and
secondary education whereas others do not have the class size rules. For
example, in secondary education, 13 countries of these 23 EU countries
had maximum class size limits in 2011 with the numbers listed in parentheses: Austria (30), Bulgaria (26), Czech (30), Germany (19), Greece
(30), Hungary (35), Italy (30), Lithuania (30), Luxembourg (28),
Portugal (28), Slovakia (30), Slovenia (32), and Spain (35), and the
numbers range from 19 in Germany to 35 in Hungary and Spain, and the
average is 30 (EACEA, 2012). However, the other 10 EU countries
including Belgium, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Iceland, Ireland, Latvia,
Norway, Poland, and Switzerland, did not have maximum class size
rules.

2. Method
2.1. Data
PISA is an influential international comparative study that assesses
15-year-old students’ competency in mathematics, science, and reading
worldwide (OECD, 2017a). It also collects information about students’
learning environments, experiences, and attitudes in home and school.
Data have been collected every 3 years since 2000. PISA 2015 is especially appropriate for use in this study because it provides the most
recent assessment of science-focused framework to examine how
15-year-old students understand, use, and reflect on science for a variety
of real-life problems, relating to students’ characteristics and their
family backgrounds and science learning environments in school
(OECD, 2017a). The science literacy framework in PISA 2015 includes
refined contexts and competencies (e.g., explain phenomena, evaluate
and design scientific inquiry, and interpret data and evidence scientifically) (OECD, 2017a).
PISA 2015 data have 71 participating countries and economies.
Forty-five countries were excluded, and 26 countries were selected
based on three criteria: (1) class size variable is available; (2) the percentage of small classes of 20 or fewer students is not less than 10% to
ensure adequate proportion of small versus non-small classes in PS
analysis; (3) PSM model covariates are available to ensure the same
statistical models. These 26 countries include 24 European countries

The cognitive outcomes that measure students’ overall science
achievement were derived from 8 sub-scale assessments developed in 3
content categories (i.e., competency, knowledge, system) in PISA science literacy framework (OECD, 2017a). Given that each student only
took a small portion of test items, PISA 2015 provided 10 plausible

sampling uncertainty for the overall science achievement. This technical
tool has been first used in the National Assessment of Educational
Progress (NAEP), and later in TIMSS, PIRLS, and PISA (Mislevy et al.,

2.3. Analysis
PS methods have been used widely to facilitate causal inference of
treatment effects (Frank et al., 2008; Guo & Fraser, 2015). The PS
analysis entails computing a PS, matching individuals in treatment and
control groups and then comparing treatment effects using a balanced
sample based on the PS distribution. There are four different PS
methods: matching, stratification, inverse probability of treatment
weighting (IPTW), and covariate adjustment (Guo & Fraser, 2015). This
study uses the IPTW approach because it allows the incorporation of
both PS weights and sampling weights simultaneously (Leite et al.,
2015).
In general, there are two frameworks to estimate the treatment effect. Rubin’s the potential outcome framework (i.e., Rubin’s causal
model) defines the average treatment effect (ATE) as the difference
between expected value of the outcomes for all the observations in the
treatment group and that in the control group (Rubin, 1974). That is,
E[Y ti ]- E[Y ci ] where subscript i stands for each individual, t stands for
treatment and c is for control. The other framework is the average
treatment of the treated (ATT), E[Y ti |T = 1]- E[Y ci |T = 1],which is the
difference between the expected value of the observed outcome for the
treated individuals and that of the potential outcome for those treated
individuals. Both frameworks have a requirement of assumptions on the
strong ignorability of treatment assignment, adequate common support
(i.e., the overlap of PS distribution) and stable unit treatment value
assumption (SUTVA). This study adopts the ATT as it has less strict
assumption requirement than ATE (Leite et al., 2015).

3.2. Propensity score analysis
The results of PS analysis are reported in Table 2, and significant
findings at 0.05 level are reported. The outcome variables are standardized, so the coefficient of small classes (i.e., 20 and fewer students)
represents the effect size of small-class effects, which is the standardized
mean difference in science scores between students in small classes and
non-small classes. The results show that small classes had consistent
significant negative effects on science achievement in 9 out of 26
countries, including Austria (− 0.09), Czech (− 0.12), Finland (− 0.06),
Germany (− 0.32), Greece (− 0.12), Hungary (− 0.16), Italy (− 0.18),
Slovakia (− 0.17), and Switzerland (− 0.08), and the effect sizes are listed
in the parentheses. This means that students had higher science
achievement in non-small classes than in small classes in these 9 countries, and the absolute values of the negative effect sizes ranged from
− 0.06 in Finland to − 0.32 in Germany. Nevertheless, in other 17
countries, small-class effects were not significant.
Regarding student demographic background variables, compared
with male students, female students had higher science scores in two
countries (i.e., Finland and Iceland); however, they had lower science
achievement in 11 countries, and in other 13 countries, there was no
gender difference in science scores. Repeat grade had a significant
negative association with science scores in 23 countries, so students who
repeated grades had lower science scores. Immigrant student status also
had a negative relationship with science scores in 17 countries. The
ESCS had a significant positive association with science achievement in
20 countries except Austria, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Slovenia, and U.
K.
With respect to student socio-emotional variables, sense of school
belonging had mixed results with both positive and negative significant
estimates. Test anxiety had a negative association with science
achievement in 25 countries. Science motivation associated negatively
with science achievement in 8 countries. Science enjoyment, interest,
self-efficacy, and belief had positive relationships to science scores in
more than 20 countries. Achieving motivation correlated positively with
science achievement in 17 countries. Parental emotional support had a
negative association with science achievement in 10 countries, while
enjoy cooperation had a positive association with science scores in 11
countries.
With respect to environmental variables, inquiry-based practices had
a consistent negative association with science achievement in 24
countries. There was a positive relationship between science climate and
science achievement in 10 countries. Science activities was a significant
predictor in 19 countries. Teacher-directed science instruction was a
positive significant predictor in 8 countries. Teacher support had mixed
results of having a positive association with science achievement in 3
countries and a negative association in 6 countries. Although science
learning time is a significant predictor, the magnitude of coefficients is
close to zero. Finally, regarding school variables, school size was not an
important predictor. The science achievement had mixed associations
with the percents of female students, repeat grade students, and immigrant students in schools. School ESCS had a consistent significant
positive association with science achievement in all 26 countries.

4. Discussion
This study examined the effects of small classes on science achievement using PISA 2015 from 26 countries. By applying the PS methods, it
provides the most comprehensive causal evidence of the small-class effects on science achievement in secondary education across 26 different
countries in Europe and North America. In addition, because PISA data
are from national probability samples of 15-year-old students, the results have high external validity and are generalizable to the target
populations of 15-year-old students in these 26 countries. Findings of
this study support the first research hypothesis that there are no statistically significant effects of small classes as well as the third research
hypothesis that there are statistically significant and negative effects of
small classes.
For the first research hypothesis, the association between small class
and science achievement were statistically non-significant in 17 out of
26 countries. This finding is consistent with prior international
comparative studies that used large-scale data from multiple countries
which showed that class-size effects on student achievement were not
detectable for most school systems around the world (Hattie, 2005). The

students’ science achievement at least in these 9 countries, but the effect sizes were small.

One potential limitation of this study is on PS covariates. Although
PS methods are hypothesized to facilitate causality, the degree to which
PS methods facilitate causality may depend on whether the measured
variables included in the procedure eliminate pre-existing differences

implications for class size effects require detailed qualitative investigation (e.g., observation, and interview) to gain the knowledge of real
classroom processes and dynamics.

By and large, class size policy of maximum class size rules was not
related with the findings. For example, Austria, Czech, republic, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Italy, and Slovenia had maximum class size
rules and had significant estimates of small classes whereas Bulgaria,
Lithuania, Luxembourg, Portugal, Slovenia, and Spain also had
maximum class size rules, but the results were not significant. Two
reasons are possible: first, these rules may change frequently from year
to year over time in each country; second, these rules may not be followed in some countries. For example, Germany had the maximum class
size rule of 19 students in secondary classrooms in 2012 (EACEA, 2013),
but because Germany has a decentralized education system, and the rule
may not be followed strictly nationwide.

Finally, it is important to measure both quality and equity in
educational effectiveness research including class size research (Kelly,
2012; Kyriakides et al., 2018). For example, early meta-analysis showed

grades (Glass & Smith, 1979). The differential effects of small classes on
achievement for minority students were also found in project STAR
confirming that small class may reduce racial and ethnic inequality in
achievement (Nye, et al., 2004). Therefore, for future direction, researchers could use large-scale data and advanced research methods to
examine not only the effects of small classes or CSR on different learning

outcomes, but also the heterogeneity of small class size effects for students from different backgrounds. In this way, we will build up
theory-based and evidence-based knowledge on whether small classes
may contribute to reducing the gaps in learning outcomes between
students with different background characteristics (e.g., SES, gender,
ethnicity).
