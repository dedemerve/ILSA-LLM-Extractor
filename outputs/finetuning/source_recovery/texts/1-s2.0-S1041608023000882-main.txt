Students’ expectancy and value beliefs are critical to reading achievement. However, past studies have predominantly focused on these constructs at the student level. Whether and how school-level expectancy-value
beliefs—defined as the expectancy and value beliefs of one’s schoolmates—are associated with students’ reading
achievement has seldom been examined. This study aimed to investigate the relationships among student-level
and school-level expectancy-value beliefs and their reading achievement. Furthermore, we examined whether
these relationships generalized across cultures. The doubly-latent multilevel structural equation modeling
method was adopted. The sample came from the 2018 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
data with 103,627 15-year-old students from Eastern (Mainland China, Hong Kong SAR, Macau SAR, Taiwan,
Korea, and Japan) and Western (USA, UK, Canada, New Zealand, and Australia) cultures. The relationships
among the variables varied across cultures. In the East, both student-level and school-level expectancy-value
beliefs were associated with better reading achievement. However, in the West, students’ reading achievement
was only significantly tied to student-level but not to school-level expectancy and value beliefs. The association
between student-level expectancy-value beliefs and reading achievement was stronger in the West than in the
East. This study highlighted the importance of taking both student-level and school-level expectancy value beliefs
into account as well as the need to consider cultural differences. These findings have important implications for
recent work highlighting the situated nature of expectancy and value beliefs.

Educational relevance and implications
Interventions that aim to enhance students’ expectancy and value
may need to be provided to students in both Eastern and Western cultures. Teachers may promote expectancy by helping students recognize
their capabilities, providing a clear structure, and having high expectations. Teachers may promote value by selecting materials that and
making the learning experience enjoyable. Creating a supportive school
context where schoolmates endorse high expectancy and value beliefs
may also be needed, especially in Eastern cultures.
1. Introduction
Reading is embedded in every aspect of daily life. Improving students’ reading proficiency is crucial for their college enrolment, jobseeking, and career development (McGeown et al., 2015).

Unfortunately, students’ reading performance has been declining across
countries over the past decades (OECD, 2018a). One in five 15-year-old
students suffer from reading difficulties especially when confronted with
unfamiliar, long, or complex materials (OECD, 2018a). The poor performance of adolescent readers highlights the importance of examining
the motivational factors that underpin their reading achievement.
One of the most important motivation theories applied to the reading
domain is expectancy-value theory (e.g., Cantrell et al., 2018; Wigfield
& Cambria, 2010; Wolters et al., 2014). It posits that expectancy-value
beliefs are important motivational factors that are associated with
reading achievement (e.g., Durik et al., 2006; Hebbecker et al., 2019;
Orellana et al., 2020; Tonks et al., 2021; Toste et al., 2020). Students
with high levels of expectancy and value beliefs spend more time on
reading, search for more diverse reading materials, get more pleasure
from reading, and therefore attain better reading achievement (e.g.,
Geng et al., 2022; McGeown et al., 2015; Wolters et al., 2014).

A vast number of studies on the relationship between expectancy,
value beliefs, and reading achievement have focused on the student
level. Students are embedded in the school context where they interact
closely with their peers and other social partners. Hence, schools might
also be characterized in terms of their collective or school-level expectancy and value beliefs (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002). Most recently, Eccles
and Wigfield (2020) refined the expectancy-value theory and emphasized the situated nature of expectancy and value beliefs. They argued
for the necessity of considering the impact of the context and situation
(e.g., school characteristics, and peer attributes), accordingly, they
updated the name for their theory to situated expectancy-value theory
(Eccles & Wigfield, 2020).
Existing studies, however, have not yet caught up with these theoretical developments, and seldom have researchers examined schoollevel expectancy and value beliefs. This research addresses the gap by
exploring how students’ reading achievement is linked with studentand school-level expectancy and value beliefs.
Another research gap is that studies on the impact of expectancy and
value beliefs on achievement are still dominated by research drawn from
Western societies. Cross-cultural research on the relationship between
expectancy, value beliefs, and reading achievement is scarce. Hence,
additional work is needed to tap into whether the relationships among
expectancy, value beliefs, and achievement outcomes at the student and
school levels would generalize across cultural contexts. Therefore, this
study aimed to uncover the relationships among student- and schoollevel expectancy, value beliefs, and students’ reading achievement by
using a large cross-cultural dataset (i.e., PISA 2018) that included both
Eastern and Western cultures.

Having high expectancies for success and high value are crucial antecedents for individuals’ academic achievement as well as other
academic-related outcomes (e.g., Bong et al., 2012; Eccles & Wigfield,
2002). Expectancy-value theory assumes that motivated learners not
only exhibit a higher level of expectancy belief but also attach more
value to learning tasks, devote more effort, show more persistence, and
have better academic performance (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002, 2020).
Past studies have found the critical roles of expectancy and intrinsic
value on students’ reading achievement (e.g., Logan et al., 2011; Ma
et al., 2021; Susperreguy et al., 2018). For example, Susperreguy et al.
(2018) drew data from three large-scale longitudinal datasets and
observed that self-concept facilitated students’ reading achievement. In
another research, Logan et al. (2011) highlighted the pivotal roles of
intrinsic value beliefs on reading achievement among students with
different ability levels. Based on PISA 2018 data, Ma et al. (2021) and
Geng et al. (2022) found that students’ reading achievement was
significantly associated with their expectancy and intrinsic value for
reading. Hence, we posited the following:
H1a. : Student-level expectancy beliefs would be positively associated
with their reading achievement.
H1b. : Student-level value beliefs would be positively associated with
their reading achievement.

2. Literature review

The role of the school context has drawn researchers’ attention for
decades (e.g., Gully et al., 2002; Koivuhovi et al., 2022; Lüdtke et al.,
2009; Maxwell et al., 2017). Researchers suggested that schools are
“social arenas” for students wherein they are exposed to peers. Hence,
school contexts play a pivotal role in influencing students’ academic
success, motivation, and emotions (big-fish-little-pond theory of Marsh
et al., 2008; social contagion theory of Levy & Nail, 1993; social
cognitive theory of Bandura, 1997; socialization of expectancy and
value of Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). The importance of the social aspects
of schooling has been highlighted in several theories (Skinner et al.,
2022; Wentzel & Skinner, 2022). For example, the big-fish-little-pond
theory by Marsh et al. (2008) implies that students surrounded by
high-achieving peers would have lower self-concepts compared to those
with less academically accomplished peers. Research on social goals
emphasize the important role played by peers in shaping one’s motivational pursuits (King, 2017; Wentzel et al., 2018). Using the lens of
social cognitive theory, Bandura (1997) proposed the important role of
peers in schools and claimed that observing the achievement of one’s
peers is one of the most crucial sources of competence beliefs. In a
similar vein, research on the contagion effect, which pertains to the
spread of psychological states from one person to another person, has
also found that motivational and emotional states spread across peers
(King, 2020; King & Mendoza, 2020; Levy & Nail, 1993; Radel et al.,
2010).
Though not focusing simultaneously on expectancy and value, some
empirical studies have been conducted to consider the impact of schoollevel factors which reflect the school contexts students are likely to be
exposed to (e.g., Dicke et al., 2018; Martin et al., 2022; Martin & Lazendic, 2018; Wang et al., 2022). For instance, Dicke et al. (2018)
decomposed students’ self-concept into the student- and school-level (i.
e., the aggregated score of student self-concept in the school) and
demonstrated that school achievement predicted both student- and
school-level self-concept. Employing multi-level structural equation
modeling, Martin and Lazendic (2018) found that school-average
motivation mediated the relationship between school factors such as
school-average mathematics ability and mathematics achievement. In

2.1. Expectancy-value beliefs at the student level
Expectancy and value beliefs are central constructs in expectancyvalue theory (Wigfield & Cambria, 2010), which is one of the dominant theories for understanding students’ motivation. Expectancy denotes an individual’s expectations of how well one can succeed in an
upcoming task (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Value beliefs concern the
extent to which learning is perceived as useful, enjoyable, and interesting (Wigfield et al., 2016). Value is further decomposed into four
types, including intrinsic value (i.e., the enjoyment of a task), utility
value (i.e., the degree to which a task fits one’s goals), attainment value
(i.e., the relevance of a task to one’s identity), and cost (i.e., the
perceived negative aspects of engaging in a task) (Eccles & Wigfield,
2020).
In this study, we conceptualize expectancy in reading as reading selfconcept and value as intrinsic value. This decision was rooted in both
practical and theoretical considerations. In terms of practical considerations, this study relied on secondary analyses of the PISA 2018 data and
these two measures were the best measures available in PISA 2018 to
operationalize expectancy and value. In terms of theoretical considerations, using self-concept and intrinsic value has been frequently
adopted in previous studies (e.g., Geng et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2015; Guo
et al., 2017; Yeung et al., 2022). With respect to expectancy in reading,
reading self-concept is an appropriate proxy measure (Eccles & Wigfield,
2020). Several motivational scholars used them interchangeably in past
studies (Guo et al., 2015; Guo et al., 2017).
Regarding task value, intrinsic value was regarded as the most
powerful and frequently examined type (e.g., Gaspard et al., 2018; Geng
et al., 2022; Parrisius et al., 2020; Wigfield et al., 2016). Though the four
components of task value were differentiated theoretically, researchers
noted that individuals’ enjoyment (i.e., intrinsic value) relates strongly
to what they think is useful (i.e., utility value), their sense of self (i.e.,
attainment value), and what they have to put forth to the task (i.e., cost)
(Rosenzweig et al., 2022). Scholars also acknowledged there might be
overlapping elements among diverse aspects of task values, and

In their recent attempts to highlight the situated nature of

depicted the potential role of school motivational context in facilitating
student- and school-level academic achievement. They underscored the

to explore expectancy-value beliefs at both the student and school levels.
These school-level constructs represent the aggregated expectancyvalue beliefs of students within a school, forming an important part of
the school’s motivational context.
Schools with high levels of expectancy might reflect a context where
students are exposed to more positive role models with high success
expectations, wherein individuals would have more opportunities to
observe efficacious behaviors of peers, imitate adaptive behaviors,
enhance their expectancy, and in turn, boost others’ exposure opportunities to optimal learning environments (Bandura, 2006; Duesund &
Ødegård, 2018; Guay et al., 2016; King, 2022; Sheffler & Cheung, 2020).
A high degree of school-level value reflects a context in which students
are exposed to more schoolmates who value learning. Through socialization, students may be subtly influenced by their peers and develop
positive values about reading as well. For example, schoolmates who
value reading would share interesting reading materials and entice other
students to study together. In addition, due to the selection process,
some schools might recruit students with similar backgrounds and
motivational beliefs who were purposely streamed to different classes.
Hence, a shared motivational culture might exist among students from
the same school. High levels of expectancy and value could be viewed as
critical components of the school’s context and might have important
implications for students’ reading achievement. Nevertheless, how
school expectancy-value beliefs were associated with students’ reading
achievement needs further attention. Such investigation could offer
meaningful insights into fostering a supportive school context and
directing the pathway for leveling up school quality. Given the situated

2.4. Cross-cultural comparisons of the relationship between expectancyvalue beliefs on reading achievement
Though inconsistent findings were obtained, the cultural differences
in the relationship between expectancy and reading achievement were
uncovered in past studies. For example, Chiu and Klassen (2009) illustrated that the relationship between self-concept and reading achievement was stronger in collectivist (the East) than in individualistic
contexts (the West). On the contrary, Inoue et al. (2021) noted a stronger
relationship between self-concept, intrinsic value, and accuracy as well
as fluency of reading in the West than in the East. To our knowledge,
limited prior studies focused specifically on cultural disparities in the
association between the value of reading and reading achievement.
Cheema’s (2018) study is one of the very few that explores the crosscountry differences in intrinsic value and its effect on reading achievement. He took data from 65 economies to unravel significant differences
in predicting reading performance across countries and concluded that
the value for reading was not always adaptive in countries with low
average levels of performance. Hence, it is reasonable to postulate that
expectancy and value beliefs might have distinct associations with
reading achievement in the East and the West. We hypothesized that:
H4a. : The magnitude of the relationship between expectancy and
reading achievement at the student level would vary significantly across
cultures.
H4b. : The magnitude of the relationship between value and reading
achievement at the student level would vary significantly across
cultures.
H4c. : The magnitude of the relationship between school-level expectancy and student reading achievement would vary significantly
across cultures.
H4d. : The magnitude of the relationship between school-level value
and student reading achievement would vary significantly across
cultures.

H2a. : School-level expectancy beliefs would be positively associated
with student reading achievement.
H2b. : School-level value beliefs would be positively associated with
student reading achievement.

2.3. Cross-cultural generalizability of the relationship between
expectancy-value beliefs and reading achievement

This study is a secondary analysis of the PISA 2018 data (OECD,
2018b), which aims to assess the capacity of 15-year-old students in
mathematics, reading, and science around the world. The PISA 2018
data is a publicly accessible dataset (http://www.oecd.org/skills/piaac/
publicdataandanalysis/). We obtained ethics approval from the corresponding authors’ university to engage in secondary analyses of this
dataset. The latest round of PISA 2018 focused on students’ reading
literacy. Our sample included 41,872 students from Eastern (Mainland
China, Hong Kong SAR, Macau SAR, Taiwan, South Korea, and Japan),
and 61,755 students (USA, UK, Canada, New Zealand, and Australia)
from Western cultures1. There were 51,435 girls (49.7 %) and 52,190
boys (50.3 %), with an average age of 15.79 years (SD = 0.29).

Much of the existing research on expectancy-value theory is based on
samples from Western cultures, but few studies have explicitly explored
the cross-cultural universality of this theory. Among the limited crosscultural studies in this regard, there is a consensus that the higher expectancy and value beliefs exhibited by students, the better their academic performance. This phenomenon seems culturally universal in
both the East and the West (e.g., Cheema, 2018; Geng et al., 2022; Wang
et al., 2020). For example, Geng et al. (2022) analyzed data from 80
PISA countries and noted the positive associations between self-concept,
intrinsic value, and reading achievement held across cultures. Inoue
et al. (2021) also found a linkage between self-concept, intrinsic value,
reading amount, fluency, as well as accuracy in both Eastern and
Western countries. Nonetheless, previous studies particularly focused on
the role of student-level expectancy and value beliefs on reading
achievement. Whether these results are cross-culturally universal needs

Second, we employed the doubly-latent multi-level structural equation modeling (ML-SEM) approach which is a recent development in
multi-level analysis. It was designed to deal with latent variable
contextual models that are nested. Doubly-latent ML-SEM disaggregates
and contrasts effects occurring across levels (i.e., student-level and
school-level). Instead of relying on means of variables, this method is
based on latent variables with several indicators as in traditional CFA
models. The aggregation of students’ responses to items was used to
form latent factors and the aggregation of scores by students within the
school was employed to build latent school-level expectancy and value
beliefs (Jia & Konold, 2021).
Notably, in contrast to the climate constructs which concern students’ shared perceptions of the L2 environment, our study focused on
the contextual constructs deriving from students’ self-ratings which
pertain to the characteristics of students composing the group. In
particular, we focused on the contextual effects, defined as the effects of
students’ shared perceptions over and above students’ individual perceptions (Marsh et al., 2009).
Doubly-latent ML-SEM integrates multilevel models and structural
equation models, which could simultaneously consider the student-level
(L1) and school-level (L2) variance of the constructs (Marsh et al.,
2012). There are two major advantages of doubly-latent ML-SEM: (1)
sampling error is controlled by aggregating L1 ratings to form L2 constructs; (2) measurement error is controlled by relying on multiple indicators to form latent constructs at L1 and L2 (Jia & Konold, 2021;
Marsh et al., 2012). In the current study, the MG-CFA and doubly-latent
ML-SEM used the final student weights to adjust the unequal chances in
sampling selection (OECD, 2009).
At the student level, reading achievement was regressed on value,
expectancy, SES, and gender, while value and expectancy were correlated with each other. When analyzing the association between schoollevel expectancy-value beliefs and students’ reading achievement, the
corresponding student-level effect was controlled. Students’ reading
achievement was regressed on school-level value, school-level expectancy, and school SES, and school-level value was correlated with
school-level expectancy. The relationship is operationalized as the
school-level effect on the school-level outcome minus the corresponding
student effect on student-level outcome and performed by “Model
Constraint” instruction in Mplus (Marsh et al., 2012; Morin et al., 2022).1
As participants were invited to respond to a portion of items from the
entire item pool, the sampling and measurement error would be large in
using traditional estimation. Hence, plausible values were used to
indicate reading achievement. We analyzed the model ten times for each
plausible value and aggregated them using Rubin’s method as the final
estimate of CFA and SEM models (Rubin, 2004; Rutkowski et al., 2010).
This is the most rigorous way of estimating students’ scores in PISA data
by providing more consistent and less biased estimates (OECD, 2018b;
Wu, 2005).

3.2.1. Expectancy and value at the student level
Expectancy was operationalized in terms of students’ self-concept
which was measured using three items (e.g., I am a good reader). Five
items of enjoyment of reading were used to represent students’ value (e.
g., Reading is one of my favourite hobbies). Three items were reversely
coded before primary analysis. All items were rated on a 4-point Likert
scale (1 = strongly disagree, 4 = strongly agree). These scales were based
on the PISA Technical Report (OECD, 2018b). Both scales showed good
internal consistency, with the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the Expectancy and Value scale being 0.81 and 0.83 for Eastern, and 0.84 and
0.86 for Western cultures, respectively.
3.2.2. Expectancy and value at the school level
School-level expectancy and value were indicated by the latent aggregation of student expectancy and value, respectively. As in traditional CFA models, the scores on items were utilized to form latent
factor, and scores on expectancy and value by students within the same
school were employed to form latent school-level expectancy and value.
3.2.3. Reading achievement
Reading achievement was operationalized as students’ reading
achievement score in PISA 2018, which assesses students’ abilities to
process text to achieve their goals and potential in society (OECD,
2019a). PISA 2018 calculates ten plausible values of reading as random
values drawn from the distribution. All plausible values were included in
this study.
3.2.4. Covariates
Gender (0 = girl, 1 = boy), socioeconomic status (SES), home language
use, and immigrant status were included as covariates at the student level.
Student SES was indicated by economic, social, and cultural status. We
used a composite score (i.e., ESCS) computed by PISA 2018. It is derived
from students’ family background information such as parental education, home educational resources, and ICT resources (OECD, 2019b).
Home language use was generated by comparing the test language and
students’ home language (e.g., Kong et al., 2022). A dichotomous variable was employed in which the code of 1 refers to the test language and
the home language was consistent and 0 otherwise. Immigrant status is a
dichotomous variable, with a code of 1 denoting the nonimmigrant and
0 otherwise (e.g., Karakus et al., 2022; Martin et al., 2022; Meunier,
2011). In terms of covariates at the school level, the aggregates of
student-level SES were adopted to indicate school SES, which has been
extensively applied in existing studies (e.g., León et al., 2022; Xue et al.,
2020). We also included school type (public = 1, private = 0) as well as
offering additional lessons out of the school (yes = 1, no = 0) as school-level
covariates (e.g., Delprato & Antequera, 2021; Martínez-Abad et al.,
2020).
3.3. Data analysis
The proportions of missing data for all items were small, ranging
from 0.0 % for gender to 6.8 % for the item “offering additional lessons out
of the school”. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) imputation was
applied to deal with the missing data using SPSS version 26.0 (SoleyBori, 2013). MCMC has a high accuracy rate in imputing missing data,
which enables us to analyze complex data (Marjoram et al., 2003). All
items were standardized into zero mean and unit SD to facilitate the
interpretation of results and reduce nonessential multicollinearity
(Morin et al., 2014).

to SEM. First, multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (MG-CFA) was

4.3. Doubly-latent multilevel structural equation modeling
Before implementing doubly-latent ML-SEM, we computed the
intraclass correlation coefficients (i.e., ICC1, ICC2) for value and expectancy. ICC1 represents the proportion of variance at the student level
that can be explained by the school level. ICC2 shows the reliability of
the aggregated student-level data (Lüdtke et al., 2008). For expectancy,
ICC1 was 0.15, and ICC2 was 0.84. For value, ICC1 was 0.10, and ICC2
was 0.77. In terms of reading achievement, ICC1 was 0.26 and ICC2 was
0.91. According to LeBreton and Senter (2008), ICC1 and ICC2 values
exceeding 0.1 and 0.7 justify the use of doubly-latent ML-SEM. Hence,
the multilevel measurement was justified (e.g., Gonzàlez-Romà &
Hernàndez, 2017).
The two-level model fit the Eastern data well (CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.95,
RMSEA = 0.01, SRMRwithin = 0.02, SRMRbetween = 0.07). The model also
showed a good fit to the Western data (CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.97, RMSEA =
0.01, SRMRwithin = 0.02, SRMRbetween = 0.05). Fig. 1 displays the
standardized estimates of the models.
At the student level, 20.8 % and 35.2 % variance in reading
achievement can be explained in Eastern and Western cultures, respectively. The results indicated that students’ reading achievement was
positively associated with student-level expectancy (Eastern: β = 0.33, p
< .001; Western: β = 0.41, p < .001) and value (Eastern: β = 0.29, p <
.001; Western: β = 0.36, p < .001). According to Keith (2006), a β at or
over 0.05, 0.10, and 0.25 were deemed as small, medium, and large
effect sizes, respectively. Hence, large effect sizes were detected in
Eastern and Western cultures regarding the effects of expectancy and
value on reading achievement at the student level (Keith, 2006).

Students who had higher levels of expectancy and value were more

Regarding the relationship between school-level expectancy-value
beliefs and students’ reading achievement, results imply that Eastern
students’ reading achievement was positively related to school-level
expectancy (β = 0.25, p < .001) and value (β = 0.18, p < .001). The
effect sizes are large and medium for the relationships between school
expectancy and value and students’ reading achievement in the East,
respectively (Keith, 2006). Eastern students in schools with higher levels
of expectancy-value beliefs reported higher reading achievement.
However, the association between school-level expectancy-value beliefs
and reading achievement was not significant in the West. This applied to
both school-level expectancy (β = 0.02, p > .05) and school-level value
(β = − 0.008, p > .05).
In both cultures, girls achieved higher reading achievement than
boys (Eastern: β = − . 03, p <. 001; Western: β = − . 02, p <. 001. At the
school level, school socioeconomic status was positively linked with
reading achievement (Eastern: β = 0.40, p < .001; Western: β = 0.50, p
< .001).

4.4. Multi-group multi-level structural equation modeling
Results of the multi-group multi-level SEM depict the cross-cultural
differences in the relationship between expectancy, value, and reading
achievement after controlling covariates. We found the magnitude of
relationships at both levels varied across cultures. Notably, school-level
expectancy and value beliefs related more with students’ reading
achievement in the East, with χ 2(138) = 141.02, p < .001. At the student
level, the associations between expectancy, value, and reading
achievement were larger in the West, with χ 2(138) = 850.83, p < .001.
Analyses were also performed to reveal whether there were cultural
differences concerning each path. The non-invariant relations among
variables of interest across cultures were observed.

value, and reading achievement across cultures. Consistent with our
hypotheses, results of the doubly-latent ML-SEM demonstrated that, in

5.2. H2a and H2b: school-level findings
H2a and H2b were partially supported. Both school-level expectancy
and value were identified as factors that were positively associated with
Eastern students’ reading achievement, with large and medium effect
sizes according to Keith’s (2006) benchmark. The results are robust with
the inclusion of covariates. This indicates that schools in the East with
higher reading expectancy and value were significantly associated with
better reading achievement of students, pointing to the critical importance of the school context. Such large effect sizes indicated that school
expectancy and value are key socio-motivational elements that might
comprise an important part of the school’s context (e.g., Cwir et al.,
2011; Duesund & Ødegård, 2018).
Perhaps, this result can be accounted for by the “stronger getting
stronger” effect (Burns et al., 2021). Students are not randomly streamed
into schools since achievement levels normally act as the reference
standard. Due to the strong correlation between students’ reading
achievement and school-level expectancy-value beliefs, those students
with high expectancy and value beliefs are likely to be clustered within
the same school, leading to higher school reading achievement. Another
possibility is that students may encounter more positive role models in
schools with high expectancy-value beliefs. They are more likely to
emulate peers with high expectancy and value and consequently
compose a facilitative reading context for better reading achievement

5.1. H1a and H1b: student-level findings
Supporting H1a and H1b, in both Eastern and Western countries,
reading achievement was positively predicted by both expectancy and
value after accounting for gender, SES, home language use, and immigration status. Large effect sizes were found in Eastern and Western
cultures according to the benchmarks suggested by Keith (2006). Such
magnitudes of effects were similar to those found previously (e.g., Ma
et al., 2021). These strong associations echo the vast literature on the
relationships between expectancy, value, and reading achievement (e.g.,
Geng et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2015; Wigfield & Cambria, 2010). The
study also supported the core ideas of expectancy-value theory by using
large-scale cross-culture data, attesting to the robustness of the theory.
In summary, students with higher expectancy in performing reading
tasks and attach greater value to reading can achieve higher reading
achievement, which holds in both Eastern and Western cultures (e.g.,
Qian & Lau, 2022; Susperreguy et al., 2018).

Methodologically, the current study extends the previous studies by
introducing a rigorous method–doubly-latent ML-SEM–justifying the
applicability of this method in examining students’ expectancy-value
beliefs. More importantly, measurement error and sampling error
were controlled.
Practically, our results demonstrated that interventions on students’
expectancy and value should be provided in both Eastern and Western
cultures. Teachers may promote expectancy by helping students recognize their capabilities in reading and guiding them to exhibit a high
expectancy for success (e.g., De Boer et al., 2018; Lazowski & Hulleman,
2016). For instance, teachers could consider bolstering students’ expectancy by expressing their beliefs about students’ potential to succeed
in reading, offering opportunities for success, providing scaffolding, and
adopting positive feedback (e.g., Bandura, 2006; Ma et al., 2021).
An enjoyable reading experience can be ensured by selecting reading
materials carefully and incorporating innovative teaching techniques.
Moreover, taking a macro perspective on students reading behavior, and
creating reading-supportive school surroundings with more role models
feeling competent and enjoying reading are also promising avenues for
improving students’ reading achievement. For example, more school
reading activities could be organized such as building reading corners,
inviting students from different grades to share favorite prints or digital
books, and organizing competitions on book review sharing or role play
(e.g., Boakye, 2021; Wandasari et al., 2019).

5.3. H3 and H4: cross-cultural generalizability of and differences in the
relationship between expectancy-value beliefs and reading achievement
The relationships among expectancy, value, and reading achievement at the student level held across the two cultures. This partially
supports H3. Such cross-cultural universality demonstrates the crosscultural applicability of expectancy-value theory, that is, expectancy,
value, and reading achievement are relevant either among the Eastern or
among the Western populations (e.g., Lee, 2014; Qian & Lau, 2022).
In line with H4a-H4d, our results demonstrated the relative importance of expectancy and value attached to reading for reading
achievement across cultures. This investigation appears to be one of the
first studies that unraveled whether there were cross-cultural differences
in the relation between expectancy-value beliefs and reading achievement. We found evidence for cross-cultural differences. Compared with
Western students, school-level expectancy-value beliefs were more
strongly associated with Eastern students’ reading achievement. This
finding replicates previous research which claimed that school motivational context could be viewed as a more critical factor in Eastern cultures (e.g., Talebzadeh et al., 2020; Yeung et al., 2022). On the one hand,
students in Eastern countries might be more affected by peer pressure
and want to fit in with the school surroundings (King et al., 2018; King &
McInerney, 2014). When students around them find value in reading
and show high expectancy, they would be more likely to imitate others’
behaviors, be affected by others’ emotions, have similar expectations in
reading, and endorse values on reading tasks (e.g., Khajavy et al., 2018;
Nalipay et al., 2020). Furthermore, Eastern cultures are characterized by
collectivist values, wherein people recognize the group values and are
more comfortable with schools’ expectancy (e.g., King, 2022; Li, 2012;
Tao & Hong, 2014).
However, Western students may not be as likely to be influenced by
school-level beliefs as their Eastern peers, denoting that students’
reading expectancy beliefs are generally influential when being
conceptualized as individual difference variables in the West (Bandura,
2006). This might be attributed to Western students being more driven
by individual values and a more independent sense of self. Taken
together, the intervention targets should be tailored for certain cultures,
perhaps more priority should be given to school climate in the East,
whereas individual motivational beliefs may need to be given more
emphasis in the West.

5.5. Limitations and directions for future research
Notwithstanding the above-mentioned implications, some limitations need to be noted. First, the cross-sectional nature of PISA data
prevents us from drawing causal conclusions. Experimental or longitudinal studies are needed in future research. Second, we only used selfreports from students in the current study. Future research is recommended to explore the influences of parental and teacher factors such as
parents’ expectations and teachers’ support. Third, this study only relied
on a quantitative approach. Future researchers can consider including
qualitative approaches, such as observations and interviews, to complement the quantitative data and get a more in-depth insight into how
these constructs play out in students’ lives. Fourth, measures of expectancy and value were represented by only a few items in PISA. Despite
showing high levels of reliability and validity, they might not be able to
cover the full complexity of the relevant constructs. For example, task
value was only indicated by one dimension (i.e., intrinsic value),
neglecting the multidimensionality of task value. Future research is
suggested to include other aspects of task value such as utility value,
attainment value, and cost to enhance the robustness of the findings of
this study. Fifth, interaction effects between expectancy and value beliefs were not included in the current study. We suggest future lines of
research to investigate how they synergistically affect reading achievement at both student and school levels. Last, the study treated cultures in
a dichotomous way (i.e., East and West). Future research may benefit
from more diversified culture classifications, as there are different ways
in which cultures could vary.
6. Conclusion
The present study found positive associations among expectancy,
value, and reading achievement at the student level in Eastern and
Western cultures. It also highlighted the relative importance of school
expectancy-value beliefs in explaining the variance in students’ reading
achievement. These findings provide strong support to the theoretical
postulates of situated expectancy-value theory. Educators and researchers who want to improve students’ reading performance may need
to develop interventions that not only target the individual but also the
broader school context.

5.4. Implications
Our study contributes to the existing literature from theoretical,
methodological, and practical perspectives. Theoretically, the findings
of this study provide empirical evidence for the situated expectancyvalue theory and flesh out this framework by investigating the relevance of expectancy, value, and reading performance at both student
and school levels. In addition, the study also attests to the situated
