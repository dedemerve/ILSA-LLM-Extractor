ABSTRACT

The academic achievement of students is shaped by a complex interplay of personal, familial, and environmental factors. This
study makes a novel contribution to the educational and public health literature by systematically investigating how various
forms and contextual dimensions of physical activity relate to students' academic achievement across diverse socioeconomic
settings. Utilising the comprehensive PISA 2015 dataset and applying ordinary least squares analysis, this research investigates
the consistency of these relationships across regions and countries at varying levels of development. The results revealed a positive correlation between engaging in moderate physical activity a certain number of days per week and students' achievement in
math, reading, and science. Conversely, the frequency of physical education classes per week and the intensity of vigorous physical activity per week had a negative relationship on student's achievement in these subjects. These findings were found to be consistent among developed countries in various regions. Additionally, the study observed that engaging in physical activity before
and after school also influenced students' academic achievement in all three subjects, although the specific relationships varied
across regions. By adopting a cross-­national comparative perspective, the study yields both theoretical and practical insights,
highlighting the potential multiplier effects of context-­specific physical activity policies on educational outcomes worldwide.

1   |   Introduction
Education is one of the most vital institutions in modern society. It is both the key to socio-­economic growth and technological progress (Goldin and Katz 2018) and the primary means
by which parents transfer their social status to their children
(Blau 1999). The level of education an individual attains can
have a significant relationship on their future development.
Academic achievement is widely recognised as a key predictor of future socioeconomic outcomes among young adults.
Academic achievement, in a narrow sense, refers to a student's
grades and completion of school courses, while in a broader
sense, it encompasses the knowledge, skills, and cognitive abilities acquired through education and training. Both types of

achievement reflect a student's learning and personal growth
(Sacco 1997). Numerous factors influence a student's academic
achievement, such as family support, family openness (Yunus
et al. 2014; Ye and Hong 2024), family socioeconomic status
(White 1982; Ye and Yuan 2022), personal effort (Stewart 2008),
social relationships (Zhao and Zhano 2022), and school climate
(Stewart 2008; Ye and Yuan 2022), among others. Among these
factors, physical well-­being may play a significant role in students' academic achievement. Many studies have explored the
positive relationship between physical activity and academic
achievement in teenagers (Pellicer-­Chenoll et al. 2015; Lorenz
et al. 2016; Stevens et al. 2008). However, most studies have
f­ocused on specific countries or regions when investigating
these correlations.

The contribution of the study lies in two key aspects. First, this
study proposes a novel analytical framework that concurrently
investigates the intensity and timing of physical activity in relation to students' academic performance. This comprehensive
approach provides a more nuanced understanding compared to
studies that only consider one aspect of physical activity. While
previous research (Nelson and Gordon-­L arsen 2006; Gomez-Fernandez and Albert 2020; Singh et al. 2019) has highlighted
the importance of the relationship between physical activity
and academic achievement, quantitative analyses considering
both intensity and timing in large-­scale samples are scarce.
Utilising publicly available data from PISA 2015, we found a
significant positive correlation between the frequency of moderate physical activity (MPA) per week and students' achievements in math, reading, and science. Conversely, the number
of PE classes per week and the frequency of VPA negatively affected academic achievement in these subjects. Moreover, the
relationships of pre-­and post-­school exercise varied across different contexts, while certain variables consistently impacted
academic achievement across sub-­samples.
Second, it offers new insights by examining how the association between physical activity and academic achievement varies
across countries at different stages of socioeconomic development. We found that MPA positively correlated with academic

achievement across the entire sample. However, the association
between VPA and academic achievement was negative in developed countries but positive in developing country samples.
Through cross-­country analyses, we delved deeper into these

While prior research has investigated the relationship between
physical activity and student academic performance, few
studies have concurrently accounted for the intensity and timing of physical activity as well as cross-­national differences in
economic and educational contexts. This study addresses this
gap by offering a comprehensive, large-­scale analysis of the
complex associations between physical activity and academic
achievement from a global perspective. The primary objective
is to examine the extent to which various dimensions of physical activity influence student performance in mathematics,
reading, and science, while explicitly considering contextual
heterogeneity between developed and developing countries.
By incorporating a diverse set of physical activity indicators
and a broad array of control variables, this study seeks to
generate robust and generalisable findings to inform educational and public health interventions. Ultimately, it aims to
contribute to the growing body of literature on student well-being and academic success by providing empirical evidence
to guide policy efforts that support both physical and cognitive
development worldwide.

2   |   Literature Review
Many studies have pointed to a positive correlation between
more time allocated to physical activity in schools and higher
student achievement (Nelson and Gordon-L arsen 2006;
Gomez-­Fernandez and Albert 2020; Singh et al. 2019). Lima
et al. (2019) conducted a questionnaire survey of 902 students
aged 7–12 in Denmark from 2010 to 2013 and, in conjunction
with national exam data, found a positive correlation between
physical activity and academic achievement. Pellicer-­Chenoll
et al. (2015) analysed the records of 444 Spanish students
from 2007 to 2011 and found that students with higher energy expenditure and better physical fitness had lower body
mass index (BMI) and higher academic achievement. Stevens
et al. (2008) and Lorenz et al. (2016) separately analysed data
on American adolescents. Stevens et al. found that physical
activity had a significant positive correlation with children's
math and reading scores, while Lorenz et al. also found that
physical activity had a positive relationship on students' academic achievement in school. The Organization for Economic
Co-­operation and Development's (OECD 2017) 2015 survey
report (Volume III) also mentioned that there is a positive
correlation between students' physical activity and academic
achievement.
The causes of this relationship have also been discussed by
scholars. For example, Singh et al. (2019) argued that physical

In terms of the stability of this positive correlation, Donnelly
et al. (2016) concluded in his literature review based on the
relationship between physical activity and academic achievement from 1990 to 2014 that the majority of studies supported
the idea that physical activity is beneficial to children's academic achievement, which is in line with the conclusions
reached in several other literature reviews (Barbosa et al. 2020;
Trudeau and Shephard 2008; Fedewa and Ahn 2011; Rasberry
et al. 2011).
However, it remains doubtful whether this relationship behaves
consistently across regions and countries with different social
contexts. Suárez-­Cano et al. (2023), in their evaluation of a sample of 104 students in Colombia, concluded that students' academic achievement was not affected by their physical condition.
Additionally, a study using data from elementary school questionnaires showed a negative correlation between physical activity in 12-­year-­old students and their math and reading scores
(Tremblay et al. 2000).

3   |   Methodology
3.1   |   Data
Launched in 2000, PISA tested 15-­year-­old students every 3 years
who were enrolled in educational institutions in grades 7 and
above. In 2015, approximately 540,000 students completed the
assessment, representing around 29 million 15-­year-­old students
in schools in the 72 participating countries and economies. PISA
employed a two-­stage, stratified sampling design. In the first
stage, a sample of schools was systematically drawn from all
schools in the sample area that met the PISA criteria, with a probability proportional to their size. In the second stage, a sample
of all 15-­year-­old students within the sample schools was drawn
with medium probability and surveyed to create the final data.
For this experiment, we sampled 397,388 students from 52 different countries and territories from data from the OECD's 2015
Program for International Student Assessment (PISA). To maintain appropriate levels of sample size, representativeness, and
diversity, a multi-­step sampling refinement procedure was implemented. First, regions with sufficiently large student samples
were selected to ensure statistical reliability. Second, regions
composed exclusively of either developed or developing countries were excluded to preserve a balanced comparative framework. Third, territories reported separately but administratively
belonging to the same sovereign country were aggregated into a
single national dataset. Following these steps, the final analytical sample consisted of 397,388 students from 52 countries and

regions across Asia, Europe, and North America, encompassing
14,526 schools—9862 located in developed countries and 4664
in developing countries.
PISA was an ongoing program that provided insights into education policy and practice and helped monitor trends in the
acquisition of knowledge and skills by students across countries and different demographic subgroups within each country.
This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of
the College of Economics and Management, Zhejiang Sci-­Tech
University, China.
Although the PISA sampling process was random, the final
probability of students being drawn after stages one and two was
not equal, and there were some omissions in the content of the
variables we needed to use in the sample survey dataset. To ensure that the data were representative and reliable, we weighted
the sample using the weighting variables provided by PISA and
supplemented the missing data using multiple interpolations to
finalise the sample dataset used in this study.

3.2   |   Variables
Our study utilised 12 relevant variables to construct indicators for assessing physical activity, with five as independent
variables and seven as control variables. Although some of
these indicators were previously used in studies like Gomez-Fernandez's, they were predominantly examined in smaller
regional samples. In contrast, our study encompassed a total
of 397,388 students across 52 countries, allowing for a more
extensive examination. We employed weighted performance
indicators and rationalised multiple interpolations across
sub-­samples to ensure the indicators accurately reflected the
educational and physical activity contexts. This approach not
only enhances the generalizability of the indicators across a
wider range of countries but also facilitates observations regarding the consistency of physical activity's relationship with
academic achievement across countries and regions of varying
developmental levels.
3.2.1   |   Dependent Variables
In the study, we assessed student academic achievement in
math, reading, and science using the PISA 2015 data. Math
proficiency measures students' capacity to formulate, use, and
interpret mathematics in various contexts. Reading comprehension assesses students' ability to understand, use, reflect on, and
engage with written texts to achieve their goals, develop their
knowledge and potential, and participate in society. Finally,
science competence reflects the ability to engage with science-related issues and scientific ideas as a reflective citizen. These
three variables were considered dependent variables in our research analysis.

3.2.4   |   Covariance
Based on the correlation matrix (Table 2), it is evident that explanatory variables exhibit correlations with the dependent
variables. Regarding Math, the physical activity factor with the
strongest positive correlation is MPA (0.101). On the other hand,
the physical activity factor with the strongest negative correlation is EBS (−0.155). For Read, the physical activity factor with
the strongest positive correlation is MPA (0.102). Similarly, the
physical activity factor with the strongest negative correlation
is EBS (−0.210). In terms of science, the physical activity factor
with the strongest positive correlation is MPA (0.103). Similarly,
the physical activity factor with the strongest negative correlation is EBS (−0.187). Overall, MPA shows a strong positive relationship with math, reading, and science scores, while EBS
demonstrates a significant negative relationship. Among the
control variables, ESCS exhibits the strongest correlation with
increases in all three scores, while shortage of educational material demonstrates the strongest correlation with decreases in
all three scores.
Furthermore, the correlation coefficients indicate that there is
no strong covariance among the independent variables. PES has
a positive relationship with both MPA (0.089) and VPA (0.187),
indicating that effective physical activity at school can promote
both moderate and VPA outside of school. A moderate positive
correlation (0.306) indicates that students participating in VPAs
are more likely to engage in exercise or sports before school
(EBS). A very low correlation indicates no significant relationship (0.017) between VPA and participation in sports or exercise
after school (EAS). A weak positive correlation (0.039) indicates
that students who engage in sports before school (EBS) may also
be involved in sports after school (EAS), but this relationship is
not strong.

Additionally, several school-­level control variables were introduced, including student-­teacher ratios, school type, a school
physical resource shortage index, and a school employee shortage index. Extensive literature reviews by Donnelly et al. (2016),
Brunello and Rocco (2013), Bankston (2004), Straits (1987), Koc
and Celik (2015), Newhouse and Beegle (2006), and Warm (1989)
have affirmed the relationship of these variables on academic
achievement. Specifically, the studies have examined the relationships of gender and ESCS on academic achievement as well
as the relationships of the migration index, student-­teacher ratio,
school type, school physical resource shortage index, and school
employee shortage index. It is crucial to consider these variables
in our analysis, as neglecting them could introduce significant
biases in estimating the associations.

4   |   Results
To address the initial inquiry of our study regarding the relationship between physical activity factors and academic
achievement, it is imperative to assess the collective impact
of various physical activity elements on learning outcomes.
Thus, we conducted regressions on the entire dataset, and
Table 3 illustrates the regression outcomes following multiple imputations. We opted for the outcome with the highest
Cronbach's alpha coefficient as the final result. It is crucial
to emphasise that this outcome merely illustrates the correlation between dependent and independent variables, without
implying causality.
Concerning the final regression findings, all coefficients exhibit high statistical significance at the 95% confidence interval, facilitating our investigation into the first inquiry: this is
the relationship between physical activity factors and academic
achievement. The selected physical activity variables manifest
significant associations with students' math, reading, and science scores.
Upon scrutinising the relationship of physical activity factors
on academic achievement, we observed that engaging in MPA
per week correlates positively with an increase of 0.048 points
in math exam scores, 0.067 points in reading exam scores, and
0.063 points in science exam scores. Conversely, participation
in VPA per week correlates negatively with a decrease of 0.020
points in math exam scores, 0.027 points in reading exam scores,
and 0.026 points in science exam scores.
Regarding the temporal dimension of physical activity, involvement in physical activity before school is linked with a decline
in academic achievement, while engagement in physical activity after school is positively correlated with academic achievement. Additionally, a higher frequency of weekly PE classes
at school was found to be detrimental to students' academic
achievement. Apart from the aforementioned findings, the results suggest a relatively smaller average relationship of physical activity factors on math exam scores, indicating a more
significant relationship on students' reading and science scores.
Second, in addressing the second inquiry, this is the relationship
between physical activity factors and academic achievement
within different social environments. We recognised the complexity of this perspective. Hence, we divided the entire sample

students' math, reading, and science scores across different regions (Table 4) and countries with differing developmental levels (Table 5).
Our conclusion stems from an examination of the relationships
of various physical activity variables on math, reading, and science scores across distinct regions. Among the selected variables, PES, MPA, and VPA demonstrate similar relationships in
Europe and North America. However, MPA positively impacts
all three scores, whereas the other two variables negatively impact all three scores. Conversely, in Asia, all three variables positively affect math, reading, and science scores. Furthermore,
the EBS variable positively impacts math scores in Europe and
North America, but its impact on reading and science scores is
positive for European samples and negative for North American
samples. The EAS variable negatively impacts all three scores in
both regions. In Asia, both EBS and EAS negatively impact all
three scores (Table 4).
Concerning the developmental level, the relationships of
these variables on the three scores remain consistent in both
developed and developing countries. In developed countries,
all variables except MPA consistently have negative relationships with the three scores. Conversely, in developing countries, MPA and VPA have positive relationships with the three
scores, while other variables have negative relationships with
the three scores (Table 5). This suggests that the relationship of physical activity factors with academic achievement
remains stable among countries with similar developmental
levels, while the relationship between MPA and academic
achievement remains consistent across all segments of the
sampled population.

Furthermore, to delve deeper into the stability of the relationship between physical activity factors and academic achievement across diverse social environments, acknowledging the
potential cultural disparities inherent in different regions, we
aimed to assess whether the relationship of physical activity
factors on academic achievement remains consistent within
broader social contexts. Previous studies have indeed highlighted cultural differences between regions (Lau et al. 2007).
Our objective is to scrutinise whether the relationship of physical activity factors on academic achievement remains uniform across broader social environments. We partitioned the
sub-­datasets into more focused and targeted datasets (each
comprising a sample size of over 1000) and conducted separate
regressions to derive statistical outcomes regarding the association between physical activity and academic achievement
in different regions and countries with varying developmental
levels (Table 6).
Table 6 unveils intriguing findings. In samples from developed
countries, the relationships of the selected variables on the three
scores persist consistently across different regions, except for
EBS. Regional disparities did not alter the direction of these
relationships, implying their stability irrespective of cultural
nuances.
Conversely, in the developing country sample, the relationship between EAS and EBS and the three scores almost always
showed negative correlations across regions, while MPA was
consistently positively correlated. Nevertheless, the relationships of other variables on the three scores fluctuate across regions. Specifically, in developing countries in Asia and North
America, VPA positively impacts the three scores, whereas
it negatively impacts them in Europe. PES negatively impacts
the three scores in developing countries in Europe and North
America, yet they yield a positive relationship in Asia.
The observed disparities in the stability of physical activity factors between developed and developing countries within various
regions suggest that the relationship between these factors and
academic achievement is more susceptible to cultural disparities
in developing countries.
In conclusion, while all variables exert significant relationships
on the three achievement indicators in countries with diverse

regions and developmental levels, MPA demonstrates the highest degree of stability. Conversely, the relationships of other
variables are subject to regional and developmental influences.
Specifically, the association with academic achievement remains more stable in developed countries across diverse regions
than in developing countries.

5   |   Discussion
The findings of this study underscore the significant influence
of physical activity on students' academic performance. The
analysis consistently revealed that MPA is positively associated with academic achievement across mathematics, reading,
and science. This result corroborates prior research, such as
Zhai et al. (2022), which also identified a beneficial relationship between physical fitness and academic outcomes. The
positive effects of MPA may be attributable to the cognitive
and psychological benefits associated with moderate exercise,
including enhanced concentration, improved memory retention, and stronger problem-­solving abilities, as noted by Singh
et al. (2019).
In contrast, the study also found that both VPA and the frequency of PE classes per week were negatively associated with
academic performance, particularly in developed countries. This
pattern is consistent with the findings of Tremblay et al. (2000),
who reported a negative direct effect of physical activity on academic outcomes among Canadian students. One possible explanation relates to measurement strategies that emphasise the
frequency of VPA. High-­intensity physical activity can induce
fatigue, potentially impairing students' ability to concentrate on
academic tasks. Additionally, extensive involvement in sports or
other physically demanding activities may lead to time trade-offs, whereby time spent on academic pursuits, such as studying
or completing assignments, is reduced, ultimately hindering academic performance.
An important point of comparison can be drawn between our
study and that of Suárez-­Cano et al. (2023), who found no significant relationship between physical fitness and academic
achievement in a Colombian sample. We believe this discrepancy may be attributed to the use of a physical fitness index
in their study, which does not directly measure the frequency

The timing of physical activity—whether before or after
school—also emerged as a critical factor in academic performance. Physical activity before school (EBS) was consistently
linked with lower academic achievement except for developed
countries in Europe and North America. One possible explanation is that students who engage in early morning physical
activities may experience increased fatigue throughout the day,
negatively affecting their concentration. Moreover, participation
in EBS often involves training in specific sports, which is not
accessible to every student. These students are more likely to be
pursuing a sports career, where physical training takes precedence over academic success. Early start times and intense physical activity before school might also contribute to physical and
mental exhaustion, further detracting from academic performance. Although the EBS coefficients are positive in developed
countries in Europe and North America, they are very small.
Moreover, due to the design of the PISA questionnaire, it is not
possible to determine the types of physical activities students
participate in before school and after school, so the impact of
this variable requires further investigation.
Conversely, after-­school physical activity (EAS) yielded mixed
results. While some research suggests that after-­school physical activity provides a balance between relaxation and cognitive
engagement, our findings showed that the relationship of EAS
to academic performance was inconsistent across regions and
social environments. In some cases, EAS positively influenced
academic outcomes, possibly due to its refreshing relationship
with students' cognitive states. However, in other regions or
countries, the intensity of after-­school physical activity may
lead to exhaustion, which could negate any potential cognitive
benefits.
The contrasting findings between developed and developing
countries regarding VPA underscore the critical role of contextual factors in shaping the relationship between physical
activity and academic achievement. In developed countries,
frequent engagement in VPA was negatively associated with
academic performance, whereas in developing countries, a positive association was observed. These divergent patterns may
be attributed to differences in school scheduling structures,
cultural attitudes toward physical activity, and the availability
of extracurricular opportunities. In many developing countries–particularly in parts of Asia–both participation in physical education (PES) and engagement in VPA were positively

12 of 15

linked to academic outcomes. This may reflect limited access
to structured physical activity opportunities, whereby vigorous
exercise plays a more significant role in supporting students'
cognitive and academic development. Conversely, in developed
countries, where students often have greater access to organised physical activity, additional high-­intensity exercise may
yield diminishing academic returns due to potential time trade-offs or physical fatigue.
Our study also sheds light on the complexities of how PE classes
affect academic achievement. While engaging in physical activity is generally beneficial for health, the frequency of these
classes had a negative relationship on academic outcomes in
developed countries. This could indicate that excessive time
spent on physical activity reduces time allocated to academic
subjects or that the intensity of these classes tires students out,
negatively affecting their focus and performance in core academic areas.
There are several limitations to this study that warrant consideration. First, although our sample is diverse, it is concentrated primarily in the northern hemisphere, limiting the
generalisability of our findings to the southern hemisphere.
Cultural factors may also play a significant role in how physical activity relates to academic performance, but our study
did not include direct measures of cultural variables, making
it difficult to quantitatively assess these relationships. Lastly,
the discrepancies observed in the relationships of EAS across
different contexts suggest that further research is needed to
better understand how regional characteristics and cultural
norms shape the relationship between after-­school physical
activity and academic performance. We noted that EBS and
EAS are different in PISA 2022 (Figure 2), compared with EBS
and EAS in PISA 2018. The codes for the two questions in PISA
2022 and PISA 2018 are different, and in PISA 2022, they are
given examples of sports such as running, cycling, aerobics,
soccer, and skating. We can figure out EBS and EAS in PISA
2022 are refer to VPA. But in PISA 2018, there are no examples of sports, so we can not figure out what kind of sports
refer to EBS and EAS in PISA 2018. Therefore, as the kinds
of sports are not given, the relationships of EBS and EAS in
different situations are not always the same. Moreover, PISA
2022 only involves EBS and EAS; there are no other three variables, so we choose PISA 2018 as our research database. There
are no light physical activity (LPA) variables in PISA 2018 and
PISA 2022.
In conclusion, while MPA consistently benefits academic outcomes not all forms of physical activity have the same relationship. The intensity and timing of physical activity must be
considered in developing educational policies that promote students' holistic well-­being. The findings of this study highlight
the need for a balanced approach to physical activity that considers the specific social and educational context of the students.
Future research should focus on incorporating cultural factors
and socio-­economic disparities within regions to deepen the understanding of the complex relationship between physical activity and academic achievement.

6   |   Conclusions
The objective of the study was to determine the correlation between physical activity factors and students' academic achievement in schools across regions and countries with varying levels
of development. We employed the PISA 2015 database and OLSs
to address these questions.
Our findings can be summarised in three main points. First, the
frequency of engaging in light physical activity per week consistently demonstrated a positive relationship with academic

outcomes. Conversely, physical activity after school had a
consistently negative relationship with academic achievement
across all subjects.
Second, the number of PE classes per week and the frequency
of VPA per week were found to have a consistently negative relationship on math, reading, and science scores. This negative
correlation was observed in developed countries across different
regions. However, when considering only the level of development of the country, the frequency of VPA per week exhibited a
positive relationship consistently in developing countries.
