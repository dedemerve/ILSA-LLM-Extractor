A B S T R A C T  
Student motivation is a multifaceted construct with empirically supported associations with educational out­
comes. This study used latent variable methodology to examine the factor structure of the engaging teaching and 
motivational constructs in the fourth- and eighth-grade USA student context questionnaires of the 2015 Trends in 
International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS). Measurement models revealed adequate fit of correlated 
factors for self-concept, enjoyment, and importance and usefulness of learning mathematics, after controlling for 
negative wording. Self-concept was the strongest predictor of achievement in both grades; importance and 
usefulness beliefs had a weak relationship with eighth-grade performance. Engaging teaching and enjoyment 
were positively associated; accounting for self-concept, the effect of engaging teaching on TIMSS performance 
was negative and mediated by enjoyment. The results suggest that a multifaceted conceptualization of moti­
vation is needed in studies of student achievement. The strong effect of student self-concept on performance 
should be acknowledged in educational reforms.   
1. Introduction 
International large-scale assessments aim to measure student 
achievement in different subject areas and evaluate academic progress 
worldwide (International Association for the Evaluation of Educational 
Achievement [IEA], 2013; Organisation for Economic Co-operation and 
Development [OECD], 2014). High-quality data collected from such 
assessments can be analyzed to support educational program develop­
ment and evaluation, system comparisons, and policy decision making 
(Debeer, Buchholz, Hartig & Janssen, 2014; OECD, 2016). IEA, with a 
history of over 60 years in cross-national testing, administers interna­
tional large-scale assessment programs, such as the Trends in Interna­
tional Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS; Wagemaker, 2014). 
Since 1995, TIMSS assessments are conducted every four years and focus 
on fourth-grade and eighth-grade student mathematics and science 
achievement (Mullis, 2013). 
Besides student achievement, classroom characteristics, school 
context, and student background information and attitudes are also 
assessed and reported in TIMSS. The additional data provide rich 
ancillary evidence about the effect of different policy implementations 
and potential issues arising from these implementations. Such knowl­
edge can contribute to a better understanding of the approaches used by 
different education systems around the world to promote learning, 
which can lead to educational policy reforms that are beneficial for 
educational effectiveness (Hooper, Mullis, & Martin, 2013; Mullis, 
2013). Student attitudes towards learning, including student achieve­
ment motivation (e.g., Marsh et al., 2013; ¨Ozen, 2017; Singh, Granville, 
& Dika, 2002) and perceptions about teaching behavior in class (Fauth, 
Decristan, Rieser, Klieme & Büttner, 2014), which are ordinarily 
measured in large-scale assessments of school achievement, have been 
previously found to relate to academic performance. This relationship 
may differ across countries, so valid measures of students’ attitudes in 
TIMSS are necessary for the examination of potential differential effects 
of non-cognitive factors on test performance. 
1.1. Measurement of motivation in TIMSS 
Motivation is an important factor in human behavior, life goals, and 
performance. Achievement motivation is what makes us strive for 
achievement (Bakar et al., 2010; Reeve, 2009) and can be defined as 
“the process whereby goal-directed activity is instigated and sustained” 
(Pintrich & Schunk, 2002, p. 5). It reflects an individual’s choice of 
tasks, engagement, and persistence on these tasks (Wigfield & Eccles, 
2001).

As a latent multifaceted construct (Shavelson, Hubner, & Stanton, 
1976), motivation has been the focus of multiple theoretical frame­
works. Student motivation has been assessed in TIMSS, but with no 
specific theoretical rationale adopted in the assessment framework 
(Hooper, Mullis, & Martin, 2013). The intrinsic and extrinsic motivation 
components in self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985), 
self-concept (Marsh & Craven, 2006), and self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997) 
are briefly referenced in the TIMSS background questionnaire frame­
work. Ekl¨of (2007) suggested that the motivational factors empirically 
measured in TIMSS can be interpreted using the expectancy-value model 
(Wigfield & Eccles, 2000), a useful framework describing achievement 
motivation and incorporating all motivational components measured in 
the assessment (enjoyment, importance, usefulness, and self-concept). 
The expectancy component of achievement motivation relates to stu­
dents’ self-concept in a school subject, whereas the value component 
highlights the significance of high achievement. Individual values are 
influenced by: (a) attainment values, i.e., the importance of high 
achievement, (b) intrinsic values, i.e., the satisfaction of engagement 
with the learning process, (c) utility values, i.e., the fit of the success on a 
domain into individual future plans, and (d) perceived cost, i.e., the 
negative aspects of engaging in the learning process, such as anxiety and 
effort needed (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). 
Since 1995, sets of items measuring motivation components for 
mathematics and science have been included in the TIMSS student 
background questionnaires. These scales have been revised and refined 
across administrations, although the need for psychometric evaluation 
has been highlighted (Marsh et al., 2013). Liu and Meng (2010) sug­
gested a two-factor structure of the motivational construct in the TIMSS 
2003 eighth-grade student questionnaire with self-concept and percep­
tion of subject importance factors. Using latent variable methodology, 
Marsh et al. (2013) analyzed data from the TIMSS 2007 eighth-grade 
student questionnaire. The four-factor solution, including three moti­
vational factors (i.e., enjoyment, self-concept, and value) and a 
single-item factor reflecting students’ desire to pursue further course­
work, was confirmed only when the effect of negatively- and 
parallel-worded items was accounted for. Yang, Chen, Lo, and Turner 
(2012) suggested a two-factor structure of the motivation construct 
measured in the TIMSS 2007 fourth-grade student questionnaire, pre­
senting motivation as a function of positive affect and self-confidence. A 
better fit of the proposed model to the data was found when the negative 
wording method effect was taken into consideration. Michaelides 
(2019) suggested a three-factor structure of the motivational construct 
measured in TIMSS 2011 fourth-grade student questionnaire, including 
student enjoyment, engagement, and competence in mathematics, after 
accounting for negative wording. Different studies mainly focused on 
just one of the grades assessed or have examined selected items or as­
pects of motivation measured across different TIMSS administrations. 
Prior research has not examined the latent structure of the TIMSS 2015 
motivational components for both fourth and eighth grade students. 
Motivational constructs in TIMSS 2015 were assessed with three 
separate subscales: enjoyment, self-concept, and importance and use­
fulness of learning mathematics. The former measured students’ 
enjoyment and interest in learning mathematics. In the context of the 
expectancy-value theory, the enjoyment subscale could be interpreted as 
an indicator of intrinsic value of learning a subject. The self-concept 
subscale assessed students’ confidence in their mathematics abilities, 
reflecting the expectancy for success component of achievement moti­
vation. The importance and usefulness subscale consists of questions 
about the importance of students’ success in mathematics for their 
future plans, signifying their attainment and utility values. 
1.2. Relationship between motivation and achievement 
Multiple studies have explored the association between student 
motivation components and performance; the strength of these associ­
ations, however, varied across studies depending on the motivational 
dimensions and performance outcomes included in the analysis. In 
metanalyses this relationship has been shown to be positive, weak to 
modest, depending on how the components were measured (Hattie, 
2009; Lee & Stankov 2018; Osborne, Simon, & Collins, 2003). The ex­
pectancy factor, as measured by self-concept or self-confidence scales – 
or in other frameworks as task-specific self-efficacy (Michaelides, 2008) 
– has been found to be significantly and positively related to perfor­
mance. Turner and Lindsay (2003) noticed that college student’s 
self-confidence was the most effective non-cognitive predictor of student 
achievement in organic chemistry. Across TIMSS administrations, stu­
dent self-concept in mathematics was a moderate to strong predictor of 
performance compared to other motivation components, for both 
fourth- and eighth-grade students (Ekl¨of, 2006; Hooper, Mullis, Martin 
& Fishbein, 2017). 
Research on the relationship between diverse value beliefs and stu­
dent performance has presented conflicting results. Some studies have 
suggested that valuing science was unrelated to science achievement 
(Ekl¨of, 2006), while others have found a positive relationship between 
the two (Guo, Marsh, Parker, Morin, & Dicke, 2017). When all moti­
vational components were jointly considered, expectancies were pre­
dicting performance, while utility and attainment values were weakly or 
not significantly related to mathematics achievement (Ekl¨of, 2006; Lee 
& Stankov, 2018; Meyer, Fleckenstein, & K¨oller, 2019), English test 
scores (Meyer et al., 2019) and science results in the Programme for 
International Student Assessment (PISA) in some countries (Oskarsson, 
Kjarnsli, Sorensen & Ekl¨of, 2018). 
A person-centered analysis of student motivation in TIMSS has 
shown that examinee self-concept and intrinsic value of mathematics 
have similar score distributions within student clusters in a number of 
countries (Michaelides, Brown, Ekl¨of & Papanastasiou, 2019). Both 
constructs were positively related to mathematics performance. In 
clusters where confidence and interest did not present similar score 
distribution, students with higher confidence performed better than 
students with high enjoyment but lower confidence. In eighth grade, 
where perceived importance of mathematics was also measured, 
self-concept and intrinsic value were more aligned with average math­

1.3. Relationship between teaching behavior and achievement 
The approach a teacher takes in the classroom is a contextual factor 
that relates to both student motivation and achievement. Teachers’ 
negative behavior demotivated students to do their best in a course 
(Gorham & Christophel, 1992). When teachers’ behaviors, beliefs, per­
sonality, and subjects were added together as predictors of achievement, 
educators’ behavior had the strongest direct effect on academic per­
formance; such behavior included: promoting a stable classroom 
climate, providing immediate feedback, using a variety of teaching 
strategies, and keeping students engaged in the lesson (Muijs & Rey­
nolds, 2002). When students perceived a teacher as more liked and more 
supportive, they tended to be more interested in the subject and more 
motivated to learn (Fauth et al., 2014). Teachers’ engagement in the 
learning process, as providing guidance and feedback, listening and 
showing empathy, and allowing students to work independently, was 
found to foster pupils’ intrinsic values, and has been incorporated in 
TIMSS background questionnaires (Hooper et al., 2013). 
Classroom management and supportive climate have also been found 
to affect student engagement. Effectively managed classrooms, which 
provided more opportunities for learning, were shown to foster student 
engagement in the learning process (Lipowsky et al., 2009) and have a 
positive effect on the learning outcomes (Seidel & Shavelson, 2007).

1.4. Purpose of the study 
The purpose of the present study was to model the student motiva­
tion and engaging teaching constructs measured in the eighth- and 
fourth-grade TIMSS 2015 Student Context Questionnaires (SCQ). First, it 
examined the factor structure of the motivational constructs and, then 
assessed the relationships of student motivation, teacher classroom 
behavior, and achievement in mathematics. To our knowledge, a 
comprehensive structural model of the associations between motiva­
tional scales, engaging teaching, and performance in TIMSS 2015 has 
not been previously examined. Modeling these relationships will 
contribute to the literature by providing a better understanding of the 
student perceptions of teacher behavior in class and its effect on the 
enjoyment of school subjects and learning outcomes to benefit instruc­
tional practice. 
The hypotheses of the study were:  
1) The motivational construct measured in the SCQ would fit into a 
three-factor model of enjoyment, self-concept, and importance and 
usefulness of learning mathematics for eighth-grade students when 
negative item wording is accounted for. Similarly, a two-factor 
model of enjoyment and self-concept would fit the fourth-grade 
students’ data.  
2) The motivational factors would differentially predict mathematics 
achievement, and self-concept would have the strongest effect on 
performance.  
3) Engaging teaching behavior would predict student enjoyment of 
mathematics.  
4) Engaging teaching behavior will predict test performance.  
5) Enjoyment of mathematics would mediate the effect of engaging 
teaching on mathematics achievement. 
2. Method 
2.1. Sampling and assessment procedure 
Fifty-seven countries and seven benchmarking entities participated 
in TIMSS 2015. Each country in cooperation with the National Research 
Coordinator and TIMSS sampling experts developed a national sampling 
plan for obtaining a representative sample of schools and students that 
conforms to the international TIMSS standards (LaRoche, Joncas, & Foy, 
2016). The current study was based on data from the TIMSS 2015 
administration in the United States of America (USA) which are publicly 
available.1 The USA data were selected because of the large sample sizes 
for both grades, which is recommended for multivariate analysis with 
latent factors. The target population in TIMSS consists of students 
enrolled in fourth and eighth grades. Table 1 contains information about 
the sample composition. 
According to TIMSS sampling guidelines, a two-stage stratified 
cluster sample design was applied. Educational institutions were first 
sampled from a national list of schools with fourth or eighth grade 
classes, with a probability of being selected proportional to school size. 
At a second stage, typically one or two intact classes, depending on the 
average class sizes, were selected from each participating school. Stu­
dents with functional or intellectual disabilities and non-native speakers 
could be excluded from the TIMSS sample; the number of excluded 
students could not exceed 5% of the national target population (LaRoche 
et al., 2016). 
During the test administration, students were given a booklet with 
achievement items divided into two sections. Participants were allowed 
to take a thirty-minute break between the two sections. The whole ex­
amination lasted 90 min for the eighth-grade and 72 min for the fourth- 
grade students. After completing the assessment, pupils were required to 
complete the Student Context Questionnaire (Johanson, 2016). 
2.2. Measures 
The scales included in the current study were derived from the TIMSS 
2015 fourth- and eighth-grade SCQ, which collected demographic data 
and information concerning student attitudes about learning, family 
background, and school environment. Examinees responded to items on 
a four-point Likert scale (1 = agree a lot, 4 = disagree a lot); a higher score 
on a scale indicated that a student was situated lower on the construct. 
However, for presentation purposes, coefficients in the results section 
were reversed such that higher scores on the motivation factor reflect 
endorsement of the measured construct. 
The attitudes about learning mathematics scale were divided into 
four subscales in the TIMSS 2015 eighth-grade SCQ. Nine items 
measured student intrinsic value of learning mathematics; the enjoy­
ment subscale (E) included items such as “I enjoy learning mathe­
matics”, and “Mathematics is boring”. The engaging teaching (ET) 
subscale consisted of ten items examining student perception of the 
teacher’s classroom behavior, e.g., “My teacher is easy to understand”, 
and “My teacher listens to what I have to say”. The student self-concept 
in mathematics subscale (SC) measured their expectancy of success in 
the domain and consisted of nine items, e.g., “I usually do well in 
mathematics”, and “Mathematics makes me nervous”. Perceived 
importance and usefulness of learning mathematics (IU; attainment and 
utility value) was measured with nine items, e.g., “I would like a job that 
involves using mathematics”, and “It is important to do well in mathe­
matics”. In the fourth-grade SCQ, the IU subscale was not included; the 
E, ET, and SC subscales consisted of the same number of statements and 
items were worded in a similar way as in the eighth-grade questionnaire 
with two minor wording adjustments. 
Student performance in mathematics, estimated with plausible value 
methodology (von Davier, Gonzalez, & Mislevy, 2009), served as a 
dependent variable in the latent model. The relationship between 
background variables and student’s estimated proficiency was taken 
into consideration when calculating the five plausible values for each 
student (Martin, Mullins, & Hooper, 2016).

structure of student attitudes about learning mathematics scale in the 
TIMSS 2015 SCQ and to assess the fit of the motivational construct to a 
three-factor model (two-factor model for the fourth-grade students). The 
predictive paths of student attitudes on performance and the effect of 
engaging teaching on student enjoyment of mathematics were examined 
via Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). SEM was preferred over a 
regression model using the readily available TIMSS scale scores, because 
it is a comprehensive approach for evaluating the fit of models with 
multiple latent factors accounting for measurement error (Marsh, Hau, 
Artelt, Baumert & Peschar, 2006). All CFA and SEM analyses were 
conducted using Mplus 8.3 software (Muth´en & Muth´en, 1998). The 
robust weighted least square estimator (WLSMV) was applied in the 
current study, since it has been recommended for non-normal, ordinal 
data with less than five categories (Finney & DiStefano, 2006). 
First, the three-factor structure (two-factor for fourth-grade students’ 
data) of the motivational construct was examined with CFA analysis. 
Previous studies have shown that when a scale includes negatively 
worded items, attempts to confirm factorial validity may fail if the 
wording of the items is not taken into consideration (e.g., Michaelides, 
Koutsogiorgi & Panayiotou, 2016; Schmalbach et al., 2021). When 
conducting research using achievement motivation in TIMSS, the use of 
latent variable modeling, accounting for negative wording method ef­
fect, is recommended over the use of motivation scores presented in the 
TIMSS database (Marsh et al., 2013). Since two of the motivational 
subscales included negatively worded items, an alternative model with 
an additional latent factor loading on negatively worded items was 
assessed. The third CFA model aimed to confirm the unidimensional 
structure of the ET factor. Next, all student attitude factors (i.e., 
engaging teaching and motivational factors) were included together in a 
measurement model. 
Finally, a structural equation model included all student attitudes 
factors as predictors of mathematics performance and engaging teaching 
as a predictor of student intrinsic value (i.e., enjoyment). The mediating 
effect of enjoyment of mathematics on the relationship between 
engaging teaching and achievement in TIMSS was also estimated. All 
five plausible values (PV) were entered in the model simultaneously as 
imputed values. PVs were rescaled to address convergence problems; 
each PV was divided by 100 to reduce the variance of the achievement 
variable. 
After examining the SEM model fit, bootstrap analysis with 500 
samples was conducted to estimate standard errors of direct and indirect 
effects in the SEM model and to produce 99% confidence intervals (99% 
CIs) of the beta estimates. Bootstrap confidence intervals are not avail­
able when all PVs are entered in the model as imputed values (Muth´en & 
Muth´en, 1998), therefore bootstrap analysis was run with each PV 
separately. Results obtained with the first PV are discussed in the results 
section, while the confidence intervals estimated when using the rest of 
the PVs are presented in Appendix A (Table A1). 
Because of the large sample size and the considerable degrees of 
freedom in some of the models, χ2 was highly likely to be statistically 
significant. Three global fit indices were also evaluated: comparative fit 
index (CFI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and 
standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Cut scores values of 
CFI≥0.90, RMSEA≤0.08, and SRMR≤0.08 were considered to suggest 
adequate model-data fit, while values of CFI≥0.95, RMSEA≤0.05, and 
SRMR≤0.05 were used as an evidence of good model-data fit (Geiser, 
2013). Model fit comparison indices, such as AIC and BIC, are not 
available when using WLSMV estimator and chi-square values cannot be 
used to compare models in a standard way (Muth´en & Muth´en, 1998), 
therefore the DIFFTEST option was applied in the current study to es­
timate the chi-square model differences. Local indices, such as stan­
dardized residuals, which provide information for misfit of the data at 
specific parts of the model, were also not available with the WLSMV 
estimator (Muth´en & Muth´en, 1998); instead, modification indices were 
examined to evaluate local misfits of models that do not meet the global 
fit criteria set in the paper. Because of the large sample size, a 
significance level criterion of p < .01 was used. 
Two approaches of handling sampling weights are presented in the 
literature: the model-based approach, assuming that the data are a 
product of a model with unknown parameters; and the design-based 
model, which focuses on the study or sample design, assuming that 
the model is known. The use of sample weights in a design-based model 
approach is highly recommended to avoid bias in the parameters, while 
the utility of sample weights in a model-based analysis is questionable 
(Snijders & Bosker, 2012). Since a model-based approach was applied in 
the present study, sample weights were not taken into consideration. All 
analyses were conducted using eighth-grade SCQ data and then repeated 
with fourth-grade data. 
3. Results 
3.1. Data screening 
Across the fourth- and eighth-grade datasets, all items had ≤4.8% 
and ≤3.2% missing values respectively. Most of the items had univar­
iate |skewness| < 2 and |kurtosis| < 3, except for two out of the nine 
items measuring importance and usefulness of learning mathematics (i. 
e. IU8 and IU9) in the eighth-grade SCQ (with |kurtosis| > 3) and five 
out of the ten items, measuring student perception of engaging teaching 
behavior (i.e., ET1, ET6, ET8, ET9, and ET10) in the fourth-grade SCQ 
(with 2 < |skewness| < 3 and |kurtosis| > 3). Since most of the uni­
variate outliers included a particular group of participants disagreeing 
with the statements on the 1-to-4 response scale, deleting or changing 
the values of the outliers would lead to loss of essential information. 
Therefore, outliers were kept in the analysis.

3.3. Structural model: contextual factors as predictors of test performance 
A model with the student attitude subscales as predictors of mathe­
matics performance and the ET factor as a predictor of enjoyment (i.e., 
model 1.5; Fig. 1), was examined. When an imputation of the five 
plausible values was applied, the model had adequate global fit indices 
(Table 2). 
The ET factor positively predicted E (β = 0.588, p < .001), and 
34.5% of the variance in E was explained by ET. The predictive value of 
E on performance was estimated to be weak and negative (β = −0.331, 
p < .001), while the estimated predictive value of SC on performance 
was shown to be strong and positive (β = 0.785, p < .001). The IU and 
ET factors significantly predicted performance, but their coefficients 
were very small (β = 0.078, p < .001 and β = −0.107, p < .001 
respectively). Attitude predictors explained 30.2% of the variance in 
students’ performance. Moderate correlations of ET with SC and IU were 
found (r = 0.497, p < .001 and r = 0.494, p < .001 respectively). Cor­
relations of SC with E and IU were moderate and positive (r = 0.625, 
p < .001 and r = 0.450, p < .001 respectively), as well as between E and 
IU (r = 0.383, p < .001).

4. Discussion 
The purpose of the present study was to examine the factorial 
structure of the motivational construct measured in TIMSS 2015 SCQ, 
and to estimate the effect of student motivation and engaging teaching 
on mathematics performance. The rationale behind this study was the 
lack of conclusive empirical evidence about the impact of motivational 
components and engaging teaching on TIMSS achievement. Since stu­
dent motivation and attitudes were previously found to influence 
achievement and learning, investigation of their effect on TIMSS per­
formance may be essential for better understanding of the factors 
affecting students’ learning processes and achievement in large-scale 
low-stake assessments. 
Expectancy-value theory was used as a theoretical framework when 
modeling the motivational construct measured in TIMSS 2015. Student 
motivational subscales in the eighth-grade SCQ were expected to fit into 
a three-factor structure, namely self-concept, enjoyment, and impor­
tance and usefulness of learning mathematics. A measurement model for 
the three correlated motivational constructs fitted adequately after 
negative wording was modeled as a latent factor. Similar results were 
observed when examining the two-dimensional structure of the moti­
vational construct measured in the fourth-grade SCQ. These findings are 
in line with former literature, examining the method effect in SCQ in 
previous TIMSS administrations, which highlights the importance of 
taking into consideration the wording effect when examining the 
structure of the survey subscales (Marsh et al., 2013; Michaelides, 2019; 
Yang et al., 2012). The unifactorial structure of the engaging teaching 
factor was verified in the fourth-grade SCQ, while an error covariance 
was required in the eighth-grade SCQ. 
The structural equation models described adequately the relation­
ships between the constructs for both grades. As in previous studies 
using TIMSS data (Marsh et al., 2013; Michaelides, 2019), moderate to 
strong 
positive 
intercorrelations 
were 
observed 
between 
the 
non-cognitive constructs. Students’ expectancies of success, as measured 
by the self-concept in mathematics subscale, was the strongest predictor 
of performance among all motivational components and its predictive 
value was higher for younger students. High expectancies of success in 
mathematics were associated with higher performance in TIMSS 2015. 
Results agreed with previous research findings that self-concept is the 
strongest non-cognitive predictor of achievement among motivational 
constructs (Ekl¨of, 2006; Turner & Lindsay, 2003). Even among multiple 
individual and context variables, measures of self-confidence and 
self-efficacy have been systematically found to be the strongest pre­
dictors of performance in TIMSS and PISA (Lee & Stankov, 2018). 
The effect of student attainment and utility values of mathematics, as 
measured by the importance and usefulness subscale, on TIMSS per­
formance was close to zero, after controlling for other motivational 
variables. These results are in line with previous findings of weak or non- 
significant effect of attainment and utility values on performance when 
other motivational factors are taken into consideration (Lee & Stankov, 
2018; Oskarsson et al., 2018). While enjoyment was strongly correlated 
with self-concept, its estimated predictive effect on mathematics per­
formance was negative, after controlling for the other motivational 
constructs. Other studies have found near-zero or non-significant effects 
of students’ intrinsic values on performance (Ekl¨of, 2006; Lee & Stan­
kov, 2018) or weak positive relationship between values and achieve­
ment when self-concept was held constant (Oskarsson et al., 2018). 
The differential effects of the motivational variables on mathematics 
performance suggest that some components of motivation may play a 
more critical role in student achievement compared to others. Student 
self-concept was strongly related to success in mathematics in both 
grades. Such strong relationships with performance were not observed 
for enjoyment, and importance and usefulness of learning mathematics, 
suggesting that student value beliefs may not have the desired effect on 
academic achievement when expectancies of success are considered. 
The relationship of engaging teaching with enjoyment was positive 
in both grades as suggested in prior literature showing that classroom 
climate, classroom management, and teacher behavior during lessons 
are related to student interest in a subject (Fauth et al., 2014; Hooper 
et al., 2013; Lipowsky et al., 2009). Therefore, teacher’s engagement in 
the learning process may affect student motivation in learning, 
improving their enjoyment of studying a particular subject. The effect of 
engaging teaching behavior on performance was partially mediated by 
the enjoyment of the subject. Results support previous findings illus­
trating that student intrinsic values of a subject mediates the effect of 
teacher behavior on student academic outcomes (Lipowsky et al., 2009; 
Wubbels, Brekelmans, & Mainhard, 2016). 
Intrinsic value and engaging teaching behavior had negative effects 
on mathematics performance over and above self-concept. Previous 
studies have found that supportive teachers can foster holistic learning 
and achievement (Cornelius-White, 2007; Muijs & Reynolds, 2002; 
Seidel & Shavelson, 2007). Including multiple motivational subscales in 
the current predictive models may have allowed for the negative re­
lationships between engaging teaching behavior and achievement as 
mediated by enjoyment to emerge in the two USA samples examined. 
Engaging teaching and enjoyment were positively correlated with 
self-concept, which strongly predicted performance, and at the same 
time negatively predicted performance over and above self-concept. The 
counterintuitive coefficients should be interpreted in the presence of 
other motivational constructs like self-concept in the model, and not 
independently. A second explanation may be the way engaging teaching 
was measured in TIMSS: what is being recorded is the perception of 
students about teaching behavior and the degree this behavior is 
engaging; positive perceptions may be associated with higher enjoyment 
with mathematics, but may not necessarily reflect quality of teaching 
and effective instructional practices. 
Overall, the results of the present study indicate a presence of a 
negative wording effect in the motivational constructs measured by 
TIMSS 2015. There were positive associations among the motivational 
factors, and in combination they explain a large part of the variance in 
performance, even though they have differential effects on the outcome. 
The strong relationship between self-concept and performance should be 
acknowledged in educational reforms as a strong predictor for 
enhancing school mathematics achievement, especially for younger 
students.

4.1. Limitations and future directions 
Self-reported data from a single country were used to test the hy­
potheses of the study; therefore, the results may be country specific and 
dependent on socially desirable and biased responses that have been 
well-documented in survey data (Podsakoff, MacKenzie, & Podsakoff, 
2012). Replicating the study using data from countries other than the 
USA can provide a better understanding of student motivation and 
engaging teaching behavior and their impact on test performance. The 
three-factor model of motivation (two-factor for fourth-grade students) 
was confirmed when wording effects were accounted for; generalization 
of this structure requires invariance analyses with cross-national 
samples. 
Motivational components and engaging teaching behavior were 
found to account for about one-third of the variance of student’s 
mathematics performance in TIMSS 2015, therefore other factors may 
play an important role in predicting the test results as well. Future 
studies may investigate the potential effects of additional student, 
family, school, or country-level factors. For instance, a person-center 
approach studying motivation in TIMSS, has revealed a relationship 
between students’ gender and home educational resources, and their 
motivational profiles. Different motivational profiles differed in 
achievement as well, implying that test performance was not indepen­
dent of students’ demographic characteristics (Michaelides, Brown, 
Ekl¨of & Papanastasiou, 2019). Test-taking motivation can also be an 
example of a factor contributing to the variance of student performance 
since low test-taking motivation may impact achievement in low-stakes 
assessments (e.g., Ekl¨of, 2010; Wise & DeMars, 2005). 
Finally, it is essential to emphasize that the results of the present 
study do not imply causal relationships. Cross-sectional data were used 
to examine the mediating effect of the intrinsic value beliefs in the 
relationship between engaging teaching and math performance. While 
this particular mediation model was proposed and tested, it is likely that 
the direction of the effects may differ, therefore, results should be 
interpreted with caution. Future studies, using experimental or longi­
tudinal designs, are needed to examine the above-mentioned 
relationships. 
Nevertheless, the present findings contribute to the literature by 
presenting a comprehensive model to understand the relationships be­
tween motivational components and engaging teaching behavior, as 
well as their combined and unique associations with test performance. 
They can serve as a steppingstone for researchers aiming to explore the 
effect of teacher behavior and student motivation on achievement and to 
propose school interventions fostering school effectiveness.
