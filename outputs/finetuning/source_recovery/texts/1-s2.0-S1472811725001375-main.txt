A B S T R A C T
Students are expected to display effective problem-solving skills to resolve challenges in the 
workplace successfully. This study analyses students’ problem-solving skills using the OECD PISA 
problem-solving framework and the extent to which they display four problem-solving processes: 
(1) exploring and understanding (EU), (2) representing and formulating (RF), (3) planning and 
executing (PE), and (4) monitoring and reflecting (MR). 188 problem-solving written assignments 
with 8080 paragraphs were analysed using natural paragraph analysis across the four processes 
encompassing 21 core tasks. Students generally show strong EU and RF processes and tasks but 
could improve some RF and MR processes. This means that students are generally successful at 
information processing, building representations, formulation and modelling, and use of tools to 
solve problems. They showed difficulty in organising and revising solutions, finding the rationale 
behind the problem, seeking alternative solutions and critically evaluating given information and 
assumptions. The paper concludes with practical implications for teaching in higher education.
1. Introduction
Developing students’ problem-solving skills has been identified as one of the most important aims in management education 
(Carriger, 2016; Isaksen, Johnson & Werner, 1975; Puccio, Mance & Murdock, 2011). Management students increasingly require 
problem-solving skills to successfully manage complex and challenging tasks and “navigate an increasingly uncertain, volatile and 
ambiguous world” (Csap´o et al., 2017: 1; Simpson & Marshall, 2010)—the ‘real life’ in the world of work, professional practice and 
managing a business. Graduates will increasingly face complex problems, make decisions and find solutions to address them (Hendry, 
2006). Those solutions rely on the effective use of problem-solving skills, as incorrect solutions and decisions can be costly. However, 
there have been questions about the adequacy of business schools in preparing students to cope with complex business problems 
(Bennis & O’Toole, 2005; Pfeffer & Fong, 2002; Waddock & Lozano, 2013). Contemporary problems are not just complex but also 
inherently “wicked” (Rittel & Webber, 1973), often ill-defined, complex, ambiguous and requiring multi-stakeholder inputs. While 
various frameworks in management pedagogy, such as problem-based instruction and using cases, have been used to enhance 
problem-solving skills, these approaches have been criticised for their limited connection with experience-rich, industry-based wicked 
problems (Dutta, 2018; Mesny, 2013). While business schools respond to this criticism and acknowledge the criticality of 
problem-solving in students’ employability (AACSB, 2017, 2023; Heller, 2002; Tipi & Killian, 2023), dissatisfaction persists with 
graduates’ lack of problem-solving and other important skills (e.g., Bennett, 2002; Routon, Rawls, & Bontrager, 2023). And as artificial 
intelligence (AI) rapidly becomes embedded in the workplace, advanced problem-solving capabilities will continue to constitute a core 
dimension of graduates’ employability (Chuang, 2024; Margaryan, 2023; Raisch & Fomina, 2025).

This study critically examines a sample of written assessments designed to test problem-solving skills, aiming to determine evidence

1.1. Defining and teaching problem-solving
Problem-solving “engages the students in thinking about the subject matter in ways designed to improve their understanding of it” 
(Laurillard, 1997, p. 126). The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) adopts Mayer’s (1990) definition of 
problem solving as “cognitive processing directed at transforming a given situation into a goal situation when no obvious method of 
solution is available” (OECD, 2012a, p. 4). This could involve utilising familiar patterns of thinking (Laurillard, 1997), welcoming 
unfamiliar ones, representing and manipulating information (Mayer & Wittrock, 2006, chap. 13; Sutherland, 2002), influenced by the 
student’s interest, motivation and ability to solve the problem (Mayer, 1998). Thus, problem-solving describes “situated, deliberate, 
learner-directed, activity-oriented efforts to seek divergent solutions to authentic problems through multiple interactions amongst 
problem solver, tools, and other resources” (Kim & Hannafin, 2011b, p. 405). It is most prevalent in situations where problems are new 
and have no known solutions (Raisch & Fomina, 2025; Winter, 2003).
Teaching students to solve problems is twofold: framing or formulating a problem and solving it. Repenning, Kieffer and Astor 
(2017: 40) describe problem formulation as the “single most underrated skill in all of management practice.” Bhardwaj and colleagues 
(2018) argue that managers often take problem formulation for granted, leading to failure, as conclusions are drawn from limited 
information due to a lack of a clear understanding of the problem, its complexity, ambiguity and consequences (financial or otherwise).
In solving a problem, decisions require understanding different perspectives and alternative courses of action, as well as being 
conscious of efficiencies in resource use, such as time, money, and energy. Both formulation and solution processes are taught to 
develop a deeper understanding of how to approach problems effectively. This is similar to what Laurillard (1997) contends—that 
there are two (human) problem-solving approaches: Gestalt (meaning: whole) theory and human information processing. The key is the 
perception of the problem and failure is often attributed to a failure in perception. Human information processing, on the other hand, 
focuses on the procedures to create a representation of the problem and solve it.
1.2. Problem solving in a business analysis context
In quantitative subjects such as business analysis, it is essential to understand what causes a problem, what data is available, and 
how a decision-maker can utilise the appropriate tools to solve the problem. Thus, management educators strive to balance theory- 
oriented learning with the development of a broader set of employability skills and specific managerial skills, such as communica­
tion, teamwork, critical and analytical thinking, and problem-solving (Goltz et al., 2008; Huang, Wright, and Middleton, 2022).
Problem-solving is a necessary component of curriculum design, particularly in business analysis subjects. Teaching, learning, and 
assessment activities and tasks are designed to help students understand, unpack, formulate, and solve problems, including weighing 
all possible scenarios and understanding the implications of their decisions. Helping students enhance their problem-solving skills may 
involve increasing the difficulty and rigour in the classroom (Choudhury & Valencia-Miller, 2024) and offering complex, cross-border 
case problems with global impact (i.e., social or environmental concerns), not only addressing issues in corporate or local contexts. 
This might also necessitate a broader conversation about why and how problems are becoming increasingly complex, as well as what 
tools are needed to tackle them, followed by a redesign of the curriculum to better equip students in problem-solving.
For example, in optimisation problems, where the objective is to arrive at a solution whilst considering several constraints, 
problem-solving skills are paramount. Students face dilemmas in formulating or solving problems and manipulating and exper­
imenting with alternatives to devise a solution that utilises resources such as time and money most efficiently. Given that problems can 
be complex, the added ambiguity, novelty, unfamiliarity, incomplete information, and the financial costs of bad decisions could put the 
students in a simulated view of the manager’s difficult situation. Thus, in many problem-solving contexts designed in management 
education, students are exposed to unfamiliar situations in which they can apply what they learn in class, and often in fields outside 
their majors.
A frequent issue in written project submissions is that students fail to understand the problem, break it down into interrelated parts, 
and formulate or apply the tools to solve it. The cognitive processes used, or lack thereof, can be inadequate or irrelevant to the 
problem. This ‘initial framing’ is so critical that when performed correctly, it leads to productive solutions, decisions or outcomes 
(Katz-Buonincontro & Ghosh, 2014). Beyond cognitive aspects are also the affective and conative components at play (Masui & De 
Corte, 1999). Those who “get it” are often successful because they “started right and ended right”. This has less to do with getting “that 
number” right, but more with seeing evidence of a clear understanding of the variables at play, being in the shoes of the 
decision-maker, the processes used, appropriate tools and techniques employed, and the sound and supported conclusions drawn from 
correct analyses made. If the written assessments are supplemented with individual or team presentations, these characteristics are 
even more apparent in their presentations. The opportunity to articulate their team’s problem-solving processes behind their solutions 
and present to an audience presents an invaluable experience.
1.3. Student engagement with problem solving
Several approaches, methods, theories, perspectives, and frameworks have been employed to teach problem-solving in business 
and management education. These offerings can be grouped into two: making pedagogical changes around (1) teaching and learning 
and (2) assessment. The literature is richer on the former than the latter. However, they share one common purpose—to narrow the

Rather than positioning these approaches as independent, they are presented here as a collection that has developed over time as 
our understanding of developing and improving students’ problem-solving skills has grown. Significant overlap exists; some ap­
proaches build upon, relate to, or underlie others. For example, case-based learning could be experiential and problem-based, and 
some authors may also refer to it as an active learning approach (e.g., Damnjanovic & Mijatovic, 2017). Essentially, they are part of a 
repertoire of innovative teaching, learning, and assessment practices that have evolved. Instructors have applied them in classrooms 
with accompanying successes and challenges designed to help students develop key business and problem-solving skills.
Teaching and learning innovations include approaches that have been part of the management academic’s toolkit, such as case- 
based, experiential, problem-based, and others (details later). Case-based learning brings the real world into the classroom by using 
cases to apply theoretical knowledge to practical situations, helping students analyse and solve problems and make decisions 
(Damnjanovic & Mijatovic, 2017; Farashahi & Tajeddin, 2018). Proponents of this approach have found it useful in effectively building 
theory and application (Christensen & Carlile, 2009). Cases have been at the heart of management education (Gamble & Jelley, 2014) 
for more than 100 years and are also a vital feature of law and medical education (Goldberg et al., 2010; Khosa & Volet, 2013). 
Management is a multi-faceted practice after all (Mesny, 2013), and using cases emphasises the context of the problem to shed light on.
Experiential learning that integrates simulations, internships, consulting, immersion and fieldwork, relies on sending students ‘out 
there’ and experiencing problems hands-on, promoting action, engagement and reflection, thus promoting problem-solving skills 
(Kolb & Kolb, 2009). There is evidence that experiential learning approaches improve both cognitive and affective domains (Chavan, 
2011) and the development of entrepreneurial knowledge, skills and mindset (De la Gala-Vel´asquez et al., 2024; Karia, Bathula & 
Abbott, 2015; Shiri et al., 2025), resulting from experience and performing situated, repeated tasks.
Research on problem-based learning (PBL) is no different. A plethora of research in this area, not only in business disciplines, 
suggests that this approach, if designed and executed well, is particularly helpful in developing and enhancing students’ business skills 
as individuals or even in collaborative contexts (Lovelace et al., 2016), such as when students solve real problems (Muerza et al., 2024). 
PBL is also found to be useful in developing further skills, such as critical thinking (Huang & Liao, 2024; Kek & Huijser, 2011), despite 
criticism that it does not directly teach problem-solving (Bhardwaj et al., 2018; Ungaretti et al., 2015).
There are also other known approaches, such as project-based learning and action learning, where key activities devote significant 
time to immersive and simulated projects to improve problem-solving. Design thinking has also been argued to achieve the same goal, 
with the difference being its orientation towards altering cognitive schemas to improve creative idea generation (Meisiek et al., 2023), 
thereby enhancing problem-solving skills. Service learning has also been found useful (Godfrey et al., 2005) when framed in a civic 
engagement context, with a greater focus on practical content and less emphasis on theoretical knowledge. Another example is 
technology-mediated scaffolding (Janson et al., 2020), which highlights technology’s important role in fostering problem-solving skills 
as well as competencies such as digital entrepreneurial competencies (Ivanovi´c et al., 2025).
1.4. A framework for analysing problem-solving written assessments
Similar to Kelly and colleagues’ (2016) analysis of the presence or absence of problem-solving cognitive processes, this study also 
uses the OECD Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) problem-solving framework. Unlike Kelly and colleagues’ use 
in science and a purpose-built assessment, this study uses an existing collaborative business analysis essay of 6000 words that requires 
problem formulation, model building using the tools learned in class, iterative use of the model by considering a number of constraints 
and, finally, generating the most optimal solution that meets all requirements and constraints. The other rationale for its use in 
university management education, specifically business analysis, is because the framework: (1) emphasises solving ill-defined, com­
plex and unstructured problems common in business, (2) fosters the development of analytical and critical thinking skills and crea­
tivity which is common in business analysis that requires gathering, interpreting and identifying patterns in data, leading to 
innovative, evidence-based decisions, (3) offers a structured approach in its processes that suits business professionals’ systematic 
approach to solving problems in the workplace, and (4) encourages collaborative problem solving which applies to the specific context 
in this study which simulates students’ future roles in working in teams to solve problems.
While the OECD PISA framework introduced in 2012 (OECD, 2012b) was originally designed to test 15-year-olds’ general 
problem-solving skills, it underwent a significant conceptual shift in 2022. Moving beyond the traditional scope of literacy and 
numeracy, the updated framework emphasises the cognitive processes that occur during problem-solving (OECD, 2019). This ‘creative 
thinking’ in problem solving highlights the importance of generating innovative ideas to tackle increasingly complex problems in the 
21st century (OECD, 2022). Its extended relevance and application to higher education, therefore, was natural. The framework aligns 
with the demand for graduates equipped with creativity, critical thinking, and other essential employability skills that employers 
highly value and necessary for navigating volatile, uncertain, complex, and ambiguous (VUCA) environments—especially in an era 
marked by information overload and widespread misinformation and disinformation (Saroyan, 2022).
This group assessment supports the notion that deeper problem-solving processes are more prevalent in collaborative rather than 
individual assessment tasks (Heller, Keith, & Anderson, 2012). The advantage of using a longer written assignment includes iterative 
processes of solving a problem faced by the company chosen by the students, enabling them to go through several exploration, 
experimentation, monitoring and formulating opportunities before the assignment is submitted in the final week of the semester. This 
makes the collaborative assessment piece most suitable for the OECD PISA framework.
The OECD PISA problem-solving framework categorises the processes associated with problem-solving as exploring and under­
standing (EU), representing and formulating (RF), planning and executing (PE), and monitoring and reflecting (MR). This is illustrated 
in Fig. 1 below. Much research has been conducted in the area of problem-solving processes; however, this model has had limited

application in various disciplines, and it has little use in management education generally and business analysis specifically. In this 
framework, EU is described as observing and looking for limitations in the problem, understanding what information is available and 
what concepts are applicable. RF is representing the problem, such as using tables or graphs and formulating the problem to establish

Lastly, MR consists of monitoring the results at each stage, taking corrective actions as needed, and reflecting on the solution. As

This study’s purposeful use of written assignments supports Docktor and Heller’s (2009a, 2009b) assertion that student written 
work provides authentic examples of problem-solving processes. Rubrics have also been found to improve transparency in the marking 
process, particularly in articulating assessment expectations (Jonsson & Svingby, 2007; Reddy & Andrade, 2010) despite their criti­
cisms (Kenworthy & Hrivnak, 2014; Panadero & Jonsson, 2020). This increases the internal validity of the assignments in this sample, 
as well as their consistency and alignment with the learning outcomes being assessed, since they are collected for both subject grading 
and assurance of learning. In this sample, rubrics are included with the written assessments.
1.5. The assessment and study context
The 6000-word group assignment was presented to the students in week 1 of the semester to be submitted in two written essays: 
2000-word Assignment 1 (A1) due in week 5 and 4000-word Assignment 2 (A2) due in week 11. There were 12 weeks in the semester, 
with week 12 being the presentation of the team projects. A1 discusses the companies the teams have selected and their optimisation 
problems. They were asked to search library databases for articles on linear programming (LP) related to the type of problem they 
wished to solve.
Typically, LP problems relate to optimisations in the airline, transportation, energy, telecommunications, and manufacturing in­
dustries. For example, Delta Airlines may wish to optimise its crew roster and routes to efficiently use its resources and consider all the 
constraints that could yield the best possible outcome (e.g., maximum profit, reduced cost, satisfied customers, shortest flight paths, 
etc.). In A2, students formulate their LP model, use available tools, and solve the problem. The complexity of A2 required all the 
problem-solving processes described in the OECD PISA framework, as presented in Table 1 below.
Students were expected to apply and extend their understanding of LP concepts and solve a real current problem in their chosen 
company. This supports the real-world simulation, as students can include interviews with actual employees faced with the problem. 
For example, a team of students could interview the manager of their local Domino’s Pizza branch, identify a problem they face (e.g., 
staff roster timetabling, supplies delivery management), and determine whether an LP model could be used to solve the problem. 
Company data may not be disclosed to the student teams due to the sensitivity and privacy of the information; however, students were 
encouraged to conduct research, establish their project scope, identify limitations, and make reasonable assumptions where necessary.
There was no prescribed format for the assignments, except for the word limit requirement, the inclusion of a cover page and the 
attachment of records of meetings (i.e., minutes or similar) to indicate the students who attended those meetings and their agenda. This

2. Methodology
2.1. Data
A total of 188 group assignments were analysed, with an average prescribed word count of 4000 words (±10 %) collected over six 
years, either in Semester 1 or 2 of each year, with the addition of one summer class. As described in the previous section, the 
assignment requires complex collaborative problem-solving tasks on unique cases. Students might have selected the same company (e. 
g., a popular restaurant or supermarket chain in the city) but the problems they were working on were dissimilar. This made the 
assignments unique and authentic, as they varied in length, presentation, format, etc.
The assignments had an average of 43 paragraphs, two tables and six figures–a total of 8080 paragraphs, 397 tables and 1046 
figures. References were excluded from the paragraph count and analysis. A few assignments included glossaries, which were als 
excluded. Where a table is an image from another source, it was counted as a figure. Where equations are included, they are counted as 
part of the paragraph. Most of the figures include decision trees, Excel screenshots, schematic representations, model structures, 
process flows, charts, or network diagrams.

2.2. Data analysis
A spreadsheet was prepared for the 188 assignments in line with the framework used in this study (Fig. 1 and Table 1). The analysis 
was similar to Liang and Wang’s (2004) natural paragraph analysis of case studies, rather than sentences or cases. That meanings and 
perspectives within paragraphs offer more richness than individual sentences, which can be too detailed to lose the narrative, or within 
assignments which can be too broad.
Each paragraph was coded based on its respective problem-solving framework process (EU, RF, PE, MR). If more than one process 
was observed in a paragraph, multiple processes were coded. Since multiple processes can be coded into one paragraph, the total 
percentages of the four processes may exceed 100 % (see Results, Table 2). This same coding technique was also applied to the 21 core 
tasks described in Table 1. That is, multiple codes were also assigned to a paragraph where applicable. For example, a paragraph may 
refer to or include both EU1 and EU2 (within a process), or in some cases, both PE6 and MR2 (across processes).
Intra-rater and inter-rater reliability measures were employed to improve coding reliability. For intra-rater reliability, a random 
sample of 15 assignments were evaluated by the same researcher on two separate occasions, and the results did not vary significantly. 
For inter-rater reliability, two markers independent of the researchers who are also academics performed the ratings across the four 
framework processes on the same sample. The weighted Cohen’s K at 95 % CI was calculated to compare their judgments, resulting in 
substantial agreements across EU (.881, CI [.773, .990]), RF (.798, CI [.615, .982]) and PE (.787 [.615,.959]) and a fair agreement on 
MR (.363, [.091, .636]). There were neither differences in scores between ratings nor personal biases or preferences from the raters 
that would otherwise affect their ratings. The Open Science files for both the coding and inter-rater ratings are shared here.
The assignments were analysed for patterns, explicitly identifying any or all the core tasks presented in Table 1. The analysis aimed 
to answer the following questions: (1) How did the student teams contextualise the problem and did they understand it? (2) Did they 
identify all the relevant variables surrounding the problem? (3) How did they represent the problem, their model and solution? (4) 
Were the solution steps reflective of successful plans of action? (5) How were the results checked and compared with alternative 
solutions (either using sensitivity analysis or other methods)? Finally, did the teams appropriately discuss the managerial implications 
of their solutions and their limitations?

3. Results
All assignments (i.e., paragraphs) strongly indicate EU and RF processes and weak PE and MR (Table 2). The MR result was ex­
pected due to the assignment’s focus on EU and RF. The rubric used, and given to the students in week 1, was also skewed towards 
emphasising problem formulation and solution. Having greater grading weights on these areas could have influenced the length of 
discussion, thus the number of paragraphs, devoted to EU, RF, including some PE processes. However, there are discrepancies in 
performance across 21 core tasks (Table 2) while significant correlations exist between the four processes (Table 3).
The results show that students are generally successful at building representations of the problem (EU1), including a clear idea of 
the available information they have and any assumptions they included if such information was not accessible or unavailable. Model 
building (RF4) was also performed effectively. The assignments clearly demonstrated knowledge of linear programming formulation of 
constraints when building each line of constraints and non-negative equations. This was important for the models to work and can be 
attributed to the four weeks devoted to the topic, the materials used, and the amount of ‘drill’ in problem-solving exercises discussed in 
class where students work in groups, run tests, and work on various problems.
There was also evidence of the effective execution of solutions and the use of tools, such as Excel Solver, LINDO, and other ap­
plications (RF5). For this particular cohort, it could be argued that their Gestalt (Laurillard, 1997) processing is effective, except 
perhaps in determining sub-goals (PE2) or understanding the problem deeply enough to appreciate why optimisation techniques 
would be helpful (EU2). Their information processing (i.e., creating a representation of the problem and solving it) was also effective 
given their graphical and tabular representations, but most especially their network diagrams or decision trees. The latter have been 
used extensively, and for good reason. They are much more complex to build and require substantial logical reasoning, perhaps from a 
motivation to perform well in the assignment, and a Gestalt view to think of the problem as a whole.
Perhaps the most surprising result is the students’ performance on MR3. This received the highest coded proportion of the para­
graphs, which meant that the students could articulate, based on their solutions, the likely impact on the business. This was expressed 
in either cost savings or profitability. Those whose objective functions are neither cost nor profit figures have meant overall improved 
efficiency (e.g., better assignment of people to tasks, rostering or timetabling, employee satisfaction, or resource use, such as the 
number of beds in a hospital ward). The students could draw from the solutions what they would mean for the company and its 
business.
However, there was a significant focus on the constraints in the model and less attention was given to other variables that could 
have been considered, resulting in weaker results on MR2 and MR4. This could be deliberate. Students tend to write little about what 
they could have missed in their models, perhaps to limit the risk of further questions from the marker, change the solutions they 
already have, or see it as a weakness in their model and solutions.
The other tasks worth noting are those that show little evidence of problem-solving. These steps primarily involve taking necessary 
actions to organise a solution, revising when necessary, seeking alternative solutions, and critically evaluating the given information or 
assumptions incorporated into the solution (specifically, RF2, PE4, PE5, and MR4). Describing the rationale behind the problem (EU2) 
could also be a concern, particularly if this relates to testing theoretical or conceptual understanding. Students may view this as less 
important than the model formulation and solution processes, but this process is essential for a full understanding of the problem. It 
directs decision-makers to the relevant problem-solving tools to use.
Lastly, PE2 could be overlooked here, as the prior weeks of learning the topic focused solely on determining a single objective 
function (i.e., one Z equation only). The low number of codes in this core task is most likely explained by the prior learning transfer 
effect (Mayer, 2022, pp. 229–234) or the triggering of memory processes used during those weeks for information recovery and 
transfer (Wiley & Guerrero, 2022, pp. 107–116), which occur at the time of activating problem-solving processes during assignment 
preparation.
4. Discussion
There is evidence of problem-solving processes in the assignments examined in this study using the OECD PISA framework. The 
teams of students demonstrated strong skills in problem identification, model building, and solution impact. They also showed weaker 
performance in critically evaluating the given information and what goes into their solution, ensuring each step is checked for ac­
curacy, revising solutions as necessary, and identifying other variables that could have been included.
There is further evidence of problem-solving processes during the teams’ consultations with their respective lecturer. Often, this 
happens between A1 and A2 or between when feedback on A1 was given and when A2 was submitted. During those weeks, students 
were allowed to present their understanding of their chosen problem, the objective function they wished to pursue, the constraints they

steps, but it also confirmed their understanding of the tools required. This has reinforced EU, RF, PE and MR processes. This feedback

various sources and stakeholders to devise a more informed decision and develop problem-solving skills (Duch et al., 2001; Jackson & 
MacIsaac, 1994).
During these consultation weeks, the most critical steps were those in EU and RF, as students cannot successfully proceed with PE 
and MR if the problem is incorrectly formulated and solved. This is evident when a feasible solution cannot be found, the linearity 
conditions are not satisfied, and no sensitivity analysis was performed. Several ways can go wrong, so effective problem-solving is key, 
particularly in monitoring, as this has been considered important for students’ self-regulation of problem-solving skills (Bars et al., 
2014).
The framework’s unique value and contribution lie in its adaptability to various business analysis contexts, including other 
management or business courses and higher education settings. Business analysts are problem solvers who consider sustainable 
practices and their impact on local communities and the environment. Applying this framework in a classroom may mean that students 
participate in engaging activities that reflect authentic problem-solving and decision-making processes as managers do, which have 
implications for the company, its employees, and the community. This calls for a curriculum redesign with strong alignment between 
learning outcomes, teaching and learning and assessment activities and a greater sense of ownership of case projects that foster 
effective problem-solving.
There is also added value in allowing students some flexibility in choosing case problems that are authentic, such as the various 
applications of linear programming in the assignments assessed in the study, which supports the development of employability skills 
(Sotiriadou et al., 2020). Students will adopt the role of a problem solver and decision maker, visualising the impact of their solutions 
and decisions. This reflects real-world scenarios as decisions have causes, effects, and financial or other implications. The instructor 
also assumes a supervisory role, similar to that of a team leader or consultant in a workplace team setting. This can move students away 
from solely focusing on their assessments for grades to motivating tasks that develop real and effective problem-solving skills, which 
can help reduce problems with team dynamics (Case, 2020; Conley & French, 2014; Lysne et al., 2023). Two examples from the as­
signments reflect this. One student team solved an optimisation problem to reduce the number of days to process immigration visas, 
resulting in fewer delays and thousands of dollars of operational cost savings, while another optimised the staff rostering at a Uniqlo 
store, also resulting in positive gains (e.g., employee satisfaction and efficient staffing on busy store sections).
5. Conclusion
Assessing problem-solving skills using the OECD PISA problem-solving framework is a novel application to management education, 
particularly in business analysis courses. The use of a business analysis assignment in this study has shown strong alignment with the 
four OECD PISA problem-solving framework processes. This framework and its application, underpinned by meaningful problem- 
solving tasks associated with formulation and solution generation, have shown student performance in exploring and understand­
ing, representing and formulating, planning and executing, and monitoring and reflecting.
To conclude, the main contribution of this study is providing a visual representation and application of the OECD PISA problem- 
solving framework in the management education context. It also showed the value of (1) developing engaging and authentic cases that 
trigger problem-solving processes in which students have a voice in selecting cases and problems that they feel motivated to pursue and 
solve, (2) focusing on impactful and meaningful assignments and shift students’ reliance on grades, and (3) offering feedback op­
portunities during milestones to activate problem-solving processes, thereby focusing on both the draft and final assessment artefacts. 
While this study focused on a group assignment in a single subject, it nonetheless contributes to our understanding of specific problem- 
solving processes that occur. Future research could adopt a more nuanced approach by incorporating a diverse cohort of management 
students and extending the descriptive analysis to examine core problem-solving processes alongside variations in student charac­
teristics, majors, skills, and dispositions.
