Competitiveness, country economic inequality and adolescent well-being:
Analysis of 60 countries

A B S T R A C T

Introduction: Competitiveness might impact on adolescent well-being considering the social salience distinct from
the other age groups. However, there is only sizable research on competitiveness and well-being. Also, most
previous studies did not distinguish between individual-level and country-level competitiveness and lacked
consideration of contextual effects such as country inequality.
Methods: Using cross-sectional data from the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA), we analyzed
individual- and country-level competitiveness and its interaction with country inequality in relation to wellbeing (life satisfaction and purpose of life) in approximately 350,000 adolescents from 60 countries. Competitiveness and well-being were assessed via questionnaires. The Gini index retrieved from the World Bank was used
for country inequality. A multilevel model was applied.
Results: Individual-level competitiveness was positively associated with both life satisfaction (β = 0.087, 95%CI
= 0.084 to 0.091) and purpose of life (β = 0.214, 95%CI = 0.211 to 0.217), while country-level competitiveness
was not. Although adolescents in the least unequal countries were satisfied with life the most and those in the
most unequal countries reported the strongest purpose of life, there observed a nonlinear effect modification as a
stronger association in middle unequal countries (p for interaction <0.05) compared to both less and more
unequal countries.
Discussion: Individual-level competitiveness was positively associated with life satisfaction and purpose of life.
The stronger association between competitiveness and well-being in middle unequal countries suggested that
encouraging adolescents to compete within their activities or competition may work to mitigate the impact of
country inequality.

1. Background
Health in adolescence is critical in its own right and for future health
and well-being. However, we still lack knowledge on what is best for
adolescents (Ross et al., 2020; Viner et al., 2012), who might experience
some distinct life from childhood and adulthood since adolescence is a
time of social maturation. They spend more time with their peers and are
subjected to more social engagement and responsibility (Choudhury,
2010). Also, they are more sensitive to social context (Schriber and
Guyer, 2016): adolescents are most vulnerable to peer pressures; and
their behaviors are more likely to be transmitted among friends (Gardner and Steinberg, 2005; Steinberg, 2008). Therefore, determinants of
adolescent well-being should be explored within the context of such
adolescent-specific social life.
Seeking others’ attention by achieving dominance over peers, or

competition is one such social life. Competitiveness can be defined as the
tendency to compete against an object between oneself and others with a
desire to win (Smither and Houston, 1992) and is often perceived as a
risk factor for well-being. Studies of adolescents showed that high
contextual competitiveness (i.e., an environment that favors competition), hypercompetitiveness (i.e., individual personality to show excessive striving to compete), and less self-confidence in competitive
situations were associated with negative psychological states such as
depression, psychological distress, and psychosomatic symptoms (Chan
and Cheung, 2020; McEwan et al., 2012; Piko et al., 2016; Salazar-Ayala
et al., 2021; Skead et al., 2020). These negative impacts might be due to
social insecurity that comes from the feeling of deviating from what they
are expected to be by their society (e.g., being competitive is not
encouraged in one’s society, one’s ability that does not reach that of
their competing peers) (Chan and Cheung, 2020; McEwan et al., 2012;

Piko et al., 2016; Salazar-Ayala et al., 2021; Skead et al., 2020). However, previous studies failed to clearly distinguish between the effects of
a competitive environment and competitiveness as a personal trait,
which would be an important difference to clarify the relationship between competitiveness and adolescent well-being (see Supplementary
Text 1 for a review of this topic).
Competitiveness could be beneficial to well-being since competitive
individuals are more likely to win the competition and achieve what
they want (Casto and Edwards, 2016). This could be held, however, only
if winners are appreciated and got what they want by winning the
competition. Therefore, environments, where competitive individuals
do not receive adequate rewards, might explain the undiscovery of the
potential positive association between individual competitiveness and
well-being. We hypothesized social inequality makes environments not
acknowledge winners with adequate rewards and two mechanisms of
differential associations between individual competitiveness and
well-being by social inequality. The first is regarding the actual change
in rewards. Under extreme inequality, individuals would be treated
unfairly since winning the competition will not give them the same
levels of wealth or status as those at the top of the hierarchy due to a
large gap between the top and the bottom (Andrews and Leigh, 2009);
likewise, in an extremely equal environment, competitive individuals
would be penalized since both winners and losers will end up with the
same level of wealth and status. The second is related to relative
deprivation. Inequality is known to make the difference between self
and others more apparent, and as a result, competitive individuals
would be always pressured by relative deprivation (Kondo et al., 2008;
Smith and Huo, 2014).
The association between social inequality and worse well-being has
been well-established. For example, relative deprivation that individual
experiences would lead to negative emotion including anger and
resentment, which itself has a bad influence on one’s well-being and
potentially provoke aggressive reactions that would damage surrounding people (Smith and Huo, 2014). Another study showed higher relative deprivation independent of absolute income was associated with
worse self-rated health (Kondo et al., 2008). If adolescent well-being
would be benefited from their archives of competition (Casto and
Edwards, 2016; Lu et al., 2013) (hypothesis 1), competitive adolescents
would enjoy well-being more than non-competitive peers in
not-too-inequal nor not-too-equal countries although we do not have a
priori hypothesis on whether this association would be strong enough to
explain away the established damage from inequality. Whereas, if
adolescent well-being is diminished as a difference with peers becomes
evident and social pressure to come over peers gets stronger (hypothesis
2), competitive adolescents would penalize their well-being more than
non-competitive peers in unequal countries.
In this study, we aimed 1) to elucidate the association between individual- and country-level competitiveness and adolescent well-being,
and 2) to examine whether the association between competitiveness and
adolescent well-being differ according to the levels of economic
inequality within each country. We used data from the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA) by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) in 2018 (OECD, 2019a).
This survey covers approximately 710,000 students from diverse cultures, a representative sample of 31 million 15-year-old adolescents
from the 79 participating countries and economies all over the world
(OECD, 2019a). Identifying the link between competitiveness and
well-being in light of the country’s context would provide insights into
improving adolescent health.

2.2. Measurements
2.2.1. Competitiveness
The participants self-valuated how competitive they were. They were
asked whether they agree (“strongly agree”, “agree”, “disagree”,
“strongly disagree”) with the following statements about themselves: “I
enjoy working in situations involving competition with others”; “It is
important for me to perform better than other people on a task”; and “I
try harder when I’m in competition with other people” (OECD, 2019b).
The PISA working group created an individual-level competitiveness
score that has a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 across OECD
countries from the response to these statements (a composite score was
available in the original PISA 2018 dataset). Country-level competitiveness score was calculated by averaging over the individual-level
scores within countries and standardizing, of which the distribution is
shown in Fig. 1. The positive values correspond to being more
competitive than the average students or countries.
2.2.2. Well-being
We obtained a response on items regarding life satisfaction and the
purpose of life as an indicator of well-being. PISA 2018 defines life
satisfaction as an overall evaluation that an individual makes about their
perceived quality of life based on their chosen criteria and asked students to rate their life satisfaction on a scale from 0 (not at all satisfied)
to 10 (completely satisfied) (OECD, 2019b). Purpose of life is considered
as a provider of impetus to set the goal that steers people in positive
directions and as a necessity to achieve meaningful goals and live a
fulfilling life. In PISA 2018, students were asked whether they agree or
disagree with the following statements: “My life has clear meaning or
purpose”; “I have discovered a satisfactory meaning in life”; and “I have
a clear sense of what gives meaning to my life” (OECD, 2019b). The
responses (“strongly agree”, “agree”, “disagree”, strongly disagree”) to
these three questions were combined as a standardized score to have a
mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 across OECD countries (a
composite score was available in the original PISA 2018 dataset). Higher
scores indicate higher levels of life satisfaction and purpose of life.
2.2.3. Country inequality level
The levels of country inequality were defined by the Gini index from
the World Bank (World Bank). Briefly, the Gini index is a summary
measure of the degree of inequality, i.e., the extent to which the distribution of income among individuals or households within an economy deviates from a perfectly equal distribution, thus mostly accounting
for relative wealth (World Bank). The World Bank calculated the Gini
index based on the data on primary household survey by government
statistical agencies and the World Bank country departments. The Gini
index of 0 represents perfect equality while the index of 100 implies
perfect inequality.

2.3. Statistical analysis
First, we analyzed the association between individual- and countrylevel competitiveness and adolescents’ well-being (life satisfaction and
purpose of life) using multilevel models with random intercepts at both
individual- and country-level and fixed effects of all variables. Individual- and country-level competitiveness scores were standardized before
being entered into the model. Then, we stratified the analysis by regions
as well as sex, according to the previous reports on the role of sex in
differences in competitiveness (Casto and Edwards, 2016).

3.1. Competitiveness and well-being of adolescents
Individual-level competitiveness was scored through a self-report
questionnaire and country-level competitiveness score was calculated
by averaging the individual-level scores within countries (Fig. 1). In
countries where there are greater inequalities, adolescents reported a
higher degree of competitiveness and in countries where there are fewer
inequalities, adolescents reported less competitiveness.
Multilevel modeling (linear mixed modeling) revealed the association of individual-level competitiveness with life satisfaction (β = 0.087,
95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.084 to 0.091) and purpose of life (β =
0.214, 95% CI = 0.211 to 0.217), as well as that of country-level
competitiveness with purpose of life (β = 0.115, 95% CI = 0.058 to
0.172), but not with life satisfaction (β = 0.002, 95% CI = − 0.057 to
0.062) (Table 2).
We then stratified countries into three regions (North/South America; Europe/Africa/Middle East; Asia/Oceania) based on geographical
proximity (Supplementary Table 2). We classified countries according to
the World Bank analytical grouping, i.e., North America, Latin America
and Caribbean, Europe and Central Asia, Middle East and North Africa,
Sub-Sahara Africa, South Asia, and East Asia and Pacific. Then, we
defined North/South America, Europe/Africa/Middle East and Asia/
Oceania regions by integrating North America and Latin America and
Caribbean; Europe and Central Asia, Middle East and North Africa and
Sub-Sahara Africa; and South Asia and East Asia and Pacific, respectively due to a small number of participating countries. We confirmed
the same pattern of association between individual- and country-level
competitiveness and well-being of adolescents in these three regions
except for Asia/Oceania, where country-level competitiveness was not
associated with purpose of life (β = 0.084, 95% CI = − 0.178 to 0.346).
Interestingly, we found statistically different associations of individuallevel competitiveness, as adolescents in Asia/Oceania showed stronger
associations of competitiveness personality with well-being (life satisfaction: β = 0.105, 95% CI = 0.097 to 0.113; purpose of life: β = 0.256,
95% CI = 0.249 to 0.264) than their counterparts in Europe/Africa/
Middle East (life satisfaction: β = 0.078, 95% CI = 0.074 to 0.082;
purpose of life: β = 0.202, 95%CI = 0.198 to 0.205) and in North/South
America areas (life satisfaction: β = 0.067, 95% CI = 0.058 to 0.076;
purpose of life: β = 0.182, 95% CI = 0.173 to 0.191, all p-values for
interaction terms < 0.01).

play ambivalent roles, that is, it reflects distrust and strong negative

engagement (Swab and Johnson, 2019). Although our findings highlighted the positive roles of individual-level competitiveness, some
previous studies on adolescents showed competitive personality was

et al., 2012; Piko et al., 2016; Salazar-Ayala et al., 2021), suggesting
effects of pressure from competitors and fear of social rejection. One
longitudinal study of university graduates, however, showed a marginal
association between higher competitiveness and a lower risk of
depression (Lahortiga-Ramos et al., 2018). Competitiveness has multiple
dimensions: e.g., desire to win (feeling the need to win), personal
development (focusing on mastery of a given task), and enjoyment
(positive affection to the competitive process itself but not achievement)
(Swab and Johnson, 2019; Urbig et al., 2021). Therefore, the inconsistent findings may be explained by differences in assessed aspects across
studies. For example, competitiveness that resulted from eagerness to do
better than others could enhance task performance while competitiveness due to fear of being worse than others would impair performance
(Murayama et al., 2021). Also, hypo-competitiveness characterized by
avoidance of social conflict and fear of competition was linked to health
risk behaviors of adolescents, while any types of competitiveness
including enjoyment of competition was linked to more physical activity
(Tari-Keresztes et al., 2015).

3.3. Differential association by sex
A sensitivity analysis that stratified the population by sex suggested a
stronger association of individual-level competitiveness and well-being
in boys (life satisfaction: β = 0.100, 95% CI = 0.095 to 0.105; purpose
of life: 0.239, 95% CI = 0.234 to 0.243) than in girls (life satisfaction: β
= 0.068, 95% CI = 0.063 to 0.072; purpose of life: β = 0.187, 95% CI =
0.183 to 0.191, both p-values for interaction terms for life satisfaction
and purpose of life < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 5).
4. Discussion
Positive associations between individual-level competitiveness and
well-being (i.e., life satisfaction and purpose of life) were found. Associations between individual-level competitiveness and well-being were
heterogeneous across countries, which was partly explained by the degree of country-level inequality. That is, adolescents in middle Gini
index countries showed stronger association compared to those in high
or low Gini index countries, suggesting that not too high nor too low, but
the middle level of the Gini index, may slightly increase the impact of
individual competitiveness on well-being. We note, however, that
country economic inequality exerted still a strong impact on adolescent
well-being: adolescents in less inequal countries reported higher life
satisfaction, and adolescents in more unequal countries reported a

Country-level competitiveness, on the other hand, was associated
with purpose of life, but not with life satisfaction. Competitive environment was associated with lower well-being (Skead et al., 2020),
greater stress (Fletcher et al., 2008), higher anxiety (Gordils et al.,
2021), and more immoral behaviors (Gordils et al., 2021; Han et al.,
2021). However, recent studies incorporating more realistic situations,
including uncertain accomplishment, scarce resources, and familiar
competitors, showed competitive environment increased excitement
and enthusiasm (Swab and Johnson, 2019). Differences in measurement
may also matter: several previous studies utilized personal evaluation of
competitive environmental atmosphere as contextual competitiveness
(Li et al., 2021; Skead et al., 2020), which could be discrepant from the
objective measurement. For example, in the current sample, subjective
competitiveness of environment and objective competitiveness of environment (i.e., aggregated values of individual competitiveness within
countries) showed a lower degree of correlation (r = 0.14), suggesting
that subjective assessment might be biased. In addition, studies utilizing
subjective measurement would suffer from reverse causation. We utilized aggregated competitiveness score as a country-level competitiveness and multilevel modeling to simultaneously and independently
account for individual- and country-level competitiveness, thus managing to somewhat overcome these limitations that existed in the previous studies.
Interestingly, the strength of the association was different by the
levels of the country’s economic inequality. The strongest association
among adolescents in middle Gini index countries supports our hypothesis that personal competitiveness pays off in the medium levels of
unequal contexts. Highly equal environments, such as societies with
welfare systems that emphasize equality, may have detrimental impacts
on the association between competitiveness and well-being since those
who work hard and those who do not do much would be treated equally,
and disincentivize individuals to acquire higher status (Macchia et al.,
2020). Likely, highly unequal countries do not allow those in present
lower status to get future higher status, i.e., low upward mobility, which
may also damage health (Andrews and Leigh, 2009; Browman et al.,
2019). In short, an equal environment demotivates individuals by not
rewarding them for their efforts, while an unequal environment makes
them give up by not offering any hope of earning rewards, both of which
lead to impaired well-being. Concepts of social rank and social comparison also support the modification of the association between
competitiveness and well-being by indicating potential differences in
the levels of involvement in competition (as distinct from individual
competitiveness as a characteristic) due to social inequality. The social
rank theory posits that those of lower social status tend to behave submissively and are suffering from depression and anxiety, while those of
higher status tend to behave aggressively (Gilbert et al., 2002). Therefore, if competition is not guaranteed to bring desirable results, i.e.,
higher status for winners and lower status for losers, they do not engage
in the competition despite their competitive traits. Social comparison is
another important perspective of competition since achievement that
resulted from competition including personal development was often
evaluated by relative scale (Swab and Johnson, 2019). Social comparison theory identifies that comparison and competitiveness become
notable when similar others are involved, i.e., not extremely unequal
condition (Festinger, 1954; Lange et al., 2018) as well as the number of
competitors is not so high (N-effect), i.e., not extremely equal condition
(Garcia and Tor, 2009).
Notably, although associations differed by country economic
inequality levels, overall, adolescents in equal countries reported better
life satisfaction. Numerous health-related outcomes including mental
and physical health status, health behaviors, sociality, and well-being
were worse among people in unequal societies (Kondo et al., 2008;
Smith and Huo, 2014). Counterintuitively, adolescents in unequal
countries reported a higher level of purpose in life. Although the general
population may recognize that an unequal situation was out of their
control (Browman et al., 2019), adolescents, who are new to the social

community and thus might perceive a strong connection between how
they act and what they will achieve in the future, may regard their
current situation as a chance to get upward social mobility and find
motivation and purpose in life (Browman et al., 2019). Further studies
including various metrics of well-being and a wide range of generations
would give insights to explain this discrepancy.
Competitiveness is known to be different between sexes, and we
found a stronger association between individual-level competitiveness
and well-being among boys. This is consistent with a previous study that
reported competitive personality resulted in greater mental health
problems in female college students, but not in males, indicating a
stronger association between competitiveness and social pressure in
females (Chan and Cheung, 2020). Competitiveness is considered to be
biologically driven by testosterone (Casto and Edwards, 2016). The level
of testosterone increases in anticipation of competition, during competition, and further, an increase in testosterone promotes aggressive behaviors (Casto and Edwards, 2016). Although further studies are
needed, female-specific physical characteristics including menstrual
cycle, hormonal birth control, and other hormones such as estradiol may
obscure the relationship between testosterone and competitiveness in
females (Geniole and Carré, 2018).
We note some limitations in the current study. Our study was based
on a self-administered questionnaire that was conducted crosssectionally. Variables of interest, i.e., competitiveness and well-being,
were reported by adolescents themselves. Although we utilized multilevel modeling (linear mixed modeling) with a random intercept for
each country to exclude contextual effects, potential unmeasured confounders such as personality (González-Hernández et al., 2020) may
influence our findings. Also, cross-sectional design limits the inference
of causality due to the missing temporal information. That is, reverse
causation may explain this association: adolescents with poor well-being
may shun competitive situations to avoid losing, and adolescents with
good well-being may achieve something through a competitive situation
and whose competitiveness may be accelerated. Thus, a further longitudinal study is warranted.
In conclusion, we showed that individual-level competitiveness was
associated with better well-being in adolescents. We also showed that
although the strength of the association was stronger in middle Gini
index countries compared to low or high Gini index countries, the
general direction is the same. By and large, adolescents in unequal
countries reported lower levels of life satisfaction and those in equal
countries reported lower levels of purpose of life. On top of this
contextual effect, higher competitiveness would be an important
determinant of adolescent well-being. Adolescents are often engaged in
competitive activities at school, thus intervention aiming to increase
adolescent individual-level competitiveness within such activities may
support adolescent well-being.
