A B S T R A C T
This study transformed the conventional Graphic Combination Task (GCT), a typically used 
creativity assessment tool, into an online testing version. Using the rich behavioral data collected 
from the digital platform, we developed machine learning models (MLMs) to achieve automated 
creativity assessment. The primary objective was to ensure accurate evaluation of individual 
creativity while effectively mitigating the influence of subjectivity and reducing costs. Specif­
ically, the following steps were taken: First, by integrating a literature review, expert evaluations, 
and participant behavioral data, a creativity assessment framework that incorporated process- 
based indicators was constructed. Second, the validity of this framework was evaluated from 
three aspects: content validity, construct validity, and criterion-related validity. Third, several 
features from the framework were selected to train the MLMs. Subsequently, four MLMs were 
compared, and the best-performing model (i.e., the Weighted Ensemble) was identified for future 
automated creativity assessment. Results indicated that this model could accurately evaluate 
creativity with high efficiency and reliability.
Creativity as a crucial skill play a significant part in human development and social progress in the 21st century (Dede, 2010). 
However, as early as Guilford, 1950, Guilford already proposed that creativity is the intellectual ability exhibited by individuals in the 
process of solving problems, generating novel ideas and pioneering new fields (Sternberg, 2003). Creativity is being realized the 
measurability and cultivability by a growing number of people and also prioritized in the blueprint for life. In the OECD’s publication 
"Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2021 Creative Thinking Framework (Third Edition)" released in 2019, 
creativity was recognized as one of the important abilities for assessing global students’ problem-solving skills. Creativity also serves 
society (Cropley, 2006). It has always been seen as a vital resource in business and government (Higgins, 1994). Accurate and efficient 
evaluation and measurement of creativity is a prerequisite and foundation for cultivating creativity. It is only through accurate 
assessment of individuals’ creativity that we can effectively undertake screening, cultivation, and placement of creative talents across 
diverse fields. Creativity, as a complex and multidimensional higher-order cognitive ability, has long been debated regarding its 
assessment paradigms. Divergent thinking (DT), which refers to the ability to generate multiple solutions to open-ended problems 
(Guilford, 1966; Runco, 2008), captures only the ideation phase of creativity rather than its entirety. Despite measuring just one facet

1. The classical creativity assessment
The assessment of creativity is typically conducted through DT tasks, which use open-ended tasks to quantify the fluency and 
originality of ideas (Organisciak et al., 2023). The theoretical foundation of DT can be traced back to Guilford’s Structure of Intellect 
(SOI) model, which proposed DT as a core operationalized indicator of creativity (Guilford, 1988). Guilford proposed three dimensions 
of divergent thinking: fluency, originality, and flexibility. These three dimensions form an essential framework for assessing creativity. 
Fluency refers to an individual’s ability to generate a large number of ideas in a short amount of time; originality emphasizes the 
novelty and statistical infrequency of ideas; flexibility reflects an individual’s capacity to shift between different conceptual categories 
in their thinking. Collectively, these dimensions constitute a multifaceted evaluation of creative potential, offering educators and 
researchers better actionable insights for nurturing creativity. Building upon this foundation, the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking 
(TTCT) emerged as a prototypical DT assessment, establishing standardized scoring systems through figural and verbal tasks such as 
picture completion and unusual uses (Torrance, 1972). Meanwhile, the Wallach-Kogan tests operationalized DT measurement through 
the Alternative Uses Task (AUT), specifically designed to minimize cognitive constraints during ideation (Cheung & Lau, 2010).
While DT has long dominated creativity assessment, researchers increasingly recognize the essential role of convergent thinking 
(CT), defined as the capacity to integrate information and identify optimal solutions (Cropley, 2006). Runco (2007) and Runco and 
Acar (2012) argues that although creativity research has historically neglected the evaluative function of CT, it is critical during idea 
selection and refinement, where individuals must apply CT to assess and choose among DT-generated ideas. CT assessments incor­
porate several representative measurements, including but not limited to the Remote Associates Test (RAT; Mednick, 1962) for 
evaluating conceptual integration through word associations, as well as creative problem-solving tasks like Duncker’s candle problem 
that assess practical insight formation (Fleck & Weisberg, 2004). Subseguently, neuroscientific evidence further indicates that in­
dividuals with high creative potential exhibit coordinated activation patterns between neural networks underlying both divergent and 
convergent cognitive processes (Beaty et al., 2018), pointing to their functional interdependence in creative cognition.
Most creativity assessments, particularly those measuring DT, face two major challenges: subjectivity in evaluation and high 
implementation costs (Barbot, 2018). The first challenge involves establishing inter-rater reliability in determining what constitutes 
creative responses. The second stems from the labor-intensive nature of manual scoring, which creates significant barriers to 
large-scale administration due to expenses associated with rater training, paper-based testing, and evaluation procedures.
2. Automated creativity assessment
Automated scoring methods are increasingly developed to address the limitations of subjective evaluation. Unlike manual scoring, 
these "data-driven" approaches utilize machine learning and textual analysis techniques to generate innovation metrics.
In recent studies, semantic network analysis has emerged as a promising approach for assessing creativity. In fact, Paulus et al. 
(1970) had already achieved the automated scoring of DT by utilizing text data such as word count and average word length. Currently, 
the most widely used technique is Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA), which evaluates the remoteness or novelty of ideas by calculating 
the semantic distance between responses. For instance, Johnson et al. (2023) employed Divergent Semantic Integration (DSI), utilizing 
transformer-based neural networks to analyze the distances between all words in a text, thereby assessing the degree of connection 
between different ideas. The study found that DSI could explain 72 % of the variance in human raters’ creativity evaluations of 
narratives. Multiple studies have demonstrated a high correlation between semantic distance measures and human ratings (Hass, 
2017; Kenett, 2019). Moreover, large language models (LLMs) can enhance traditional semantic analysis approaches through su­
pervised fine-tuning, transforming them from descriptive tools into predictive models optimized for specific tasks. Researchers have 
successfully used LLMs to improve automated scoring performance in creativity assessment. For instance, fine-tuned versions of 
RoBERTa (r = 0.72) and GPT-2 (r = 0.70) significantly outperformed conventional semantic distance methods (r = 0.42) in evaluating 
metaphorical creativity. These models also demonstrated robust generalization to novel and untrained metaphor prompts (DiStefano 
et al., 2024).
In addition to automated assessment using the LLMs, machine learning models (MLMs) have demonstrated substantial utility in the 
automated assessment of non-verbal creative tasks. Kovalkov et al. (2021) pioneered the use of MLMs to assess creativity in Scratch 
programming projects, aiming to develop automated alternatives to expert evaluations. Using the XGBoost model to predict creativity 
scores, the authors evaluated model performance using Kendall’s τ, achieving a value above 0.41, surpassing the inter-rater agreement 
among most expert pairs. Their work represents a significant advancement in computational creativity assessment. However, the 
requirement for programming knowledge limits participant accessibility. Alternative approaches, such as behavioral metrics in 
game-based assessments, have also emerged. For example, Shute and Moore (2017) developed Newton’s Playground (NP), a 
problem-solving game that evaluates creativity by analyzing process data from participants’ interactions. The engaging nature of the 
game enhances participants’ motivation and focus, though prior physics knowledge may influence creative performance (Shute & 
Wang, 2016). These studies demonstrate that creativity measurement is often shaped by researchers’ objectives, available tools, and 
participant characteristics. It should be noted that domain-specific and domain-general tools each serve distinct but equally valuable 
purposes. Domain-specific measures (e.g., Newton’s Playground) excel at capturing contextualized creative performance within 
particular fields, while domain-general tools (e.g., GCT) can assess transferable creative cognition (e.g., ideational originality) theo­
rized to underlie cross-context creative potential (Guilford, 1967; Runco, 2008). This distinction is particularly relevant for

Recent studies have investigated machine learning approaches to automate the assessment of the Test of Creative Thinking- 
Drawing Production (TCT-DP). Cropley et al. (2024) developed a neural network-based system that classifies drawings into the

automated scoring method differs from manual scoring based on the TCT-DP manual (Urban & Jellen, 1996), it retains the inherent 
measurement uncertainty of the test while enhancing efficiency. The model provides both categorical classifications (e.g., A-G) and 
continuous numerical scores (e.g., 0–66), ensuring maintained assessment resolution. As a holistic creativity test, the TCT-DP captures 
both cognitive (e.g., DT) and non-cognitive aspects of creative potential.
Despite the progress made in the current automated creativity assessment methods, they still face some inadequacies. Firstly, 
current automated assessment systems exhibit substantial dependency on machine learning performance, which is inherently con­
strained by both the quantity and quality of training datasets. As demonstrated by Hoorn (2023), even state-of-the-art computer vision 
systems fundamentally depend on recursive adjustments to training data patterns, which inherently limits their capacity to handle 
novel response categories. Secondly, these assessments primarily evaluate an individual’s thinking process based on static test results 
(Getzels & Jackson, 1962). This approach ignores the dynamic nature of the thinking process and poses challenges in ensuring the 
validity of creativity evaluations, as it fails to capture the fluid characteristics of creativity (Walia, 2019). Thirdly, the current auto­
mated assessments predominantly focus on verbal tasks, extraneous variables such as participants’ linguistic competence and cultural 
background may confound the measurement of creative performance. These methodological constraints underscore the critical need to 
develop diversified assessment tools to advance the field of automated creativity evaluation.
3. Present research
Graphic Creativity Tests (GCT) have attracted significant research attention due to their unique advantages in assessing creative 
potential. Kim (2017) found that the TTCT-Figural demonstrates greater comprehensiveness, reliability, and effectiveness in 
measuring creative potential compared to its verbal counterpart (i.e., TTCT-Verbal). As visual creativity is strongly associated with DT 
(Wang et al., 2023), GCT specifically evaluates individuals’ ability to employ visual elements, offering a more comprehensive 
assessment of creative potential. Unlike traditional verbal measures, these graphic-based assessments can capture distinct aspects of 
creativity that might otherwise remain undetected (e.g., visual synthesis of shapes, spatial innovation), thereby providing a more 
complete evaluation of creative capabilities. Moreover, empirical evidence supports a robust connection between visual creativity and 
visual mental imagery (Palmiero et al., 2015), further substantiating the validity of GCT. Visual creativity extends beyond artistic skill 
to encompass cognitive processes fundamental to DT, including idea generation and evaluation. Given this perspective, GCT serves as a 
valuable tool for both understanding and fostering visual creativity, particularly in educational contexts where cultivating creative 
thinking is essential.
Given many advantages of the GCT, this study aims to develop an automated assessment tool based on GCT to address critical 
limitations in traditional DT evaluation, particularly concerning subjectivity and high manual costs. The integration of real-time 
behavioral data streams with expert ratings enables the construction of MLMs for objective and instantaneous DT assessment. The 
proposed MLMs reduce reliance on manual scoring while enhancing participant engagement through gamification design, making it 
particularly suitable for large-scale online assessment scenarios. Furthermore, this work contributes to the expanding repertoire of 
automated creativity assessment methodologies.
To achieve these objectives, two sequential studies were conducted. In Study 1, an online creativity assessment task, primarily 
designed to measure DT, was first conceived and developed, along with an assessment framework that integrates process information 
based on GCT. The assessment framework was initially developed by distilling key indicators associated with fluency, originality, and 
flexibility from existing studies. Subsequently, the reliability and validity of this framework were rigorously examined using standard 
psychometric techniques, encompassing content validity, discriminant validity, construct validity, and criterion validity. Through 
refinement and adjustment of these indicators, the process ultimately yielded a creativity framework that not only aligns with expert 
consensus but also exhibits robust psychometric properties. In Study 2, the distinctive capabilities of MLMs were utilized, particularly 
their proficiency in handling complex datasets, rapid learning adaptation, and transparent decision-making processes. Using the 
behavioral features identified in Study 1, multiple MLMs were trained and compared. Then, the optimal performing model was 
selected for automated scoring. This advanced modeling approach enables efficient evaluation of creative tasks and generation of 
precise individual final feedback, thereby providing researchers and educators with enhanced assessment tools while offering par­
ticipants meaningful developmental insights.
4. Study 1
4.1. Constructing the GCT
4.1.1. Test format of the GCT
The GCT prototype is a type of test within the TTCT assessment (Torrance, 2017), and we have developed it in a computerized 
format. Specifically, in the assessment, participants are motivated to derive meaning from randomness. They are prompted to use three 
given graphics (e.g., circle, triangle, and square) to create a novel work. The shape of the base graphic could be modified by dragging

integrates the concept of assembling incomplete information within a given structure, similar to the Picture Completion task 
(Torrance, 1964). Participants can modify the size, color, and position of the given graphic, fostering creativity while maintaining the 
base form. Upon starting the game, a 10-minute countdown timer appears in the upper right corner to manage time constraints. The 
bottom of the page features four function keys: "Copy," "Delete," "Adjust Hierarchy," and "Color." The "Adjust Hierarchy" option allows 
participants to move the graphic forward, backward, to the top, or to the bottom. By selecting "Color," participants can choose from 
nine colors, including black, white, red, orange, yellow, green, blue, and purple. It aligns with the concept of The Circles/Lines Activity 
from the TTCT-Figural, inspiring participants to create new works based on existing ones (Torrance, 1964) and break free from the 
limitations of two-dimensional space by using multiple colors to revamp and innovate. Table 1 provides the task description, while

Throughout the development process of the test interface, we started from practical experience to meet the following requirements: 
Firstly, the interface must ensure cross-platform usability, meaning it should be extendable for use on mobile terminals such as Android 
and iOS. Secondly, the system should record and save participants’ actions and their sequence. In the event of an accidental exit or 
network interruption, the system automatically saves the responses. Participants can resume answering after re-entering the interface. 
Thirdly, when participants need to perform a criterion validation, a prompt window will pop up after completing the GCT, providing a 
5-minute break to avoid fatigue effects. If no rest is needed, participants can click "Continue Test."
The development process is as follows: Firstly, formulate a development plan, explain the functional requirements of the GCT to the 
designers, communicate design intentions, and discuss applicable development technologies. Secondly, design the interface, ensuring 
that the interface aligns with the initial intentions of the task through communication and modifications. Next, programmers write 
programs based on the plan, followed by detailed testing of the task. This involves verifying the proper functionality of all components, 
ensuring the database accurately records the operational processes, and evaluating the stability and smoothness of the interface. 
Feedback results are used for the next round of modifications until the interface fully aligns with the intended design and meets the 
practical needs of the test.
4.1.2. Assessment framework of the GCT
Based on the TTCT (Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking), in the GCT, we recorded each procedural behavior of participants during 
the test. Key features were labeled and categorized into three dimensions: fluency, flexibility, and originality. During the dimension 
categorization phase, we extensively referenced prior research on the scoring of the DT. Specifically, current DT assessments tend to 
focus on the fluency, flexibility, and originality of the test, with only a few studies addressing precision as a dimension (Hornberg & 
Reiter-Palmon, 2017). Consequently, our creativity framework also concentrates solely on these three dimensions.
Fluency is a criterion that measures the quantity of relevant responses, and the most commonly used feature in the DT test is the 
number of responses generated by participants, which is also the most easily operationalized metric. Barbot (2018) suggested that 
fluency might best be defined as the time required to generate a predetermined number of responses. Therefore, this commonly used 
time metric has been incorporated into the framework. Accordingly, for the fluency dimension, features include the quantity of 
graphics, the quantity of colors, and the average time for each action.
Flexibility is a measure of the diversity of categories or responses. The features commonly used in the DT test are typically related to 
the diversity of responses, often determined through rating-based or frequency-based methods. Following this logic, the GCT evalu­
ation framework incorporates features related to participant’s operations in graphic size, direction, rotation angle, scaling ratio, and 
other manipulations into consideration.
Originality typically refers to the quantity of responses in different categories, but its meaning can vary across different tasks in this 
dimension, exhibiting potential domain differences (Butler et al., 2003; Reiter-Palmon et al., 2009). In the GCT, we refer to the 
standards of extending or breaking boundaries emphasized in the TTCT, and assess the originality of the participants from the dif­
ferences in the process data. This means evaluating the level of originality among participants in terms of their specific operations in 
composing graphics, such as changing the size, area, or overlap between graphics can create a sense of hierarchy and space. In 
summary, the preliminary creativity assessment framework is showed in Supplementary Table 1.

Materials. GCT and creativity assessment framework. For details, see the "Constructing the GCT" section above. Then, we select two 
assessments for criterion validation: an Improvement Toy Task and the Picture Completion Task.
Improvement Toy Task. The task has been widely used as an effective assessment tool and criterion questionnaire in previous 
creativity research, with demonstrated high reliability and validity (Gilhooly et al., 2007). In this task, participants are presented with 
a simple drawing of a toy elephant and are asked to devise the most creative, interesting, and unconventional ways to enhance the toy, 
making it more enjoyable for children. The scores obtained from this task are evaluated based on three dimensions: originality 
(assessing the ability to generate novel ideas and products distinct from conventional ones), flexibility (measuring the number of 
answers generated across various categories), and fluency (counting the total valid answers provided).
Picture Completion Task. This task requires participants to complete drawings on various curves. Its requirements and test format 
of this figural test are highly similar to the GCT, which is the key reason for choosing it as the criterion validation tool. The task is 
scored on five dimensions: fluency (the quantity of drawings), originality (the uniqueness of drawings), elaboration (the level of detail 
in drawings), title abstraction (the abstractness of the drawing titles), and resistance to premature closure (the comparison fluency 
between closed and open levels of curves in the drawings). To better correspond with the dimensions of the GCT, we select the fluency 
and originality from the task in this study.
Data Rating. The scoring process of criterion assessments and the GCT is carried out by a team of three psychology graduate 
students, all of whom had undergone comprehensive training and adhered to a standardized scoring protocol. All three raters scored 
independently. In the GCT, the performance of each participant is evaluated by three psychology graduate students across three di­
mensions: fluency (e.g., the number of graphics used), originality (e.g., the uniqueness of graphic combinations), and flexibility (e.g., 
the variety of graphics and colors employed). Scores for each dimension range from 0 to 3, with higher scores reflecting greater 
proficiency in that dimension. For each dimension, the mean score from the three raters is calculated to determine the final score for 
that dimension. Finally, the aggregate of the three scores across all dimensions constitutes the participant’s overall creativity score.
Analysis. Data analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.2.2; R Core Team, 2023). We utilized the lavaan package 
(Rosseel, 2012) for structural equation modeling and the psych package (Revelle, 2024) for psychometric analysis. Data wrangling, 
tidying, and visualization were performed using the dplyr (Wickham et al., 2023), tidyr (Wickham et al., 2024), broom (Robinson 
et al., 2024), purrr (Wickham & Henry, 2023), and semPlot (Epskamp, 2022) packages. All data and code have been made openly 
accessible and are available at: https://github.com/gsq7474741/ML4GCT. In order to check the consistency and reliability of the 
scorers’ ratings, the Cronbach’s α coefficients among the scorers are calculated (Furnham & Bachtiar, 2008). Model evaluation is 
conducted using the fit indices and information criteria: Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square 
Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Acceptable model fit is typically indicated by 
CFI and TLI values exceeding 0.90, RMSEA values below 0.90 indicating good fit (Jackson et al., 2009), and SRMR values below 0.08 
indicating good fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999).
Criterion validity: to validate the effectiveness of the GCT as a measure of creativity, a correlation analysis can be conducted 
between participants’ performance on the task and the criterion assessments.
Process. Firstly, the validity of the creativity assessment framework was assessed by multiple experts in the field of creativity, and 
modifications were made based on their recommendations. Subsequently, participants were recruited to take the GCT test online. 
Regular contact with the participants is maintained through an online chat group, where they are provided with standardized in­
structions regarding the assessment process, timing, and other important considerations. Upon completing the task, participants are 
compensated with a certain amount of remuneration, serving both as a reward for their participation and as motivation to ensure their 
engagement to the study. During the GCT test process, articipants will access the GCT interface through WeChat and will be provided 
with comprehensive task instructions before starting the assessment. These instructions will offer clear guidance on how to use the 
various function buttons in the GCT and how to submit their work. Upon clicking "Start Test," participants will enter the operation 
page, where a 10-minute countdown timer will commence in the upper right corner. Once the task is completed, participants can click 
"Finish" and upload their work. Finally, participants will be asked to provide their personal information. After successful submission, 
they will be redirected to the initial page. Lastly, for the examination of criterion-related validity, 300 participants out of 700 valid 
participants were randomly selected for criterion assessment.

Expert Rating Scores. The results indicate that all the coefficients exceeded 0.80, with most of them surpassing 0.90. These high 
reliability coefficients demonstrate that the scorers’ ratings are consistent and reliable in evaluating participants’ creativity. These 
results also fall within the acceptable range of scorer reliability, as suggested by Baer et al. (2004) and Landis and Koch (1977).

(a) For dimension A, particularly regarding the definition of "fluency" as the "ability to quickly build a large number of graphics,"

it to "ability to quickly build different graphics." The term "different" provides better clarity and specificity in distinguishing the 
usage of the three basic graphics.
(b) For feature A1, which measures "average operating time," the experts recommend modifying its explanation to "total time spent 
on composition." This change aligns it with other computed total features within the framework. Accordingly, the explanation 
for the rotation angle of graphics will also be adjusted accordingly to ensure consistency.
(c) For dimension B, the experts propose introducing new features related to the presence of functional keys on the operating 
interface. These features include "number of deletions," "number of copies," and "number of changes in hierarchy." They are 
commonly used functional keys during the operation process, they can serve as reflections of the participants’ flexibility.
(d) For feature B2, "changing the size of the graphic," the term "size" has been identified as unclear, requiring a more specific and 
quantitative expression of the feature for process data. It is more intuitive and specific to measure the scale of scaling the basic 
graphics. Thus, the suggestion from the experts to modify it to "changing the scale of the graphic" is accepted.
(e) Duplicate features are identified and subsequently removed. Upon considering the task format and the data types that can be 
recorded, it becomes evident that B3, "changing the graphic orientation" duplicates B4 and B5, which are related to rotation. As 
every instance of rotating the graphics results in a change in orientation, this redundancy can be avoided. Thus, the experts’ 
suggestion is accepted, and B3 is removed from the framework, streamlining the assessment process.
(f) For feature B6, which measures "graphic scaling ratio", the experts suggest reclassifying it under the dimension of originality. 
The reasoning is that in the actual operating interface, there are evident differences in the composition range among different 
participants, making it more relevant to originality than flexibility. Considering this input, the experts’ recommendation is 
accepted.
(g) The experts indicate that the rationality of C1, "number of graphic coverage," and C2, "area of graphic coverage," falls slightly 
below a rating value of 3. However, they recommend retaining these features with modifications and further validating their 
reasonableness during subsequent data analysis. Therefore, C1 is changed to "composition range", while C2 is modified to 
"square root of the degree of overlap" ultimately. Finally,the creativity assessment framework modified by expert suggestions is

5.1.2. Model
Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) is a statistical method that examines the linear relationship between a dependent variable and 
multiple independent variables (Zhang et al., 2019). In this study, MLR is utilized to investigate the influence of various features on 
both the total creativity score and three dimensional scores. The objective of MLR is to construct a regression model as a baseline to 
assess the impact of each feature on the creativity scores.
CatBoost is a gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT) framework based on oblivious trees, which requires fewer parameters, 
supports categorical variables, and achieves high accuracy (Dorogush et al., 2018). This algorithm supports categorical variables and 
addresses the issues of gradient bias and prediction shift, thereby reducing overfitting and improving the accuracy and generalization 
ability of the algorithm. In this study, out of the eleven features, seven are count features, which is why it is chosen.1
Weighted Ensemble: Combining the predictions of multiple models tends to outperform individual models and often significantly 
reduces the variance of the final predictions (Zuckerman & Chong, 2017). The three commonly used methods for model combination 
are bagging (Breiman, 1996), boosting (Cao et al., 2010) and stacking (Ting & Witten, 1997). In this study, an improved version of 
stacking, a greedy ensemble method known as Weighted Ensemble (Caruana et al., 2004), is employed, achieving superlinear scaling 
and higher accuracy compared to ordinary parallel simulations (Zuckerman & Chong, 2017) .2 Therefore, this study attempts to use 
this model, hoping to leverage its potential to reduce model variance, enhance predictive performance, exploit the advantages of 
hyperlinear scaling, and achieve superior performance compared to individual models.
LightGBM model belongs to the ensemble learning models and has advantages such as fast training speed and high accuracy (Ke 
et al., 2017). In addition, when dealing with datasets that have many sparse features, this model benefits from the application of 
Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB) to reduce dimensionality and optimize model performance. The use of Gradient-based One-Side

Two indicators, namely the coefficient of determination (R²) and the root mean square error (RMSE), are used to evaluate the

conducted on the results between MLMs, examining whether the means of R² for any two models under the same settings are 
significantly different. By fixing the same training conditions for all models, each model will be trained and tested 100 times with 
different random seeds. Independent sample t-tests will be performed on the every array of experimental results group by model. The 
null hypothesis is that the means of R² for any two models are equal.

6. General discussion
Creativity represents a complex and highly abstract cognitive ability. Traditional paper-based assessment methods face well- 
documented limitations, including test anxiety among participants (Cassady & Johnson, 2002) and resource-intensive administra­
tion requirements (Kwon, 1996). The emergence of computational psychometrics has introduced innovative approaches through 
process data analysis, offering transformative potential for conventional assessment paradigms. As emphasized in the Guidelines for 
Psychological Big Data Research, process data provides researchers with unprecedented opportunities for both data-driven method­
ological advances and theoretical innovation (Chen & Wojcik, 2016). Process data encompasses not only behavioral actions and verbal 
language but also various data that can reflect complex thinking processes, such as response time (Van Der Linden, 2009) and 
eye-tracking (Gorin, 2006). Compared to traditional assessment techniques, the integration of data-driven and theory-guided ap­
proaches enables more sophisticated extraction of behavioral features. This methodological synergy offers valuable insights for 
measuring higher-order cognitive skills, particularly creative thinking abilities.

6.1. Evaluation task development
There is currently limited research on creativity assessment based on process data. And existing forms of creativity assessment 
based on process data often come with certain limitations. For instance, some assessments may be problem-solving oriented (Torrance, 
1972), focusing primarily on participants’ from specific cultural backgrounds (Kim, 2010), or requiring specific disciplinary abilities 
(Noh & Lee, 2020). These limitations can potentially restrict participants’ creativity and hinder a comprehensive understanding of 
their creative potential. However, the GCT devised in this study has successfully overcome these limitations. Designed as an engaging 
game-like activity, GCT offers participants an enjoyable and interactive assessment experience. Unlike problem-solving-oriented as­
sessments, this task provides a more open-ended and flexible approach, allowing participants to explore and express their creativity 
within a relatively structured framework rather than rigid constraints. Moreover, it does not rely on specific cultural backgrounds, 
making it applicable and relevant to participants from diverse cultural contexts.
6.2. Establishment of evaluation framework
Both traditional and computer-based assessment methods rely on establishing valid connections between observable indicators and 
target constructs. While computerized platforms can systematically capture behavioral process data, the fundamental challenge lies in 
developing robust psychometric linkages between these process indicators and the underlying cognitive abilities they purport to 
measure. This evidentiary connection forms the essential foundation for task-based measurement (Schum, 1987).
Moreover, existing frameworks for creativity assessment that incorporate process information are also limited, mostly limited to 
closed-ended tasks with clear behavioral paths (standard answers). Closed-ended standard answers have certain limitations on the 
development and measurement of the creativity (Niu et al., 2007; Henriksen et al., 2020).
Study 1 employed computational psychometrics theory (Davier, 2017) to construct and refine a creativity assessment framework 
based on an online GCT through a combined theory-driven and data-driven approach. Specifically, creativity experts evaluated the 
validity and rationality of participants’ behavioral data streams during task performance. The selected features aligned with creative 
properties were incorporated into the assessment framework, while other irrelevant behavioral features were excluded from the final 
evaluation framework.
Traditional creativity assessments typically focus solely on evaluating the final products, whereas the GCT introduces an innovative 
approach by comprehensively documenting behavioral data throughout the creative process. This study analyzes not only the 
completed products but also systematically collects real-time operational actions (e.g., the order of graphic selection, modification 
frequency, et al.). These process data, when combined with traditional outcome scoring, provide a more comprehensive understanding 
of the dynamic characteristics of creativity. This includes revealing cognitive flexibility through element-switching patterns, 
demonstrating iterative refinement strategies in composition development, and capturing decision-making processes under constraints 
such as color selection. This approach effectively addresses the limitations of traditional creativity assessments in capturing the dy­
namic nature of creative thinking.
6.3. Selection of automatic evaluation models
Creativity is a multidimensional and complex assessment structure, and selecting an appropriate assessment model is crucial. 
Therefore, the chosen MLMs include both linear and nonlinear models, as such a cross-complexity comparison algorithm is better 
suited to reveal the relationships between the data. Using only linear relationships would overlook nonlinear effects, while relying 
solely on nonlinear relationships might lead to obtain results that are more complex than the actual relationships (Jacobucci & Grimm, 
2020). This also suggests that the "best" model to some extent depends on the measurement of data variables and the selection of model 
types.
Through regression analysis, the accurate quantitative relationships between features and the degree of influence of different 
features on creativity values are investigated. The results show that the MLR fits well, although the performance in the originality 
dimension is relatively poor. Taking into account the participants’ behavioral process data collected in this study, an attempt is made 
to assess creativity using other MLMs such as CatBoost, Weighted Ensemble, and LightGBM. The results reveal that using these MLMs to 
assess creativity, a complex ability, yields better predictive results. Through pairwise comparisons between the models, it is found that 
the Weighted Ensemble model outperforms other models. Importantly, the GCT developed in this study demonstrates significant 
practical advantages. It transcends temporal and spatial constraints to expand assessment applicability. Additionally, its ability to 
provide instant scoring feedback upon online completion facilitates large-scale standardized administration. Collectively, these fea­
tures position the GCT as an innovative tool for advancing creativity assessment in future practice.
6.4. Limitation and future direction
The forms of assessment tasks need to be diversified. Although the GCT possesses certain strengths in terms of process data 
recording and the flexibility of assessment, it also has several limitations. Specifically, the online GCT developed in this study primarily 
uses graphics to test participants’ divergent thinking. It employs basic graphic elements such as circles, triangles, and rectangles, and 
the possibilities for shape transformation are limited. Additionally, only seven basic colors are used, which is not very diverse. To more 
comprehensively assess creativity, future applications of this test could consider combining the GCT with other assessment methods, 
especially existing automated ones, such as CT tests and verbal divergent tasks. Also, usefulness is a critical dimension of creative

output, but its exploration falls outside the scope of the current study. To determine the usefulness of creativity assessment in the GCT 
task, expert ratings of participants’ product names and descriptions are essential, along with training language-based machine learning 
models for automated evaluation. This process entails considerable complexity and cannot be succinctly addressed here. We further 
intend to investigate this direction in future study.
The framework for evaluating creativity needs to be enriched and improved. The creativity evaluation framework constructed, 
which integrates process data, is based on the GCT. All features depend on the evaluation form of the task, that is, the behavior that 
participants can observe when operating the task. Therefore, this framework is difficult to generalize to other creativity evaluations. 
That is to say, the evaluation framework is only useful for the GCT. In addition, the number of features developed by this study may be 
limited and may not cover all behavioral manifestations. Future research can expand and improve the dimensions and features of the 
evaluation framework, and on the other hand, build a more universal evaluation framework.
Further research is needed on predictive models based on process data. The study is based on the paradigm of evaluating the 
creative process, but the evaluation of individual creativity should place equal emphasis on both the process and the outcome. Both 
focusing solely on the outcome and the process cannot comprehensively evaluate the creativity. Therefore, in the future, static results 
can also be included in the model, such as using graphic recognition technology, and the frequency of participants created drawings 
appearing in all works as an originality feature. Utilize more information to construct predictive models, further explore individual 
cognitive thinking processes, and more comprehensively reflect the creativity.
It should be noted that the definition of originality remains multidimensional and complex, making it challenging to establish a 
fully universal and precise operational definition at this stage, as demonstrated in the physics playground study where originality was 
defined as the difference between a solution’s ball trajectory and the expected trajectory (Shute & Wang, 2016). The complexity of 
originality is well-documented in prior research. For example, Shibayama and Wang (2020) operationalized originality as the 
betweenness centrality of a paper in a citation network, yet their empirical results showed this metric only effectively captured 
theoretical originality (r = 0.142, p < 0.001), not methodological originality (p > 0.1). This highlights the multidimensional nature of 
originality. Further, Shibayama and Wang (2020) emphasized that the contradiction in defining originality lies in its multidimen­
sionality, domain specificity, measurement limitations, and the interaction between subjective and objective evaluations. Future 
research should further explore how to balance these dimensions in operational definitions.
7. Concluding remarks
This study investigates a novel process-data-based approach to creativity assessment through the development and validation of the 
GCT, utilizing process data analysis and MLM construction. By verifying the validity and feature analysis of the creativity framework, it 
is proven that the GCT can effectively measure the creativity in three dimensions: originality, flexibility, and fluency. In addition, by 
comparing the performance of different MLMs, it is found that the Weighted Ensemble model performs the best in predicting creativity 
scores.
Overall, this study provides a feasible method for evaluating creativity based on process data, and provides an effective tool and 
theoretical basis for measuring and evaluating the creativity. This is of great significance for psychology researchers and educational 
practitioners in evaluating and promoting the development of creativity, and also provides new ideas and directions for future related 
research and applications.
