Teaching practices are considered critical in promoting students’ reading literacy; however, research remains
inconclusive regarding the structures of effective teaching practices across countries. Using data from PISA 2018,
this study explores the commonalities and differences in student-perceived teaching practices across the 16 topperforming countries and regions through hierarchical clustering analysis and social network analysis. Four
patterns of teaching practices were identified: teaching-practice-oriented, teacher-oriented, student-oriented,
and minimalistic approaches. Network analyses revealed strong positive correlations among practices across
most countries, with the structures of these networks varying in ways that reflected their respective pedagogical
foci. The results underscore the importance of teacher support, a critical factor that is strongly linked to other
teaching practices and is effective in enhancing reading literacy.

1. Introduction
Reading literacy refers to the ability to comprehend, use, evaluate,
reflect on, and engage with texts to achieve one’s personal goals,
develop one’s knowledge and potential, and participate in society (Mo,
2019). It is a critical skill needed for students to access subject-specific
content, develop critical thinking, and foster lifelong learning dispositions (Heyne et al., 2023). Research has consistently highlighted the
pivotal role teachers play in shaping student learning outcomes
(Cordero & Gil-Izquierdo, 2018), with teacher-related factors accounting for approximately 15–25 % of outcome variance (van de Grift,
2014). Teachers’ implementation of high-quality instructional strategies
and use of effective materials benefit students’ cognitive and metacognitive learning, thereby facilitating comprehension and knowledge
retention (López-Martín et al., 2023; Taylor, 2023). Additionally, by

creating a supportive learning environment, teachers can motivate and
engage students, which indirectly affects their learning performance (Li
& Xue, 2023).
The importance of teaching practices in promoting student learning
has been widely recognized; however, there is ongoing debate about
whether specific teaching strategies are universally effective. From a
theoretical perspective, the concept of effective teaching practices is
rooted in educational, psychological, and sociocultural theories, which
can be generally categorized into two philosophies: instructionism and
constructivism (Vieluf et al., 2012). The former emphasizes the teacher’s
role in using clear instruction to communicate knowledge to students
and maintain student concentration in the classroom, underscoring the
importance of classroom discipline, teacher support, and structure. The
latter posits that students are active learners rather than passive recipients, highlighting the teacher’s role in providing adaptive

Large-scale international assessments, such as the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA), offer empirical evidence to
identify effective teaching practices and inform educational policy
through standardized evaluation data that transcend cultural and
contextual boundaries. For example, Lau and Lam’s (2017) analysis of
PISA 2015 data found that adaptive instruction, teacher-directed instruction, and interactive application were positively associated with
students’ science performance. Similarly, based on PISA 2015 data,
Chen et al. (2021) found that students’ perceived feedback, instructional
adaptation, and teacher-directed instruction were among the most significant predictors of student performance. These findings suggest the
potential for certain teaching practices to be universally effective,
providing insights for educational policymaking.
Despite discussion on the commonality of effective teaching strategies, teaching is a culturally embedded practice that is deeply influenced
by educational philosophies, school systems, and sociocultural norms
(Lavy, 2016; Schwerdt & Wuppermann, 2011). For instance, using a
sample of 35,000 students from PISA 2018, Macasaet (2021) found a
consistent positive effect of adaptive teaching on reading scores in the
Philippines, Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand, but that direct instruction only benefited reading literacy in the Philippines, while a negative
effect was found in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand. These variations
underscore the importance of cultural and national educational priorities in shaping teaching practices.
When examining which teaching practices contribute to student
performance across cultures, an important issue relates to the measurement of teaching practices. Two strategies are often used: selfreported measures, including teacher- and student-reported measurements, and classroom observations (Cheng et al., 2023). Some researchers believe that classroom observations are the “gold standard”
for assessing instructional quality, while self-reported measures are also
considered valid and necessary for this research (Wisniewski et al.,
2022). In particular, student-reported measurement is widely used in
research as it provides insights into the classroom experience from the
learners’ perspectives (OECD, 2019a).
Studies have shown that student ratings have a moderate correlation
with low-inference behavioral observations by external raters (Kunter &
Baumert, 2006; Scherzinger & Wettstein, 2019), supporting their relative reliability. Within PISA’s methodological framework, analyses of
construct and criterion validity revealed minimal bias from student
perspectives, including teacher popularity (Kunter & Baumert, 2006).
Furthermore, PISA addresses potential reporting biases by controlling
student background variables and applying statistical techniques to account for differences across countries and student groups (OECD, 2016).
Student-reported data are especially relevant to this study, as effective
teaching practices associated with student achievement likely operate
through students’ ongoing perceptions and internalization beyond immediate classroom interactions. Student feedback arguably provides
direct insight into whether teaching methods resonate with students and
promote learning outcomes. Therefore, incorporating student feedback
as data is methodologically appropriate for the current research.
Research remains inconclusive regarding the structure and characteristics of effective teaching practices and their potential associations
with reading literacy across diverse countries (Cordero & Gil-Izquierdo,
2018). The aim of this study is to investigate the commonalities and
differences of teaching practices as perceived by students across 16
top-performing countries and regions in reading literacy by examining
the frequencies, patterns, and relational networks of teaching practices.

Classroom management involves establishing clear rules and procedures, managing transitions between lesson segments smoothly,
keeping track of students’ work, planning and organizing lessons well,
managing minor disciplinary problems and disruptions, stopping inappropriate behaviors, and keeping a whole-group focus (Klieme et al.,
2009). Classroom management is considered a prerequisite for quality
learning time and is rooted in behaviorist theories of learning; it means
identifying and strengthening desirable behaviors and preventing those
that are undesirable (Hochweber et al., 2014). It requires teachers to
monitor various situations that arise during instruction and to establish
a coherent structure in classroom content and norms. Teachers should
foster appropriate student behavior through encouragement and positive language while addressing negative behaviors in a timely manner
through practices such as planned ignoring, prolonged stares, and verbal
reprimands (Klieme et al., 2001). Scholars have indicated that teachers’
use of verbal guidance and supervision in classroom management can
affect students’ academic performance (Stein et al., 2019; Usman,
2015). Piwowar et al. (2013) found that teachers’ efficient use of
instructional time, clear expectations, and proactive supervision were
linked to increased student engagement.
Teacher support refers to the guidance teachers provide to facilitate
students’ academic progress. It can manifest in various forms, such as
individual learner support, constructive feedback, and assistance with
classroom-related issues. Most of these factors are emotional or social in
nature, so they predict social-emotional outcomes such as student
happiness and motivation to learn. This dimension is rooted in selfdetermination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000), which conceptualizes autonomy, competence, and relatedness as basic psychological needs,
providing a useful lens for understanding how specific teaching practices may influence students’ reading engagement and motivation
(OECD, 2019a; Praetorius et al., 2018). Teachers’ support for autonomy
involves creating classroom conditions in which students feel free to ask
questions, express opinions, and pursue interests, thereby fulfilling their
need for autonomy and enhancing their school engagement (Reeve,
2012). A supportive learning environment improves student
self-efficacy and fosters close teacher–student and peer relationships,
thus fulfilling their needs for competence and relatedness (Praetorius
et al., 2018; Reeve, 2012). One meta-analysis showed that teacher
support was consistently associated with improved student academic
achievement, with emotional support demonstrating particularly strong
effects (Tao et al., 2022).
Teacher-directed instruction pertains to the teaching practices that
heavily rely on teachers’ competencies to deliver lessons orderly and
clearly (Le Donné et al., 2016). Research has demonstrated that
teacher-directed instruction is positively correlated with student outcomes (Ruzek et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2021). For example, a study of
8963 middle school students in Jordan confirmed the positive relationship between teacher-directed instruction and students’ self-efficacy

Cognitive stimulation refers to engaging students in constructive and
reflective high-level thinking activities, and facilitating their engagement in knowledge construction and high-level thinking through the
reconstruction, elaboration, and integration of information when
completing challenging tasks (Klieme et al., 2009). Teachers should
utilize students’ prior knowledge to create challenging problems that
stimulate cognitive conflicts and support metacognition (Baumert et al.,
2010). Research has shown that cognitive activation in instruction enhances both student interest and academic achievement (Förtsch et al.,
2017).
Adaptive teaching involves modifying instruction to meet students’
individual learning needs, interests, and mastery levels (Bernard et al.,
2019). This approach is rooted in student-centered instruction and
draws theoretical and methodological support from constructivist theories, as outlined by Jean Piaget and Lev Vygotsky (Bernard et al.,
2019). According to the constructivist view, the acquisition of new
knowledge should build on students’ previous knowledge and ways of
thinking (Prawat & Floden, 1994). Adaptive teaching can also be framed
through the lens of self-determination theory as a means to support
students’ psychological needs (Vansteenkiste et al., 2019). By aligning
the content and complexity of tasks with students’ existing knowledge
and skill levels, educators can foster students’ sense of competence
(Bernard et al., 2019). Providing students with choices in their learning
materials, tasks, or problem-solving approaches gives them a sense of
control and cultivates autonomy (Vansteenkiste et al., 2019).
Acknowledging individual perspectives helps foster a safe and supportive classroom environment, which strengthens students’ sense of
relatedness (Wisniewski et al., 2018). Empirical studies have indicated
that adaptive teaching enhances students’ enjoyment of reading, ultimately improving their reading performance (Chen & Li, 2025).
Teacher enthusiasm refers to “the degree of enjoyment, excitement,
and pleasure that teachers typically experience in their professional
activities” (Kunter et al., 2008, p. 470). The importance of teacher
enthusiasm has been demonstrated by studies on positive classroom
climate. For example, teachers with stronger enthusiasm tend to establish closer relationships with students and enhance student enjoyment of
the learning process (Frenzel et al., 2009). Studies have suggested that
when students perceive stronger teacher enthusiasm, their reading
enjoyment increases, leading to improved reading performance
(Retelsdorf et al., 2011).

In general, the role of teaching practice depends largely on sociocultural contexts, and what may be effective in one nation might not

increase in learning support often corresponds with higher reading
performance in many top-performing education systems, except in
Germany and Belgium (OECD, 2019c). Comparative analysis of teaching
practices across top-performing countries revealed that while adaptive
teaching was the strongest predictor of reading performance in Estonia,
Canada, and Finland, it was negatively associated with reading outcomes in Macao (Ho & Gan, 2023). Moreover, scholars have found that
traditional structured instructions have a positive effect on student
performance only in Japan, while in other Asian countries and regions,
they either have a negative effect or no effect (Cheng, 2014). Similarly,
certain inquiry-based teaching practices were negatively associated with
academic achievement in students from Taiwan and had no effect at any
educational level in other countries or regions (OECD, 2019c).
2.3. Research questions
As demonstrated by the literature review, teaching practices across
countries exhibit a certain degree of commonality, particularly with
regard to practices widely recognized as effective, while also reflecting
differences shaped by cultural contexts and policy preferences (Stigler &
Hiebert, 2009). Although scholars have investigated teaching practices
in specific countries (e.g., Lau & Lam, 2017), most studies have analyzed
these practices in isolation, rather than identifying broader patterns or
relational networks to explore the commonalities and differences of
teaching practices. This study seeks to address the following research
questions.
1. What are the frequencies of each type of teaching practices in 16 topperforming countries and regions in reading literacy?
2. Do teaching practices among these countries and regions demonstrate specific patterns? If so, what are the characteristics of these
patterns?
3. What are the commonalities and differences in the network of relationships between teaching practices across these countries and
regions?

2.2. Teaching practices in different cultures
In today’s globalized knowledge economy, there is a growing
consensus on the core knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values that students need to succeed and contribute to the development of a competitive workforce. Advances in educational and psychological sciences,
coupled with cross-national comparisons of education systems using
international assessments such as PISA, have fostered cross-cultural
dialogue and driven educational reforms toward identified “best practice” models to remain competitive globally (Niemann et al., 2017). This
suggests a notable convergence of teaching practices across the world.
However, the concept of “best teaching practices” varies considerably across countries and regions in practice (Lavy, 2016; Schwerdt &
Wuppermann, 2011; Shi et al., 2014; Vieluf & Klieme, 2011). Practices
that foster reflectiveness, student autonomy, and adaptive teaching are
particularly valued in the United States, while East Asian cultures, such
as Hong Kong, South Korea, and Japan, tend to adopt direct teaching
based on well-structured curricula and strategies promoting students’
cognitive development (Kinoshita & Utani, 2021; Kogan, 2024; Mok,
2009; Pang, 2009). In Germany, feedback, addressing student mistakes,
classroom discipline, cognitive activation, and social-emotional support

3. Methodology
3.1. Sample
The data used in this study were obtained from the PISA 2018 Student Survey Database, focusing on 16 countries and regions identified in
PISA report as top-performing in reading literacy (OECD, 2019b). The
countries initially included in the study were China (B-S-J-Z), Belgium,
Canada, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Ireland, Japan,
South Korea, New Zealand, Poland, Singapore, Slovenia, Sweden, and
the United Kingdom (OECD, 2020). Canada was excluded from the
analysis due to the absence of certain components in the student questionnaire regarding language teachers, resulting in a sample of 16
countries (OECD, 2018).

This study examined six types of teaching practices included in the
PISA 2018 assessment framework: disciplinary climate, teacher support,
cognitive stimulation, teacher-directed instruction, adaptive instruction,
and teacher enthusiasm. For teacher enthusiasm, students were asked to
indicate their level of agreement with items on a 4-point Likert scale
ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” For the other
teaching practices, students were asked to report the frequency of specific practices occurring during reading lessons, on a 4-point Likert scale
from “never or hardly ever” to “every lesson.” Reverse-worded items
under disciplinary climate, teacher support, and teacher-directed instruction were recoded so that positive values reflected more favorable
student perceptions (OECD, 2019c). Detailed information of items is
included in Table 1.
In the PISA 2018 dataset, the teaching practice items reported by
individual students were analyzed using item response theory (IRT) to
synthesize them into latent variables rather than relying on composite
scores. IRT generates standardized latent trait scores (θ-values) that
account for variations in item difficulty and discrimination parameters.
This approach offers superior cross-sample comparability—particularly
critical for cross-cultural research designs—by positioning individuals
along a continuum of “perceived teaching practice” more accurately
than raw aggregate scores. This method helps reduce the impact of individual response tendencies (e.g., extreme response styles or cultural
response patterns) and reporting bias (Bolt & Newton, 2011).

3.3. Data analysis
First, a descriptive statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS
Statistics version 26. We calculated the mean values and standard deviations of various teaching practices across the 16 top-performing
countries and regions.
Second, we used hierarchical clustering analysis to identify the
commonalities and differences in the focus of teaching practices across
these countries and regions. The average score of each teaching practice
was used as key characteristic, and the 16 countries and regions were
considered the cases to be clustered. The process started with each
country in its own cluster and iteratively merged clusters based on their
similarity until countries were combined into a single cluster. The
analysis used an average linkage method, employing the square of the
Euclidean distance as the relevant measure and the original standardized Z-value to construct the dendrogram. Then, variance analysis and
post hoc analysis were performed to examine the differences in the
identified clusters and evaluate the practical significance of the observed
differences through effect size analysis.
Third, we conducted social network analysis using the R packages
qgraph (Epskamp et al., 2012) and NetworkComparisonTest (van Borkulo et al., 2023). To reduce the influence of scale differences on the
estimation of nodes strength in the network analysis, we used a stratified
simple random sampling without replacement to select 1000 students
from each country or region, resulting in a sample of 16,000. A partial
correlation network was estimated for each country or region to analyze
the relationships between teaching practices. The average scores for
each teaching practice were used as nodes in the networks, and the edge
weights were estimated based on the partial correlation between
teaching practices while controlling for the influence of all other indicators in the network. A correlation matrix was produced using the
cor_auto function within the qgraph package. To produce sparser and
interpretable network models, the least absolute shrinkage and selection
operator (LASSO) was applied with a Gamma parameter of 0.5 for all
models. Thus, the absence of a connection between two types of teaching
practices in the networks suggests a certain degree of independence in
their relationship (Christensen et al., 2018). A nonparametric bootstrap
test with 1000 resamples was performed for each network to evaluate
the accuracy and stability of the estimates of the edge weights and

4.3.1. Partial correlation network of teaching practices
Nonparametric bootstrap test indicated that the networks exhibited
high estimation accuracy. The strength centrality index demonstrated
strong stability with all coefficients (CS cor = 0.70) exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.5.
Table 5 shows the partial correlation network estimates for each
country or region, which reveal several notable patterns. First, teaching
practices were generally positively correlated with each other, except in
Japan, Denmark, and Finland, where negative correlations were
observed. Notably, a consistent positive correlation (r ≥ 0.3) was found
between teacher support and teacher-directed instruction across all
countries and regions. Second, disciplinary climate showed weak correlations with most other teaching practices, except for teacher enthusiasm, where a strong correlation (r > 0.2) was observed in Germany,
France, Finland, Poland, and the United Kingdom. Third, cognitive
stimulation, teacher enthusiasm, and adaptive instruction formed a
closely connected triangle at the center of the teaching practice network.

Across the 16 countries and regions, the overall frequency distribution ranged from − 2 to 2, with mean values generally fluctuating around
0 and a standard deviation of approximately 1. As shown in Table 2,
disparities were observed in the frequencies of the teaching practices
across countries and regions. South Korea and China consistently ranked
among the top two across most domains, including disciplinary climate,
teacher-directed instruction, cognitive stimulation, adaptive instruction,
and teacher enthusiasm, with relatively high mean scores and moderate
standard deviations (e.g., disciplinary climate: Korea, M = 1.07, SD =
1.01; China, M = 0.79, SD = 1.03). In contrast, countries like Slovenia,
France, and Belgium reported lower frequencies in several practices,
particularly in adaptive instruction and disciplinary climate, with
smaller deviations indicating less within-country variation (e.g.,
Slovenia adaptive instruction: M = − 0.40, SD = 1.02). Teacher support
was highest in China and the United Kingdom, while Slovenia and
Poland reported the lowest levels. Across all domains, the standard deviations suggest substantial variability within countries, reflecting
diverse classroom experiences.

5.2. Patterns of teaching approaches across top-performing countries
The top-performing countries and regions can be clustered into four
distinct groups according to the patterns of teaching practices. China (BS-J-Z) and South Korea adopt a teaching-practice-oriented approach,
consistently and actively implementing a wide range of teaching practices. Japan, on the other hand, relies more heavily on teacher-centered
practices, placing a strong emphasis on classroom discipline and hierarchical structures. New Zealand, the United Kingdom, Singapore,
Ireland, Denmark, Finland, and Sweden predominantly adopt studentoriented practices, which is consistent with previous PISA reports
(Shiel et al., 2016). Lastly, Belgium, France, Estonia, Slovenia, Germany,
8

P. Li et al.

Teaching and Teacher Education 172 (2026) 105363

and Poland employ a minimalistic teaching approach, characterized by
relatively lower levels of teacher involvement and a greater focus on
fostering students’ natural and independent development.
These findings are largely consistent with the previous research (e.g.,
Govorova et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2025), which highlights that
teacher-centered instruction does not significantly enhance reading literacy in most OECD countries but has demonstrated effectiveness in
certain Asian contexts. Schwartz’s cultural dimensions—hierarchy
versus egalitarianism and autonomy versus embeddedness—further
elucidate the distinction between the teacher-oriented, student-oriented, and minimalist countries identified in the present study. In
teacher-oriented contexts, such as that of Japan, classrooms tend to
embody strong hierarchical and embedded structures in which teachers
are regarded as authoritative figures who set clear expectations for
students’ behavior and academic achievement, guiding students within
a framework of respect and authority (Badawi, 2024; Schwartz, 2013).
In student-oriented contexts, such as those in Nordic and British
Commonwealth countries, cultural values prioritizing egalitarianism
and intellectual autonomy foster environments in which students are
encouraged to express their views and engage in dialogue, thereby
enhancing the effectiveness of student-centered education (Frambach
et al., 2014; Schwartz, 2013). In countries and regions with a minimalistic approach, such as those found in parts of Western Europe,
cultural norms emphasize both intellectual and affective autonomy.
These values promote integration into the social environment rather
than efforts to transform it through assertive action (Schwartz, 2013),
leading teachers in such contexts to deprioritize the use of teaching
methods that have been recognized as effective. In these contexts, students’ strong reading literacy cannot be attributed to the frequency of
such methods; rather, it is a product of the favorable structures of
teaching practices or of their interactions. Alternatively, teaching
practices may not be an effective factor in promoting reading literacy in
this group. This observation aligns with the PISA 2018 findings from
Costa Rica, in which teacher variables do not seem to exhibit strong
associations with reading literacy compared to other student-related
factors (Rojas-Torres et al., 2021).
No universally optimal emphasis—whether on all teaching practices,
the role of teachers, or the role of students—is present for promoting
reading literacy. Rather, it is crucial to consider congruence with cultural values and traditions when developing educational approaches.
Therefore, educators are encouraged to develop teaching practices with
diverse patterns that align with students’ needs and sociocultural
contexts.

e., in teaching-practice-oriented practice), it can exert a positive influence on reading literacy, regardless of the correlations between the
different teaching practices. Conversely, when frequency is relatively
low (i.e., in student-oriented practice), each practice must have a relatively consistent and strong correlation to positively impact students’
reading literacy. Additionally, countries adopting a minimalist approach
also exhibited marked variability. Given the generally low frequency of
instructional practices in these contexts, reading literacy outcomes are
likely influenced by other factors, leading to substantial cross-cultural
differences in network structure.
A synthesis of information on network structure and connectivity
further underscores the influence of instructional typologies. It reveals
that within the high-connectivity cluster, the contrast between teacheroriented and practice-oriented approaches disrupts structure coherence.
Conversely, within the low-connectivity cluster, minimalistic and
student-oriented approaches generate compatible network structures,
enabling coherence across differing pedagogical categories. Nevertheless, the observed differences in connectivity among high-performing
systems were not extreme, indicating that a certain degree of interconnectedness among teaching practices may be universally essential to
optimize their impact on reading literacy. These comparative findings
collectively support the view that teaching practice is culture- or
country-based (Blömeke & Delaney, 2012). As such, it is advisable for
scholars, practitioners, and policymakers to build on the developmental
experiences of teaching practice from high-performing countries and
regions that share similar backgrounds.
Furthermore, a minimal disparity was observed in the overall
network centrality of teaching practices in the 16 countries and regions.
All teaching practices were found to be well connected, with teacher
support being the most closely related to each teaching practice, followed by teacher enthusiasm and cognitive stimulation. This finding
provides important guidance to educators looking to establish or
improve a type of teaching practice by pointing out its core element.
Considering the identified positive impact of teacher support on reading
literacy (Ma et al., 2021), teachers offering effective and timely support
to students may improve their reading literacy. Moreover, given that
students’ intrinsic motivation to read has been shown to positively influence their reading literacy outcomes (Özdemir, 2024), activating
students emotionally and cognitively can boost reading literacy in the
classroom.
The network findings reveal nuanced interconnections among specific teaching practices beyond their overall frequencies, suggesting that
successful reading literacy does not result from the mere accumulation
of teaching strategies but from the adoption of a well-coordinated and
culturally responsive pedagogical approach. In the high-performing
context of China (B-S-J-Z), for instance, the effectiveness of teacherdirected instruction is reinforced through its culturally grounded integration with complementary practices. Teachers in China (B-S-J-Z)

5.3. Network relationships of teaching practices
The 16 countries and regions shared several characteristics related to
the networks of teaching practice. First, a visual examination of the
relationship network structure indicated that 13 of the 16 countries and
regions had positive connections among all teaching practices, with
particularly strong correlations observed between teacher support and
teacher-directed instruction. This suggests that effective teachers tend to
implement multiple teaching strategies, particularly those involving
teacher–student interactions, simultaneously. Most networks shared a
common feature: a central triangle encompassing teacher enthusiasm,
cognitive stimulation, and adaptive instruction. This configuration indicates that a holistic approach integrating emotional, cognitive, and
instructional components is key to fostering optimal learning environments (Ma et al., 2021; Arik, 2022).
In contrast, disciplinary climate appeared more independent,
showing weaker correlations with the other practices. This aligns with
previous findings (Govorova et al., 2020), suggesting that classroom
management may represent a more traditional and independent pedagogy. This disconnect implies the possibility of a certain degree of
mutual exclusivity between modern teaching practices and traditional
teaching practices. In other words, when teachers employ more

Conversely, a misalignment between teaching practices and cultural

perceived as highlighting deficiencies, students may interpret the feedback as punitive, thereby diminishing their motivation (Bozkurt, 2022).
Moreover, some educational systems exhibit pedagogical fragmentation
(i.e., prioritizing disciplinary climate while underutilizing teacher
enthusiasm), resulting in classrooms that are orderly yet disengaging
(Zhang, 2023). To address this imbalance, teachers may adopt strategies
that integrate classroom management with interactive pedagogical
techniques, such as involving students in the co-construction of classroom rules (Egitim, 2025) and showing genuine enthusiasm through
verbal and non-verbal cues (Scrivener, 2012). Incorporating autonomous approaches into the classroom, such as student-led discussions and
flipped classroom, can also promote students’ learning motivation
(Sergis et al., 2018). Deliberate planning is critical to ensure meaningful
engagement; teachers may start with structured discussions for simple
tasks and progressively transition to more student-driven sessions (Lo &
Hew, 2017).
These findings provide implications for educational systems seeking
to improve instructional outcomes. Rather than directly transplanting
seemingly “best” teaching practices from high-performing countries,
educators and policymakers should prioritize contextual adaptation,
ensuring compatibility among instructional approaches and cultural
values. For example, teaching approaches that draw on local narratives,
community practices, or indigenous knowledge systems can make
reading materials more accessible and meaningful to students, thereby
enhancing both engagement and comprehension (Anlimachie et al.,
2025). Additionally, instructional methods should align with prevailing
cultural norms. In collectivist cultures, for instance, collaborative
learning activities that emphasize group-oriented cooperation may be
more effective than individualistic approaches, as they align with locally
valued principles of social harmony and shared responsibility (Tibenda
et al., 2021).

5.4. Limitations and future directions

References

This study has several limitations. First, we used data from the PISA
2018 survey, which was conducted at the school level rather than at the
individual-classroom level, meaning the data collected from teachers do
not directly relate to their students. The study also relied on students’
perceptions of the practices of their language teachers, as reported in a
questionnaire. This questionnaire only investigated teachers’ current
practices as perceived by students; however, students’ reading performance can be influenced by the long-term accumulation of knowledge,
which implies that previous teachers’ practices might have a greater
impact on students than the practices of their current teachers (Connor
et al., 2013). This means that a potentially weak relationship exists
between new teachers and students’ learning, which could lead to
certain biases in understanding the influence of teaching practice. Second, this study was limited to data from 16 countries and regions;
therefore, caution should be exercised when generalizing its findings to
infer links between teachers’ practices and reading literacy in other
countries and regions. The present findings should be considered preliminary, and they require further validation and development in larger
or more diverse samples.
Although the existence of a common teaching model in different
countries and regions remains controversial, certain teaching practices

consistently demonstrated by educational researchers. Due to the complexities of various teaching contexts, teaching practice is an amalgamation of different factors (Klieme, 2020), which makes it challenging
