Glossary

Term

Description
Teaching strategies aimed at tailoring the lesson to the specific needs and readiness levels of different students within
a class.

Classroom diversity

The wide range of differences in students’ attributes and needs. In the context of schooling, relevant differences may
include students’ interests, experiences, aptitudes, abilities, learning styles, exceptionalities, gender, sexual
orientation, socio-economic status, immigrant and/or minority background such as cultural, ethnic, national, religious
and language backgrounds.

Competence

The ability to meet complex demands in a given context by mobilising various psychosocial (cognitive, functional,
personal and ethical) resources (Guerriero and Révai, 2017[1])

Conditional knowledge

Knowledge of when, why and under what circumstances to apply declarative and procedural knowledge

Creative thinking

The competence to engage productively in the generation, evaluation and improvement of ideas that can result in
original and effective solutions, advances in knowledge and impactful expressions of imagination (OECD, 2019[2])

Critical thinking

The capacity to analyse and evaluate information, reasoning and situations, according to appropriate standards, for
the purpose of constructing sound and insightful new knowledge, understandings, hypotheses and beliefs (Heard
et al., 2020[3])

Declarative knowledge

Knowledge of facts and concepts, including theoretical and scientific knowledge

Differentiation (in teaching)

An approach to teaching in which teachers proactively adapt curricula, teaching methods, resources, learning
activities, and student products to address the diverse needs of individual students and small groups of students to
maximise the learning opportunity for each student in a classroom (Tomlinson et al., 2003[4])

Diversity, diverse

Differences in the backgrounds of individuals in a classroom, school or education system. In the context of TALIS, it
refers to students and staff. In the case of cultural diversity, it refers most notably to variations in cultural or ethnic
backgrounds.

Empirical research

Based on, concerned with, or verifiable by observation or experience rather than theory or pure logic

General pedagogical knowledge

The specialised knowledge of teachers for creating effective teaching and learning environments for all students
independent of subject matter (Guerriero and Révai, 2017[1]).

Item

A question or task used to measure a specific aspect of a construct. In reference to the GPK assessment component
of the TKS, the term “item” refers to a task or question. In the contextual questionnaire component, a question may
consist of a question stem followed by several items.

Metacognition

Involves the knowledge or personal theories or beliefs individuals have about their own cognition. It also includes
individual control and monitoring of their own thinking processes (Tarricone, 2011[5]).

Pedagogy

The interactive process between teacher/practitioner and the learner, which involves the education instructional
methods or strategies that allow learning to take place.

Pedagogical/psychological
knowledge

Knowledge needed to create and optimise teaching and teaching-learning situations across subjects, including
declarative and procedural knowledge of classroom management, teaching methods, classroom assessment, and
student heterogeneity (Voss, Kunter and Baumert, 2011[6]).

Procedural knowledge

Knowledge of how to perform a specific skill or task.

Social and emotional learning

Comprises five core abilities: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills and responsible
decision making (Borowski, 2019[7]).

Teaching effectiveness

The extent to which teaching (methods, strategies, planning and decisions of a teacher) fosters or produces gains in
student learning – e.g. Bardach and Klassen (2020[8]).

Teaching methods

The general principles, design and management strategies used for classroom instruction to facilitate student
learning, such as direct instruction, discovery learning, and project-based learning.

Teaching quality

The extent to which teaching is likely to be effective in facilitating gains in student learning, often measured by the
quality or level of classroom and time management, clarity of teaching, cognitive activation, formative assessment
and feedback, adaptation, supporting students’ socio-emotional learning, and support for consolidation

1 Introduction
This document articulates a Conceptual and Assessment Framework for the Teacher Knowledge Survey
(TKS), an optional module of the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) programme in 2024.
It reflects major concepts from research literature, specifically in relation to general pedagogical knowledge
(GPK) as a measurable aspect of teachers’ professional competence.
TALIS is a large-scale survey of teachers, school leaders and their learning environments that provides
comparative perspectives on teachers, teaching and learning across education systems of OECD member
and partner countries/territories, with a focus on factors affecting learning environments and the working
conditions of teaching staff. The study relies on teachers’ and principals’ self-reports on factors that are
believed to enhance teaching and student learning, and through that information helps inform educational
policy and practice in participating countries. The addition of a direct measurement of GPK through the
optional TKS module allows TALIS to expand upon what can be measured in the core study. While the
TALIS core survey asks, “What do teachers tell us about their work: their beliefs, attitudes, opportunities
and practices?”, the TKS module, while including some of these questions, additionally asks, “What do
teachers know about pedagogy?”.
Drawing on work undertaken by the OECD’s Centre for Educational Research and Innovation (CERI)
Innovative Teaching for Effective Learning (ITEL) project, the present conceptual framework is
underpinned by a broader conceptualisation of teachers’ professional competence (Guerriero and Révai,
2017[1]) which considers teacher knowledge generally – and GPK specifically – as its main pillar (Ulferts,
2021[2]). As an optional component of TALIS that complements its core survey, the TKS offers the
opportunity to investigate synergies with other themes that are also relevant to teachers’ professional
competence, including contemporary education issues such as diversity, technology, and social and
emotional learning, as well as teachers’ affective-motivational competencies and beliefs and their teaching
practices.
The TALIS 2024 TKS is valuable for participating countries in that it generates measures of teachers’ GPK
at a country level and, thereby, provides insights into the knowledge base of the profession in relation to
the demands of contemporary teaching. The issues that the TKS touches on are relevant to all participating
countries, and results can inform discussions on wider educational policy. The outcomes of the survey
might also be used to improve teacher education and professional learning programs, “…to ensure a robust
knowledge base in the profession in line with national policy priorities […] support policies in attracting and
retaining a high-quality teaching workforce [and to] empower the teaching profession to take charge of its
knowledge base.” (OECD, 2020[3])
Following this introduction, the remainder of this document is composed of an additional five sections.
Section 2 provides an overview of the policy relevance of the TKS as a new module of TALIS. Section 3
establishes the conceptual basis for the TKS, particularly relating to GPK, and provides the definition and
framework for the assessment of GPK that underpins the survey instrument. This is followed, in Section 4
by an outline of the emergent priorities for teachers’ pedagogical knowledge that have informed the
instrument’s development, and an explanation of the process by which the GPK construct has been
internationally validated. In Section 5, links to indicators and constructs from TALIS, and how they can be
used to provide context for the analysis of teacher's general pedagogical knowledge, are explained with
reference to facets of the teacher professional competence framework (Guerriero and Révai, 2017[1]).

2 The role and policy relevance of the
TALIS Teacher Knowledge Survey

Overview of the Teacher Knowledge Survey 2024
The TALIS Teacher Knowledge Survey (TKS) is a new optional module of TALIS 2024, which is
administered to teachers teaching at ISCED level 2 in participating countries and territories. It is a
large-scale international survey that assesses teachers’ general pedagogical knowledge (GPK) and
gathers relevant background and self-reported information from teachers. Therefore, the TKS
complements the self-report data about teaching gathered through the TALIS core survey by providing
objective information about what teachers in participating countries and territories know in relation to
pedagogy and classroom instruction.

Objective and purpose of the TKS as a measure of GPK
The TALIS 2024 TKS has been developed based on the work undertaken by the OECD’s Centre for
Educational Research and Innovation (CERI) as part of its Innovative Teaching for Effective Learning
(ITEL) project. The ITEL project centred on investigating teaching as a knowledge profession by
undertaking research into the work of teachers, the various professional demands placed on them, and
the factors that underpin high-quality teaching. Key to this research was to understand the pedagogical
knowledge basis of teaching, which is central to its conception as a profession. A pilot of the TKS was
conducted in 2016 as a means of measuring the GPK of teachers, as well as teacher educators and
pre-service teachers, in five OECD countries (Estonia, Greece, Hungary, Israel and the Slovak Republic),
and to examine possible associations with their learning opportunities, and their motivational and affective
competencies – see Sonmark et al. (2017[1]).
Now integrated into TALIS in 2024, the overall objective of the TKS is to provide robust international
indicators of the GPK of ISCED level 2 teachers, and how this is associated with teachers’ opportunities
to learn about general pedagogy, their affective-motivational characteristics, and professional/teaching
practices. By examining the nature and level of teacher pedagogical knowledge, and exploring how GPK
is associated with other factors, the TKS serves to address the policy issues that underpin the broader
TALIS programme (OECD, 2020, p. 27 and Figure II.1.2[2]), including:
•

attracting and selecting high-calibre candidates into teacher education and leadership preparation

•

developing teaching professionals through high-quality pre-service preparation and in-service
professional development

•

supporting teaching professionals’ growth through induction, mentoring and collaboration

•

empowering teaching professionals through autonomy, leadership and opportunities for career
progression

•

retaining teaching professionals through fulfilling and rewarding work conditions, well-being and

Main areas for policy consideration
The TKS aims to provide empirical and internationally comparable measures of GPK across three
dimensions of knowledge: knowledge of (i) classroom instruction, (ii) student learning and (iii) assessment.
The results of this survey will enable countries to consider relative strengths and weaknesses in teacher’s
general pedagogical knowledge across these dimensions and, potentially, over time. Such information is
useful for countries in strengthening the knowledge base of their teacher workforce. An understanding of
these results relative to those of other countries may also provide insights as to the context-bound nature
of GPK and may be useful in prompting an examination of system influences on teacher knowledge.
Taken with the results of the TALIS core survey, these insights are also intended to inform reflections on
policies related to teacher standards, teacher training and ongoing professional learning opportunities.
Policy and reform aimed at increasing the professionalisation and stature of teaching, such as developing
teacher accreditation and qualification standards, are usefully informed by an internationally validated
framework and a valid measure of teachers’ GPK. Similarly, the results of the TKS could inform a review
of initial teacher education programmes and guide the foci of ongoing professional learning for in-service
teachers.
It is also anticipated that analyses arising from the TKS will contribute to the emerging body of international
research examining GPK by providing a rich source of data to inform further analyses and research. This
includes a range of other outcomes, beyond teachers’ opportunities to learn, that have important policy
implications. GPK is seen as one of several important aspects of teachers’ professional competence that
interacts in a dynamic way with other factors, including their affective-motivational competencies and
beliefs, and the quality of their teaching and other work practices. GPK is positively associated with
teaching quality (Ulferts, 2019[3]), for example, as well as measures of teacher well-being and job
satisfaction (Voss et al., 2015[4]). The inclusion of questions gathering self-report data related to such
outcomes taken from the TALIS core survey will enable a contribution to this research that includes
analyses within and across diverse national and regional contexts.

3 Teacher knowledge as an aspect of

teachers’ professional competence

Introduction
Following the work of the OECD’s Centre for Education Research and Innovation (CERI) Innovative
Teaching for Effective Learning (ITEL) project, the focus on measuring GPK in the TALIS 2024 Teacher
Knowledge Survey (TKS) module stems from a conceptualisation of teaching as a knowledge profession.
The existence of a specialised body of knowledge such as GPK, relevant to and maintained by teachers
generally, is often considered crucial to defining teaching as a knowledge profession (Ulferts, 2021[1])
(Ulferts, 2021). However other professional characteristics and competencies (see Figure 3.1) are also
required of teachers and interact with their pedagogical knowledge. Teacher knowledge (including GPK)
can thus be considered one aspect of teachers’ broader professional competence, with competence
defined as “the ability to meet complex demands in a given context by mobilising various psychosocial
(cognitive, functional, personal and ethical) resources” (Guerriero and Révai, 2017, p. 261[2]).
Interest in teacher knowledge as an aspect of teacher competence extends to its relationship to teaching
effectiveness and to high-quality teaching. Effectiveness is usually considered the extent to which teaching
results in measurable gains in student learning, while quality teaching describes those practices that are
most likely to lead to improved learning (Bardach and Klassen, 2020[3]). Much research agrees that these
practices at least include clarity of instruction, cognitive activation, classroom management and a
supportive climate - e.g. Klieme, Pauli, & Reusser (2009[4]); Nilsen, Gustafsson, & Blömeke (2016[5]); Van
de Grift (2014[6]). Standards for teaching and teacher education from five high-achieving countries
(Australia, Canada, Finland, China, and Singapore) have recently been shown to define quality teaching
as deeply rooted in a teacher’s knowledge: of content, pedagogy and learners and learning, focused on
meeting students’ diverse social, emotional, and academic needs (Darling-Hammond, 2021[7]). As
definitions of teaching quality increasingly encompass knowledge as an enabler of effective teaching, an
interest in GPK is warranted.
The CERI conceptual framework of teachers’ professional competence, which provided the basis for the
2016 ITEL Teacher Knowledge Survey Pilot, also implicitly underpins the 2024 TALIS TKS, and is
presented in Figure 3.1.

development of the TKS instrument is also presented, as well as an explanation for the inclusion of themes
arising from the TALIS 2024 core survey as emergent priorities for teacher knowledge. The section ends
with an outline of the international validation process for the GPK assessment framework.

Teacher knowledge
Teacher knowledge is regarded as a multidimensional construct (König, 2017[8]) and provides a
fundamental basis upon which to develop the TKS. Teacher knowledge refers to the different types of
professional knowledge required by teachers in their practice. Shulman’s (1987[9]) conceptualisation of
teacher knowledge is regarded as seminal, describing seven different types:
•

Content knowledge, which is assumed to comprise knowledge of subject matter and its organising
structures.

•

General pedagogical knowledge, particularly knowledge of “principles and strategies of
classroom management and organisation” common to teachers regardless of subject matter.

•

Curriculum knowledge, which includes knowledge of the “materials and programmes” teachers
use to deliver the curriculum.

•

Pedagogical content knowledge, which comprises the “amalgam of content and pedagogy”
knowledge that is unique to teachers of particular subjects or domains, including knowledge of a
variety of forms by which subject matter knowledge can be represented and adapted by the teacher
to the learning needs of students.

•

Knowledge of learners and their characteristics, which is thought to entail knowledge of factors
such as cognitive, affective, socio-cultural characteristics that can determine students’ learning
needs.

•

Knowledge of educational contexts, which ranges from localised teaching contexts, such as
groups and classrooms of students, to wider knowledge of school governance and financing and
the broader school, community and culture.

•

Knowledge of educational purposes, ends and values and their philosophical and historical
groundings.

Subsequently, education scholars have proposed other forms and sub-categories of teacher knowledge,
e.g. Ball, Thames and Phelps (2008[10]); however, most identify that teacher knowledge falls generally into
three core categories among the seven originally proposed by Shulman: content knowledge (CK),
pedagogical content knowledge (PCK) and general pedagogical knowledge (GPK) (Baumert et al.,
2010[11]; Leijen et al., 2022[12]). Results from several studies have been interpreted as indicating that all
three categories of knowledge contribute to high-quality teaching (Baumert et al., 2010[11]; Guerriero and
Révai, 2017[2]; Ulferts, 2019[13]). Teachers have been found to apply PCK when teaching well-known
content but rely on GPK when they have little related PCK to draw upon (Hashweh, 1987[14]). Integrated
training in content-specific and content-general pedagogy is found to increase the simultaneous application
of both in teaching tasks and is, therefore, thought to enhance teaching quality (Harr, Eichler and Renkl,
2014[15]).
Of these categories of teacher knowledge, CK and PCK have attracted the most research and policy
attention (Ulferts, 2019[13]), as teachers’ domain-specific knowledge is seen as centrally important to
teaching quality (Seidel and Shavelson, 2007[16]). PCK refers to knowledge about how to teach CK and
involves teachers’ understanding of the difficulty of the content being taught and having a range of
knowledge representation strategies, such as examples and demonstrations, to enable subject content to
be understandable by learners. Referring to research on the teaching of secondary school mathematics,
Baumert et al. (2010[11]) explain that PCK is necessary for instruction and requires teachers to be adaptive
and respond to the learning needs of the students, while CK is necessary to stimulate teaching methods
or strategies. A lack of CK decreases the development and effectiveness of PCK (Baumert et al., 2010[11];
Krauss, Baumert and Blum, 2008[17]). In fact, empirical evidence has shown that CK is necessary but not
sufficient for solving PCK items (Agathangelou and Charalambous, 2021[18]; Depaepe et al., 2015[19]). A
longitudinal experimental study, Trobst et al. (2018[20]), also suggested that supporting CK could be a route

TALIS TEACHER KNOWLEDGE SURVEY 2024 CONCEPTUAL AND ASSESSMENT FRAMEWORK © OECD 2025

18 |
to developing PCK. Additionally, research studies have shown that both CK and PCK are influenced by
the quality of teacher training, teaching experience and expertise, and both are critical for effective teaching
and learning (Baumert et al., 2010[11]; Krauss et al., 2008[21]).
By contrast, GPK has received far less research attention than CK and PCK (Blömeke and Delaney,
2012[22]; Guerriero and Révai, 2017[2]; Ulferts, 2019[13]), and this is likely due to its general nature. GPK
has been found to be devalued by many pre-service teachers for a perceived lack of practical use (Merk
et al., 2017[23]) despite the fact that it provides the generic theoretical underpinnings for the more
domain-specific PCK that teachers apply to their subject teaching. Recent research is however beginning
to establish evidence in support of the importance of GPK for teaching quality.
As with all forms of knowledge, it must be recognised that GPK includes declarative knowledge (knowing
that), procedural knowledge (knowing how) and conditional knowledge (knowing when, where and why)
(Guerriero, 2017[24]; König, 2017[8]; Ulferts, 2019[13]; Xu, 2015[25]). Teachers’ declarative knowledge is often
theoretical or scientific in nature, gained through initial teacher education, ongoing professional learning
opportunities and other forms of self-education, such as professional reading. In the context of GPK,
declarative knowledge includes conceptual knowledge regarding such aspects of teaching as instructional
strategies, classroom management approaches, learning theories and assessment practices. Conversely,
procedural, and conditional knowledge, gained from teaching experience, is considered applied
knowledge. Declarative knowledge often informs procedural and conditional knowledge through its
application in different teaching and learning contexts and through experience (Guerriero, 2017[24]). It has
been argued that exposure to both theory and practical teaching experience are, in fact, necessary to
acquiring GPK and also to enable its application in pedagogical situations (Ulferts, 2019[13]).
While applied (procedural/conditional) knowledge often distinguishes expert teachers from novice teachers
(Putnam and Borko, 2000[26]), it is also often tacit or implicit, making it harder to consciously access and to
fully codify (Guerriero, 2017[24]; Ulferts, 2019[13]). Research on teacher expertise suggests that, while
declarative knowledge contributes to expert performance in the classroom, widespread differences
between experts and novices concerning declarative knowledge are not expected (König et al., 2021[27]).
Thus, the validity of measures of teachers’ GPK require the inclusion in the assessment instruments of
items capturing both applied (procedural and conditional) and declarative knowledge (Guerriero and Révai,
2017[2]; König, 2014[28]).

Defining GPK
As discussed previously, the research into teacher knowledge tends to identify three main categories: CK,
PCK and GPK. In contrast to CK and PCK, and as per Shulman’s original formulation, GPK is considered
domain-general knowledge related to pedagogy. Several recent definitions accentuate this point. GPK has
been described as the “…general knowledge of teaching and learning that is cross-curricular…” (Guerriero
and Révai, 2017, p. 262[2]). GPK is also described as the “…foundation of teaching that is shared by
teaching professionals across disciplines and educational levels.” (Ulferts, 2019, p. 8[13]), or simply the
“…generic knowledge of effective teaching…” (Voss, Kunter and Baumert, 2011, p. 953[29]).
Other definitions of GPK extend Shulman’s (1987[9]) definition, which refers only to knowledge of the broad
principles and strategies of classroom management and organisation. Grossman and Richert (1988,
p. 54[30]) suggested GPK includes “…knowledge of theories of learning and general principles of
instruction, an understanding of the various philosophies of education, general knowledge about learners,
and knowledge of the principles and techniques of classroom management.” More recently, Nehls et al.
(2020, p. 344[31]) defined GPK as “…knowledge concerning the preparation, structuring and evaluation of
lessons, the motivation and support of students as well as their management in the classroom, dealing
with heterogeneous learning groups, and students’ assessment.” The development and validation of a

survey in Singapore of pre-service and beginning teachers’ self-perceived pedagogical knowledge (and
skills) identified broadly similar knowledge factors:
•

student learning: using different strategies to capture students’ interest and stimulate their
thinking

•

lesson planning: writing lesson plans and preparing appropriate resources

•

instructional support: selecting appropriate resources and assessment modes to support
instruction

•

accommodating diversity: catering to students’ different needs

•

classroom management: managing student behaviours and discipline

•

care and concern: providing care and helping students with problems (Choy et al., 2012,
p. 592[32])

In a recent systematic literature review (Leijen et al., 2022[12]), definitions of GPK such as those above
were commonly found to involve any of three characteristics: student-related knowledge (such as learning
processes, student development and motivation), teaching-related knowledge (such as classroom
management and teaching processes), and/or contextual knowledge (such as curriculum and philosophies
of education).
Developing an agreed conceptualisation of GPK among the countries participating in the TKS is an integral
part of the development of a framework for cross-national assessment. While several models and
frameworks of GPK explicate the construct, Guerriero (2017, p. 109[24]) highlights that these different
perspectives have made the construct difficult to define and have exposed the need for a “…cross-cultural
conceptualisation of general pedagogical knowledge…”. There is evidence, for example, that regardless
of country, quality teaching requires GPK in two main areas of instruction: teaching methods and classroom
management (König, 2014[28]; König et al., 2011[33]; Nehls et al., 2020[31]; Voss, Kunter and Baumert,
2011[29]). Central to the task of teaching is understanding how to meet the learning needs of students
through the planned adoption of teaching methods (König et al., 2011[33]). Classroom management,
meanwhile, is an element of quality teaching as it creates the conditions in the classroom that make
effective teaching possible (Oliver and Reschly, 2007[34]).
Acknowledging that instruction is central to GPK was the basis of the further development of the construct
for the TEDS-M study and the development of a seminal assessment of GPK (König et al., 2011[33]). König
and colleagues’ (2011[33]) framing of the GPK construct was influenced by the work of Slavin’s (1994[35])
QAIT model of instruction aimed at identifying aspects of effective teaching, which was itself a revision of
Carroll’s (1963[36]) model of school learning. Slavin’s model focused on those elements of effective teaching
that are adaptable and under the teacher’s control: quality of instruction (Q) involves knowing and applying
a range of knowledge representation strategies; appropriate levels of instruction (A) involve differentiating
and adapting instruction for diversity; incentive (I) includes strategies to motivate students; and time
assigned to learning (T) involves classroom management strategies to ensure student time on task.
According to Slavin’s model, “…the alterable components under the teacher’s control combine with student
inputs (student aptitude and student motivation) to result in student achievement.” (Guerriero, 2017,
p. 102[24]), thus making a direct connection between teacher quality, instruction, and student achievement.
Drawing on this model, König et al. (2011, p. 189[33]) defined GPK as “…knowledge about learners and
learning, assessment, and educational contexts and purposes.” and provided a framework of GPK that
comprises four task-based dimensions that were used as the basis of the TEDS-M GPK assessment. The
four task-based dimensions include:
•

structure: structuring of learning objectives; lesson planning and structuring the lesson process;
lesson evaluation

König et al.’s (2011[33]) model is similar to that of Voss, Kunter and Baumert’s (2011[29]), used as the GPK

As part of the background research for the CERI ITEL Teacher Knowledge Survey Pilot, König (2014[28])
conducted a review and analysis of existing assessments of GPK, namely TEDS-M, LEK (Longitudinal
Survey of Student Teachers’ Pedagogical Competencies), BILWISS (Broad Pedagogical Knowledge and
the Development of Professional Competence in Teacher Education), COACTIV-R and PLT (Praxis II:
Principles of Learning and Teaching). This synthesis identified three overarching fields or dimensions of
the construct – instructional process, student learning, and assessment – that reflect the historical
conceptual work that underpins these assessments, such as Voss, Kunter and Baumert’s (2011[29]) PPK,
and König et al.’s (2011[33]) four task-based dimensions. König provides a description of these three fields
or dimensions:
•

instructional process (including teaching methods, didactics, structuring a lesson, and classroom
management)

•

student learning (including their cognitive, motivational, emotional individual dispositions; their
learning processes and development; and their learning as a group that, therefore, takes into
account student heterogeneity and adaptive teaching strategies)

•

assessment (including diagnosing principles irrespective of the subject, and evaluation
procedures) (König, 2014, p. 49[28]).

These three dimensions of GPK were subsequently used to structure the assessment framework for the
2016 ITEL Teacher Knowledge Survey Pilot study, and they also provide the basis for describing GPK in
the present assessment framework.

4 Measuring and assessing GPK
Measurement of GPK in the TALIS 2024 TKS
While general pedagogical knowledge remains under-researched relative to CK and PCK, it is important
to measure for several reasons. GPK and domain-specific knowledge are both considered important
determinants of quality teaching (Baumert et al., 2010[1]), and distinguishable from each other. Leijen et al.
(2022, p. 207[2]) claim that the focus of research on PCK and CK, without due attention to GPK, has
highlighted a “…risk of narrowing the issues actually related to GPK down to the subject-specific, i.e. PCK,
level.” König et al. (2016[3]) found that a three-dimensional model of teachers’ knowledge comprising latent
variables of CK PCK and GPK is stronger than a one-dimensional model, with PCK and GPK being more
highly intercorrelated than CK and PCK. This makes it essential to measure and investigate GPK.
The measurement of GPK in an international survey of teacher knowledge provides countries with
information about their teachers’ GPK that could inform initial teacher education courses and teacher
professional learning opportunities. König (2014[4]; König, 2013[5]) and Choy et al. (2013[6]) have claimed
that GPK growth does not end after starting work as a teacher, and so it is important to have standardised
measures of GPK to systematically investigate the development of GPK during teacher education and the
in-service phase. In addition, more research is required to learn about the individual, social and institutional
factors that influence the development of GPK. While research has been investigating associations
between aspects of GPK and outcomes for teachers – e.g. Voss et al.’s (2017[7]) German study of
classroom management knowledge and teacher emotional exhaustion – there is a need for more
information at an international scale.
Researchers have emphasised the need for instruments that measure teacher knowledge as a necessary
precondition for its systematic study (Tittle, 2006[8]; Voss, Kunter and Baumert, 2011[9]). Measures of
teacher knowledge, including GPK, are best conducted as direct assessments, rather than inferred from
observation (Voss, Kunter and Baumert, 2011[9]). Results from studies of teacher knowledge involving
self-assessment have shown that there is only a weak correlation between self-assessments and
performance in standardised knowledge tests (König, Kaiser and Felbrich, 2012[10]). This emphasises the
need for standardised ways to measure teacher knowledge, including GPK. Such measures are needed
to investigate the impact of teacher competence on outcomes such as student learning and instructional
quality (Voss, Kunter and Baumert, 2011[9]).
Following the Innovative Teaching for Effective Learning (ITEL) pilot study (Sonmark et al., 2017, pp. 108110[11]), several recommendations for the development of the present TKS were made. These were taken
into account in the final instrument design for the TALIS 2024 TKS module and encompass the following:
•

The inclusion of more items that better measure knowledge regarding judgement and decision
making. A balance of items that ensures some coverage of declarative knowledge of theories and
concepts, but also practice-based items has been achieved, acknowledging the importance of
teachers’ procedural and conditional knowledge that draws upon professional judgement.

•

The inclusion of the role of technologies in teaching and learning. While a small number of
self-report items in the ITEL pilot enquired about opportunities to learn about ICT in teaching, none

TALIS TEACHER KNOWLEDGE SURVEY 2024 CONCEPTUAL AND ASSESSMENT FRAMEWORK © OECD 2025

26 |
enquired about the role of technologies in teaching and learning as part of the knowledge
assessment. The recommendation to include items on teachers’ knowledge of technology as it
relates to pedagogy was heeded in the development of items for the pilot and field trials, and is in
keeping with the contemporary education system issue of the educational use of technology from
the TALIS 2024 core survey.
•

The inclusion of specific thematic foci of high relevance, such as diversity. Similarly, there were
few knowledge items in the ITEL pilot relating to teaching in diverse classrooms. A focus on
teachers’ knowledge relating to teaching both cognitively and socio-culturally diverse classrooms
has been maintained, again in keeping with the TALIS 2024 core survey’s focus on diversity and
equity in education.

Assessment framework for the measurement of GPK in the TALIS 2024 TKS
The TALIS 2024 TKS assessment framework is based on the conceptual analysis presented previously in
this section. It retains the three dimensions of the Centre for Educational Research and Innovation (CERI)
ITEL assessment framework, based on the work of König (2014[4]): instruction, learning and assessment.
1. Instruction: focuses on teachers’ knowledge of teaching. This includes the theoretical and
practical aspects of planning and facilitating lessons and ways of managing students’ on-task
engagement during lessons.
2. Learning: focuses on teachers’ knowledge of how students learn. This encompasses the cognitive
and affective dimensions that influence learning.
3. Assessment: focuses on teachers’ knowledge of assessing student learning. It incorporates

reference to the very broad concept of “research literacy” improves the theoretical validity of the
sub-dimension within the Assessment dimension.

Emergent priorities for teacher knowledge
A professional expectation of teachers is that they regularly update their pedagogical knowledge. Existing
measures of GPK tend to focus on established or traditional theories of instruction, learning and
assessment at the expense of new knowledge or approaches related to emergent priorities (König, 2014[4]).
However, if GPK is an evolving field of knowledge, with teachers “…expected to process and evaluate new
knowledge relevant to their core professional practice and to regularly update their profession’s knowledge
base.” (Van Damme, 2017, p. 3[25]), an instrument for the measurement of teachers’ GPK must include a
portion of items relating to relevant emerging pedagogical priorities.
In response to the contemporary issues in education that were defined as relevant for the TALIS 2024 core
survey, the themes and recommendations emerging from the ITEL TKS pilot study (see Figure 4.2), and
initial consultations with countries participating in the TKS module of TALIS 2024, four emergent priorities
for teachers’ pedagogical knowledge were identified:
•

teaching diverse learners

•

using technology for teaching

•

supporting social and emotional learning

•

developing 21st century thinking skills.

These four areas, or sub-themes, constitute the “Emergent priorities for teacher knowledge” theme in the
TKS instrument. A selection of items addresses teachers’ general pedagogical knowledge related to these
sub-themes, across the three dimensions of instruction, learning and assessment.

Teaching diverse learners
The GPK construct recognises implicitly that teachers cater for a range of student needs and adapt their
teaching according to individual learner characteristics and capacities. Globally, there is increasing
recognition that understanding learner diversity is a priority for teachers (Rowan et al., 2020[26]), who are
required to update their knowledge to accommodate diverse student needs (Ulferts, 2019[27]). “Diversity”
relates to equity in education because teachers who adapt pedagogies according to their students’ needs
engender classroom cultures of learning and inclusiveness (Toledo-Figueroa, Révai and Guerriero,
2017[28]). Adaptive teaching competency has also been shown to have a significant positive effect on
student achievement (Brühwiler and Vogt, 2020[29]).
That the demonstration of this adaptive capacity has become a professional standard required of teachers
in some national contexts is an indicator of the growing emphasis on the diverse nature of learners.
Toledo-Figueroa, Révai and Guerriero’s (2017[28]) analysis of teaching standards from five
English-speaking countries identified adaptation to diverse needs, abilities, strengths, interests, skills, and
knowledge through differentiation as a common expectation of teachers. At the same time, the TALIS 2018
results revealed that teachers reported a relatively high need for professional development on teaching
culturally and/or linguistically diverse classrooms (OECD, 2019[30]). This level of need is consistent with
the findings of Rowan et al. (2020, p. 113[26]) in their review of literature addressing teacher education and
the preparation of teachers, which identified teachers’ lack of confidence in working with “diverse learners”
– a term they state is increasingly in use.
A question of interest in relation to GPK has been how to conceptualise the notion of classroom diversity,
and whether it is, in fact, treated as synonymous with concepts of student heterogeneity present in other
models of GPK, e.g. Voss, Kunter and Baumert (2011[9]), and, therefore, associated with teacher’s
adaptivity (König et al., 2011[15]). In earlier research by König et al. (2011, p. 191[15]), teacher adaptivity, as
a dimension of GPK, was covered by “strategies of differentiation” and “use of a wide range of teaching
methods”. While adaptivity assumes some level of student heterogeneity in the classroom, it could perhaps
be interpreted as referring only to diverse learning needs, and thus not necessarily implying the need for

TALIS TEACHER KNOWLEDGE SURVEY 2024 CONCEPTUAL AND ASSESSMENT FRAMEWORK © OECD 2025

| 31
teachers to adapt to and accommodate cultural or social diversity. This lack of specificity means that
adaptivity could simply refer to meeting the cognitive, affective or developmental learning needs of
individual students through adaptive instruction (Smale-Jacobse et al., 2019[31]) rather than, for example,
attending to cultural inclusiveness and fostering intercultural understanding in whole-class instruction.
Indeed, Voss, Kunter and Baumert’s (2011[9]) model of general pedagogical and psychological knowledge
(PPK) defines student “heterogeneity” in exactly these psychological, rather than socio-cultural, terms.
In many countries, teachers now have a wider responsibility to educate students with special educational
needs, which, historically, was an area for special education professionals to a greater extent.
Heterogeneous classrooms require teachers to respect each child’s individuality and be adaptive in
immediate interactions (Choy et al., 2012[12]). With educational philosophy moving towards inclusion,
teachers are expected to have certain pedagogical knowledge related to special needs (Happo and Määttä,
2011[32]). This knowledge is necessary for supporting students operating at different levels or rates of
learning, but also students that require more attention due to emotional or behavioural difficulties, or
physical disabilities (e.g. visual impairment). Even though inclusive education has become a widely
recognised priority, research still shows that many teachers lack the specific knowledge that is needed to
appropriately support children with special needs, e.g. Bruggink, Goei and Koot (2016[33]) and Leko et al.
(2015[34]). Co-ordinators of initial teacher education and professional development programmes have also
indicated that it is challenging to prepare teachers for working with special needs students (Rock et al.,
2016[35]). This raises the importance of including knowledge about special educational needs as part of a
focus on classroom diversity in teacher training.
Additionally, in certain national contexts, diversity might also be seen to include a particular focus on
cultural diversity caused by demographic change, and imply a need for teacher knowledge specifically to
accommodate students with diverse ethnic, linguistic, religious or migrant backgrounds (Florian and Pantić,
2017[36]; Forghani-Arani, Cerna and Bannon, 2019[37]). Such an expansion of the concept of a diverse
classroom would, for example, require knowledge related to instruction for cultural inclusion and cultural
responsiveness (Gay, 2015[38]; Ladson-Billings, 1995[39]).
A broad definition of classroom diversity, therefore, is the most suitable for a range of national and cultural
contexts. It is described by Forghani-Arani, Cerna and Bannon (2019, p. 9[37]) as a descriptive term that
…refers to the wide range of differences in students’ attributes and needs. In the context of schooling, relevant
differences may consist of students’ interests, experiences, aptitudes, abilities, learning styles, exceptionalities,
gender, sexual orientation, socioeconomic status, immigrant and/or minority background such as cultural,
ethnic, national, religious and language backgrounds.
The TALIS 2024 TKS thematic focus on teachers’ knowledge of educating in diverse classrooms uses a
similarly broad definition of “diversity”, with “educating”, meaning both catering for the individual needs of
a range of learners, and acknowledging and acting in response to the diversity within the classroom –
cultural or otherwise – for the benefit and betterment of all students.

Using technology for teaching
A recommendation from the ITEL TKS pilot study report was to include a focus on the role of technologies
in teaching and learning via “…a series of questions on technology as it relates to general pedagogical
knowledge…” (Sonmark et al., 2017, p. 110[11]).
General pedagogical knowledge, as it relates to the use of technology, has received its own label in the
Technological Pedagogical and Content Knowledge (TPACK) literature, e.g. Mishra and Koehler (2006[40]):
“technological pedagogical knowledge” (TPK). Conceived as the intersection between the “pedagogical
knowledge” and “technological knowledge” components of the TPACK framework, TPK refers not simply
to an understanding of how to use available technologies, but the pedagogical affordances and constraints

TALIS TEACHER KNOWLEDGE SURVEY 2024 CONCEPTUAL AND ASSESSMENT FRAMEWORK © OECD 2025

32 |
offered by them, and how they may enhance, alter or detract from teaching and learning processes (Mishra
and Koehler, 2008[41]).
Understanding teachers’ pedagogical knowledge in relation to technologies became more prominent due
to the widespread, accelerated adoption of online platforms and other technologies to support teaching
and learning during the COVID-19 pandemic (OECD, 2021[42]). While education mediated through
technology during COVID restrictions was found to have enabled a degree of collaboration, personalisation
and authenticity in student learning, it was also shown to highlight the importance of supportive pedagogies
to enhance students’ motivation to learn (Yates et al., 2020[43]) and to have created challenges for teachers
and students in maintaining authentic engagement (Ewing and Cooper, 2021[44]). A German study found
that teachers’ TPK strongly predicted their capacity to maintain communication with students during
COVID-19 lockdowns and deliver adaptive teaching to meet the challenges on online teaching and
assessment (König, Jäger-Biela and Glutsch, 2020[45]).
As digital technologies become increasingly ubiquitous, such knowledge is consequently becoming integral
to teacher’s GPK base (Scherer and Teo, 2019[46]), with some authors regarding TPK as an extension of
GPK (Cox and Graham, 2009[47]). It is an open question, therefore, whether the dissociation of these two
constructs in the literature serves some useful purpose or may in fact be an artifice caused by there being
separate research traditions in the first place (Ulferts, 2019[27]).
Despite the omission in the ITEL TKS pilot study of any GPK items related to technology use (Sonmark
et al., 2017, pp. 109-110[11]), the recommendation made in the report to introduce such items in future
iterations of the survey appears well justified. Recent research provides promising evidence of the ability
to construct valid measures of pre-service teachers’ TPK (Baier and Kunter, 2020[48]), while other studies
demonstrate strong links between teachers’ TPK and pedagogical knowledge (Lachner, Backfisch and
Stürmer, 2019[49]) and their elicited pedagogical reasoning (Heitink et al., 2017[50]).
Disparities in access to technology (OECD, 2021[42]) and the rapid pace of technological development
mean it is difficult to establish a stable and consistent body of technological pedagogical knowledge to
assess (Willermark, 2021[51]), making it challenging to develop items that fairly and validly assess teachers’
TPK across countries. However, consultations with participating countries revealed near-unanimous
agreement as to its relevance to GPK and inclusion as a thematic focus in the 2024 TKS.

Supporting social and emotional learning
A further sub-theme within the TKS relates to teachers’ general pedagogical knowledge of students’ social
and emotional learning (SEL). While SEL in schools can be taught formally through specific programmes,
this presents a risk that it is seen as an “add-on” to general teaching and, therefore, peripheral to GPK.
Early GPK research suggested some uncertainties about the extent to which there was widespread,
cross-cultural agreement on how best to nurture students’ social and moral development, and so this
important aspect of teaching has often held an uncertain place within measures of GPK – see, for example,
König et al. (2011[15]). However, attending to students’ social and emotional development and providing
emotional support in classroom interactions with students is widely considered as a key domain in models
of quality teaching, e.g. Hamre and Pianta (2013[52]), that significantly improves not only students’ social
and emotional skills but also their academic performance (Durlak et al., 2011[53]). With research advocating
the teaching of SEL through its integration into daily academic instruction rather than through stand-alone
programs (Jones and Bouffard, 2012[54]; Kendziora and Yoder, 2016[55]), it is, perhaps, increasingly likely
that knowledge of SEL will be seen as forming a part of teachers’ pedagogical knowledge more broadly.
The Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) framework defines SEL as
comprising five core competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship
skills and responsible decision making (Borowski, 2019[56]). This framework provides the theoretical
background for items on SEL in the core TALIS survey. By contrast, the OECD study on students’ social

TALIS TEACHER KNOWLEDGE SURVEY 2024 CONCEPTUAL AND ASSESSMENT FRAMEWORK © OECD 2025

| 33
and emotional skills (SSES) was conceptually based on the well-known “Big Five” model of personality,
and defined 17 SEL skills in relation to these five domains: task performance, emotional regulation,
collaboration, open-mindedness and engaging with others, as well as additional indices relating to
self-efficacy and motivation (Kankaraš and Suarez-Alvarez, 2019[57]). This framework formed the basis of
this recent international survey, which assessed students’ social and emotional skills, as well as factors
influencing the development of these skills, including teaching methods and practices (OECD, 2021[58]).
Within the assessment framework of GPK, knowledge related to SEL is included, most notably, in the
sub-dimensions relating to learner development and affective-motivational dispositions towards learning,
but it extends beyond these. Knowledge related to social and emotional learning has significant
implications for positive classroom management (Jennings and Greenberg, 2009[59]) and instructional
decisions (Yoder, 2014[60]). Accessing and understanding data on factors such as behaviour, self-esteem
and motivation may also have implications for how educators make data-driven decisions in their teaching
(Reeves, Wei and Hamilton, 2022[61]). As a consequence, SEL potentially also relates to data literacy,
interpretation and use as part of the assessment dimension of GPK.
As teachers are often the primary implementers of SEL instruction in schools (Brackett et al., 2011[62]),
developing an understanding of teachers’ knowledge of factors that relate to students’ social and emotional
well-being is crucial. A nationally representative survey of teachers in the United States showed strong
majorities advocating the importance of teaching SEL skills, believing these skills to be teachable and to
have significant impacts on the academic performance of students (Bridgeland, Bruce and Hariharan,
2013[63]). Effective implementation of SEL teaching relies also on teachers’ own social and emotional
competence, including the knowledge required for developing classroom climates conducive to fostering
students’ social, emotional and academic growth (Jennings and Greenberg, 2009[59]). Studies examining
pre-service teacher education programmes and teacher preparedness in the United States find that – in
general – little attention is paid to providing pre-service teachers with knowledge and skills related to
promoting SEL in their future classrooms (Schonert-Reichl, Kitil and Hanson-Peterson, 2017[64]) and that
there is a need to increase and improve the provision of training in SEL for both pre-service and in-service
teachers (Murano et al., 2019[65]).
Several items within the TKS, therefore, address teachers’ knowledge of concepts and skills relevant to
the teaching of SEL, such as self-efficacy, growth mindset, collaboration and motivation; constructs which
are common to both the CASEL and SSES frameworks.

Developing 21st century thinking skills
The transition in the 21st century from industrial modes of production to technological and
knowledge-based economies has prompted many national education systems to establish policies and
guidelines designed to equip learners with the skills necessary to flourish in this emerging economic reality
(Ananiadou and Claro, 2009[66]). Often referred to as “21st century skills” for this reason, such
cross-curricular skills are widely valued by countries across the OECD and the world, and they are explicitly
included in the education vision and mission statements, policy or curriculum documentation of over 120
national education systems (Care et al., 2018[67]).
Although these skills are variously described across different education systems, e.g. as “transversal” skills
“general capabilities” or “common skills” (Weldon, 2020[68]), the inherently generalised nature of these skills
suggests that they can be taught and learned across learning areas. Consequently, a knowledge of what
these skills entail and how to teach them can be considered an important part of teachers’ GPK. As the
authors of the ITEL pilot study note:

Helping students develop “21st century” skills, such as creativity, innovation, critical thinking, and problem
solving has become a basic requirement for teachers. Hence, teachers must regularly integrate emerging
evidence on how to create learning environments that support the development of these competencies into
their knowledge base (Sonmark et al., 2017, p. 74[11]).
The skills that are classified as 21st century according to various frameworks is vast (Dede, 2010[69]). Such
skills can often be categorised as belonging to at least one of three domains of competence: cognitive,
intrapersonal, and interpersonal. These domains are considered integral to enabling deeper learning,
through facilitating the transferability of knowledge to new contexts and challenges; they are skills that are
considered necessary for future work (Pellegrino, 2017[70]). Of these, the TKS maintains a specific focus
on cognitive skills, such as critical thinking, creativity and problem solving. Indeed, such cognitive skills are
among the most frequently cited within national policy documents (Care et al., 2018[67]); thus, it is probable
that teachers around the world are more likely to have updated their knowledge of these skills rather than
others.
Heeding the recommendation from (Guerriero and Révai, 2017[71]), and following from the ITEL TKS pilot
study, the TALIS 2024 TKS module includes 21st century thinking skills as a thematic focus, with items
designed to elicit teachers’ GPK related to the teaching of critical thinking, creativity and problem solving.

International validation of the GPK construct and assessment framework
Establishing the theoretical and external validity of the TKS conceptual and assessment frameworks has
been viewed as key to the success of the TKS. Much comparative pedagogy research seeks to find a
balance between identifying universal aspects of quality teaching while remaining sensitive to the
differences of cultural contexts in which they arise. For example, the suitability, or the true extent, of
learner-centred policy reforms within developing nations – e.g. Nykiel-Herbert (2004[72]); Serbessa
(2006[73]) – or those with a strong Confucian Heritage Culture – e.g. Nguyen, Terlouw and Pilot (2006[74]);
You (2019[75]) – have been the subject of much commentary. Inferences made about the need for education
policy reform based on what are often perceived and portrayed as “Western” pedagogies, are subject to
the criticism of assuming without validation that “good teaching” is context-neutral (Alexander, 2000[76];
2009[77]; Kelly, 2013[78]; Zyngier, 2016[79]). Three methods of validation have supported the development
and external validity of the TKS conceptual and assessment frameworks:
•

reviews of teaching models, frameworks and national teaching standards

•

support by the specifically established Teacher Knowledge Expert Group (TKEG)

•

expert feedback from participating countries.

Review of national teaching standards
Given the relative recency of international research into GPK (Guerriero, 2017[80]; Ulferts, 2019[27]), the
construct applied in the TKS is still somewhat nascent and its validity across diverse nations and cultures
appeared largely untested at the time of its inception. The model of GPK developed by CERI, upon which
the present study is based, divides GPK into the three main knowledge dimensions of instruction, learning,
and assessment, and was derived from an extensive review of the existing research into assessing GPK
(König, 2014[4]). However, this research had, at the time, mainly been conducted in Western European
countries (Austria, Germany, Switzerland) with fewer studies involving other countries (e.g. TEDS-M:
Chinese Taipei, Germany and United States; PRAXIS II: United States).
While the 2016 ITEL pilot study represented a significant step forward in establishing the construct across
a broader range of national cultures and education systems (Estonia, Greece, Hungary, Israel, and the
Slovak Republic), the participant countries were still predominantly European, or with strong cultural links
to Western Europe. A review of the literature concerning GPK published since the ITEL study reveals that

the research field has remained quite Eurocentric. This necessitates other means of establishing the
external and international validity of the construct.

countries. Teacher professional standards are used by governments for the purpose of licensure,
accountability, and the promotion of teaching excellence within the profession and, so, constitute a
reasonable source of evidence of the professional knowledge (including general pedagogical knowledge)
teachers are expected to maintain in different countries (Toledo-Figueroa, Révai and Guerriero, 2017[28]).
Existing research in this area suggests significant agreement between a select number of national teacher
standards and the present construct of GPK, in terms of the knowledge teachers need to acquire and

the standards of five major education systems in the English-speaking world: Australia, England (United

In case study research examining the interaction between teacher professional standards and teacher

nation’s teacher standards directly against the CERI/OECD assessment framework of GPK to determine
the extent to which these standards required knowledge related to the dimensions of instruction, learning
and assessment. Where standards did not explicitly identify areas of knowledge, but were described using
verbs (e.g. plans, notices, analyses) to denote tasks or skills of teachers, the author inferred the implicit
knowledge requirements associated with these standards (what knowledge would be necessary to perform
such tasks). Though each hailed from geographically and characteristically distinct education systems, all
three sets of national standards were found to require general pedagogical knowledge of instruction,
learning and assessment, either explicitly or implicitly.
Following a methodology similar to that used by Révai (2018[81]), teacher professional standards from four
countries participating in the 2024 TKS module have been analysed against the dimensions and
sub-dimensions, and thematic orientation, of the present TKS assessment framework for GPK:
1. Council for Educators: Professional Teaching Standards (South Africa)
2. National Council for Education and Training: Framework of National Qualification Standards for
Teachers in Primary and Secondary Schools (Croatia)
3. Council of Chief State School Officers: InTASC Model Core Teaching Standards (United States)
4. Center for Improvement, Experimentation and Pedagogical Research: Framework for Good
Teaching (Chile)

This represents an important contribution to the validation of the construct of GPK, as the countries
represented in the analysis reflect significant cultural and geographical diversity.
The analysis intended to reveal whether – at a minimum – teacher professional standards in such countries
require general pedagogical knowledge related to each of the six sub-dimensions in the TKS assessment
framework, either explicitly or implicitly. Three significant findings from this analysis emerged:
1. In all sets of teacher professional standards analysed, each sub-dimension of GPK is reflected in
at least one standard (and often several). This strongly suggests the GPK assessment framework
has a degree of international validity.
2. All four of the sub-themes reflected in the “Emergent priorities for teacher knowledge” category of
items are also reflected across the four frameworks analysed, particularly those related to diversity
and technologies, which are present in all four sets of professional standards. The framework from
Croatia includes a significant focus on standards relating to social and emotional learning, while
those from Chile and the United States also include explicit references to the teaching of critical
thinking and creativity skills.
3. All four sets of teacher professional standards indicate the need for teachers to have knowledge
of how to analyse, interpret and effectively use assessment data to plan instruction and/or to
monitor students’ progress. This supports the inclusion of the Assessment sub-dimension relating
to data literacy, interpretation and use.

federal systems, national sets of teacher professional standards may not universally apply.

Establishment of a Teacher Knowledge Expert Group (TKEG)
A Teacher Knowledge Expert Group (TKEG) has been established for consultation and feedback on the
development of the conceptual and assessment frameworks and review of GPK items. The members of
the TKEG are:
•

Drew Gitomer - Professor, Rose and Nicholas DeMarzo Chair in Education, Graduate School of
Education, at Rutgers University, United States

•

Charalambos Y. Charalambous – Associate Professor, Department of Education, University of
Cyprus, Cyprus

•

Liina Malva - Lecturer of Special Education and Inclusive Education, School of Educational
Sciences, Tallinn University, Estonia.

The role of the TKEG members was to provide feedback on the construct of GPK and on drafts of the
conceptual framework for the assessment of GPK developed by ACER, including feedback on the
identification of thematic foci for items included in the item pool. For item review, the TKEG provided ratings
and suggestions or feedback on new and existing items for consideration in the context of an international
survey. Item ratings independently provided by TKEG members were then analysed and aggregated to
determine final items for the pilot and field trial with countries, and again for inclusion in the main survey.
For the final 52 items included in the main survey, TKEG item ratings showed a high degree of consensus,
with all three members agreeing to include 44 items and exclude six items, while two of the three members
agreed to include a further six items and exclude two items. Members of the TKEG have also guided the
selection of background and contextual questions to ensure consistency and links with the core TALIS
questionnaire.

Expert feedback from participating countries
Through the national project managers (NPMs), representatives from professional teacher standards
bodies, national experts and teacher education institutions in the countries that participate in the TKS
module were invited to review a refined version of the TKS conceptual and assessment frameworks. The
national experts’ review considered cultural sensitivity, alignment with teacher professional standards, and
the overall applicability of the framework to teachers and teaching in their country. This was achieved by
providing them with a confidential, revised draft of the conceptual framework and inviting structured
feedback via a pro-forma response. Feedback from participating countries was initially reviewed
and assessed by the members of the TKEG, who made recommendations for further revisions before drafts
of the TKS conceptual framework were submitted to the TALIS Governing Board (TGB).

Annex 4.B. Research studies’ contribution to the
GPK dimensions and sub-dimensions
The following tables present a selection of teaching standards from each of four sets of professional
teaching standards from TKS participating countries, aligned to the dimensions and sub-dimensions of
general pedagogical knowledge. The general pedagogical knowledge requirements associated with each
standard are taken from the description of each GPK sub-dimension as presented in Table 4.1.
These standards have also been aligned with the thematic orientation categories of the TKS assessment
of GPK: as being related either to core knowledge content or content related to the four emergent priorities
for teacher knowledge sub-themes: teaching diverse learners, using technology for teaching, supporting
social and emotional learning, and developing 21st century thinking skills.
The purpose of these tables is to validate the GPK assessment framework: to demonstrate its alignment
to national sets of standards at the level of GPK sub-dimensions. The tables are not intended to validate
national sets of standards by reference to the GPK assessment framework. They do not, therefore, reflect
an exhaustive mapping of all possible standards. For example, standards describing highly specific
pedagogical practices have not been included where to do so is superfluous to the requirements of the
task. Similarly, where standards appear to be more relevant to teachers’ content knowledge, pedagogical
content knowledge, or to non-pedagogical aspects of teaching, they have not been included in the tables
below.

5 Examining GPK in the context of

teachers’ professional competence

Introduction
While general pedagogical knowledge is considered a “main pillar” of teaching as a knowledge profession
(Ulferts, 2021[1]), the CERI conceptual framework of teachers’ professional competence (Figure 4.1)
suggests that it does not operate in isolation of other factors. Rather, it is informed and influenced by – and
in turn influences – other professional characteristics, inputs and competencies. Therefore, it is of interest
to measure teachers’ GPK concurrently with measures of other professional attributes of teachers.
Thus, the TKS includes a questionnaire that helps to contextualise the results of the GPK instrument and
allows them to be analysed in conjunction with other professional attributes, as well as background
characteristics of teachers. In keeping with the design of the Innovative Teaching for Effective Learning
(ITEL) TKS pilot instrument, these questions intend to measure three aspects of teacher professional
attributes: opportunities to learn (OTL), affective-motivational characteristics and reported teaching
practices. These foci were largely maintained for the TALIS TKS in 2024 using synergies with
corresponding constructs and questions in the TALIS ISCED level 2 core survey to allow a validation of
the ITEL instrument design in a larger and more culturally diverse range of countries. This presents
opportunities to gauge whether valid analytical inferences can be made at a country level between the TKS
and TALIS lower secondary school core survey and explore possibilities to combine information collected
in each (Ulferts, 2021[1]).
The number of questionnaire items was constrained by the need to make the length of the TKS comparable
to the TALIS ISCED level 2 core survey and avoid excessive burden on respondents. Priority has been
given to the module assessing GPK, which constitutes the main distinctive component of the TKS. As such,
a reduced number of questionnaire questions from those developed for the ITEL TKS instrument or the
TALIS ISCED level 2 core survey were included. Where it has been necessary to reduce the number of
questions asked, questions have been retained on the basis that they are believed to be those that are
most strongly correlated with GPK based on existing GPK research.
This section provides a conceptual mapping of links to the three teaching and learning content areas
included in the conceptual framework for the TALIS 2024 main survey that align strongly with aspects of
the teacher professional competence framework and are, thus, contextually relevant as self-report
components of the Teacher Knowledge Survey: teacher learning and development, occupational
perceptions (including teacher motivation), and teaching practices. A summary of these links can be found
in Table 5.1.

Opportunities to learn (OTL)
The opportunity to learn (OTL) concept is highly relevant to the development of teacher knowledge and
guided the section on teacher education and initial preparation in TALIS 2018 (Ainley and Carstens,
2018[2]). The conceptual framework for the TALIS 2024 ISCED level 2 core survey suggests that an
examination of differences in teachers’ opportunities to learn can help to explain and understand variation
in teacher knowledge development within, as well as across, countries. The framework proposes two main
ways in which teachers experience opportunities to learn and develop their knowledge: through initial
teacher education (ITE) and through continuing professional learning (CPL).
The framework of teachers’ professional competence posits that teachers’ OTL influence their pedagogical
and content knowledge (including their GPK). A measure of the opportunities teachers have had, or taken,
to learn about instruction, learning and assessment is, therefore, important in understanding their
demonstrated level of GPK. While it is reasonable to hypothesise a relationship between topics learned
and demonstrated knowledge, there is little research and scarce evidence that the content of teachers’
OTL can predict their level of GPK (Sonmark et al., 2017[3]). Some researchers have found correlations,
albeit only along some dimensions of GPK (König et al., 2017[4]), while others have shown pedagogical
OTL to be an important predictor for the development of GPK, significantly affecting the development of it
in pre-service teachers (Klemenz, König and Schaper, 2019[5]; Watson, Seifert and Schaper, 2017[6]).
Therefore, the opportunity exists for the TKS in 2024 to explore these associations further.
National policy can be informed by examining international variation in the nature and number of general
pedagogy-related topics teachers learn in their pre-service training (Schmidt, Cogan and Houang, 2011[7];
Schmidt et al., 2008[8]; Tatto and Hordern, 2017[9]) and in their continuing professional learning.
Comparisons with the requirements of national professional standards, or (at a country level) with the
perceived needs of teachers for professional learning as indicated in the main TALIS survey, are two such
ways in which those data could be informative.
The TKS module includes a detailed set of pedagogical OTL questions. While these are related to the
teachers’ learning and development content area of the ISCED level 2 core survey, they are not derived
from it. OTL questions from the core survey relate to the features and qualities of teachers’ OTL and are
too general in nature to infer any meaningful relationship with GPK specifically. Therefore, items have been
developed based on those used in the ITEL TKS pilot study.
The OTL questions included in the TALIS 2024 TKS ask specifically the extent to which teachers’ initial
teacher education or subsequent professional learning has prepared them in topics related to the three
dimensions of GPK: instruction, learning, and assessment. A further set asks teachers about their
opportunities to learn about topics relating to the emergent priorities of classroom diversity, technology in
education, social and emotional learning and 21st century thinking skills. This ensures greater alignment
with the content of the knowledge items within the GPK instrument, to allow for more meaningful analysis
of the relationships between them.

Affective-motivational characteristics
As noted in the occupational perceptions section of the conceptual framework for the TALIS 2024 main
survey, research has established interrelationships between aspects of teachers’ affective outlook on
teaching – their motivations, beliefs, job satisfaction and well-being – and levels of teacher learning and
effectiveness. Teachers’ occupational perceptions are tied to their perception of the demands of teaching
and the resources available to manage those demands. One such resource is their knowledge of general
pedagogy and, so, understanding the relationship of GPK with affective-motivational factors provides an

TALIS TEACHER KNOWLEDGE SURVEY 2024 CONCEPTUAL AND ASSESSMENT FRAMEWORK © OECD 2025

64 |
additional dimension by which the interrelatedness of occupational perceptions and teacher effectiveness
can be understood.
Guerriero and Révai (2017[10]) explain that, as an aspect of teachers’ professional competence,
affective-motivational factors include different dimensions of teacher motivation, such as career choice
motivation, achievement motivation and goal orientation, as well as beliefs about teaching and learning,
perceptions of teaching and of the profession, teachers’ self-efficacy and (sense of) professional
responsibility. Their teachers’ professional competence framework theorises that teachers’
affective-motivational characteristics – as well as their knowledge base – influence their teaching practices.
Indeed, models of teacher competence suggest affective-motivational characteristics can influence
whether and how teachers use available knowledge in their teaching (Blömeke and Delaney, 2012[11]). Yet,
little is known from research about the interplay between teachers’ beliefs and motivations and their GPK
regarding its effect on teaching quality (König, 2017[12]) and so examining the relationship between these
two dimensions of teacher competence could provide relevant insights for TALIS.
Some existing research has reported associations between in-service or pre-service teachers’ GPK and
various affective-motivational characteristics, such as their intrinsic and extrinsic motivations to teach
(König and Rothland, 2012[13]). Though recent investigation has found no significant direct association
between teacher self-efficacy and GPK (Depaepe and König, 2018[14]), earlier research found negative
associations between teacher burnout and GPK directly, as well as indirectly (via a positive correlation with
teaching-specific self-efficacy) (Lauermann and König, 2016[15]). High levels of GPK are thought to provide
a buffering effect on teacher burnout (Dicke et al., 2015[16]). Other research suggests that GPK is more
important for teachers’ professional persistence than their self-efficacy beliefs (Leijen et al., 2024[17]), and
that pre-service teachers with a competence profile indicating both a strong professional knowledge base
(including GPK) and a high degree of motivation and engagement are more likely to have remained in the
profession two years later, and report lower levels of emotional exhaustion and higher levels of job
satisfaction (Holzberger et al., 2021[18]). Teachers with lower levels of knowledge are more likely to have
dropped out of teaching in that same two-year period.
There is scope, therefore, for the TKS in 2024 to examine further the relationship between GPK and
teachers’ affective-motivational characteristics, such as whether teachers’ knowledge of pedagogy and
their self-efficacy have mediating effects on their enjoyment of teaching. Similarly, it may emerge from
analysis that more knowledgeable teachers report an intention to remain longer in the teaching profession.
Questions included in the TKS derive from those posed in the ISCED level 2 core survey as part of the
occupational perceptions content area, and include questions related to self-efficacy and indicators of
teacher well-being such as work-related stress, enjoyment of teaching and planned persistence with
teaching as a career.

Teachers’ work practices
Teaching practices – what teachers do in the classroom – are critical to student learning. The investigation
of teaching practices in TALIS necessarily focuses on generic practices, since covering subject-specific
practices in a single survey intended for teachers from different subject domains would be unrealistic.
Therefore, these naturally lend themselves to analysis in light of teachers’ knowledge of general pedagogy.
It has been proposed that teacher knowledge including GPK is important for high-quality teaching and,
therefore, improved student learning (Baumert et al., 2017[19]; Guerriero, 2017[20]; König, 2014[21]; Malva,
Leijen and Baucal, 2020[22]; Ulferts, 2019[23]). Research in this area suggests that measures of teachers’
GPK predict certain aspects of the quality of teachers’ instruction – such as their classroom management,
learning support, teacher clarity, student-teacher relationships and provision of structure - as taken by
other measures, such as student ratings and teacher reports (Depaepe and König, 2018[14]; König and
Pflanzl, 2016[24]; Voss et al., 2014[25]; Voss, Kunter and Baumert, 2011[26]).

One important study, for example, found that teachers with advanced GPK had increased student cognitive
activation and task engagement, and reduced student behavioural issues (Voss, Kunter and Baumert,
2011[26]). Higher levels of GPK are also associated with higher levels of situation-specific classroom
management skills (König and Kramer, 2016[27]) and, more recently, measures of GPK and classroom
management expertise have both been found to positively affect students’ learning achievement (Lenske
et al., 2016[28]) and predict instructional quality (König et al., 2021[29]). Skills in pedagogical adaptivity
demonstrated in written lesson planning have also been shown to be predicted by declarative knowledge
of adaptivity as measured in a standardised test of GPK (König et al., 2020[30]). It may also be the case
that greater general pedagogical knowledge is not only important for the level of a teacher’s instructional
quality but may also be a factor that reduces variation in teaching quality across their classes (Voss et al.,
2022[31]).
Though it is not possible to gather direct measures of teaching quality as part of TALIS, the TKS includes
questions from the TALIS core survey’s content area on teacher’s work practices, with a particular focus
on their reported teaching practices. These questions measure teachers’ approach to teaching against
seven identified teaching practices: classroom and time management; clarity of teaching; cognitive
activation; formative assessment and feedback; adaptation; supporting students’ social and emotional
learning; and support for consolidation. These questions include items adapted from material used in the
OECD Global Teaching InSights video study (OECD, 2020[32]) and the Classroom Assessment Scoring
System (CLASS) (Pianta, Hamre and Mintz, 2012[33]). Additional questions ask teachers about their level
of success in recent lessons in achieving a range of lesson aims, as well as their use of class time.
Beyond the inclusion of a selection of teaching practices questions, one additional question arising from
the TALIS core survey’s professional practices content area has been included in the TKS. This question
examines teachers’ professional collaboration with colleagues in their school. Though little research
appears to have been conducted on collaborative teacher practice in relation to GPK, it may be of interest
for countries to consider this relationship. For example, an argument can be made that collaboration can
assist in making individual teachers’ tacit knowledge codified and explicit (Révai and Guerriero, 2017[34])
and, therefore, more easily accessed. It may also be possible that more knowledgeable teachers are more
inclined towards collaboration with colleagues. While not providing a foundation for causal explanation,
relationships between level of knowledge as measured in the GPK component of the TKS and reported
collaborative practices in the questionnaire may, nevertheless, be of value to TALIS when analysing
teacher collaboration.

•

•

•

•

relationships between levels (or profiles) of GPK and teachers’ self-reported success in achieving

•

6 The TALIS 2024 TKS design
Population definitions and sampling
The target populations for teachers in the TALIS core survey and the TALIS Teacher Knowledge Survey
module are identical: teachers in schools providing education at ISCED level 2. To ensure consistency and
comparability, the definition of teachers is the same as that used in previous cycles of TALIS.
Teachers are those persons who, as part of their regular professional duties in the school, provide student
instruction in programmes at the respective ISCED level. Teachers who teach a mixture of programmes at
different levels, including the respective ISCED programmes, in the target school are included in the target
population. There is no minimum specified for how much teaching for the respective target population they
need to be engaged in. Principals are not considered as teachers, for the purpose of sampling, regardless
of whether they also teach alongside their duties as principal. Substitute and other emergency teachers
are not part of the target populations.
As in all previous cycles of TALIS, schools entirely devoted to students with special needs and schools
exclusively offering adult education are considered out of scope for the survey. However, teachers working
with students with special needs in regular schools are in scope.
The sampling approach in TALIS 2024 is a two-stage design, with schools as primary sampling units and
teachers as secondary sampling units. In the first stage, schools are selected with a systematic random
sampling approach, selection with probability proportional to size yet in some circumstances with equal
probability. In the second stage, teachers are randomly selected within each school. Where possible,
stratification of schools in the sampling frame is used to provide greater precision.
Samples for the main survey are defined in relation to known characteristics of school systems in each
country. These samples typically consist of approximately 200 schools per participating country. The same
schools are sampled for the TALIS core survey and the TKS, with teachers randomly assigned to each.
In schools with 35 or more eligible teachers, 20 teachers are selected for the core survey and 15 teachers
selected for TKS. This means that the nominal sample size for each country for the TKS includes about
3 000 teachers.
As part of the sampling process, schools that decline to participate may be replaced by other schools,
following approved procedures that ensure that replacement schools are similar to the original schools. To
minimise the risk of replacement bias, replacement schools have to belong to the same stratum and are
of similar size to their sampled school. Potential replacement schools are selected at the same time as the
original sample
If countries are prevented, for various reasons, from surveying teachers from all schools that are in scope
(e.g. in very remote schools or schools in areas affected by natural disasters), there are provisions to
exclude schools. However, no more than 5% of teachers within each target population may be excluded.

Instrument design for the measurement of general pedagogical knowledge
Various item formats, including constructed and selected response items, could be included in an
assessment of general pedagogical knowledge (GPK) and each has advantages and disadvantages
(Wilson, 2023[1]). The TEDS-M study assessed mathematics CK and PCK among teacher education
students using a few constructed-response items in addition to most selected-response items (Tatto, 2013,
p. 41[2]). Limitations on the time available to respondents, and constraints on resources for coding,
prevented constructed-response items being included in the TALIS 2024 TKS. Accordingly, and as with
the CERI/ITEL TKS pilot, all TKS items are selected response.
The TALIS 2024 Teacher Knowledge Survey (TKS) is expected to take approximately 45 minutes to
complete. It has two parts:
•

Part 1: An estimated 18-20-minute set of self-report background and contextual questions. All
teachers answer the same questions. The questions are in selected response format and include
Likert scale matrix questions. The questions derive mainly from the TALIS ISCED level 2 core
survey teacher questionnaire, with the OTL questions adapted from the ITEL TKS pilot study.

•

Part 2: An estimated 25-minute set of 39 selected-response items measuring general pedagogical
knowledge. The GPK section follows a rotated block design, meaning teachers will not all answer
the same GPK questions. Based on the results from field trial, simple multiple-choice items are
expected to yield dichotomous scoring while the complex multiple-choice items will yield
polytomous scoring.

Based on feedback from the TKEG, as well as from national experts and teacher focus groups in countries
participating in the pilot study, the intention was to afford more weight overall to items that measure
practice-based knowledge than theoretical/scientific knowledge. This feedback suggested practice-based
knowledge items measure the kinds of procedural and conditional knowledge considered to be more
directly relevant to, or reflective of, high-quality teaching. However, in the case of the Learning dimension,
as so much of what constitutes knowledge of learning emerges not from classroom practice but from
psychological theory, cognitive science and neuroscience, the weighting was reversed, favouring
theoretical/scientific knowledge.

field trial of items deemed to be practice-based as opposed to those with theoretical/scientific content. This

the item. As such, an overall weighting approaching a 50/50 balance, albeit slightly in favour of
practice-based items, has been achieved (see Table 6.2). This greater balance has the potential to allow
sub-scale analysis of the main survey data along these dimensions, to consider differences in teachers’

The limitations of measuring knowledge via selected-response items are well understood; open-response
items provide flexibility in accessing aspects of teachers’ knowledge that are expressed through
explanations of their own experience or classroom instructional decision making. While similar instruments
have employed constructed-response item formats, this requires human scoring. Future iterations of the
TKS may look to include open-response and innovative item formats – e.g. as flagged in Ulferts (2021[3])
– once the GPK construct and assessment framework have been validated across diverse national and
cultural contexts in the present survey.

Response time considerations
Unlike the TALIS core survey, respondents to the TKS are given a 24-hour window to complete the
instrument, which they are notified of prior to logging in. The purpose of this is to encourage completion of
the survey (and particularly the GPK section) in one sitting, without making the GPK section a speeded
test. These conditions were applied in the field trial and a 93% completion rate was achieved.

Field trial timing data for the TKS indicated that the average median response time for the GPK items was
38 seconds per item. When responding to the GPK component of the TKS, teachers are, therefore,
presented with 39 items, estimated to take 25 minutes, on average, to complete if teachers move through
the items without interruption.

coverage of the GPK framework by increasing the amount of test material overall, thereby providing a
basis for a more comprehensively described scale, without extending the time necessary to complete the

The GPK assessment items will be scaled using statistical models based on Item Response Theory (IRT

GPK from data collected with a rotated block design. Assessment items will be used to describe teachers’

Operational design
In line with TALIS 2024, the TKS comprised three major phases of international large-scale surveys: a
pilot, a field trial, and a main survey.

TALIS TEACHER KNOWLEDGE SURVEY 2024 CONCEPTUAL AND ASSESSMENT FRAMEWORK © OECD 2025

76 |
After an initial review of the pool of existing GPK items from the CERI ITEL TKS pilot study, and subsequent
development of new item material, a pilot study was conducted in early 2022. In total, 110 GPK cognitive
items were piloted in participating countries and, also, included self-report questionnaire material. A small
number of teachers in each country individually completed a test booklet using pen and paper. It further
collected qualitative feedback from teachers through moderated focus group discussions, mainly in the
form of a retrospective think-aloud exercise. The feedback collected, as well as parallel reviews by national
project managers (NPMs) and experts, produced useful information to further develop instruments and
operational plans.
The field trial of 78 GPK items and two forms of questionnaire material in early 2023 had two main
objectives: to test the survey instruments and operational procedures in all participating countries. Sample
sizes ranged from 133 to 513 teachers (from 24 to 40 schools) across participating countries. A school
co-ordinator, identified for each school, facilitated required communication, listing, distribution of materials,
and follow-up.
The main survey data collection was planned to be conducted in two waves relative to the different school
calendars in northern and southern hemisphere countries (Northern Hemisphere: February to April 2024,
Southern Hemisphere: June to August 2024). The main survey involves a nominal sample of approximately
3 000 teachers in 200 schools. A final selection of 52 items and a single form of questionnaire material
was included.
The TKS is a self-administered survey with online data collection as the exclusive response mode. All
source instruments were made available in English. National centres adapted and translated the
instrument and submitted it for international translation verification.
More generally, technical standards and operational manuals define the rules and guidelines for national
centres to follow when preparing and implementing the surveys within their country. The international
research consortium provided training to national centres on all operational activities and tasks.
International quality observers (IQOs) observed national implementations and collected information from
a sample of schools in each country.
Following all data collection, adjudication in consultation with the international sampling referee determines
the overall quality of the data, leading to recommendations on data quality, use, and reporting for the OECD
Secretariat and TALIS Governing Board. Finally, a fully documented international database, a technical
report, and a database user guide will be produced.

The Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) is the world’s largest international survey of teachers and principals. In 2024, TALIS added
the Teacher Knowledge Survey (TKS), a special module to investigate teachers’ general pedagogical knowledge (GPK). GPK is the specialised
knowledge of teachers for creating effective teaching and learning environments for all students, independent of subject matter; it is part of the
specialised body of knowledge that makes teaching a knowledge profession.
The TKS conceptual and assessment framework underpins the survey, describes its contents and explains its importance. Key themes in the
framework include teaching diverse learners, using technology for teaching and supporting social and emotional learning.
