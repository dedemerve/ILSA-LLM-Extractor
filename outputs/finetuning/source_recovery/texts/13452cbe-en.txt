Abstract 
This paper explores the state of teenage career development in England. It sets out findings 
from the 2022 round of the OECD Programme for International Student Assessment 
(PISA), a comparative international survey of young people in secondary education.  PISA 
2022 provides considerable data related to young people’s engagement in, and perceptions 
of, career development, including information on occupational and educational intentions 
and participation in career development activities.  This paper compares student responses 
in England to OECD averages, disaggregated by a range of student characteristics, 
including gender, socio-economic background, academic proficiency and school type 
attended.  This comprehensive analysis finds that in many ways student career development 
in England compares well with many other OECD countries, particularly countries 
identified as providing most relevant comparisons.  However, analysis of longitudinal 
cohort studies shows that students in England and across the OECD fail to engage 
sufficiently in career development by the age of 15.  In England, students from lower socio-
economic backgrounds engage less consistently in career development than their more 
socially advantaged peers.  As in many countries, low performing students in England 
demonstrate forms of career development that raise particular concerns.  The career 
expectations of all students align poorly with patterns of labour market demand.

Introduction 
This paper explores the state of teenage career development in England. It sets out findings 
from the 2022 round of the OECD Programme for International Student Assessment 
(PISA), a comparative international survey of young people in secondary education. In 
addition to assessing the academic proficiency of students at age 15 in reading, mathematics 
and science, PISA (http://www.oecd.org/pisa) provides considerable data on the career and 
educational expectations of students, the extent to which they participate in career 
development activities and the thoughts they have about external factors that can influence 
success in achieving career plans. It enables the exploration of factors that can be expected 
to shape success in career development, examining for example the ways in which gender, 
social background and academic proficiency shape engagement in career development 
activities and influence career thinking. Findings from the analysis can be considered in 
light of what is known about the ways in which teenage career development enhances or 
undermines success in the later labour market. Many career development questions in PISA 
link with insights from longitudinal datasets in multiple countries, including the 
United Kingdom, which find consistently positive relationships between forms of teenage 
career development, typically assessed at age 15, and employment outcomes, commonly at 
age 25 (OECD, 2021[1]).

Box 1. The career education system in England 
The Department for Education in England sees careers guidance as essential to setting individuals on the 
path to success and allowing talent to flourish, regardless of background.  The career education system in 
England is designed to deliver high-quality, coherent careers guidance, enabled through strong local 
collaboration between educators, training providers and employers, as recognised by the House of 
Commons Education Select Committee’s 2023 inquiry into careers. This brief introduction describes the 
framework that is in place to deliver young people’s careers guidance and the support available to help 
schools and colleges to develop their careers programmes. 
The Education Act 1997 introduced a statutory duty for publicly funded schools managed by local 
authorities (‘maintained schools’) to provide independent careers guidance for young people. The duty was 
extended by the Education (Careers Guidance in Schools) Act 2022 and now applies in both maintained 
schools and academy schools (academies are state-funded institutions (mostly schools), independent from 
local authorities and funded directly by the Department for Education) to all year 7-13 pupils (aged 11-18). 
Schools and colleges are expected to provide high-quality, impartial, tailored advice and guidance, 
including opportunities to learn about skills and apprenticeships, driven by the internationally recognised 
Gatsby Benchmarks. The eight Gatsby Benchmarks were created in 2013, drawing on international 
evidence to define what a world class careers provision in education looks like.    They provide a clear 
framework for organising the careers provision in schools and colleges. To ensure a strong focus on quality, 
the Office for Standards in Education, Children's Services and Skills (Ofsted) is legally required to 
comment, in an inspection report, on the careers guidance provided at colleges to 16- to- 18-year-olds and 
students aged up to 25 with an education, health and care plan. 
The government-funded Careers and Enterprise Company (CEC) supports schools and colleges to develop 
and deliver careers programmes in line with their own requirements and the Gatsby Benchmarks through a 
network of 44 Careers Hubs that facilitate partnerships with key employers committed to improving careers 
across a local area. Enterprise Coordinators work with around 400 major employers (Cornerstones) to bring 
together business and education. Some 4 000 volunteer Enterprise Advisers (senior business volunteers) 
support schools and colleges to help develop their career strategies and employer engagement plans. In 
addition, the CEC has trained over 3 000 qualified Careers Leaders in schools and colleges to design and 
deliver careers programmes tailored to the needs of their learners, including those with Special Educational 
Needs and Disabilities and from economically disadvantaged backgrounds, and to ensure parity of esteem 
between qualification routes. 
In 2022/23, 92% (4,616) of secondary schools and colleges in England were part of a Careers Hub, and 
4,534 schools and colleges reported progress against the Gatsby Benchmarks. 5.5 out of 8 
Gatsby Benchmarks were achieved on average in 2022/23 (up from 4.9 in 2021/22) and across the system 
this has increased from 1.8 in 6 years. Evidence from the CEC shows that there is a positive relationship 
between Gatsby Benchmark achievement and outcomes for young people, with double the impact for those 
economically disadvantaged. A recent report by the CEC shows that the longer schools and colleges are part 
of a Careers Hubs the more breadth and depth of exposure students have to employers. For example, 96% 
of young people in secondary education had at least one employer encounter last year, opening their eyes 
to choices and opportunities. 
In 2024, the Department for Education in England intends to publish a Strategic Action Plan for Careers 
setting out a vision for the next phase of England’s careers education system, building on the foundations 
described here to further develop careers provision in schools and colleges and beyond to be work-leading, 
responsive to trends, and to be fully inclusive.  
This description of the career education system in England is authored by the Department for Education, 
England (United Kingdom).

About the Programme for International Student Assessment 
Since the year 2000, OECD members and a growing number of non-member countries and 
economic areas have participated in the Programme for International Student Assessment. 
In 2022, the PISA survey gathered data from some 690 000 students, representing about 29 
million 15-year-olds in schools in the 81 participating countries and economies. Within 
PISA, students undertake tests on mathematics, reading and science to allow comparisons 
between education systems. In addition, students complete a background questionnaire, 
providing information about themselves, their attitudes, dispositions and beliefs, their 
homes, and their school and learning experiences. School principals completed a 
questionnaire about school management, organisation, and the learning environment. PISA 
provides sufficient data to categorise respondents by key equity characteristics: gender, 
economic, social and cultural status (ESCS), academic proficiency, migrant status, 
geographic location and school type).  
In the United Kingdom, 12 972 students from 451 schools participated in PISA 2022, 
representing about 731 200 15-year-old students (an estimated 97% of the total population 
of 15-year-olds) (OECD, 2023[2]). In England, total responses were received from 4 763 
students from 165 schools largely representative of all schools in England with regard to: 
school gender (mixed or single), average school attainment at GCSE (General Certificate 
of Secondary Education), the percentage of pupils eligible for special educational needs 
support, percentage of pupils with English as their first language, and the percentage of 
pupils eligible for free school meals (Ingram, 2023[3]). In England and a number of other 
OECD countries, the final sample of pupils demonstrated somewhat higher levels of 
academic attainment and somewhat lower proportions of students eligible for free school 
meals than the general population. Consequently, these groups may be over and under-
represented respectively and the OECD recommends that some caution is applied in 
reviewing results (Ingram, 2023[3]).   
For the first time in 2022, the PISA Student Questionnaire included an extensive range of 
questions concerning career development (OECD, 2021[4]). While most previous rounds of 
PISA had included some questions related to the career and educational plans of students, 
detailed questions on participation in career development activities were included in PISA 
2012 and 2018 within an optional separate Educational Questionnaire (OECD, 2017[5]). 
The United Kingdom opted into the 2018 Career Education Questionnaire. In PISA 2022, 
to reduce the length of the survey for students, matrix sampling was employed (OECD, 
2024[6]), meaning that smaller, but still representative, numbers of students responded to 
questions on participation in career development activities. On average, each question was 
responded to by 1 471 students. In sum, PISA 2022 provides an unprecedented source of 
national and comparative international data allowing new insight into the effectiveness, 
efficiency and equity of provision delivered by career guidance systems. In this discussion, 
provision is seen to be more effective when it provides students with interventions which 
are most strongly associated with better long-term employment outcomes, more efficient 
when it enables strong labour market signalling to students and more equitable where 
additional barriers hindering the progression of students linked to their personal 
characteristics are more actively addressed. Provision in England is contextualised through 
comparisons to the average PISA results across all OECD countries, excluding 
Luxembourg for which data is not available. The analysis cannot provide a comprehensive 
OECD assessment of the state of career development in England as it was not accompanied 
by a full review of guidance provision in the country.

Effective career development 
Effective career development provides students with opportunities to engage in guidance 
and work-related activities and to develop career thinking which are most confidently 
associated with better ultimate employment outcomes. OECD analysis of longitudinal 
datasets in ten countries, including the United Kingdom, provides a framework for 
understanding such effectiveness. The analysis has explored relationships between forms 
of teenage career development at age 15 and employment outcomes ten years later in terms 
of lower levels of being Not in Education, Employment and Training (NEET), higher 
wages and/or greater job satisfaction after taking into consideration an individual’s gender, 
social background, academic achievement and other personal characteristics. Integrating 
new findings into existing research literature, OECD analysis finds considerable evidence 
linked to specific forms of career development clustered around how teenagers explore, 
experience and think about their potential futures in work. The analysis confirmed 11 strong 
predictors of better outcomes and three predictors for which evidence is more partial 
(Covacevich, 2021[7]) (OECD, 2021[1]). As Table 1 indicates, the 2022 round of PISA 
provides considerable opportunity to assess the extent to which teenagers engage in or 
demonstrate such predictors of better ultimate employment outcomes.

Career exploration: participation in career development activities 
PISA asks students across the OECD about their participation in nine common career 
development activities. Students in England engage in such activities to a comparable or 
greater degree than the average for OECD peers in all areas but one. They are particularly 
more likely to have attended a job fair – which can provide important opportunities for 
students to explore their career interests with multiple people from the world of work 
(OECD, 2023[8]) and a tour of an institution offering ISCED 3-7 programmes which allows 
students to understand potential post-secondary educational pathways. Participation in job 
shadowing or work-site visits (“employer visit”) which offer opportunity for learning about 
potential careers in an authentic workplace setting is less common in England than across 
other OECD countries (29% v 45%) (Table 2). 
Participation levels in England compare favourably to OECD averages, but participation 
levels in many areas remain lower than quantitative research suggests is optimal for 
successful transitions. Career development is an incremental process where the capacity of 
students to visualise and plan their futures builds through engagement in different guidance 
activities over time (Moote, 2024[9]). In PISA 2022, only 22% of students in England report 
having taken part in each of three key activities (Table 46) which collectively can be 
expected to provide students with the opportunity to actively explore their career plans 
through authentic engagement with the working world and then to reflect upon their 
experiences: visiting a workplace, attending a job fair and speaking with a career advisor 
in school. However, this level of engagement is greater than in nine of ten comparable 
countries, save for Denmark (49%) (Table 46). As in other OECD countries, substantial 
numbers of students in England report that they have not engaged in these important 
activities: 71% have not visited a workplace, 41% have not attended a job fair and 30% 
have not spoken with a career advisor either in or out of school by the age of 15. Around 
two-third of students in England (and across the OECD) have not gained first-hand 
experience of the labour market through an internship/work placement (Table 6).  
During the COVID-19 pandemic, concerns were raised globally that career development 
activities with employers and people in work would be permanently constrained, but this 
does not seem to have been the case. A comparison of PISA data for 2018 and 2022 shows 
that UK student participation in internships grew by 12 ppt over the period (compared to 
an average growth of 2 ppt across OECD countries) and in job fairs by 21 ppt (compared 
to an OECD decrease of 2 ppt). However, participation in job shadowing/work-site visits 
fell in the UK by 23 ppt (compared to an average growth of 5 ppt across OECD countries) 
(Table 31). 
Student perspectives on obstacles and enablers in preparation for adult life 
In 2022, PISA introduced a series of new questions exploring student perceptions related 
to preparation for adult life. Across all OECD countries, the results suggest that many 
students are concerned about their futures and the capacity of their schools to plan 
effectively for them. In England, half of students say that they worry that they are not 
prepared for life after school or that they will not have the financial resources needed to do 
what they would like. A similar proportion feel that school has not given them confidence 
to make decisions and do not feel well-prepared for their future path.  One quarter of 
students in England agree with the statement that “School has been a waste of time” 
(Table 8). This is an indicator of instrumental motivation, the belief that investment in 
education will lead to positive personal outcomes. In longitudinal studies, instrumental 
motivation is associated with poorer employment outcomes than would be expected given 
the academic achievement and personal characteristics of students (Covacevich, 2021[7]).

Career thinking 
Significant relationships can be identified in PISA data between teenage participation in 
guidance activities and forms of career thinking associated with better employment 
outcomes. PISA participants are asked to write in the “kind of job” they expect to have 
when they are “about thirty”. Many do not respond to the question or only submit an answer 
that is too vague to classify. Given that more than three-quarters of students (76%) across 
the whole PISA sample who failed to provide details of an occupational expectation did 
write in responses to questions on parental occupation, it is likely that this career 
uncertainty is real and it is an important predictor of employment outcomes (Covacevich, 
2021[7]). Between 2015 and 2022, the proportion of students with a clear career plan fell 
dramatically both in England (from 83% to 53%) and across the OECD (from 80% to 61%) 
(Table 35). Career uncertainty at 15 is linked in multiple longitudinal studies, including a 
number from the UK, with poorer outcomes than would otherwise be expected 
(Covacevich, 2021[7]). It is not necessary that students have a fixed and final career plan at 
15, but the absence of any job expectation suggests a lack of critical engagement in career 
planning and a difficulty in investing strategically in education decisions that relate to not 
just choice of subjects, but how engaged they will be in their studies (Staff, 2010[10]) (Yates, 
2011[11]). 
When students in England do express an occupational plan, they can be categorised as very 
ambitious. Seven out of ten students in England (compared to six out ten students on 
average across the OECD) expect to work as a senior manager or as a professional (ISCO 
major categories 1 and 2) (Table 37) and in the samples in both England and the OECD, 
around two-thirds of students expect to complete tertiary education (Table 39). Such 
ambitions are commonly associated with better employment outcomes for youth 
(Covacevich, 2021[7]). However, a sizeable minority of students who plan on working in a 
career requiring tertiary education do not expect to achieve this level of education. Such 
students can be categorised as ‘misaligned’ and such apparent confusion has been 
associated in many longitudinal studies with poorer ultimate employment outcomes 
(Covacevich, 2021[6]).  In 2022, 27% of students in England can be categorised as 
misaligned. While this proportion has fallen since 2015, it is higher than the OECD average 
for 2022 at 21% (Table 41).  
Analysis of PISA 2018 data has shown strong connections between participation in 
different career development activities and forms career thinking that is associated with 
better ultimate employment outcomes: career certainty, ambition, alignment and 
instrumental motivation (Covacevich, 2021[7]). PISA 2022 data, using the same set of 
controls (performance in reading, gender and ESCS) also finds in England that participation 
in career development activities links to a comparable extent with more productive career 
thinking, an indication of the effectiveness of the provision received (Table 43).  However, 
the table also shows that, participation in career development activities on average across 
the OECD is consistently more strongly associated with higher levels of forms of beneficial 
career thinking. 
Efficient career development

the concentration of career expectations has fallen between 2015 (when 54% of all students

Equitable career development 
PISA data allows for disaggregation of student attitudes and experiences across a range of 
student characteristics, including gender, ESCS and migrant status. Such aspects are 
important to guidance policy as OECD analysis shows that such groups face additional 
barriers in converting the qualifications and experience they accumulate through education 
into successful employment (OECD, 2024[13]). PISA data also allows for student 
experiences to be segmented by academic performance, school type attended and 
geographic location.  
Gender 
PISA data from across OECD countries tends to show that boys engage more in activities 
that bring them into direct contact with employers and people in work and that girls are 
more likely to undertake activities independently. With regard to the former, only modest 
gender patterns are evident in England in relation to student participation in job 
shadowing/work-site visits, job fairs (Table 4) and internships (Table 7). However, girls 
in England are more likely to undertake such activities as using the internet to research 
careers and educational programmes (Table 4). One reason for this may be that boys feel 
parental occupational expectations much more strongly than girls, possibly dampening 
career curiosity. The data speaks to a sense of detachment among boys:  53% agree that 
school had done little to prepare them for adult life and 30% that school had been a waste 
of time (Table 9). Boys are more likely than girls to express uncertainty in terms of 
occupational expectation (Table 11) and when they do have job plan they are much more 
likely to show confusion about what they need to achieve to secure it: compared to 21% of 
girls, 34% of boys demonstrate career misalignment, planning on working in job that 
usually requires a tertiary education (ISCO 1-3 major categories), but not planning on

Social background 
Participation in career development activities in England is patterned by social background. 
Comparing the participation rates of the highest and lowest quartiles of students using the 
OECD index on Economic Social and Cultural Status (ESCS) (OECD, 2023[15]), the most 
socially advantaged students are more likely to engage in most activities, and notably more 
so with regard to job fairs and job shadowing/work-site visits – activities that bring students 
into direct contact with people in work and which are predictive of better ultimate 
employment outcomes (Table 4 and Figure 2). This equity gap has not closed since 2018 
and in some important fields, including job shadowing/work-site visits and job fairs, it has 
grown larger (Table 32). The pattern of inequity however is less pronounced when 
comparing students attending public and fee-paying independent schools (Table 4).

Young people from more socially disadvantaged backgrounds tend to have less resources 
outside of school to draw upon in planning their transitions through education into 
employment (OECD, 2024[13]) and this is seen in the PISA 2022 data in terms of regularity 
of discussions with family members about education plans (Table 5) and in accessing 
online information about careers and post-secondary education (Table 4). Students from 
the lowest ESCS quartile are consistently less likely than peers from the highest ESCS 
quartile to agree that they feel well prepared by their schools for the future or to see the 
extrinsic value in education in enabling transitions after the end of schooling (Table 9). 
However, many express ambitious jobs plans (Table 11): 61% of low ESCS students 
expect to work as senior managers or professionals (ISCO major categories 1 and 2), 
compared to 81% of high SES students (Table 15). Looking solely at the plans of high 
performers on the PISA mathematics assessment, the career expectations of high and low 
ESCS students are comparable. However, a large gap is clear in terms of the plans of high 
performers for tertiary education: 62% of low ESCS expect to go on to this level of post-
secondary education compared to 88% of high SES students (Table 23). PISA data show

As in other OECD countries, a particular concern in England emerging from the data relates 
to low performing students who might be expected to leave full-time education and enter 
the labour market earlier than high performing peers who most often plan on continuing 
into full-time secondary education (Marks, 2019[16]). Low performing students are much 
more likely to express career uncertainty. Fewer than half of such students in England have 
a clear idea about the type of employment they expect around the age of 30, a lower 
proportion than the OECD average (Table 11). When they do have a clear job plan, the 
proportion of low performing students whose expectations can be classified as misaligned 
has fallen steadily since 2015, but at 41% in 2022 this is higher than the OECD average at 
32% (Table 28). Many low performers on the PISA tests – demonstrating a level of 
academic proficiency which suggests strongly that they will struggle to benefit from tertiary 
education (OECD, 2016[17]) – articulate career plans (including becoming doctors, 
engineers and lawyers) which typically require high levels of university education 
(Table 13). Half of low performers in PISA 2022 say that they expect to work as senior 
managers or professionals (Table 15) and a similar proportion expect to complete tertiary 
education (Table 17 and Table 22), a figure that has grown since 2015. However, a much 
higher proportion of low performers (12%) expect to work in the skilled trades compared 
to high performers (3%) (Table 26).  
In England, low performing students typically engage less intensely in career development 
through their schools than their high performing peers. They are less likely to participate 
in job shadowing/work-site visit, job fairs, research careers or education programmes 
through the internet or, importantly, learn about alternatives to A levels as an option after 
the age of 16. However, they are more likely to speak with a guidance counsellor and to 
have undertaken an internship (Table 4 and Table 7).  
Migrant background. 
In this analysis, comparisons are drawn between native- and foreign-born students. In 
England on the whole, foreign-born students tend to engage more strongly than native-born 
peers in career development activities (Table 4) particularly in relation to activities, such 
as the use of internet, which can take place outside of schooling. Foreign-born students tend 
to be more positive about their futures and the role they feel that their schools are playing 
in enabling them (Table 9). The career plans of foreign-born students tend to be clearer 
(Table 36) but are narrower than their peers: 58% with an occupational expectation 
anticipate working in one of ten popular jobs, compared to 41% of native-born students 
(Table 13). Such aspirations focus heavily (and increasingly so – Table 38) on jobs in 
senior management and especially the professions, with more than eight in ten expecting 
to work in these fields (Table 15) and three-quarters expecting to complete tertiary 
education (Table 17) serving to reduce levels of career misalignment (Table 28). Foreign-
born youth are considerably more ambitious for their futures than their native-born peers, 
including lower performers. Nearly two-thirds of migrant students from the lowest 
performing quartile on the PISA mathematics assessment expect to complete tertiary 
education, compared to 40% of their native-born peers (Table 23). Unsurprisingly, foreign-
born youth are three times less likely than native youth to plan on working in the skilled 
trades (Table 26).

Annex A. Explanatory notes 
Terms 
Definition 
ESCS 
Socio-economic status is measured by the PISA index of economic, social and 
cultural status (ESCS). Socio-economically advantaged students are those 
positioned within the top quartile of ESCS within their respective country or economy, 
while socio-economically disadvantaged students fall within the bottom quartile. 
Geographic 
location 
Students residing in rural areas inhabit locations with populations of up to 3,000 
people. Those in towns reside in areas with populations ranging from 3,000 to 
100,000, while students in cities reside in areas with populations of 100,000 or more. 
Immigrant 
background 
Students are categorised as "native-born" if they are completing the PISA 
assessment in the country of their birth. All other students are classified as "foreign-
born". 
ISCO 
The International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO-08) is a framework 
developed by the International Labour Organization (ILO). Its purpose is to 
systematically classify and categorise various occupations worldwide, providing a 
structured framework based on the tasks and responsibilities associated with each 
occupation. 
ISCED 
The International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED 2011) is a framework 
established by UNESCO to enable the comparison of education systems across 
different countries. ISCED classifies educational programs into levels based on their 
complexity and duration. 
Performance 
High performers in PISA are students who have reached at least Level 4 proficiency 
in one of the core subjects — science, mathematics, or reading — while attaining at 
least Level 2 proficiency in the other two subjects. Conversely, low performers are 
those who have scored below Level 2 proficiency in any of these subjects. 
School type 
In this report, public schools encompass those managed by a public education 
authority, including local authority 'maintained' schools in England, as well as 
government-dependent private schools that receive over half of their funding from 
government sources, such as academies and free schools in England. Private 
schools, on the other hand, primarily rely on student fees or private contributions for 
funding. Furthermore, selective secondary schools, commonly referred to as 
'grammar' schools, have the autonomy to select their entire pupil intake based on 
general academic ability. The majority of grammar schools are academies, with a 
minority being maintained schools.
