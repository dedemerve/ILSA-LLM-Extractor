ABSTRACT
This study explored drone-based learning in educational contexts using a mixed-method design 
to identify key learning attributes. After completing researcher-developed drone tasks, 73 learners 
demonstrated a significantly improved understanding of drone concepts and proficiency in Blockly 
coding. However, learners perceived self-efficacy as significantly lower than other self-regulated 
strategies in drone activities. Task-based drone activities, facilitated by group settings, encouraged 
learners to develop metacognition through collective scaffolding methods, such as peer discussions 
and team testing. The identified learning attributes provide valuable insights for educators in designing 
assessments for collaborative drone problem-solving. Additionally, the interplay among effort 
regulation, problem-solving, and cooperativity observed in this study offers essential references for 
the future development of distributed expertise systems.

INTRODUCTION
Drones, or small unmanned aerial vehicles, have been applied in widespread domains, including 
education. Due to the flexibility and multiple functions of drones, the sensors of small unmanned 
aerial vehicles cope with temperature, light, and distance, which could help users to monitor the 
environmental conditions of the field. In atmospheric exploration, drones could be utilized for 
observing and determining planetary boundary layers (Wang et al., 2021). In the agriculture domain, 
the photogrammetric techniques mounted on drones could enhance the efficiency of generating 
position information for precise applications (Candiago et al., 2015). When developing detective 
techniques, a low-cost unmanned aerial vehicle platform and a standard flight routine were utilized 
for simulated scenarios such as the contour mapping of nuclear radiation (Han et al., 2012). Hence,

a module on drone education has recently been designed for learners to study assembly, flight 
simulation, and semi-autonomous piloting (Abichandani et al., 2022). Besides, Abichandani et al. 
(2022) also proposed multiple assessments for drone-related knowledge to confirm the validity of a 
drone learning module. The learning arrangement, including content, practice, and evaluation, has 
to be considered to formulate a drone-based curriculum.
Drone learning is a science, technology, engineering, art, and mathematics (STEAM)-related 
context in which learners must identify the key points of the mission, edit the programs with the team 
members, and collectively try to complete the task the instructors assign. A collaborative learning 
approach has been utilized for STEAM activities such as robotics learning (Huang et al., 2017), 
whereby group learners share common goals and solve problems using programming and cognitive 
strategies. Similarly, learners in drone tasks usually have to test their programs and adjust their 
common grounds considering the situation of each flight. Collaborative processes and problem-solving 
practices are the primary learning artifacts, integrating academic knowledge and skills into hands-on 
STEAM learning (Khamhaengpol et al., 2022).
Artificial Intelligence-Based Predictive Learning Analytics in Learning Contexts
Although hands-on STEAM activities could facilitate collaborative inquiry in person, the trend 
of asynchronous problem-solving environments has also been considered due to the influence of 
the pandemic. While learners acquire more experiences from asynchronous platforms, they also 
increasingly develop motivated strategies, such as self-determination to overcome academic challenges 
(Reyes et al., 2024). Learning resources from recorded videos and multimedia materials also help 
learners to control their pace and schedule. Hence, automatic adaptive learning systems have been 
implemented for academic learning, such as in mathematics (Li et al., 2022), and for predicting at-risk 
learners (Dawson et al., 2017). Hence, artificial intelligence (AI)-based predictive analytics can be 
broadly utilized to analyze learning engagement and propose suggestions for instructors or learners 
(Lim et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2022). In addition, models of AI-based predictive systems have been 
updated constantly according to different learning objectives and theories. The learning patterns and 
interactional mechanisms of each drone task are still changing to improve algorithms, especially for 
collaborative problem-solving (CPS), such as drone education.
CPS Assessment Toward the Human-Agent Approach 
of AI-Based Predictive Analytics
Learning systems for CPS have focused on the issues of assessment. One of the assessment 
issues is the complicated definition of CPS. Programme for International Student Assessment 
(PISA) 2015 defined a 3 x 4 matrix of CPS that included collaborative skills and problem-solving 
skills (Organization of Economic Cooperation and Development, 2017), thus addressing the need for 
collaborative capacities in the digital environment. The concept framework of CPS in PISA 2015 has 
also been adopted to develop an adaptive learning system that considers the diverse characteristics 
of mission tasks and trans-disciplinary learning situations (Kuo et al., 2020). Another framework 
of CPS is the core competencies of the Taiwan 108 curriculum guidelines (Ministry of Education, 
2014). For a lifelong learner, CPS also has to consider the skills of spontaneity, communication, and 
social participation in a technology-enhanced environment. In drone education, challenging tasks 
make learners use executive self-regulatory strategies in teamwork contexts based on their lifelong 
competencies.
Since different CPS frameworks have established the fundamentals of assessment, especially 
in STEAM education, the main approaches utilized in the CPS assessment system have been 
human-human (H-H) and human-agent (H-A) (Kuo et al., 2020). The H-H approach for predictive 
learning analytics help understand human interaction processes and outcomes which cope with real 
CPS learning situations through observation. At the same time, the H-A approach for predictive 
learning analytics could construct a standardized assessment mechanism for learners to achieve

consistent CPS performance within various contexts via computer agents (Harel, 1979). To improve 
the validity and reliability of CPS assessment, the H-A approach applied in the CPS scale has also 
been validated in Taiwan to predict adolescent learners’ performance (Kuo et al., 2020).
Although drone techniques have been broadly applied in various domains, curriculum modules 
and learning analytics of drone education have been increasingly concerned with the STEAM learning 
trend. Before establishing H-A models of drone learning for Al-based prediction, the attributes of 
H-H problem-solving in drone-based tasks should be explored first in this study. The pilot data of 
CPS in drone learning would be enhanced for the future development of predictive analytics in 
STEAM education.
Three research questions are proposed in this study to investigate possible attributes in drone 
education:
•	
Which factors could appropriately predict drone programming attainment?
•	
Which factors regarding motivated strategies and computational thinking could be employed 
best for learners during drone learning?
•	
How are explicit behaviors in drone tasks related to the attainment of drone learning?
To better understand the characteristics of drone learning, case studies using learners of 11–13 
years of age were carried out to explore the potential relevance of motivated strategies, computational 
thinking, and drone learning performance.
LITERATURE REVIEW
Important Factors for CPS
CPS involves both social and cognitive skills. Social skills include participation and 
perspective-taking for the collaborative regulatory processes of group work. Cognitive skills are applied 
to the problem-solving processes of planning, executing, and monitoring. The international large-scale 
assessment, PISA 2015, therefore defined CPS as collaborative and problem-solving skills using a 
3 x 4 matrix (Organization of Economic Cooperation and Development, 2017). The collaborative 
skills defined by PISA 2015 were classified into three types: (a) establishing and maintaining shared 
understanding, (b) taking appropriate actions to solve the problem, and (c) establishing and maintaining 
team organization. The problem-solving skills defined by PISA 2015 were classified into four types: 
(a) exploring and understanding, (b) representing and formulating, (c) planning and executing, and 
(d) monitoring and reflecting. Those learning in teams would utilize self-regulating strategies in 
their communities of practice. Generally speaking, the two-axis definition of CPS means that it can 
be applied in either individual or group learning contexts.
Aside from the two-axis definition of CPS, the competency-based learning perspective has 
recognized CPS as the main content in the educational systems of the Taiwan 108 curriculum guideline. 
To cultivate lifelong learners for the changing future, three types of core competencies from the 
108 curriculum guidelines were defined as spontaneity, communication and interaction, and social 
participation (Ministry of Education, 2014). Learners are encouraged to execute self-directed learning 
in academic domains via the support of technological inventories and self-regulating strategies. 
Furthermore, learners studying domain knowledge and skills must apply their CPS competencies to 
real-life scenarios with their group members using different kinds of representations. Therefore, nine 
items from three core competencies were also verified to reflect learners’ CPS performance, such as 
logical thinking and problem-solving (the competency of spontaneity), interpersonal relationships 
and teamwork (the competency of social participation), and more.
Considering the CPS framework, learners who are well prepared for self-regulation 
might achieve better CPS learning accomplishments. Both individual and

group-level CPS learning practice should also consider the development of cognitive and 
meta-cognitive strategies. For example, the learner could monitor the progress of the team’s work and 
propose solutions for the problem using resource management strategies (Pintrich et al., 1991). Hence, 
the self-regulated learning theory has been regarded as the predictive model of learning analytics 
(Glick et al., 2024), considering the mechanism of problem-solving processes. Self-regulated learning 
factors could also be developed as CPS indicators for an H-H approach assessment.
The Mechanism of Self-Regulated Learning in STEAM Contexts
Learners in a STEAM environment encounter a diversity of stimulation and feedback. In robotics 
learning activities, robotics learners manipulate building and coding blocks to complete their designs 
with group members (Lin et al., 2010). Learners must frequently discuss their ideas and negotiate 
alternatives if the solution does not work during the design process. Hence, the primary interactions in 
STEAM learning occur between actors and materials and learners’ beliefs and outcome expectations. 
Participants (group members and instructors) might further activate advanced construction. STEAM 
learning and technology-enhanced environments could also be considered CPS situations due to the 
collective participation and argument formation involved.
Although self-processes and personal values might influence learners’ strategies, the progress 
of achievement and integration with peripheral resources could transform learners’ beliefs. In 
problem-solving tasks, learners try to regulate their outputs based on efficacy and causal outcomes. 
They can then improve domain competencies increasingly after trial and error. The reciprocal processes 
of STEAM learning also follow the “triadic model” (Figure 1) of self-regulated learning proposed by 
Bandura (1986, p. 454). Personal characteristics, behavioral tendencies, and environmental feedback 
could influence learning achievement, artifact design, explicit action, and task confidence.

Programming Learning Using AI-Based Predictive Analytics
AI-based predictive platforms have been utilized for adaptive learning in different educational 
contexts since the development of computerized adaptive testing (Wu et al., 2012). By implementing 
predictive AI systems, instructors can simultaneously access macroscopic and microscopic learning 
analytics to manage classroom activities and scaffolds. Moreover, learners can understand their learning 
progress and difficulties in seeking alternative resources or getting other materials recommended by 
the predictive systems.
In the domain of programming education, the widespread implementation of AI-based learning 
platforms for K12 and high school was established by the non-profit organization, Code.org. The 
learning structure of the Code.org platform has been developed using four hierarchies from age 
4 to age 18. As preschool and elementary learners, students can drag coding blocks to complete 
their program sequences and solve problems demonstrated by animations and games. Middle- and

high-school learners can acquire foundational concepts, such as application design, algorithms, or 
cybersecurity, by completing computer science principles courses on Code.org. Furthermore, to 
improve the efficiency of programming learning, scaffolding strategies are designed to be applied 
in computer science principles activities by providing subgoal-oriented instructions and pre-written 
comments for students and teachers (Margulieux et al., 2020). For novices in programming education, 
redesigned strategies might help both students and teachers without prior knowledge to realize 
the concept better. Therefore, learning analytics logged from AI-based systems could provide 
problematized situations for learners and accompany appropriate scaffolds to make learning more 
practical for better programming solutions.
In introductory programming science courses at university, programming learning systems have 
also been utilized to help learners be aware of errors and make corrections to each program practice. 
Predictive AI has been constructed to develop a machine learning programming system, which 
refers to the performance and over 10 programming behaviors (Pepeira et al., 2021). Learners in an 
introductory programming course demonstrate programming behaviors such as the amount of code 
change, correctness, syntax error, or copy-paste. As a result, the system “CodeBench” was trained using 
a deep learning model and genetic algorithm model to provide effective prediction and explanation 
of programming learning (Pepeira et al., 2021). However, block-based programming learning, such 
as Code.org and Scratch, can help students focus on the coding experience more without possible 
syntax errors. Ben-Yaacov and Hershkovitz (2023) found that learners in Blockly might encounter 
some essential computational thinking misconceptions, such as repetition and conditional errors. 
Moreover, decomposition and premature and redundant errors were common for novice programmers 
in problem-solving practice.
Therefore, while developing predictive AI to recognize the relationship between behaviors and 
performance in block-based programming for adolescents, research on explainable AI systems in 
programming learning is still important. Meanwhile, instructors could also utilize generative AI 
tools to facilitate more suggestions to help learners to manage their progress or produce some tests 
in either text or block programs.
RESEARCH METHOD
Context
Task-based designs of drone activities were implemented in two elementary schools and two junior 
high schools, referred to as A, B, C, and D Schools, in Taiwan Taoyuan city for 11- to 15-year-old 
adolescents. A School was located in the urban center, and most students had programming experiences 
in formal education, such as Scratch, robotics, or Micro: Bit. The researchers carried out three days 
of summer camp, recruiting nine students to participate in the drone learning experiment. B School 
was located in a rural area, and most students had less experience in programming and technological 
practice. The researchers played the role of teachers in developing the three lessons of drone learning 
activities in the weekly class schedule. 13 students in B School were recruited to engage in the drone 
learning experiment for 120 minutes. C and D Schools were both located in the rural area near the 
Taoyuan coastline. The researchers carried out either classes for educational summer camps, and 34 
junior high students from the two schools engaged in the drone learning courses.

tasks of the material included five basic units of flight command for the learners to try the

The advanced tasks of the material included two units of programming concepts, such as Loop 
and Operation, for the learners to try the functional bricks, input the parameters, and create new

Mix-Method Design
This study utilized a fully integrated mixed design, proposed by Teddlie and Tashakkori (2009), 
to explore and describe the common attributes of the learning performance in drone activities. The 
quantitative data were collected from questionnaires and an attainment test after 12 hours of drone 
learning. The qualitative data was collected from researchers’ observational notes and the participatory 
documents in the drone tasks. Different facets of the learning data, processes, and outcomes were 
employed to depict the reciprocal relationship among the predictors in drone programming tasks such 
as cognition, skills, and problem-solving.
Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire in Drone
The questionnaire was adapted from the structure of motivated strategies for learning 
questionnaires (MSLQ) (Pintrich et al., 1991). The statement of each item was also edited based on 
the learning context of Drone and Blockly. In addition, the factors selected from MSLQ were used to 
confirm learning performance, considering the problem-solving processes of robotics programming 
(Huang et al., 2017). The 30 items were compiled to identify the learners’ motivation and strategies 
in the tasks of drone learning, reflected in meta-cognition, effort regulation, critical thinking, and 
self-efficacy.
Computational Thinking Checklist in Drone Learning
The checklist was adapted from the computational thinking scale structure for junior high school 
(Korkmaz & Bai, 2019). The researchers retained the appropriate items that enabled Taiwanese 
students to understand easily. They compiled 16 items to identify the learners’ computational thinking 
in drone learning, reflecting creativity, algorithmic thinking, cooperativity, and problem-solving.
Attainment Test of Drone Concepts and the Blockly Program
After students learned about drone tasks, this study developed the attainment test as a summative 
assessment. Thirty-three items of multiple choice were designed according to the function list on 
the platform “Blockly for Robolink.” Six topics were included: basic concepts of drones (from items 
1 to 5), flight commands of drones (from items 6 to 10 and items 26 to 27) (Figure 3), conditional 
expressions (from item 11 to 15), loop function (from item 16 to 20), variables and operations (from 
item 21 to 25), and the degree adjustment of drones in advanced flight commands (from item 28 to 
33). Three experts in drone education also verified the content of items in the attainment test.

Data Triangulation and Argumentation Forming
The quantitative and qualitative data in this study should be discussed from multiple angles to 
explain the CPS of drone learning activities. The data triangulation technique has been utilized to 
examine the validity of drone learning performance (Denzin, 1978). Data triangulation in this study 
includes the survey data from questionnaires, attainment tests, and instructor observational records.

RESULTS
The Examination of Prediction for Drone Program Attainment
The attainment test of drone concepts and the Blockly program were utilized to evaluate learning 
performance before and after the drone tasks in this study. Seventy-three learners from four schools 
engaged in drone learning activities and reported different performances in pre- and post-attainment 
tests (mean [M] = 22.89, SD = 9.75; and M = 48.63, SD = 16.92, respectively). Pre-and post-test 
performance demonstrated significant differences using the paired-samples t-test (t = 24.051***). 
The development of drone instructional material and the task missions is proven to have a positive 
influence on drone learning.
The learning factors of gender (r = .295*), problem-solving (r = .231*), and creativity (r = 
-.241*) significantly correlate with the program attainment. The results of the drone learning indicate 
that personal factors (gender, creativity) and behavioral factors (problem-solving) all have linear 
relationships with environmental factors (attainment). The prediction of the programming attainment is 
about 20% from the three factors via multiple regression analysis (see Table 2). Although the explained 
variance was modest, problem-solving (β = .313, p<.01) and creativity (β = -.276, p<.05) emerged 
as significant predictors of programming attainment. In addition, the gender of drone learners did 
not significantly predict problem-solving and creativity in drone programming, even though gender 
correlated with the programming attainment scores.

The Factors of Motivated Strategies Employed in Drone Learning
The learners self-reported using MSLQ after completing all the learning tasks from the material. 
The results demonstrated stable mean scores and standard deviation in self-efficacy (M = 3.88, 
SD = 0.54). However, the results demonstrated higher mean scores but stable standard deviation 
in meta-cognition (M = 4.18, SD = 0.33), effort regulation (M = 4.28, SD = 0.32), and critical 
thinking (M = 4.06, SD = 0.39; Table 3). The learners engaging in drone learning might have positive 
metacognitive and self-regulatory and critical thinking strategies according to the descriptive results 
of the motivated strategies for learning questionnaires. Furthermore, the repeat measure analysis of 
variance (ANOVA) was employed to investigate learners’ motivated strategies for drone learning. 
The result indicated a significant effect of the condition using Greenhouse-Geisser estimates of 
sphericity (ε = 0.81, F[2.42, 70.58] = 27.97, p < 0.001). The post hoc testing showed that the scores 
of the factor, self-efficacy (M = 3.88, SD = 0.54), were significantly lower than the other dimensions, 
meta-cognition, effort regulation, and critical thinking. The result indicates that although learners 
considered that self-regulated strategies could be applied to drone tasks, most perceived less confidence 
in drone problem-solving.

The Factors of Computational Thinking Employed in Drone Learning
The learners self-reported from the inventory and computational thinking checklist after 
completing all the learning tasks from the material. The results demonstrated stable mean scores 
and standard deviation in algorithmic thinking (M = 3.62, SD = 0.36) and problem-solving (M = 
3.71, SD = 0.48), while demonstrating higher mean scores but stable standard deviation in creativity 
(M = 3.99, SD = 0.37) and cooperativity (M = 4.3, SD = 0.38; Table 4). The learners engaging 
in drone learning might have positive and creative experiences and collaboration with each group 
member according to the descriptive results of the computational thinking checklist. Furthermore, 
the repeat measure ANOVA was employed to investigate learners’ computational thinking toward 
drone learning. The result indicated a significant main effect of condition using Greenhouse-Geisser 
estimates of sphericity (ε = 0.76, F[2.29, 70.71] = 50.09, p< 0.001). The post hoc testing showed that 
the scores of the cooperativity factor (M = 4.3, SD = 0.38) were significantly higher than the other 
factors: algorithmic thinking, creativity, and problem-solving. Although algorithmic thinking and 
problem-solving did not demonstrate a significant difference after the post hoc, the Pearson correlation 
showed a significantly moderate correlation between the two factors (r = .542**). The results indicated 
that most learners considered cooperativity the most crucial factor in drone tasks, and they utilized 
algorithmic and problem-solving simultaneously in drone learning. The learners in drone tasks not 
only formulated the creative solution using reflective journals and programs collectively (Wang et 
al., 2024), but they had to convert situational problems into mathematical formulas or Blockly visual 
representations to solve the missions.
The concepts of computational thinking in the schooling context included the collaborative 
process in problem-solving activities based on the factor structure of the high school version of the 
inventory (Korkmaz & Bai, 2019). The learners could provide the learning scaffolds through either 
dialogue or questioning to elaborate on the coding of drones. The task-based approach of drone 
activities enabled the learners to formulate the hypothesis and collectively generate the solutions 
using algorithmic thinking in CPS. The instructors in drone tasks played the role of facilitators and 
designers in managing the learning resources for the learners.

The Explicit Behaviors of Drone Learning in Relation 
to the Attainment of Drone Learning
Drone learning attainment is reflected in the programming tests and explicit learning behaviors, 
such as mission execution. While considering the drone performance of the group engagement, the 
learner might develop individual perspectives and strategies to build consensus with other members. 
Drone learning includes both social and cognitive skills when finding appropriate solutions with one 
another in the CPS context.
Although the cause of flight failure is hard to identify, the points from the problems enabled 
each participant to generate a “common realization” to stimulate successive action and motivate 
problem-solving. Inquiry emerged on the level of group learning for the 12-13-year-old students in A 
School; the members collaborated to correct the drone programs for the flight path. The instructor’s 
observation from the classroom journal in July 2023 was as follows:
In the Day 3 activities in A school, the learning group consisted of four 12-year-old girls who 
faced the challenge that the drone could not fly over the circle hoop four meters away. In the 
beginning, the group members tried to set the takeoff altitude to fly using Blockly codes but still 
hit the hoop because of the unstable height of the drone. One of the girls, encoded as A(Scho
ol)-7(Grade)-G(girl)-1(number), suggested the two-stepped solution of flight. First, the group 
used the default height of the drone to fly and land in front of the hoop. Then, the group revised 
the height of the drone using Blockly codes to ensure the flight was calibrated more accurately. 
Finally, they overcame the flight challenge successfully.
While in the drone tasks, learners could be trained in collaborative learning and computational 
thinking competencies to improve their problem-solving and metacognition. The task-based drone 
activities developed from the material positively enhanced the learning growth of the competencies. 
Moreover, as learners found the potential solution to the problem, other members collaboratively 
arranged to execute the following action. The problem-solving process in drone tasks was almost 
embedded in the cooperative practice, as narrated in the classroom journal of C School from July 2024:
During the circle hoop traversal task, one group of students identified flight time as a critical 
variable. An eighth-grade student in the group suggested measuring the distance a drone could 
travel in one second at power levels of 100%, 75%, 50%, and 25%. This student proactively used 
a ruler and tape to mark the distances on the ground and enlisted the help of an eighth-grade 
female classmate to adjust the power parameters in the program and conduct repeated tests. After 
group discussions, a ninth-grade female student observed that lower power levels resulted in 
slower but more stable flight, increasing the likelihood of successfully passing through the circle 
hoop. Consequently, the group adjusted the program to reduce the drone's power percentage and 
continued flight tests, ultimately improving their success rate in completing the task.
The construct of cooperativity demonstrated a high mean value with a stable standard deviation, 
suggesting that most learners perceived improved effectiveness from the membership in collaborative 
tasks. This is narrated in the classroom journal of D School from July 2024:

DISCUSSION
This study has preliminarily discussed the learning attributes of drone education in the H-H 
assessment approach. Task-based drone learning activities facilitate CPS opportunities for learners 
to activate motivated strategies and computational thinking in their learning processes, such as 
metacognition, effort regulation, problem-solving, and cooperativity. This study also tried to examine 
the prediction of drone learning attainment with other correlated variables using stepwise regression 
and participant observation. Corresponding to the person-behavior-environment triadic model 
proposed by Bandura (1977), personal characteristics such as creativity and explicit behaviors such 
as problem-solving strongly help to predict environmental factors, such as drone programming and 
collaborative learning. Personal and behavioral attributes play important roles in drone learning, 
so effort regulation and problem-solving skills performed the most based on the result of ANOVA. 
The drone learners investigated in this study had less self-efficacy. However, they had better effort 
regulation and metacognition, showing that drone learners still lack confidence in STEAM learning 
even though they could perform well. In the future, instructors could implement decomposition 
instruction for novice learners to improve their computational thinking skills and even promote their 
self-efficacy for the tasks (Ben-Yaacov & Hershkovitz, 2023).
Furthermore, group learning in drone tasks could improve knowledge construction since students 
can share common realizations and conceive possible solutions. According to the observation of the 
learners’ behaviors, when the group members all have a common goal and are willing to immerse 
themselves in problem-solving, alternative strategies could emerge through collective inquiry.
To construct an H-A system for CPS in drone education, it is worth referring to the past research 
findings in STEAM learning for the learning variables. Instructors should also consider creating an 
environment with distributed expertise to provide real-time feedback and adequate scaffolds (Cassidy 
et al., 2020), especially for self-efficacy and self-regulation. With sufficient collaborative support from 
group members or systematic encouragement, learners could develop their learning strategies and 
self-concepts to maintain their learning and continued participation. Implementing learning scaffolds 
to enhance individual and collective inquiry is also essential for designers and educators to consider 
to improve learning efficiency (Lin & Liu, 2012). However, no motivated factors or strategies had 
significant relationships with the drone programming attainment test in this study. Other latent variables 
in the drone tasks should be considered to evaluate the effect on drone learning achievement, such 
as digital literacy or gender differences. Moreover, the precise measurement of drone performance, 
including cognitive and participatory practice, still needs to be explored. The validity of the inventories 
to assess drone concepts and CPS could still be constructed and confirmed in the future.
Following the fast growth of generative AI in 2023, learners can have study companions to provide 
guiding advice for effectively extracting learning ideas, such as academic writing activities in each 
domain. An H-A system could play the role of assessment and be a companion and collaborator for 
learners to achieve autonomous learning and have meaningful interactions.
