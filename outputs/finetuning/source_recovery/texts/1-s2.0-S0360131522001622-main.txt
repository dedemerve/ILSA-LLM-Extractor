Students’ perceptions of instruction quality have long been considered a core element of the
effectiveness of education. In particular, perceived high-quality instruction in classrooms might
motivate students to gain better digital reading performance, which helps them become wellprepared in the era of information and communication technology (ICT). Thus, this study
investigated the correlation between students’ perceptions of instruction quality features and
their digital reading performance from the aspects of perceived instructional approaches, classroom management, and supportive climate. Data on 223,807 15-year-old students in 29 Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries were extracted from the
latest Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2018 database and the study
adopted three-level hierarchical linear modeling (HLM). The findings demonstrated the positive
influence of students’ perceived adaptation of instruction, stimulation of reading engagement,
disciplinary climate, teacher interest, and teacher support. However, students’ perceived teacherdirected instruction, reading skills exercises, teaching of digital skills, language instruction time,
and teacher feedback were unexpectedly negatively correlated with their digital reading performance. Finally, the study concludes by discussing its implications for improving reading instruction quality.

1. Introduction
Students’ perceptions of instruction quality act as a major driver for enhancing their academic performance. Previous studies found
that students’ perceived classroom learning features, such as instructional approaches, disciplinary climate, and teacher-student relationships, significantly influenced students’ learning motivation (e.g., Hampden-Thompson & Bennett, 2013; Lazarides & Buchholz,
2019; Ma, Luo, & Xiao, 2021), self-efficacy (e.g., Ma et al., 2021; Scherer, Nilsen, & Jansen, 2016), and academic performance in
science (e.g., Li, Liu, Yang, & Tripp, 2022), mathematics (e.g., Tourón, Navarro-Asencio, Lizasoain, López-González, & García-San
Pedro, 2019), and print-based reading assessments (e.g., Meng, Muñoz, Hess, & Liu, 2017). Despite the well-recognized importance of
instruction quality, few studies (Ma et al., 2021; Qian & Lau, 2022) have paid attention to the role of students’ perceptions of the

However, with the continuous advancement and increasing popularization of information and communication technology (ICT),
digital competence is of great command for younger generation to actively participate in modern society (e.g., Chen, Lin, & Chen,
2021; Tondeur et al., 2017; Yang, Kuo, Ji, & McTigue, 2018; Yu, Zhou, Yang, & Hu, 2022), especially in the post-coronavirus pandemic
era when the online learning is becoming increasingly popular (e.g., Pifarré & Argelagós, 2020; Scherer, Howard, Tondeur, & Siddiq,
2021; Yu & Hu, 2022). Against this background, efforts need to be made to investigate the potential influence of the features of
student-perceived instruction quality on the performance in digital reading.
Having adopted computer-based assessment since 2009, the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), a large-scale
international survey initiated by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), might provide some insights.
Its latest survey, PISA 2018, not only used computer-based tests to assess students’ performance in digital reading, but also collected
data concerning students’ perceptions of instruction quality features related to reading in test language lessons, which could be used to
fill the current research gap. Therefore, based on PISA 2018 data, the present study aims to conduct a cross-national investigation of
the correlation between students’ perceptions of instruction quality features and their digital reading performance and to therefore
provide some practical implications for reading instruction. Specifically, students’ perceived instruction quality was conceptualized
across three categories: instructional approaches, classroom management, and supportive climate. The features that comprise these
three domains, reported by students in the PISA 2018 survey, were thus examined by constructing three-level hierarchical linear
models (HLMs) involving 223,807 secondary school students from 29 OECD countries that share a similar economic background.
2. Literature review
2.1. Conceptualization of instruction quality
Instruction quality has been broadly recognized as a core element of education effectiveness (e.g., Caro, Lenkeit, & Kyriakides,
2016; Chen, Zhang, & Hu, 2021; Dewulf, Van Braak, & Van Houtte, 2022; Hattie, 2009; Scherer et al., 2016). Although there is broad
agreement that instruction quality is a construct of multiple dimensions, there is no consensus concerning its specific domains. Among
the various theoretical frameworks for instruction quality, the framework suggested by Klieme, Pauli, and Reusser (2009) affirms other
studies (e.g., Van de Grift, 2007; Pianta & Hamre, 2009) and has been popularly cited in relevant research (e.g., Caro et al., 2016;
Lazarides & Buchholz, 2019; Lipowsky et al., 2009; Scherer et al., 2016; Tan & Dimmock, 2022). Taking process-product and
constructivist paradigms as well as self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000) into account, this framework contains three basic
dimensions, i.e., cognitive activation, classroom organization, and a supportive climate. Specifically, activating students’ cognition is
characterized by challenging tasks, prior knowledge activation, and content-related discursive practices (e.g., Lipowsky et al., 2009).
Classroom organization contains rule clarity, effective behavior management, and an established classroom structure (e.g., Klieme
et al., 2009). Finally, a supportive climate entails teacher feedback, individual learner support (e.g., Cornelius-White, 2007), and other
teacher-student interaction features. However, this classic framework has some limitations. Since the first dimension, “cognitive
activation”, is mainly related to instruction practices that encourage students’ engagement in deeper thinking, its coverage is limited to
cover various instruction practices, such as teacher-directed instruction, which focus more on the provision of information and
practices of basic skills (e.g., Lerkkanen et al., 2016; Pakarinen & Kikas, 2019). It may therefore be more useful for research on instruction quality to broaden the scope of the Klieme et al.’s (2009) first dimension by revising “cognitive activation” to “instructional
approach”.
2.2. Influence of students’ perceptions of instruction quality on academic performance
In large-scale national or international assessments, individual students’ perceptions of classroom teaching and learning features
are often considered a measurement tool for instruction quality (e.g., Fauth, Decristan, Rieser, KLieme, & Büttner, 2014; Scherer et al.,
2016). Previous studies have established that students’ perceived instruction quality significantly influences their academic performance, but the findings are mixed concerning the relationship between specific features of instruction quality that students report and
their performance in different subjects. For example, research has shown that students’ perceived teacher-directed instruction,
adaptation of instruction, and disciplinary climate tend to positively influence students’ science achievement (e.g., Li et al., 2022).
Nevertheless, the relationship between inquiry-based instruction reported by students and their academic performance was sometimes
negative, and the influence of students’ perceived feedback was inconsistent (e.g., Lau & Lam, 2017). Regarding the features of math
instruction, students’ perceptions of cognitive activation (e.g., Caro et al., 2016) and disciplinary climate (e.g., Scherer et al., 2016)
tend to have a positive influence on academic performance. With regard to students’ perceived quality of reading instruction, Meng
et al. (2017) studied the influence of different teaching factors reported by students and argued that students’ perceived classroom
learning environment and teaching strategies, such as work feedback and discussion, were positively related to the reading performance of both Chinese and American students. Furthermore, based on a Chinese sample from PISA 2018, some scholars have explored
the relationship between students’ perceived instruction quality features and their performance in digital reading (Ma et al., 2021;
Qian & Lau, 2022), which will be further discussed in the next section.
Taken together, previous studies have investigated the discrete influence of certain aspects of students’ perceptions of instruction
quality, but empirical studies that explore the correlation between various features of instruction quality perceived by students and
their digital reading performance are scarce. Furthermore, most of the studies mentioned above are limited to one or a few countries,
and more large-scale, cross-national studies are needed to demonstrate the generalizability of their results.

2.3. Digital reading and potential factors influencing students’ performance
Digital reading refers to reading and comprehending digital text that is usually arranged in a nonlinear format (e.g., Hahnel,
Goldhammer, Naumann, & Kröhne, 2016; Rasmusson & Åberg-Bengtsson, 2015; Reiber-Kuijpers, Kral, & Meijer, 2021). On the one
hand, it has some similarities to traditional print reading given that some cognitive skills, including word decoding, sentence integration, and information evaluating, are required in both types of reading (e.g., Lim & Jung, 2019). On the other hand, different from
print reading, the nonlinear nature of digital texts requires more cognitive and navigation efforts by readers to sequence text contents
to fit their reading purposes (e.g., DeStefano & LeFevre, 2007; Naumann & Goldhammer, 2017). Some models have been developed for
this process. For example, the Comprehension-based Linked model of Deliberate Search (CoLiDeS-Model; Kitajima, Blackmon, &
Polson, 2000) proposes four major processes of online navigation: parsing, focusing on, comprehending, and selecting website
information.
Recognizing the increasing importance of digital reading, scholars around the world have conducted abundant research to
investigate the potential factors influencing students’ digital reading performance, with a focus on students’ linear reading skills and
digital experience in particular. A body of studies have confirmed that good linear readers tended to be good digital readers (e.g.,
Hahnel et al., 2016; Lim & Jung, 2019). With regard to students’ own digital experience, it was found that information-oriented online
reading engagement (e.g., Gil-Flores, Torres-Gordillo, & Perera-Rodríguez, 2012; Lee & Wu, 2013), positive ICT attitude (e.g., Gil-Flores et al., 2012; Hu & Yu, 2021), and ICT skills (e.g., Hahnel et al., 2016) might improve students’ performance in digital reading,
whereas the findings on the influence of online social entertainment activities were inconsistent (e.g., Lee & Wu, 2013; Lim & Jung,
2019; Xiao & Hu, 2019). Some scholars have also argued that moderate use of video games is associated with a performance advantage,
but excessive gaming might hinder digital reading (e.g., Borgonovi, 2016).
Despite the heated discussion on the role of students’ own reading skills and digital experience, few studies have focused on the
influence of instruction quality features on secondary school students’ digital reading performance (Ma et al., 2021; Qian & Lau, 2022).
Ma et al. (2021) only focused on the aspect of supportive climate and found that students’ reading enjoyment and self-concepts
mediated the positive relationship between students’ perceived teacher support and their digital reading performance. Qian and
Lau (2022) examined the role of different reading instruction features, including teacher-directed instruction, adaptation of instruction, and stimulation of reading engagement, on students’ digital reading performance, but their study focused only on a Chinese
sample. Further investigation is needed to determine whether the influences of these reading instruction features are consistent in
other countries. In addition, more research attention is needed regarding the influence of teaching and training practices of reading
and digital skills to deal with digital texts.

2.4. Present study
To address the above research gaps, this study investigates the correlation between students’ perceptions of instruction quality
features in different dimensions and their digital reading performance in 29 OECD countries to elaborate a more comprehensive and
generalizable view of these features’ influence. Based on the theoretical framework of instruction quality features proposed by Klieme
et al. (2009) and the relevant variables available in the PISA 2018, the study tries to specify students’ perceived instruction quality
features related to reading in the language classroom, which are depicted in Fig. 1. As mentioned in the preceding section, the original
domain of “cognitive activation” is replaced by students’ perceived “instructional approaches” to incorporate more features that relate
to instruction quality.
The present study is therefore guided by the following research questions:
1. In the PISA 2018 assessment, to what extent do students’ perceptions of instructional approaches correlate with their digital
reading performance?
2. In the PISA 2018 assessment, to what extent do students’ perceptions of classroom management strategies correlate with their
digital reading performance?
3. In the PISA 2018 assessment, to what extent do students’ perceptions of supportive features correlate with their digital reading
performance?
3. Methodology
3.1. Data
Most data in the current study were obtained from the latest cycle of PISA assessment, i.e., PISA 2018, (URL: https://www.oecd.
org/pisa/data/2018database/). Launched by the OECD every three years, PISA is a large-scale international survey measuring secondary school students’ performance in reading, mathematics and science. The latest survey, PISA 2018, took reading as the major
subject, and 70 countries or economies participated in the advanced computer-based assessment of reading. Concerning the data at the
country level, the logarithm of gross domestic product (GDP) per capita and total expenditures on secondary education as a percentage
of GDP were retrieved from the database of World Development Indicators from the World Bank (URL: https://databank.worldbank.
org/source/world-development-indicators) and the database of Educational Finance Indicators from the OECD (URL: https://stats.
oecd.org/Index.aspx?DataSetCode=EAG_FIN_RATIO), respectively. In this study, all OECD countries that participated in the PISA
2018 computer-based reading assessment and whose data were available for the required variables at the student, school, and country
levels were selected, given that their similar economic status would not cause research bias in other aspects (e.g., Chen & Hu, 2020).
The resulting data included 29 OECD countries1 and 223,807 students from 8692 schools (see Table 1).
3.2. Variables
Students’ performance on the PISA 2018 computer-based assessment of reading, which was represented by 10 plausible values
(PVs), was taken as the dependent variable. In the PISA 2018, students’ digital reading performance refers to the ability to understand,
use, evaluate, reflect on and engage with digital texts for achieving one’s goals, developing one’s knowledge and potential, and
participating in society (OECD, 2019). Instead of directly estimating students’ ability θ, PISA estimated a probability distribution for
students’ θ and PVs were randomly drawn from this distribution to express the students’ possible ability range (OECD, 2009).
Following the guidance of the PISA Data Analysis Manual (OECD, 2009), the present study considered the 10 PVs simultaneously as the
dependent variable. The follow-up study ran the analysis 10 times (one for each PV) and pooled the results via Rubin’s rule (Rubin,
1987) to obtain unbiased estimates, the analytical method of which have been popularly used in PISA-related studies (e.g., Hu, Gong,
Lai, & Leung, 2018; Lim & Jung, 2019).
The independent variables involved students’ perceptions of instruction quality features related to reading in a test language
classroom, which were categorized into 3 dimensions: perceived instructional approaches, perceived classroom management, and
perceived supportive climate. Specifically, perceived instructional approaches included the variables “adaptation of instruction”
(ADAPTIVITY), “teacher-directed instruction” (DIRINS), “teacher’s stimulation of reading engagement” (STIMREAD), “teaching
practices of digital skills” (DIGITALTEA) and “training exercises for students’ reading skills” (READPRA). Perceived classroom
management included the variables “disciplinary climate in test language lessons” (DISCLIMA) and “learning time in test language
lessons” (LMINS). Perceived supportive climate consisted of the variables “teacher feedback” (PERFEED), “teacher interest”
(TEACHINT), and “teacher support in test language classes” (TEACHSUP). Among these variables, ADAPTIVITY, DIRINS, STIMREAD,
DISCLIMA, PERFEED, TEACHINT, and TEACHSUP were derived from relevant measuring items based on four-point Likert scales and
were provided in the PISA 2018 database. For example, ADAPTIVITY was based on three items: lesson adaptation, teacher’s provision

3.3. Data analyses
3.3.1. Missing values, confirmatory factor analysis and data transformation
The present study first examined the missing data of the variables of interest, and the missing proportion of LMINS (23.60%) ranked
the highest. Considering that the values were absent because of a rotated booklet design and the random assignment of students to the
booklets (e.g., Scherer & Siddiq, 2015), the data were assumed to be randomly missing, and multiple imputation (MI) was thus applied
to handle them. MI is currently considered one of the most respectable methods of addressing missing data for both continuous and
categorical variables, where a set of plausible values are generated for each missing value by predicting their distributions (e.g.,
Schafer & Olsen, 1998; Tabachnick & Fidell, 2013). Moreover, MI has already been used in previous PISA-related studies (e.g., Nalipay
& King, 2020; Scherer & Siddiq, 2015; Tan & Liu, 2018). Predictive mean matching was therefore applied, and 10 complete data sets
were generated in IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0. In this way, an efficiency of 97% for 30% of the missing data was rendered (e.g., Schafer &
Olsen, 1998). Then, following previous research (e.g., Hu & Yu, 2021), in accordance with the same tags, the MI data sets were
combined with PVs of students’ digital reading performance (i.e., MI1 was coupled with PV1, MI2 was coupled with PV2, etc.). Each
data set was analyzed individually, and Rubin’s rules (Rubin, 1987) were adopted for the final results.
Then, with reference to previous research (e.g., Chen, Lin, & Chen, 2021; Lin, Zhang, & Zheng, 2017), confirmatory factor analysis
(CFA), the goal of which is to investigate the relationship between a set of measured and latent variables, was adopted to confirm the
construct validity of the variables DIGITALTEA and READPRA. When dealing with data from international surveys, previous studies
tended to perform CFA for both the entire sample and each individual territory (e.g., Chen, Lin, & Chen, 2021). Thus, in each imputed
dataset, the present study used the cfa function in the R package lavaan (Rosseel, 2012) and performed CFA for the sample of 29 OECD
countries as a whole and for the sample in each country separately. The scores of the model fit indices, i.e., CFI ranging from 0.924 to
0.989, TLI ranging from 0.912 to 0.987, RMSEA ranging from 0.032 to 0.058, SRMR ranging from 0.040 to 0.074, all meet the criteria

Hence, regression coefficients of these variables represent the expected changes in the standard deviation of the dependent variable (i.e., students’ digital reading performance in the present study),

3.3.2. Hierarchical linear models
Dealing with the nested student-school-country structure of the PISA data, HLMs were adopted using the statistical software Hierarchical Linear & Nonlinear Modeling 8 (HLM8; Raudenbush, Bryk, Cheong, & Congdon, 2019), and the software license ID was
13437. By including predictors at every level of analysis, HLMs incorporate both between-group variance and within-group variance
(e.g., Tabachnick & Fidell, 2013; Zhang & Lin, 2020) and have been broadly applied in PISA research (e.g., Areepattamannil & Khine,
2017; Hu & Yu, 2021; Skryabin, Zhang, Liu, & Zhang, 2015).
The model building in this study followed a step-up strategy recommended by Hox (2010). First, the simplest intercept-only model
(Model 1) was built to assess the need for multilevel analysis through the calculation of intraclass correlation (ICC). Second, all the
control variables were added to the model, and the backward method (e.g., Field, 2013) was adopted to filter out the nonsignificant
variables whose p value was the largest successively until all the variables in the model were statistically significant (Model 2). Next,
the independent variables at different levels were added to the model,3 and those that were statistically significant were retained to
build the final model (Model 3). Given that CFA was conducted for both the entire sample and the sample of each country, HLMs were
based on the data after two separate CFA methods. Additionally, considering the complexity of computation and the present study’s
focus on the nature rather than the heterogeneity of the influence of students’ perceptions of instruction quality features, with references to previous PISA related HLM research (e.g., Hu et al., 2018; Hu & Yu, 2021; Skryabin et al., 2015), random-intercept and
fixed-slope models were built. The construction of the mixed model is as follows:

To guarantee the reliability of the results, HLM assumptions (e.g., Hox, 2010) were checked for the final model. Specifically,
correlation matrices and the values of variance inflation factors (VIF) for independent and control variables at each level were

in linear regression analysis with multiple predictors, the absence of multicollinearity is an important requisite to ensure the trustworthy beta values and variance component. Additionally, if the correlation coefficients of the examined variables are all below 0.8
and the values of VIF are less than 10, multicollinearity might not cause for concern (e.g., Field, 2013; Tabachnick & Fidell, 2013).

homoscedasticity is ensured if the graph dose not funnel out; and the normality is assumed to be met if data points fall around the
diagonal of the Q-Q plot (e.g., Field, 2013; Hu & Yu, 2021).

5. Discussion
Students’ perceptions of teacher behaviors and what happens in classrooms have greatly affected modern education (e.g., Caro
et al., 2016). This study conducted a cross-national investigation of the correlation between students’ perceptions of instruction quality
features in test language lessons and their digital reading performance. The results demonstrated that the relevant features examined
above explain school-level variance in particular and that the mixed influences of these features indicated a need for further discussion;
however, because of the relatively small beta values, the findings are suggested be interpreted with caution in future studies.
5.1. Correlations between students’ perceptions of instructional approaches and their digital reading performance
In the present study, different reading instructional approaches reported by students were investigated, and they demonstrated
different correlations with students’ performance in digital reading.
To the best of our knowledge, training exercises for reading skills and teaching practices for digital skills were first examined based
on PISA 2018 data. Specifically, the former entails teachers’ requests for students to write summaries, analyze main characters, discuss
in small groups, etc., and the latter related to PISA-collected items, such as teachers’ teaching of using search engine, evaluating online
information, publishing information appropriately, etc. Different from the expectation of the importance of both print reading and
digital skills, these two instructional approaches, examined in the PISA 2018 context, were negatively related to students’ digital
reading performance. Among previous studies, some researchers also found unexpected negative relationships between students’
practices of reading skills (e.g., underlining important information) and their reading performance (e.g., Meng et al., 2017). Other
researchers even discovered that teacher’s intervention of reading strategies had no significant influence on students’ reading
comprehension performance (e.g., Muijselaar et al., 2018). Similarly, with regard to the teaching of digital skills, previous research
found that ICT instruction through role modeling did not exert ideal effect on the professional use of ICT among the cohort of preservice language teachers (e.g., Hsu & Lin, 2020). In the current study, taking the design of PISA 2018 student questionnaire into
account, two potential reasons were proposed for the unexpected negative correlation. One is that all the measuring items of these two
approaches are yes-or-no questions. To some extent, students’ responses to binary items can reflect the diversity of reading exercises or
digital skills taught in school but not the frequency or quality of specific instructional strategies. However, diversity does not mean
better given that different instructional strategies exert mixed influence (e.g., Donker, de Boer, Kostons, van Ewijk, & van der Werf,
2014). With regard to digital reading, some practices of comprehension skills, such as analyzing reading materials (e.g., Hashim &
Vongkulluksn, 2018) and teaching digital skills, such as reliability reasoning (e.g., Pilgrim, Vasinda, Bledsoe, & Martinez, 2019), might
help improve students’ performance, while other strategies might not have a similar positive influence. The other reason might be that
the measuring items focus on teachers’ requests for students to complete reading exercises or teaching of digital skills, while they are
not equivalent to students’ mastery of the relevant knowledge and skills. Efforts are needed for students to internalize the instruction
practices, and in this process, students’ characteristics, such as initial language and literacy skills, influence the effectiveness of
instructional strategies (Connor et al., 2011). Therefore, the present study attempts to explore the potential impact of reading exercises
and teaching practices of digital skills at school. The frequency and quality of these instructional approaches and the interaction with
students’ own characteristics need further exploration in the future.
With regard to the other three instructional approaches, i.e., perceived teacher-directed instruction, adaptation of instruction, and
teacher’s stimulation of reading engagement, the present study’s findings of their relations to students’ digital reading performance
resonated with the findings of Qian and Lau (2022) based on the Chinese sample. Specifically, students’ perceptions of adaptation of
instruction and reading engagement stimulation were found to positively correlate with their performance in digital reading. As a
student-centered approach, adaptation of instruction entails tailoring lesson structures to students’ needs and responses. Adapting
reading instruction according to students’ literacy levels, including decoding skills and vocabulary, enables students to construct new
knowledge using their current knowledge and helps them achieve better reading outcomes (e.g., Connor, Morrison, Fishman,
Schatschneider, & Underwood, 2013; Hu, 2014; Nurmi et al., 2013), which probably promotes their performance in digital reading.
Regarding perceived teachers’ stimulation of reading engagement, stimulating and interesting tasks, activities, and materials within
instruction are associated with a higher level of students’ learning interest and motivation (e.g., Pintrich, 2003). During reading instruction, students’ perception of stimulation may activate their cognition, encourage them to be more engaged in reading activities,
and improve the classroom environment (e.g., Lau & Ho, 2016). Thus, students’ linear reading literacy might be promoted, which lays
a foundation for the improvement of digital reading performance. In contrast to the previous two approaches, perceived
teacher-directed instruction showed a negative correlation with students’ performance in digital reading. Explanations for the negative
correlation might be threefold. First, the feature of teacher-directed instruction is represented by student-reported frequency in the
PISA 2018. Teachers might be more tempted to use this approach with students who have poorer reading performance, thus leading to
a negative correlation. Second, teacher-directed instruction is often characterized by information provision, structured group lessons,
and drill and practice, and its effects vary across different age groups (Lerkkanen et al., 2016). Previous studies suggested that
teacher-directed instruction brings benefits for students with poorer reading fluency when they learn certain basic forms of knowledge;
however, for those students with average or high initial reading skills, this approach may not reach the expected effects (e.g., Kikas,
Pakarinen, Soodla, Peets, & Lerkkanen, 2018; Lerkkanen et al., 2016). Since students in secondary school have already acquired
certain reading skills through their previous learning experience, it may not be as effective as expected for the cohort of secondary
school students. Third, the relationship between perceived teacher-directed instruction and students’ digital reading performance
might be curvilinear, which was found in other studies of the influence of teacher-directed math instruction (e.g., Caro et al., 2016) and
inquiry-based science teaching (e.g., Teig, Scherer, & Nilsen, 2018). Accordingly, the appropriate use of teacher-directed instruction

may positively correlate with students’ performance in digital reading, but the relationship probably becomes negative when teachers
overuse this approach.
5.2. Correlations between students’ perceptions of classroom management strategies and their digital reading performance
Generally, classroom management comprises teachers’ actions to ensure class order and effective time use during lessons. This
study examined two features of management in test language lessons: perceived disciplinary climate and learning time in a week.
Confirming the findings of Qian and Lau (2022) for the Chinese sample, the current study also discovered a positive correlation
between students’ perceived disciplinary climate in language teaching and students’ digital reading performance in OECD countries. A
sound classroom climate is characterized by an orderly and engaging atmosphere with few interruptions, which is a prerequisite for
students’ engagement and teachers’ effective and smooth teaching (e.g., Bourdeaud’hui, Aesaert, Van Keer, & Van Braak, 2018;
Lipowsky et al., 2009; Yang, Zhou, & Hu, 2022). Thus, in reading instruction, establishing clear classroom rules, tracking students’
work, and managing disruptive noises and behaviors might help students be more engaged in reading activities and acquire more
comprehension skills and knowledge of reading strategies, which will probably further improve their performance in digital reading.
Concerning learning time in test language lessons, its slightly negative relation to the students’ digital reading performance
contradicts the research of Andersen, Humlum, and Nandrup (2016) on reading teaching. Instruction time is a central element in
educational reforms and findings on whether students benefit from having more instruction are inconsistent (e.g., Andersen et al.,
2016; Archambault & Barnett, 2010). In this study, the negative influence of learning time in test language lessons should be treated
with caution. First, in reading instruction, the amount of time used means less than how time is used. Due to PISA’s data limits and the
complexity of measuring classroom teaching duration, time allocation for teaching and practicing reading and digital skills is not
available, which indicates a need for further investigation of the influence of specific time management strategies for reading instruction. Second, given that previous studies suggested that some recreational uses of ICT outside school, such as email applications,
online news reading, and Internet browsing, are positively related to students’ performance in digital reading (e.g., Chen, Lin, & Chen,
2021; Gil-Flores et al., 2012; Hu & Yu, 2021), long learning time at school might decrease the time and opportunities for students’
extracurricular ICT experiences, thus hindering their performance in digital reading.
5.3. Correlations between students’ perceptions of supportive climate features and their digital reading performance
This study examined students’ perceived constituents of a supportive climate in language lessons, including perceived teacher
interest, teacher support, and teacher feedback.
In line with previous findings (e.g., Ma et al., 2021; Qian & Lau, 2022), positive influence of perceived teacher support in test
language lessons on students’ digital reading performance was discovered. Perceived teacher interest in teaching also demonstrated a
positive correlation with students’ digital reading performance, which extends previous studies that have primarily emphasized the
importance of students’ reading enjoyment to their performance in digital reading (e.g., Chen, Lin, & Chen, 2021; Ma et al., 2021;
Naumann, 2019). As self-determination theorists have proposed (e.g., Deci & Ryan, 2000), teachers’ motivating style meets the mental
needs of students, including their perceived autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Supportive and passionate teacher behaviors
often correlate with a positive climate in the current class (e.g., Kunter et al., 2013; Mainhard, Brekelmans, & Wubbels, 2011). Thus, in
reading instruction, providing more support and demonstrating more teaching enthusiasm might support students’ need for relatedness, enhance their reading interest and enjoyment, and motivate students to be more engaged in reading activities, thus helping
them perform better in digital reading assessments.
However, a negative correlation between perceived feedback and their digital reading performance was found, which seems to
contradict education orthodoxy that traditionally highlights the positive role of feedback (e.g., Hattie & Timperley, 2007; Van der
Kleij, Feskens, & Eggen, 2015). However, in research based on PISA data, a negative relationship between feedback perceived by
students and their academic performance has been discovered by many scholars (e.g., Lau & Lam, 2017; Li et al., 2022; Tourón et al.,
2019). Notably, the negative relation in the present study does not mean that teachers’ feedback hinders students’ digital reading
performance. Two potential reasons may help explain this phenomenon. One is similar to the negative influence of students’ perceived
teacher-directed instruction. In the PISA 2018, the feature of teacher feedback was measured from students’ perspective. Typically,
teachers often provide feedback to students as a remedial measure to help them improve their academic performance (OECD, 2016).
Consequently, the more poorly students perform in digital reading, the more feedback they probably receive from their teachers.
Second, the items for measuring teacher feedback in the PISA 2018, e.g., offering students feedback on their strengths in test language
learning (OECD, 2022), are somewhat general. Different specific feedback strategies have varied influences. For example, research
found that in computer-based assessments of learning, students’ perceived immediate correct-or-wrong response and elaborated
feedback were more useful than delayed feedback (e.g., Van der Kleij, Eggen, Timmers, & Veldkamp, 2012). Therefore, future studies
should investigate specific feedback strategies in reading instruction.
5.4. Correlations between control variables and students’ digital reading performance
In empirical studies, control variables refer to the variables that are not of primary interest and considered extraneous or third
factors whose influences are to be controlled, with the intention of reducing potential bias (Salkind, 2010). Given that the research
focus in the present study is the relationship between students’ perceptions of instruction quality features and their digital reading
performance, in the three-level HLM analyses, student and school sociodemographic factors and those related to the development level

6. Conclusion
This study examined the influence of students’ perceptions of instruction quality features in three categories, i.e., instructional
approaches, classroom management, and supportive climate, on secondary school students’ performance in digital reading, aiming to
provide a more comprehensive and generalizable view. Based on data regarding 223,807 15-year-old students drawn from a largescale international survey—the PISA 2018—the findings from three-level HLM analyses implied that some students’ perceived features are positively related to students’ digital reading performance, including perceived adaptation of instruction, stimulation of
reading engagement, classroom disciplinary climate, teacher interest and teacher support. However, other student-perceived features,
including teacher-directed instruction, training exercises for reading skills, teaching practices of digital skills, learning time of test
language lessons, and teacher feedback, had an unexpected negative correlation with students’ performance in digital reading.
This study has several implications. Theoretically, the present study specified the framework of instruction quality within the
context of reading instruction and combined it with the data of a large-scale international survey. Considering instructional approaches, classroom management, and supportive climate simultaneously, subject-specific features, whether well researched (e.g.,
perceived teacher-directed instruction) or less studied (e.g., training exercises on reading skills and teaching practices on digital skills),
were investigated based on the latest PISA 2018 survey. Practically, extending the previous studies that primarily focused on a single
country, this study employed three-level HLMs to investigate the relationships between students’ perceptions of reading instruction
features and their digital reading performance in 29 OECD countries. The more generalizable results provide insights into the role of
reading instruction in the development of digital reading literacy. Thus, it seems desirable for teachers to adapt instruction to meet
students’ needs, adopt appropriate stimulating strategies, create and maintain a sound disciplinary climate, and invest more interest
and support in reading instruction. Concerning the seemingly negative influence of some features discovered in this study, such as
perceived teacher-directed instruction and exercises of reading skills, further efforts need to be made to find effective implementation
strategies, identify target cohorts, and facilitate students’ internalization of taught knowledge and skills.
However, some limitations of the present study need to be improved in follow-up research. First, given that the PISA data are crosssectional (e.g., Petko, Cantieni, & Prasse, 2017), the links between students’ perceptions of instruction quality features and their digital
reading performance should be deemed correlational rather than causal. Longitudinal studies are thus needed to illustrate the predictive force of perceived instruction quality on students’ academic performance. Second, although this study has expanded the
theoretical framework of instruction quality and found significant influences of student-perceived reading instruction features, the
measuring items in the PISA questionnaire are still limited and the caution needs to be paid in interpreting the findings because of the
small beta values of some features. For future research, the quality and frequency of more reading instruction features as well as diverse
reading theories are advised to be integrated into a more systematic analysis of instruction quality. In addition, the potential mechanisms underlying the influences of different instruction features need further investigation. Moreover, despite the rather generalizable results compared with previous literature, the present study is restricted to 29 OECD countries that have similar economic
backgrounds and whose data on the required variables are available. Given the computation complexity and the major research focus,
the slopes of independent and control variables remained fixed in HLM analyses. Accordingly, future research might be conducted in
more countries and cultures, and models with random intercepts and random slopes might be constructed to explore the heterogeneity
of the influence of instruction quality features across schools and countries.
