Gender gaps in reading achievement have been consistently documented across countries and over time, with
girls typically outperforming boys. However, explanations for cross-national and temporal variation in these gaps
remain limited. This study addresses this research gap using international large-scale assessment data from 56
countries over a 46-year time span, representing the most comprehensive analysis of its kind to date. We examine
whether country-level trends in gender differences in reading behavior predict corresponding trends in gender
gaps in reading achievement. Using fixed-effects regression models, we find that shifts in gender gaps in free time
reading frequency are significantly associated with shifts in reading achievement gaps, even after accounting for
potential confounders. Our findings provide empirical support for the Gender Similarity Hypothesis, emphasizing
the role of social and behavioral factors. The study offers valuable implications for educational policy aiming to
reduce gender disparities in literacy outcomes.

1. Introduction
In this paper, we explore whether gender gaps in leisure reading at
home influence gender gaps in reading achievement at the end of primary school. Early gender gaps in education warrant close attention not
solely as markers of educational inequity, but also because of their
broader implications across the life course. Differences in education can
amplify or prevent disparities in other domains, such as economic or
civic outcomes (e.g., Corak, 2013; Heckman et al., 2018; Watts, 2020)
and basic (reading) literacy constitutes a foundational competency that
underlies subsequent learning across all domains (e.g., Snow et al.,
1998; Vygotsky, 1978).
Nowadays, girls and boys both have near-universal access to primary
and secondary education in most countries, and the remaining enrollment disparities—mainly in countries of the Global South—have
continued to decline in recent years (Steinmann & Rutkowski, 2023;
UNESCO GEM Report, 2020). At the same time, girls outperform boys on
standardized reading comprehension tests in most countries (Mullis
et al., 2023; OECD, 2019). Interestingly, these gender gaps vary
considerably between countries and change over time (Steinmann,
Strietholt et al., 2023b; TIMSS & PIRLS International Study Center,

2017). As gender gaps in achievement persist despite equal access to
schooling, differences and changes in out-of-school learning opportunities might help explain these gaps. In the present study, we focus on
gender-specific variations in free time reading behavior as a possible
explanation for observed trends in gender gaps in reading achievement.
To capture variation in mean reading achievement gender gaps between countries and over time, we constructed a country-level, longitudinal database spanning almost fifty years (1970–2016) by integrating
multiple international large-scale assessments that include both information about students’ free time reading behavior and scores on standardized reading tests. To isolate the effect of changes in free time
reading behavior from other features of the education systems, we
exploited within-country changes over time and estimated country-fixed
effects. By tracing decades of trends in boys’ and girls’ leisure reading
alongside achievement outcomes, we aim to better understand a potential mechanism underlying changing gender gaps in reading
achievement: changing gender gaps in free time reading behavior.

Typically, girls are found to outperform boys in school age tests of
reading achievement, but the magnitude of this gender gap varies
considerably between countries in international large-scale assessments,
often between statistically insignificant gaps to more than half a standard deviation advantage of girls (Rosén et al., 2022; Mullis et al., 2023;
OECD, 2009, 2019).
Furthermore, gender gaps in reading achievement are found to
change over time. Steinmann et al. (2023b), for example, linked data
from older and more recent international large-scale assessments on
reading comprehension at the end of primary school and found that
internationally, average gender gaps increased between 1970 and 2001,
and decreased until 2016. Furthermore, countries showed individual
trends over time that deviated from this international trend (ibid.). Such
country-specific trends are also visible in more recent assessment cycles
(TIMSS & PIRLS International Study Center, 2017). Some studies also
suggest that gender gaps in reading achievement increase over at successive assessment points over the school career (e.g., Borgonovi et al.,
2018; Reilly et al., 2019).

1.2.2. Different learning environments for boys and girls
Differences in learning environments have a larger potential to
explain gender gap differences between countries and over time than
psychological differences. In many education systems, however, boys
and girls attend the same schools and classes and have therefore very
similar learning environments at school (e.g., same teachers, curricula,
school climate). However, there are some findings suggesting that boys
and girls might react differently to the same learning environments
(Aucejo et al., 2022; Autor et al., 2016; Hochweber & Vieluf, 2018; Lavy
& Sand, 2019; Legewie & DiPrete, 2012). Furthermore, single-sex
schooling policies imply that boys and girls are segregated into separate schools, which may result in differing learning opportunities and
educational experiences (e.g., Basharat & Steinmann, 2025; Robinson
et al., 2021). Similarly, the literature discusses whether early tracking
policies, which imply placing students into separate school types after
comprehensive primary school, increase the segregation of boys and
girls into separate learning environments, potentially affecting gender
gaps in achievement outcomes (Steinmann et al., 2023a; Hermann &
Kopasz, 2019; van Langen et al., 2006).
Another strand of literature discusses that boys and girls might be
treated differently, even if they are in the same learning environment.
One example is that even though boys and girls should be randomly
distributed among different families (e.g., socioeconomic backgrounds),
parents or legal guardians report having engaged in more early literacy
activities with daughters than sons before they entered school (Baker &
Milligan, 2013; Højen et al., 2022; Ozturk et al., 2016). Similarly,
teachers might have different expectations for boys and girls, thereby
shaping their reading motivation and self-concepts (e.g., Retelsdorf
et al., 2015; Wolter et al., 2015).
Such differences in how boys and girls are treated by caregivers and
teachers might relate to cultural norms or stereotypes around skillsets,
interests, and activities that are culturally associated with femininity or
masculinity, respectively (e.g., Connell, 2002; Espinoza & Strasser,
2020; Halpern, 2012; Pansu et al., 2016). Such cultural norms or stereotypes might also shape another possible explanation for gender gaps
in reading achievement: Gender differences in reading as a free time
activity.

1.2. Explanations for gender gaps in reading achievement
The literature offers various theoretical explanations for gender gaps
in educational achievement see overviews by Connell (2002); Halpern,
(2012); Maccoby, (1998). These explanations often point to a combination of innate and environmental factors as underlying observed differences between boys and girls. While it is now widely acknowledged
that both dimensions play a role, we consider environmental influences—particularly those related to differential behaviors and experiences—as especially relevant, given that they are subject to change
through policy and practice. Moreover, although innate, psychological
factors may contribute to gender differences in reading achievement,
they cannot account for the substantial variation in gender gaps
observed across countries and over time. In the following, we summarize
key perspectives related to psychological differences, differences in
learning environments, and variations in free time reading behavior.
1.2.1. Psychological differences between boys and girls
There are longstanding assumptions that boys and girls differ in
psychological domains that are related to school achievement, for
example cognitive abilities. However, multiple large meta-analyses have
repeatedly shown that boys and girls score similarly in most psychological domains, especially general cognitive abilities (Halpern, 2012;
Hyde, 2005; Spinath et al., 2014; Zell et al., 2015). This observation
resulted in the Gender Similarity Hypothesis (Hyde, 2005, 2014).
Beyond the Gender Similarity Hypothesis, broader sociocultural theories
help explain why gendered reading behaviors may emerge and vary
across contexts. Social cognitive theory emphasizes that preferences and
behaviors develop through observational learning, social reinforcement,
and perceived norms (Bandura, 2001). Social role theory similarly argues that gendered behaviors reflect socially structured role expectations that differ across societies and over time (Eagly, 1987). While our
analyses do not test these mechanisms directly, these perspectives provide a general framework for interpreting cross-national and longitudinal variation in gendered reading behavior.
However, it should be noted that some studies suggest that in early
childhood, girls have a head start in the development of cognitive
abilities (Palejwala & Fine, 2015; Von Stumm & Plomin, 2015) and
language abilities (Zambrana et al., 2014; Zubrick et al., 2007).
Furthermore, the literature discusses whether boys and girls might differ
in relevant personality traits and self-regulation skills that play a role in
school outcomes (De Bolle et al., 2015; DiPrete & Jennings, 2012; Spinath et al., 2014).

1.2.3. Free time reading behavior as an explanation for gender gaps in
reading achievement
Reading as a free time activity can be expected to foster reading
achievement. Numerous studies have documented that being motivated
to read, liking reading, and reading frequently outside of school are
associated with higher reading achievement (Hu et al., 2024; Lepper
et al., 2022; Mol & Bus, 2011; Mullis et al., 2023; OECD, 2015; Petscher,
2010; Waxman, 2015). This may be because reading outside of school
provides valuable learning opportunities, or because those who read
better enjoy reading more (Jerrim & Moss, 2019; Mol & Bus, 2011;
OECD, 2015; Sullivan & Brown, 2015). Several large-scale studies have
shown that girls read more often than boys in their free time, and more
often read fiction (Hu et al., 2024; Jabbar & Warraich, 2023; Jerrim &
Moss, 2019; Mullis et al., 2023; OECD, 2015), which might help explain
their international tendency for reading advantages over boys.
Interestingly, an international large-scale assessment showed that
15-year-old students’ free time reading declined over time, both in
comparison between 2000 and 2009 (OECD, 2015) and between 2009
and 2018 (OECD, 2019). As a potential explanation for why free time
reading declines, the literature discusses that students seem to rather
spend their free time using digital devices (OECD, 2019; Rosén & Gustafsson, 2014, 2016). Indeed, Rosén and Gustafsson, (2014) found that
with an increasing average use of digital devices, the average frequency

This study aims to address a gap in the existing literature by investigating whether trends in gender gaps in free time reading behavior
predict trends in gender gaps in reading achievement at the country
level. We hypothesize finding a positive association between these
trends, meaning that increasing gender gaps in free time reading
behavior coincide with increasing gender gaps in reading achievement,
and vice versa. This contributes to the understanding of causes of
changes in gender gaps in reading achievement and thus helps to inform
policy-related decision making. This study utilizes country-level data
from six cycles of international large-scale assessment studies on reading
comprehension at the end of primary school between 1970 and 2016.
Specifically, we combine data from six cross-sectional studies of
different student cohorts, all assessed at the end of primary school. The
unit of analysis is the country, observed repeatedly across study cycles.
We estimate longitudinal models with country-fixed effects to account
for any time-stable differences between the countries when investigating
trends over time.

assessment, which might limit the comparability of the achievement test
and consequently the measurement of gender gaps in reading achievement (Peras et al., 2023).
We included data from all countries or regions that participated in at
least two of the six study cycles between 1970 and 2016 to be able to
measure within-country change. This resulted in 212 country-by-year
observations. From these 212, we excluded countries with large shares
of single-sex schools since our study on the role of gender gaps in free
time reading behavior rests on the assumption that boys and girls
experience the same learning conditions at school (see ‘Introduction’
section above). In some Middle Eastern education systems such as Saudi
Arabia, Kuwait, Qatar, or Iran, a large proportion of children attend
single-sex schools, which can imply that boys and girls experience very
different learning conditions such as only same-sex teachers and
different school climate conditions (Basharat & Steinmann, 2025).
Specifically, we removed countries with one third or more single-sex
schools (i.e., schools with at least 10 students in the RCS, RLS, and
PIRLS samples who were either all boys or girls).1 This resulted in 192
country-by-year observations (see Table 1) from 56 unique countries or
regions (see Fig. 1), and from altogether 897,876 individual students.

2. Methods
2.1. Data
This study builds on a unique dataset that combines various international studies on reading comprehension that have been conducted
over 46 years (Steinmann et al., 2014; Strietholt et al., 2013; Strietholt &
Rosén, 2016). Specifically, the dataset combines reading test and questionnaire data from the Progress in International Reading Literacy Study
(PIRLS) with its predecessor studies from 1970 (Reading Comprehension
Study; RCS) and 1991 (Reading Literacy Study; RLS). All three studies
are international large-scale assessments that assess the reading
achievement of children at the end of primary school in standardized
tests and in representative samples (Martin et al., 2003; Mullis et al.,
2017; Thorndike, 1973). The international target populations in RCS
were the grades with most 10-year-old students, in RLS the grades with
most 9-year-old students, and in PIRLS the fourth grades. The original
PIRLS data is available from https://www.iea.nl/data-tools/repository/
pirls and the original RCS and RLS data from https://www.gu.se/en/cen
ter-for-comparative-analysis-of-educational-achievement-compeat/stud
ies-before-1995.
The reading achievement tests were linked over time through a
linking studies, making it possible to represent achievement gaps on the
same scale across all study cycles (1970, 1991, 2001, 2006, 2011, and
2016; Steinmann et al., 2014; Strietholt et al., 2013; Strietholt & Rosén,
2016). Based on this common achievement scale, we estimated countries’ mean reading gender gaps that were comparable over time
(Steinmann et al., 2023b). In the present study, this dataset of
country-level trends in gender gaps in reading achievement is expanded
by including data on the students’ reading behavior outside of school

We included three control variables in our study. Since one was only
available for the more recent PIRLS cycles, we ran the analyses that
included control variables for a reduced dataset of countries that
participated in at least two of the PIRLS cycles (2001–2016).
The first control variable is the share of single-sex schools. As
mentioned in the ‘Data’ section above, we excluded country-by-year
observations in which more than 33 % of students attended single-sex
schools. However, since some countries had lower but still considerable shares of single-sex schools, we furthermore included this variable
as a control. This is based on the assumption that single-sex schooling
policies can potentially vary over time and create differential learning
conditions for boys and girls (Basharat & Steinmann, 2025), thus
affecting gender gaps in achievement. The share of single-sex schools (i.
e., schools with at least 10 students in the PIRLS samples who were
either all boys or girls) varied between 0–30 % in the analysis sample
(M = 3 %, SD = 6).
The second control variable is the standardized gender gap in
average student age. Changes in vertical differentiation policies (e.g.,
about school entry, grade repetition) may have different implications for
boys and girls. For instance, boys may be more likely to start school later
of repeat a grade (Anderson & Anderson, 2020; Davoudzadeh et al.,
2015). We argue that this would manifest in age differences between
boys and girls who participate in PIRLS, with boys being older than girls
in the fourth grade. We therefore computed a standardized gender gap in
age variable by dividing the weighted mean difference of girls’ and boys’
age in years by the country-by-year observation-specific pooled standard
deviation. This variable varied between − 0.37–0.14 in the sample
focusing on PIRLS cycles (M = − 0.10, SD = 0.09). This implies that on
average across observations, girls were 0.10 standard deviations
younger than boys when they participated in the PIRLS assessments.
We included the standardized gender gap in average early literacy
activities as a third control variable. We assume that changing social and
cultural norms influence how parents or legal guardians foster their
children’s early literacy in gender-specific ways (Højen et al., 2022;
Ozturk et al., 2016), which may change over time and subsequently
affect gender gaps in later reading achievement. In PIRLS, parents or
legal guardians report how often they used to do literacy activities (e.g.,
reading books, telling stories, playing with alphabet toys) with their
child before primary school entry. We computed the standardized
gender gap in early literacy activities by dividing the weighted mean
difference of girls’ and boys’ early literacy activity index by the
country-by-year observation-specific pooled standard deviation. This
variable varied between − 0.05–0.14 in the sample focusing on PIRLS
cycles (M = 0.11, SD = 0.05). This implies that on average across observations, girls’ early literacy activities were 0.11 standard deviations
higher than boys’ according to their parents/legal guardians. This variable was missing for seven country-by-year observations.

2.2.2. Gender gaps in free time reading behavior
We used student-reported questionnaire data to trace changes in how
often students read in their free time that was available for RCS, RLS,
and PIRLS. Although we assume that the variables measure the same
construct, the item wording and response categories differed across the
three studies (see Appendix A). For this reason, we calculated standardized effect sizes for the gender gaps in reading behavior by dividing
the mean differences between boys and girls by the pooled standard
deviation of the respective country-by-year distribution. We reversed
the PIRLS item responses to make sure that higher values indicate more
frequent free time reading across studies. Like for gender gaps in reading
achievement, these gender gaps in free time reading behavior can be
interpreted as Cohen’s d values. In other words, for each cycle and
country, we measure how much girls and boys differ on average in free
time reading behavior in standard deviation units.
Table 1 shows descriptive statistics of the standardized gender gaps
in the reading behavior variable. The total reading behavior gap of d =
0.27 means that on average across country-by-year observations, girls
reported 0.27 standard deviation units higher free time reading behavior
than boys. The gender reading behavior gap varied considerably across
countries and over time.

2.3. Analyses

2.2.3. Control variables
Countries with large gender gaps in reading behavior may also differ
in other important ways that might affect gender gaps in reading
achievement. To strengthen our conclusions, it is therefore essential to
account for alternative explanations—specifically, other time-varying
country-level factors that may influence changes in gender gaps in
reading achievement at the end of primary school and that could coincide with changes in gender gaps in reading behavior. In other words,
since our unit of analysis is change at the country level, the range of
potential confounding factors is limited to country-level characteristics
that vary over time. Furthermore, we assume that country-level changes
in stereotypical beliefs around boys’ and girls’ reading behavior and
skills have the potential to affect gender gaps in reading behavior
(Espinoza & Strasser, 2020; Retelsdorf et al., 2015), but not the association between gender gaps in reading behavior and gender gaps in
reading achievement.

Our analytical approach takes advantage of the repeated observations of countries over time, resulting in data with a longitudinal
component at the country level. We aim to examine whether withincountry changes in gender gaps in reading behavior predict corresponding changes in gender gaps in reading achievement. By focusing on
within-country variation, we effectively control for time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity across countries.
As a baseline, we first estimated a cross-sectional country-level
regression model in which we regressed gender gaps in reading
achievement on gender gaps in reading behavior (Model 1). In the main
model (Model 2), we included country-fixed effects, meaning dummycoded country identifiers. This way, we investigate country-level trend
analyses, controlling for any time-invariant differences in gender gaps in
reading achievement between the countries. To maximize the identifying variation, we purposely omit time-fixed effects. While countryfixed effects mitigate endogeneity from cross-sectional correlations,

including time-fixed effects would absorb broad global trends that are
central to the phenomenon under study. We retain these trends to preserve meaningful longitudinal variation and maintain sufficient statistical power for estimation.2 Effectively, this model compares countries
with themselves over time. Therefore, we included only countries that
participated in at least two out of six measurement points (see ‘Data’
section above and Fig. 1). The fixed effects specification captures general
trends over time and country-specific deviations from this general trend.
However, the estimates from the country-fixed effects models are not
informed by cross-country variation, as such variation is accounted for
and may also be influenced by unobserved confounding variables.
Results of country-fixed effects trend analyses can be interpreted as
causal effects, if certain prerequisites of fixed effects regression are
fulfilled and under the assumption that no time-varying confounders
bias the estimated relationship (Allison, 2009). First, country-fixed effects regression requires longitudinal data with comparable measurements over time (ibid.). While we made sure that the gender gaps in
reading achievement were linked across RCS, RLS, and PIRLS cycles in
prior work (Steinmann et al., 2023b), the measures of reading behavior,
and therefore to some extent gender gaps in reading behavior, were less
comparable between these three studies (see ‘Variables’ section above
and Appendix A). We therefore added a robustness check where we ran
the main model for PIRLS data only (Model 3) because the reading
behavior measure was the same across all PIRLS cycles. A second prerequisite for fixed-effects regression is sufficient within-country variation in the predictor variables (Allison, 2009). As shown in Fig. 1, there
was considerable within-country variation in gender gaps in reading
behavior. Third, reverse causality must be ruled out (ibid.). Since
country-level reading behavior cannot be experimentally manipulated,
the assumed direction of the relationship must be supported by theoretical reasoning. We argue that broader lifestyle changes—particularly
the increased use of digital technologies—have altered how children
spend their leisure time, including a decline in reading for pleasure
(Rosén & Gustafsson, 2014). While reciprocal effects are possible (i.e.,
less leisure reading may lead to lower reading achievement, which in
turn may discourage reading due to lack of proficiency; Jerrim & Moss,
2019; Mol & Bus, 2011), even in such cases, reading behavior remains
the key mechanism that hinders the development of reading achievement. Fourth, time-varying confounders that can potentially provide
alternative explanations for the observed relationship have to be
accounted for (Allison, 2009). We therefore included three control
variables (see ‘Variables’ section above) as additional predictors in
separate models (Models 4, 5, and 6).

3.2. Main findings
Table 2 depicts the results of our country-level regression models.
The cross-sectional Model 1 served as a baseline for our main analyses
and indicated a positive, statistically significant association between
country-by-year observations’ degree of gender gaps in reading
behavior and gender gaps in reading achievement. However, this association may be confounded by other features of the education systems.
The main Model 2 with country-fixed effects effectively controls for all
time-stable confounding factors at the country level. The observed effect
increased compared to the first, cross-sectional model. This indicates
that countries that showed increases in gender gaps in reading behavior
also showed small increases in gender gaps in reading achievement. This
supports our hypothesis that changes in reading behavior gender gaps
explain changes in reading achievement gender gaps.
The estimated effect is in Model 2 is 0.11. Given that the average
achievement gap across all countries and years is 0.14 standard deviations (see Table 1, column 3), a one–standard deviation reduction in
the gender gap in reading behavior would correspond to a reduction of
about 79 % of the gender gap in reading achievement. However, gender
gaps in reading behavior are typically much smaller than one standard

behavior that we observed in the present study, and thus contributes to
changing gender gaps in reading achievement.
4.1. Limitations
We would like to stress four central limitations of our study. The first
limitation concerns the generalizability of its findings. Although the
analysis draws on large random samples from 56 countries or regions,
spanning 46 years, the representativeness of the sample is limited by the
underrepresentation of countries from the Global South. As a result, the
findings may not be generalizable to these regions. Additionally, the
distribution of participating countries is uneven over time, with a
greater number of countries participating in the more recent assessment
cycles.
Second, the quality of the reading behavior measure is limited. We
chose one item from each of the six cycles that were most comparable
over time, but a scale with multiple items would have helped to quantify
and possibly reduce measurement error. Allen et al. (2022), however,
note that much of the research on single-item measures suggests they are
often as valid and reliable as multi-item scales. Furthermore, the reading
behavior items were not identical across the studies RCS, RLS, and
PIRLS. We argue, however, that standardized gender gaps in these
variables are comparable across cycles anyway and conducted a
robustness analysis based on the comparable PIRLS cycles. This analysis
replicated the main results. This issue is not new either, Chmielewski
(2019), for instance, used similar data to analyze trends in socioeconomic achievement gaps. Her study demonstrated that different items
intended to measure the same construct can still yield robust results.
Additionally, it is possible that boys underreport their free time reading
behavior in comparison to girls, if free time reading is culturally valued
differently for boys and girls (e.g., Espinoza & Strasser, 2020). This
might explain part of the pronounced differences in self-reported free
time reading behavior that we observed in some cases. Despite all these
limitations related to the measurement of reading behavior, we interpret
the observed association between gender gaps in reading behavior and
reading achievement as relatively robust. If one assumes that the use of
single-item indicators may limit validity and reliability, then it is likely
that the effects we observed underestimated the true magnitude of the
underlying relationship.
The third main limitation concerns the causal interpretability of our
findings. We used a strong longitudinal country-level trend design to
account for all (un-)observed time-invariant confounding factors (cf.,
Strietholt et al., 2014; Strietholt et al., 2014; Rosén & Gustafsson, 2014)
and included key time-variant control variables. However, we cannot
rule out the possibility that we omitted a potentially relevant control

4. Discussion
Why do gender gaps in reading achievement vary across countries
and over time? Using six cycles of large-scale assessment data, this study
investigated whether trends in gender gaps in free time reading behavior
predict trends in gender gaps in reading achievement at the country
level. Consistent with expectations, we did find a positive effect of
gender gaps in reading behavior on gender gaps in reading achievement
in our study. A one standard deviation change in the gender gap in
reading behavior is associated with a change of approximately one-tenth
of a standard deviation in the gender gap in reading achievement. This
implies that at least part of the countries’ trends in gender gaps in
reading achievement can be attributed to changing gender gaps in
reading behavior. If girls read more frequently than boys in their free
time, on average, they have a mean advantage over boys who read less,
on average. If boys’ and girls’ free time reading behavior becomes more
similar, girls lose part of their advantage in reading achievement tests.
These main results remain largely robust across different model
specifications, including those based on constrained samples and those
incorporating time-variant control variables. An exception is the model
that included the gender gap in average early literacy activities as
control variable, where the main effect of gender gaps in reading
behavior was similar in effect size but no longer statistically significant.
If trends in gender gaps in early literacy activities do indeed coincide
with trends in gender gaps in reading behavior, this might reflect underlying cultural changes, implying that due to changing cultural gender
norms, both parents and children change their literacy behavior. In this
case, the control variable is a confounder, but not an alternative
explanation for the observed association between gender gaps in reading
behavior and gender gaps in reading achievement. However, it is

4.2. Conclusion
Gender gaps in reading achievement have been well-documented
across a wide range of studies (e.g., Mullis et al., 2017; OECD, 2019,
Strello et al., 2023). However, much less is known about why these gaps
vary across countries and change over time. Overall, children report
reading less today than in the beginning of this millennium (OECD,
2015, 2019). To our knowledge, this study is the first to empirically test
whether trends in gender gaps in free time reading behavior predict
trends in gender gaps in reading achievement. Our central finding is that
changes in leisure reading gender gaps help explain changes in reading
achievement gender gaps. If the gap between girls’ and boys’ leisure
reading narrows, girls lose some of their commonly observed advantage
in reading tests. If the gap widens, girls’ advantages in reading tests
increase.
Our findings have important theoretical implications. The observation that changes in gendered reading behavior are mirrored in changes
in reading achievement gaps points to the relevance of environmental
and behavioral factors—specifically, socially shaped reading practices—for understanding gender differences in achievement. This finding
provides support for the Gender Similarity Hypothesis (Hyde, 2005). In
contrast, our study does not provide evidence about innate or psychological explanations, and we therefore refrain from drawing conclusions
in that direction.
Taken together, the results suggest that gender gaps in reading
achievement should not be treated as fixed, but as outcomes of socialization and learning processes that can change over time. From a policy
