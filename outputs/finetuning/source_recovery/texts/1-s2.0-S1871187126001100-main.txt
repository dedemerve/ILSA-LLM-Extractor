Abstract
Creative thinking is recognized as a pivotal 21st-century competency and was introduced as a
new assessment domain in PISA 2022. Ensuring fairness in such large-scale assessments is essential for
valid cross-national interpretations. This study examined differential item functioning (DIF) in 15
Creative Thinking (CT) items in PISA 2022 using a tree-based approach that applies recursive
partitioning to explore subgroup differences beyond predefined groups. Responses from 10,779 students
across all countries that administered Form 37 were analyzed with the Partial Credit tree (PCtree) model.
DIF items identifying subgroups were examined by using the logistic ordinal regression model. The
PCtree model identified six distinct subgroups defined by complex interactions of immigrant background,
gender, and economic, social and cultural status (ESCS), with ESCS emerging as the primary partitioning
factor. All 15 CT items exhibited DIF across multiple subgroup comparisons; however, individual effect
sizes were negligible according to established criteria. Nevertheless, small DIF effects may accumulate
across items, potentially resulting in differential bundle functioning (DBF) or differential test functioning
(DTF) at the assessment level. These findings reveal complex intersectional patterns of DIF that
traditional single-variable DIF approaches would miss, demonstrating how multiple demographic
characteristics combine to create measurement inequities. The study emphasizes the importance of
exploratory validation strategies in creativity assessment and highlights the need for comprehensive
fairness validation before interpreting PISA 2022 CT results across groups and countries.
Keywords: Differential item functioning, Recursive data partitioning, Partial credit tree, Creative
thinking, PISA 2022

1. Introduction
Creative thinking is widely recognized as a key competency in the 21st century, essential for
problem-solving, innovation, and critical reasoning. In response to this global emphasis, the OECD
included creative thinking as a distinct assessment domain in the PISA 2022 study. As with any newly
developed measurement tool, ensuring the fairness and validity of the assessment is a critical step toward
meaningful interpretation and responsible use of assessment results. One important aspect of assessment
validation is the detection of Differential Item Functioning (DIF). DIF occurs when individuals with the
same level of the latent trait respond differently to specific items due to external factors such as gender,
age, or socioeconomic status (SES). When such bias exists in assessment items, it may threaten the
fairness and validity of the assessment, potentially leading to misinterpretation of results and inequitable
outcomes across groups.
This research investigates potential DIF in the PISA 2022 Creative Thinking (CT) items using a
novel analytic method: a tree-based model grounded in Item Response Theory (IRT) and machine
learning (Komboz et al., 2018; Strobl et al., 2015). By applying this model to the PISA 2022 CT
assessment, the research aims to identify items that may function inequitably across student groups,
thereby providing empirical evidence to evaluate the validity of the assessment. Unlike traditional DIF
detection approaches that rely on predefined grouping variables (e.g., gender), the tree-based model uses
recursive partitioning to explore all possible subgroup splits, based on background variables available in
the dataset (e.g., gender, nationality, and SES). This exploratory capacity makes the method particularly
suitable for creativity assessments, where prior evidence is limited and interactions among multiple
characteristics may influence item functioning.

1.1. Group comparisons and DIF in creativity assessment
Investigating developmental differences in creative thinking informs theory on how creativity is
shaped and transformed into tangible products, guiding practices that foster creativity across life stages.

5

For instance, Goecke et al. (2026) compiled the results of 62 countries from PISA 2022 CT assessment
and found that students were generally more creative if they were females and from families with high
SES which was globally consistent. This strong empirical evidence can be used to support the gendered
and SES effect on creativity development if the assessment is assumed to accurately reflect students’ true
creative thinking abilities.
However, score gaps between groups do not necessarily reflect true ability differences; external
factors can distort results. Several meta-analyses on creativity group comparisons suggest that observed
differences by gender and SES are often influenced by developmental stage, cultural values, family
environment, and other propositions that are not necessarily relevant to creativity. For example, two
recent meta-analyses (Abdulla Alabbasi et al., 2025; Taylor et al., 2024) both found females slightly
outperforming males in creative thinking assessments but these differences were largely explained by task
type and cultural context. Males showed greater variability on verbal tasks emphasizing detail (Abdulla
Alabbasi et al., 2025), implying that verbal ability and effortful responding may influence scores
independent of actual creativity. However, Taylor et al. (2024) suggested that this larger male variability
did not persist when studies accounted for various creative thinking tasks and other types of creative
performance measures. Instead, they found that gender variability gaps were smaller in more genderequal societies, highlighting societal norms, policies, and practices as external influences.
The external influences are also evident in SES-creativity links. Acar et al.’s (2023) meta-analysis
found that family SES had a small but significant impact on creativity, which increased when parents
were highly educated—likely due to enriched creativity-fostering experiences offered by parents. While
personal, family, and societal factors can shape creativity, it remains unclear whether score differences
reflect true ability or construct-irrelevant variance.
In fact, group mean comparisons can mislead when score variability is unequal between groups.
For instance, He and Wong (2021) examined the Greater Male Variability Hypothesis among 206 Hong
Kong undergraduates in creative thinking tasks and found trivial mean gender differences but greater
male variability overall—especially in figural creativity—with overrepresentation of males at both the

6

low and high extremes. Abdulla Alabbasi et al.’s (2025) meta-analysis compiled 187 studies examining
exclusively DT tests and consistently found that larger variability was found in males than females in
divergent thinking, indicating that the distribution of male scores is wider than the distribution of female
scores in DT tests. Contrastingly, Taylor et al. (2024) found trivial male variability when synthesizing 194
studies examining various types of creative thinking tasks, including creative problem-solving, which is
similar to what was assessed in PISA CT assessments. However, they also included some tasks that did
not necessarily assess creative thinking, such as products and measures of creative achievement and
activity participation. Still, the gendered variability gap was not substantial across all task types. The
mixed results regarding male variability in two recent meta-analyses call for further empirical evidence.
Interestingly, Taylor et al. (2024) found that gender egalitarianism values (i.e., socially shared
values promoting gender equality) still contribute to shaping male variability, suggesting that cultural
expectations or societal values related to gender roles may indirectly influence the gendered variability
gap, potentially through differences in opportunities and personal or collective beliefs. This pattern
suggests that some items may favor or disadvantage subsets of a group, which becomes even more
complex when socio-contextual factors are involved, reinforcing the need to examine DIF—one of the
most common ways of assessing the fairness of assessment—alongside means.
Despite the importance of fairness in creativity assessments, few studies have examined it
psychometrically, and findings are mixed. Qian and Wang (2020) found DIF by gender, Hispanic
background, and English language learner (ELL) status analyzing the Creative Thinking Scale: Female,
Hispanic, and ELL students scored lower on the DIF-flagged items despite having equal underlying
creativity. Cotter et al. (2023) also found DIF in most items of the Aesthetic Fluency Scale between
artistically engaged students and non-arts students, suggesting that art exposure can influence scores
regardless of true ability. In contrast, Dumas and Alexander (2018) examined a relational reasoning
scale—partly reflecting remote associative thinking—and found no DIF by gender, language, or ethnicity,
indicating cultural fairness. Given the inconsistent results regarding DIF on creative thinking assessments,
this fairness concern is particularly salient for PISA CT, designed for various group comparisons—

7

including cross-cultural comparisons—of creative thinking. Research on DIF in PISA CT is scarce. To
date, only Jung and Chung (2025) have examined PISA 2022 CT assessment in Korean students and
found no consistent DIF on the items across iterative models, but this was limited to a single country and
only tested gender, restricting generalizability.
Scholars stress caution in interpreting group differences in PISA CT. Rutkowski and Rutkowski
(2025) advocate assessing cultural equivalence and item functioning before making cross-group
comparisons. Álvarez-Huerta et al. (2025) also critique PISA’s framework for risking homogenization
and ignoring cultural nuance, noting that definitions of creative thinking are shaped by language, values,
and local paradigms. Taylor (2025) recommends transparent reporting of fairness analyses before
interpreting group differences. In sum, simply comparing means across groups may obscure underlying
biases in creativity assessment. Both external contextual influences and psychometric properties—such as
DIF and variability—must be evaluated to ensure that group comparisons reflect genuine differences in
creative thinking rather than artifacts of measurement.
1.2. Measurement issues of PISA 2022 creative thinking
International large-scale assessments (ILSAs), such as PISA and TIMSS, are designed to compare
educational performance across countries. To make meaningful comparisons across countries that differ in
language, culture, and educational contexts, measurement invariance is required. Measurement invariance
forms the foundation of fairness in comparisons, implying that an item should function identically for
respondents of the same ability level regardless of their group membership. DIF analysis, which detects
violations of this principle at the item level, is therefore regarded as a mandatory rather than optional
procedure in ILSAs contexts.
Existing research has demonstrated that DIF is indeed substantial in international surveys and
needs to be addressed systematically (Joo et al., 2022; Oliveri & von Davier, 2014). These cross-national
comparisons are particularly challenging given that participating countries span diverse geographical
regions, linguistic families, and educational traditions, making the detection and appropriate treatment of

8

DIF essential for valid international rankings and policy interpretations. The PISA 2022 results emphasize
that comparability across countries and languages is a central consideration guiding instrument
development and the choice of IRT scaling models, specifying procedures for verifying measurement
equivalence through multi-group IRT models (OECD, 2023).
PISA 2022 CT assessment presents elevated DIF risks because it involves open-ended creative
responses where multiple valid approaches exist, necessitating extensive human judgment that introduces
additional bias sources. The high dependence on human scoring creates systematic bias opportunities
across cultural contexts, with 92.36% of CT responses requiring human evaluation (OECD, 2024b).
Moreover, the inter-rater agreement criterion was set at 70% for CT compared to 85% for other domains,
reflecting inherent evaluation challenges (OECD, 2024b). Despite comprehensive rater training, cultural
backgrounds and implicit biases may influence creative response interpretation, leading to systematic
scoring differences across countries. In the context of CT assessment, where open-ended responses
necessitate extensive human scoring, systematic rater effects or differences in rubric interpretation could
elevate the possibility of DIF across countries. This highlights the need for various DIF analyses to ensure
the fairness and validity of cross-national comparisons in creative thinking evaluation.
1.3. Tree-based approach to detecting DIF
DIF analysis traditionally relies on predefined group comparisons, in which researchers specify
focal and reference groups in advance based on theoretical or empirical evidence. It then examines
whether the probability of a correct response on an item, after controlling for overall ability, differs
systematically between focal and reference groups. While this approach is conceptually straightforward
and offers direct interpretability by indicating which group is advantaged or disadvantaged, its scope is
limited: DIF can be detected only for pre-specified groups, implying unexpected but critical sources of
DIF can be dropped. Additionally, if item bias arises from interactions among multiple background
characteristics or from unobservable subgroups, such DIF may be undetected.

9

An alternative is the latent class-based approach for DIF detection, which does not require a prior
specification of groups. Instead, the latent class-based models, such as mixture IRT models, identify latent
subgroups that exhibit different item parameter estimates. This approach is statistically rigorous and can
uncover unexpected patterns of DIF (Chen & Jiao, 2014; Cho et al., 2016; Cohen & Bolt, 2005; de Ayala
et al., 2002). However, the resulting latent classes are often challenging to interpret because their
qualitative characteristics are often not readily intuitive. To address this limitation, researchers typically
conduct post hoc analyses to see how observed covariates vary by the identified latent classes.
To bridge the gap between these two approaches, a tree-based approach that integrates the
decision tree model with statistical models such as IRT has been proposed (Komboz et al., 2018; Strobl et
al., 2015). Essentially, in a tree-based approach, the data are recursively partitioned based on repeated
statistical tests that examine differences in model parameters (e.g., item difficulties of IRT). The Rasch
tree (Strobl et al., 2015) is a tree-based approach model for detecting DIF of the dichotomous data, while
the Partial Credit tree and Rating Scale tree (Komboz et al., 2018) are extended for polytomous data.
The partitioning procedure in these models follows the three steps. In the first step, item
parameters are estimated by fitting an IRT model (e.g., Rasch or partial credit model) to the entire data. In
the next step, the instability of item parameters estimated in the first step is tested to assess significant
differences according to explanatory variables in the data. When a systematic pattern between the
estimated parameters and a given explanatory variable is detected, the item parameters are considered
instable with respect to that variable. Instability tests are repeated for all possible combinations of
explanatory variables in the data, and it makes it possible to detect DIF considering multiple covariates
within single unified analysis. The final step splits the data by the covariates that exhibit the greatest
instability of item parameters. For instance, if the instability of item parameters with respect to the age is
the greatest, their pattern shows that item parameters are lower for students younger than 15 than for those
aged 15 and older. In this case, the data are partitioned into two subsets: students younger than 15 and
students aged 15 and older. After the first data split, the estimation step (i.e., the first step) is repeated by
fitting the IRT model to each subset, followed by the subsequent two steps. These three steps are iterated

10

until no significant instability of item parameters is observed at the 0.05 significance level. In practice,
additional stopping criteria are often applied, such as minimal size of subsets to avoid overfitting and to
enhance interpretability. Computational details are provided in Strobl et al. (2015) and Strobl et al.
(2021).
In sum, the tree-based approach detects potential DIF by systematically exploring all possible
subgroups that can be defined from available explanatory variables (Effatpanah et al., 2025; Henninger et
al., 2025; Strobl et al., 2015). This approach is flexible enough to accommodate that different items may
show DIF for different subgroups requiring separate analyses for each hypothesized grouping. For
example, one creative thinking task may exhibit DIF related to students’ language background, while
another may show DIF associated with gender. The tree-based approach accommodates these variations
naturally, as each item’s DIF analysis is conducted with respect to the subgroups most relevant for that
item. This efficiency is especially valuable when examining a large pool of items, as in the PISA
framework, where the goal is not only to detect biased items but also to understand the contextual and
demographic factors contributing to such potential bias.
Moreover, the subgroups partitioned in the tree-based approach model can be defined by explicit
combinations of multiple covariates (e.g., female students who speak a non-test language at home) as a
result from the subsequent data splitting. This is particularly relevant in large-scale assessments like
PISA, where complex interactions between background factors are likely to influence performance. In
addition, continuous variables such as age can be used directly as potential split variables without
arbitrary discretization based on cut-points such as the mean or mode avoiding information loss due to
arbitrary discretization.
1.4. Purpose of the study
Given these considerations, the present study aims to examine DIF in the PISA 2022 CT
assessment using the PCtree model. Specifically, we investigate whether CT items function differently
across subgroups defined by gender, economic, social and cultural status (ESCS), and immigrant

11

background. Using the PCtree, we identify subgroups exhibiting distinct response patterns and
subsequently evaluate item-level DIF through logistic ordinal regression. This approach enables us to
detect complex intersectional patterns of DIF that traditional single-variable methods may overlook,
thereby contributing empirical evidence on the measurement fairness of the PISA 2022 CT assessment.
The following section describes the data and analytic procedures employed in this study.

2. Method
2.1. Data
2.1.1. Item responses
The PISA 2022 CT assessment was designed to administer combinations of two out of five item
clusters (OECD, 2024b). To mitigate systematic missingness due to the assessment design and potential
item-order effects, the analysis was restricted to item responses with a fixed combination of item clusters
and presentation order. Form 37, comprising 15 items—8 from one item cluster (CT1) and 7 from another
item cluster (CT2)— was selected because this contains the largest number of items among the available
item cluster combinations.
The 15 items analyzed in this study are organized into seven units, with six units having two
items each and one unit having three items. These items represent three cognitive facets of creative
thinking: Generate Creative Ideas, Generate Diverse Ideas, and Evaluate and Improve Ideas. The items
are distributed across four content domains: written expression, visual expression, social problem solving,
and scientific problem solving. Table 1 presents the distribution of the analyzed items across facets and
domains. Domain information for four items (Items 5-8) has not been publicly released by OECD.
Detailed descriptions and examples of CT items can be found in Appendix A of the PISA 2022 technical
report (OECD, 2024b) and at https://www.oecd.org/en/about/programmes/pisa/pisa-2022-creativethinking-test-questions.html.

12

The OECD provided two types of item responses for the PISA 2022 CT assessment: (1) coded
responses categorizing students’ answers for each item; and (2) shortened coded responses, which are
scored as 0, 1, and 2 to represent ‘No credit’, ‘Partial credit’, and ‘Full credit scored’ for polytomous
items, and as 0 and 1 to represent ‘No credit’ and ‘Full credit’ for dichotomous items. In Form 37, 14
items are scored polytomously, and one item is scored dichotomously. Invalid responses were coded
separately as ‘Not reached’, ‘Not applicable’, ‘Invalid’, and ‘No response’ separated from the valid
responses. We analyzed the shortened coded responses of students who had responded to more than 50%
items. Among the 12,477 students who responded to the CT items of Form 37, 861 students with invalid
responses on more than eight items (i.e., over 50% of items) were excluded to reduce potential bias.
Consequently, the shortened coded responses of 11,616 students across 124 countries were analyzed.
It should be noted that all invalid values were replaced with zero because these invalid values
may not be considered missing at random but rather systematic, as they may be related to examinees’
proficiency level (Mislevy & Wu, 1996). For instance, examinees may tend to skip an item when they
believe their response is likely to be incorrect.
2.1.2. Covariates
The PCtree model does not restrict the number or type of covariates used to detect DIF.
Nonetheless, this study limited DIF detection to demographic covariates to enhance interpretability and
reduce overfitting in tree-based recursive partitioning. The focus on gender, a standardized index of
economic, social and cultural status (ESCS), and immigrant background reflects variables routinely used
in detecting DIF research and the group differences in creativity assessment, as well.
Gender is coded as 1 = female and 2 = male. ESCS is standardized with a mean of 0 and a
standard deviation of 1 across the OECD countries (OECD, 2024a). Immigrant background is coded into
three categories: 1 = native students who had at least one parent born in the country, 2 = secondgeneration students who were born in the country with at least one parent born abroad, and 3 = firstgeneration students who were born outside the country with at least one parent also born in another
country (OECD, 2024a).

13

Data analyzed in this study included 5,917 female and 5,698 male students, with one missing
value for gender. For immigrant background, 9,587 students were native, and 686 and 689 were first- and
second-generation students, respectively; 654 cases (5.6%) were missing. ESCS had a mean of -0.258
(ranged from -6.841 to 4.419), with a missing rate of 3.7% (433 cases). Missing values of these covariates
were deleted, and a total of 10,779 students’ item responses and covariates were analyzed.

according to minor discrepancies in item parameters (Effatpanah et al., 2025). Therefore, an additional
DIF test was conducted to confirm items exhibiting significantly different function across subgroups

3. Results
3.1. Identification of subgroups exhibiting DIF: PCtree model
The PCtree model was applied to examine whether students’ responses to CT items in PISA 2022
varied according to gender, ESCS, and immigrant background. As shown in Figure 1, through five
sequential splitting nodes, illustrated as circular nodes 1, 2, 3, 7, and 8, the classification tree resulted in
six terminal nodes (i.e., Nodes A ~F). This tree structure indicates a violation of measurement invariance
assumption, that is, CT items do not function identically for all groups of students. Moreover, all three
covariates (i.e., gender, ESCS, and immigrant background) considered in the analysis exhibited
significant differences in estimated thresholds at their corresponding terminal nodes, implying DIF in
PISA 2022 CT associated with these covariates.
The first split was based on ESCS (i.e., circular node 1 at the top of the tree), and it reveals that
students’ socioeconomic and cultural background had the most substantial influence on CT item responses
among three covariates considered. The left branch represents students with lower ESCS values (i.e.,
ESCS ≤ 0.223), whereas the right branch represents those with higher ESCS values (i.e., ESCS > 0.223).
Both groups were further partitioned by immigrant background (IMMIG) and gender, suggesting an
interaction between these covariates in affecting CT item responses across both ESCS levels.

16

Among low-ESCS students, the second split occurred at IMMIG (i.e., circular node 2); the left
branch corresponds to native students (i.e., IMMIG ≤ 1), while the right branch directly leads to Node C
(n = 735), representing the first- and second-generation students (i.e., IMMIG > 1). The native students
with low-ESCS on the left branch of the IMMIG are further partitioned by gender (i.e., circular node 3),
leading to Node A (gender ≤ 1; n = 3,038) and Node B (gender > 1; n = 2,774).
Similarly, for students with high-ESCS on the right branch of the ESCS, the split at IMMIG (i.e.,
circular node 7) separated native-born students (i.e., IMMIG ≤ 1) from the students with immigrant
backgrounds (i.e., IMMIG > 1). The latter were classified into Node F (n = 610). The native students with
high-ESCS on the left branch of IMMIG (i.e., circular node 7) were further partitioned by gender (i.e.,
circular node 8) into female natives (Node D; n = 1,847) and male natives (Node E, n = 1,775).
To sum up, we defined the six terminal nodes as follows: (1) Node A is the group of low-ESCS
female native-born students; (2) Node B is the group of low-ESCS male native-born students; (3) Node C
is the group of low-ESCS non-native students; (4) Node D is the group of high-ESCS female native-born
students; (5) Node E is the group of high-ESCS male native-born students; and (6) Node F is the group of
high-ESCS non-native students. These findings demonstrate that the PISA 2022 CT items functioned
differently depending on ESCS levels. Moreover, within the similar ESCS levels, the CT item responses
were further influenced by the immigrant background and gender. This provides empirical evidence for
the presence of DIF items associated with background characteristics and their interaction.

parameters, spanning from -4 to 4. The region plot depicts the proportion of each response category (i.e.,

3.2. DIF evaluation across partitioned groups: Logistic ordinal regression model
The PCtree model does not perform an item-level DIF test, but rather a global invariance test for
identifying DIF. Accordingly, it determines whether subgroups exhibiting DIF exist and, if so, which
covariates are associated with these differences; however, it does not directly flag specific DIF items
based on statistically significant difference in parameters across groups. Consequently, a post-hoc analysis
was conducted to evaluate DIF across the six subgroups (i.e., Nodes A to F) identified by the PCtree
model. A logistic ordinal regression model was applied to assess DIF for polytomous responses.
In total, 225 logistic ordinal regression models were estimated, representing 15 pairwise
comparisons among the six terminal nodes for each of the 15 items. Of these 225 comparisons, the
analysis detected DIF in 122 cases. The corresponding test statistics (i.e., G2 values) are presented in
Appendix C, and Appendix D summarizes which node comparisons demonstrated DIF for each item. All
items exhibited DIF in more than one comparison. Item 13 was the most frequently identified as DIF,
with this item being detected in 13 out of 15 pairwise comparisons. In contrast, Item 12 showed DIF in
only four pairwise comparisons, the fewest among all items. Interestingly, while both items assess the
same creative thinking facet (i.e., Generate Diverse Ideas), they involve different topics: ‘Illustration
Titles’ for Item 12 and ‘Save the Bees’ for Item 13. In terms of the pairwise comparisons, Nodes B vs. D
showed DIF across all items except for Item 9. In contrast, Nodes A vs. C revealed only two DIF items,
specifically Items 6 and 13.
The ∆𝑅 2 values of the logistic ordinal regression models for 225 pairwise comparisons were
examined to identify the effect size of the DIF. The values ranged between 0 and .028, which can be
considered negligible according to the criteria of Jodoin and Gierl (2001). Consequently, although almost
50% of pairwise comparisons identified as DIF across all CT items, individual effect sizes were all
negligible.

3.3. Explanations of DIF types and patterns
In Table 2, nine types of DIF were defined based on the characteristics of the terminal nodes and
their pairwise comparisons. Type 1 denotes gender DIF within low-ESCS native-born students, identified
by comparing Nodes A vs. B. Type 2 represents immigrant DIF within low-ESCS students, detected by
comparing native-born and immigrant background students with relatively low ESCS level (Nodes A vs.
C for female natives and non-natives; Nodes B vs. C for male natives and non-natives). Type 3 is defined
as ESCS DIF within native-born female students and is identified by comparing Nodes A vs. D. Type 4
reflects differences associated with both gender and ESCS for native-born students, observed when
comparing groups that vary on both factors simultaneously (Nodes A vs. E and B vs. D). Type 5 denotes
immigrant and ESCS DIF, identified by two sets of comparisons: Nodes F vs. A and B, and Nodes C vs. D
and E.
Types 6 and 7 represent ESCS DIF within subgroups classified by immigrant background. Type 6
refers to ESCS DIF for native-born male students and is identified by comparing Nodes B vs. E. In
contrast, Type 7 captures ESCS DIF for non-native students, identified by comparing Nodes C vs. F. Type
8 is conceptually similar to Type 1 (i.e., gender DIF within low-ESCS native-born students) but applied to
a different ESCS level; specifically, it represents gender DIF within high-ESCS native-born students,
detected through the comparison Nodes D vs. E. Finally, Type 9 indicates immigrant DIF within highESCS students, identified by two comparisons of Nodes F vs. D and E.
Figure 2 summarizes the distribution of these DIF types across items. Each row and column
represent an item and a DIF type, respectively, with green-shaded cells indicating the presence of DIF.
Type 4 (Gender and ESCS DIF within native-born students) and Type 5 (ESCS and Immigrant DIF) were
appeared across all items, whereas Type 2 (Immigrant DIF within low-ESCS students) was the least
frequent, appearing in four items. From an item-level perspective, all items exhibited multiple DIF types.
Particularly, Item 13 was flagged across all DIF types. Item 3 exhibited DIF for eight types, whereas
Items 8, 9, 11 and 12 each exhibited four DIF types. Meanwhile, Items 5, 7, 10, 13, 14, and 15 showed

DIF across all ESCS-related types (i.e., Types 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7). This indicates that these items functioned
differently by ESCS level regardless of gender and immigrant background. All gender-related DIF types
(i.e., Types 1, 4, and 8) were observed in Items 3, 6 and 13. Likewise, Items 3, 11, and 13 were flagged as
DIF for all types related to immigrant background (i.e., Types 2, 5, and 9).
When comparing gender-related DIF across different ESCS levels within native-born students,

4, 8, and 14 exhibited immigrant DIF only at the high ESCS level (i.e., Type 9), while Item 6 exhibited
immigrant DIF only at the low-ESCS level (i.e., Type 2).

4. Discussion
This study investigated DIF in PISA 2022 CT items using the PCtree model, a tree-based
approach that enables exploratory detection of DIF across multiple demographic variables and their
interactions. Results showed the presence of DIF in PISA 2022 CT items, with all 15 items exhibiting DIF
across six subgroups. It should be noted that these findings are based on 15 items from a single form
(Form 37) and do not encompass the full PISA 2022 CT item pool. Although the effect sizes of individual
DIF items were negligible, the tree structure and the demographic characteristics of the subgroups provide
important insights for further discussion. We further look at how the results inform the design and practice
of PISA CT assessment.

4.1. Hierarchy of demographic factors contributing to DIF
The results from the PCtree model revealed a clear hierarchy of factors underlying DIF in the
PISA 2022 CT assessment. The variable used for the initial data split represents the most influential
factor contributing to DIF, with variables in the following splits indicating gradually decreasing
influence (Komboz et al., 2018). ESCS was selected as the initial partitioning variable, indicating that

20

the distinction between students from families with relatively high and low ESCS emerged as the most
fundamental driver of DIF. Not surprisingly, SES is known to be related to creativity, as suggested by a
meta-analysis synthesizing 117 studies (Acar et al., 2023). The results of the present study suggest that
creativity gaps by SES may not reflect differences in creative potential or capability found in the
previous studies, but may be partly attributable to bias in the measurement of creativity. However, it
should be noted that DIF by ESCS was identified for creative thinking in the PISA 2022 assessment,
whereas the meta-analysis accounted for various measures of creativity, including creative thinking,
creative activities and behaviors, and self-perceptions. Thus, the potential bias observed in creative
thinking assessments should not be overgeneralized to other measures of creativity.
Notably, immigrant background emerged as the second most important factor in shaping DIF. This
finding is particularly noteworthy given that immigrant status has been relatively understudied, whereas
SES (Acar et al., 2023) and gender (Abdulla Alabbasi et al., 2025) have been well-established as
significant factors to creativity assessment as documented in recent meta-analyses. Multiple issues
entangled in immigrant background may affect DIF, although this factor remains understudied.
Falavarjani and Yeh (2018) suggested that disengaging from both home and host cultures was associated
with greater creative performance than assimilating to the host culture, whereas adhering to one’s
cultural background yielded the lowest level of creative performance. This finding implies that
immigrants may score higher on creativity tasks if they can effectively marginalize themselves from
both their home and host cultures while flexibly drawing on cultural references when needed. Put
differently, some immigrants’ stronger verbal (e.g., multilingual) and meta-cognitive skills may be
associated with cognitive flexibility and perspective switching that could enhance scores on creativity
tasks regardless of their true creativity.
On the other hand, immigrants’ language barriers and challenges in integrating into the host
culture may also contribute to this process. Not all bicultural individuals become more creative unless
they successfully blend two cultures (Benet-Martínez et al., 2002). Unfortunately, several factors may
hinder the internalization of two cultures and the navigation of this process. For example, individuals

21

may move to other countries in search of better occupational, economic, or educational opportunities
because they are dissatisfied with conditions in their home countries. As this navigation process can be
both physically and mentally exhausting (Ruiz-de-Velasco & Fix, 2000), immigrants may be unable to
fully allocate their cognitive and motivational resources to creative processes, which may result in lower
scores on creativity tasks. In this vein, de Vries et al. (2015) found that the relationship between
openness and creativity was positive among non-immigrants but negative among immigrants, suggesting
that greater openness to new experiences in the host culture may be accompanied by heightened
intolerance of uncertainty, which could diminish creativity. Putting these findings together, immigrant
background could function as either a risk or a resource for creativity scores, regardless of students’ true
level of creativity. The present study highlights the intricate role of immigrant background in DIF in
creative thinking, a factor that might have been overlooked without the use of the PCtree.

4.2. Complex intersectional patterns of DIF in creative thinking assessment
One of the strengths of PCtree is its ability to reveal complex intersectionality across multiple
demographic variables in DIF patterns, while traditional DIF methods detect DIF by a priori defined
groups (e.g., gender or race). Several studies have proposed examining multiple demographic variables
simultaneously rather than testing the variables individually, one at a time for better detection and
interpretation of DIF in large-scale assessments (Bauer et al., 2020; Oliveri et al., 2013; Russell et al.,
2021). Item 4, entitled Space Comic, exemplifies this intersectional nature of DIF in the PISA 2022 CT
assessment. In this task, students are asked to provide three different titles for a comic strip describing a
dialogue between the Sun and the Earth, as the item was designed to assess creative thinking in the
domain of Written Expression (OECD, 2024a). This item relies heavily on verbal skills, requiring
students to comprehend a dyadic dialogue and generate witty and concise titles that capture the context of
the exchange. Because the item draws on verbal abilities beyond creative thinking itself, demographic
groups that differ in verbal processing may respond differently to this item, independent of their actual
creative potential.

22

Based on the nine DIF types described above, Item 4 exhibited Type 8 (Gender DIF within highESCS native-born students), but not Type 1 (Gender DIF within low-ESCS native-born students).
Similarly, Item 4 exhibited Type 9 (Immigrant DIF within high-ESCS students), but not Type 2
(Immigrant DIF within low-ESCS students). This result indicates that the DIF by gender and immigrants
appeared only in the high-ESCS group while no DIF was found by gender and immigrants in the lowESCS group. One possible explanation for this DIF pattern is that in many low-SES contexts, students
face shared constraints: they may have limited access to enrichment, fewer opportunities to practice openended creative production, and be less familiar with comics or visual storytelling. If so, the gender or
immigrant differences in verbal creativity tasks could be muted, potentially reflecting floor effects.
This interpretation is supported by prior research on verbal processing differences. Females tend
to demonstrate greater proficiency in language-related cognitive strategies and processing and to show
stronger engagement of language and social cognition networks than males, which may enhance verbal
fluency and linguistic abilities and, in turn, translate into higher scores on verbal tasks (Abraham, 2016).
Consistent with this interpretation, two recent meta-analyses (Abdulla Alabbasi et al., 2025; Taylor et al.,
2024) repeatedly confirmed that females significantly outperformed males on verbal creativity tasks,
whereas gender differences were mixed on figural tasks: Gender differences in figural divergent thinking
(DT) tasks were significant in Abdulla Alabbasi et al. (2025) but not in Taylor et al. (2024), where
broader measures of creativity—including products, activities, and achievements—were considered. In
addition, immigrant students often face challenges related to basic language and literacy skills, as well as
educational gaps that can further undermine academic performance (Ruiz-de-Velasco & Fix, 2000).
These group differences in verbal processing may explain why DIF emerged on this creativity item
primarily relying on verbal tasks.
This finding is particularly important given that no interaction effect between gender and SES was
found in Goecke et al.’s (2026) study synthesizing analyses of the total scores across 62 countries in the
PISA 2022 creative thinking assessment. Item-level DIFs can cancel out or average out in total scores,
thereby masking potential bias at the aggregate level. This implies that subtle intersectional effects may

23

systematically advantage or disadvantage certain subgroups on specific items, and such nuances can be
easily overlooked in aggregate-level analyses unless individual items are examined for DIF, as in the
present study. Thus, differences attributable to a significant three-way interaction in DIF among gender,
ESCS, and immigrant status may not be captured without item-level DIF analysis. More importantly, we
should note that we did not directly examine gaps in access to enrichment opportunities and verbal skills
by gender and immigrant status as potential sources of DIF. Instead, we suggested these as possible
sources based on a thorough examination of the content and cognitive skills required to process the
items exhibiting DIF. These findings call for further research to investigate which specific characteristics
of certain groups may trigger such subtle intersectional effects.
In a nutshell, this finding suggests that demographic backgrounds may impact how students
respond to CT not only individually but also jointly, regardless of their true level of creative thinking. As
Russell et al. (2021) noted, examining DIF separately for each demographic characteristic may mask
important interactions among characteristics. These findings highlight that DIF can be sourced by
multiple intersecting layers of demographic characteristics, emphasizing the need for intersectional
approaches like the PCtree to unveil hidden biases in the PISA 2022 CT assessment.
4.3. Negligible effect size of DIF
According to the results of the logistic regression models applied to evaluating DIF across
subgroups, DIF was determined for all items. Nevertheless, the effect sizes were negligible. However,
these effect sizes were estimated at the item level and were based on pairwise comparisons. Accordingly,
although individual DIF effects were negligible, they may be amplified when cumulated at the item
bundle level (e.g., creative thinking facet), called differential bundle functioning (DBF) (Gierl et al.,
2001; Nandakumar, 1993).
Furthermore, the presence of multiple DIF types across items suggests the potential for DIF
accumulation, which may result in substantial differential test functioning (DTF) at the assessment level
(Chalmers et al., 2015; Pae & Park, 2006). As described above, the results indicate that intersectional DIF,

24

involving multiple demographic covariates, was observed across all items. For instance, Item 13 showed
DIF across all types, and 11 out of 15 items exhibited more than five DIF types. As a result, small and
individually negligible DIF effects do not necessarily imply DBF or DTF, but when such effects
accumulate systematically across items or demographic intersections, they may produce substantial DTF.

5. Limitations and future research directions
While the PCtree approach successfully identified complex intersectional DIF items, it comes with
a few limitations. The intersectional patterns of DIF do not necessarily translate into systematic
advantages or disadvantages for specific subgroups, as would typically be expected in traditional DIF
analyses. Indeed, the nine DIF types identified in this study are difficult to interpret intuitively with
respect to which items advantage specific groups. What matters, however, is that the PCtree model
enables data-driven exploration of these complex subgroup structures without requiring prior theoretical
assumptions.
It should be noted that the sample sizes across immigrant background subgroups are imbalanced.
Native-born students comprised approximately 86% of the sample, while students with immigrant
backgrounds represented only 14%. This imbalance reflects the natural demographic composition of the
populations rather than a sampling artifact—an inherent limitation when using secondary data from largescale assessments. Although group proportions could be balanced through sampling adjustments, such
artificial manipulation may introduce its own biases. Moreover, when overall sample sizes are sufficiently
large, extreme group imbalances do not necessarily decrease statistical power for DIF detection (Paek &
Guo, 2011). Nevertheless, because PCtree is a relatively recent method, future research should examine
its robustness under varying degrees of group size imbalance.
Additionally, this study analyzed 15 items from a single form (Form 37) selected among the 32
forms used in PISA 2022 CT assessment. While this methodological choice was necessary to control for
systematic missingness and item-order effects resulting from the assessment design, examining DIF
patterns across different forms would provide evidence regarding the generalizability of DIF findings

25

across the full CT item pool, which is complementary to the present results. Such analyses are essential to
determine whether the DIF patterns identified in this study are specific to Form 37 or reflect broader
systematic issues across the assessment.
Finally, it is worth noting that the DIF observed in this study may reflect not only differences
associated with students' demographic characteristics but also systematic rater effects. Evaluating
originality in creative responses is inherently more challenging than assessing fluency-based measures,
and rater judgments may be influenced by raters’ characteristics such as personality, professional
background, or severity (Ceh et al., 2022; Hung et al., 2012). Future research could explore the extent to
which rater variability constitutes an additional source of DIF in PISA creative thinking assessments.

6. Conclusion
We examined DIF in the PISA 2022 CT assessment using the PCtree model and found that all 15
items exhibited DIF across multiple demographic factors. However, individual effect sizes were
negligible at the item level. Nevertheless, the fact that DIF was systematically detected across all 15 items
warrants attention, as cumulative effects may result in substantial DBF or DTF at the assessment level.
Given that this study analyzed only 15 items from a single form (Form 37), the extent to which these
patterns generalize to the full PISA 2022 CT item pool remains unanswered.
Notably, the DIF patterns were characterized by complex intersections, with ESCS as the primary
partitioning variable, followed by immigrant background and gender. Given these results, researchers
should recognize that DIF can manifest through complex intersectional patterns rather than single group
differences. This opens important avenues for future research on the fairness of creative thinking
assessments, particularly in examining intersectional DIF patterns across different PISA 2022 CT items
and countries. For further development and refinement of PISA 2022 CT assessment, it is essential to
consider multiple demographic characteristics and their interactions throughout item task design and
validation. Assessment developers could benefit from employing exploratory DIF detection to identify
complex bias patterns that conventional group comparisons may overlook.
