A B S T R A C T  
The aim of this study was to examine how achievement varied within and between schools at different grade 
levels, and long-term trends in variation within and across multiple countries. We used science achievement data 
from five cycles of Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) from 2003 to 2019 involving 
10 countries from Asia, Europe, and the United States. Employing exploratory data mining methods of variance 
decomposition, correlation analysis, and Gaussian mixture modeling of data distributions, we found the 
following: First, between-school variances generally remained consistent across two decades, suggesting that 
inequality between schools has not increased over time. Second, between-school variances were relatively small 
for elementary grade level but increased at secondary grade level, though marginally even for countries with 
early tracking. Third, higher-achieving schools tended to have more equal student achievement levels than 
lower-achieving schools, lending within-country support for the “virtuous” efficiency-equality trade-off. We 
further found that reduced equality within lower-achieving schools was associated with bimodality in 
achievement distribution. Overall, there is little evidence of inequality across schools changing over time. 
However, there may be evidence of increased inequalities associated with student subpopulations, particularly 
within lower-achieving schools, with implications on classroom instruction and school cohesion.     
1. Introduction 
There is recent renewed interest in analyzing the temporal trends in 
educational inequalities (Chmielewski, 2019; OECD, 2018). This inter­
est coincides with countries increasingly striving not just for higher 
overall achievement outcomes but to also fulfil certain socio-political 
goals such as equality and equity (Damme et al., 2017). Educational 
achievement gaps can be detrimental to social mobility, particularly 
across generation, thus, understanding and remediating these gaps is a 
laudable goal of an educational system (OECD, 2018). 
Numerous past studies have improved our understanding of educa­
tional inequality. However, the dominant literature has tended to focus 
on what has been termed opportunity inequality. These studies have 
tended to focus on socioeconomic factors in explaining achievement 
gaps. However, a comprehensive quantitative description of overall 
levels of achievement inequality regardless of its source is needed. Past 
studies have also utilized relatively short time-periods, preventing a 
more complete picture of trends in educational inequality. Importantly, 
studies have lacked emphasis on decomposing inequalities between and 
within schools, and how these have changed across grade levels. Schools 
are important units of any educational system as they can delimit stu­
dent learning and achievement outcomes. Finally, there is controversy 
over whether achievement and equality goals are in opposition. Some 
theoretical models and empirical data argue that educational systems 
that are efficient in terms of producing high quality average outcomes 
can only do so while sacrificing equality (Gamoran & Mare, 1989; Okun, 
2015). These important gaps and controversies necessitate a compre­
hensive description of achievement inequality. 
In the present study, we aim to analyze achievement data from 
countries that participated in the Trends in International Mathematics 
and Science Study (TIMSS) from 2003 to 2019. We focus on the extent

2. Literature review 
2.1. Educational inequality 
Ferreira and Gignoux (2014) conceptualized educational inequalities 
in two potentially distinct ways. One, termed achievement inequality 
relates to simple dispersion (variance) of achievement scores obtained 
via, for example, assessment or testing. This measures how different 
students are in learning as measured by their achievement. Second, 
inequality in educational opportunity is a measure of how much of the 
differences in achievement can be explained by background, de­
mographic or other characteristics beyond the student’s control. This 
second aspect of educational inequality has been examined quite 
extensively, with a focus on the role of socioeconomic status (SES). 
These distinct notions of educational inequalities somewhat agree with 
other theoretical frameworks such as by Brighouse et al. (2018). For 
example, Brighouse et al. (2018) in their book on educational goods, 
conceptualized inequality as involving inequality in achievement and 
inequality in distribution, with the two being orthogonal in principle: 
knowing that a group of students are achieving at very different levels 
does not necessarily tell us about any unequal distribution of education 
(p. 54) (e.g., were the least achieving given the least amount of 
resources?). 
Many studies on educational inequality leverage international large- 
scale assessments such as TIMSS and the Program for International 
Student Assessment (PISA) for cross-country comparisons. While inter­
national large-scale assessments do have their drawbacks (Johansson, 
2020), they are still valuable in providing high quality data given their 
representative sampling, extensive cross-country coverage and impor­
tantly, long-term data for trend analysis. In the most recent compre­
hensive analysis across five decades using international large-scale 
assessments, inequality in educational opportunity explained by SES has 
increased globally, suggesting a rise in opportunity inequality in edu­
cation (Chmielewski, 2019). And this SES achievement gap may be 
related to various factors such as teacher sorting, social segregation, and 
school-tracking (Burger, 2019; Strello et al., 2022; reviewed in Strietholt 
et al., 2019). SES is further argued to influence variation in educational 
achievement via differences in opportunities to learn and master more 
challenging curricula among classes and schools with higher SES (Rolfe 
et al., 2021; Schmidt et al., 2015). The results from large-scale assess­
ment are consistent with prior smaller-scale, country-specific studies 
such as in the United States, Korea, and Malaysia (Byun & Kim, 2010; 
Hanushek et al., 2019; Saw, 2016). 
However, past studies have not precisely examined the role of 
schools related to the first component of educational inequality, that of 
achievement inequality. Schools are argued to play major roles in 
affecting learning experiences and outcomes via school climate (Ber­
kowitz et al., 2017), narrowing of student subject choices (Anders et al., 
2018) and peer comparisons (Marsh, 1987), among numerous other 
effects. Schools and classrooms also delimit opportunities to learn which 
is argued to then determine educational outcomes and hence achieve­
ment inequality. Thus, there is a need to examine the role of schools in 
affecting inequality across longer time periods for a more comprehen­
sive picture of trends in achievement inequality. However, individual 
studies have either identified the source of inequality at a single 
timepoint or analyzed total inequality across multiple timepoints 
without clarifying the role of schools. For the former, an analysis of PISA 
2015 data by Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development 
(OECD) found that the school SES played a larger role in explaining 
variance in student achievement than individual student SES (OECD, 
2018). This result is further supported by analyses showing differences 
in achievement within- and between-schools having declined only 
marginally across many countries when student SES was controlled for 
(Marks, 2006). It suggests that while student SES is an important factor 
in explaining achievement inequality by some measures, other factors 
such as student ability and school characteristics may be equally

between-school factors were found to be more important than

Comprehensive recent studies have found that early school tracking

(Friedman et al., 2020), these prior studies all lacked a trend analysis 
that can provide a more comprehensive picture of where achievement 
equality is headed. Friedman et al. (2020) found that over a span of five 
decades, within-country achievement inequality rose globally, though 
with some heterogeneity across broad regions. Decomposing the 
achievement inequality to between- and within-schools, however, was 
missing, preventing conclusions regarding whether schools play exac­
erbating role in explaining achievement differences. 
2.2. Efficiency-equality relationship: vicious or virtuous? 
Originally formulated in economics, it has been argued that there is a 
trade-off between efficiency and equality: a society that wants to 
maximize overall efficiency, i.e., higher average outputs given a set of 
inputs, must settle for lower equality outcomes, i.e., larger differences in 
income distributions (Okun, 2015). When cast in educational terms, the 
argument is that an educational system can create the highest overall 
average achievement (i.e., greater overall efficiency) if schools are 
highly differentiated in terms of types and levels, and students are sorted 
based on their abilities, skills, and talents, i.e., larger differences in 
achievement outcomes among individuals. For example, higher ability 
students can maximize their learning progress when they are not held 
back by the content and progress of an otherwise undifferentiated type 
of schooling involving lower-ability students (Van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 
2010). This vicious efficiency-equality relationship had some early 
empirical support from national-level school-tracking data in the United 
States (Gamoran & Mare, 1989) with more modest support in some 
countries recently (Tsai et al., 2017). However, cross-country analysis 
has suggested the opposite relationship: that is, a virtuous 
efficiency-equality relationship in which countries with higher average 
achievement tended to have lower levels of achievement inequalities 
(narrower distribution of achievement) (Freeman et al., 2010). This 
cross-country correlation was further confirmed by within-country 
analysis by examining trends in achievement and inequality within a 
country, an analysis that can overcome difficulties of cross-country 
comparisons (e.g., latent cultural confounds) (Parker et al., 2018). 
While other results support such virtuous efficiency-equality relation­
ship (reviewed in Van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 2010), a few questions 
remain, such as the nature and extent of the relationship between 
achievement efficiency and equality, effects of grade levels and how 
they have changed over longer time periods, if at all.

3. Methods 
3.1. Participants 
We used achievement data for general science from five cycles of 
TIMSS (2003, 2007, 2011, 2015, 2019) for Grade 4 (elementary grade 
level) and Grade 8 (secondary grade level). Marshall et al. (2022) 
recently remarked that there is a lack of studies on extent and nature of 
inequalities in science at elementary levels, particularly in the United 
States. Though they advocated a political lens in understanding science 
inequality discourse, we believe our study adds to the discussion via a 
quantitative lens. Moreover, not all of the educational systems tested 
students in specific science subjects. Thus, to make our results compa­
rable, we focused on general science scores at both Grades 4 and 8. 
Given that our major objectives were to examine long-term trends, we 
identified 10 countries across 3 regions that had complete data for all 
five cycles across the two grade levels. First, for Asia, we analyzed data 
from Singapore, Hong Kong SAR, Chinese Taipei and Japan. These 
countries generally have high-performing educational systems that are 
frequently compared in studies using international large-scale assess­
ments (Ow-Yeong, Yeter, & Ali, 2023). For Europe, we examined data 
from the Russian Federation, England, Hungary, Italy and Norway. 
Finally, data from the United States was included. Because of the small 
number of countries used in our analyses (limited to only those with 
complete data for both grades across 16 years), we do not make any 
claims about these countries representing broader geographical regions. 
For early-tracking, we relied on the most recent comprehensive study by 
Strello et al. (2021). They combined data from multiple sources to 
categorize systems into early tracking or non-early tracking up to TIMSS 
Grade 8 (approximately 14 years of age in the countries analyzed). 
Singapore and Hungary were categorized as early tracking systems 
whereas all other countries were categorized as non-early tracking. 
3.2. Data analysis 
Because we were interested in comprehensively describing the 
achievement inequality, we adopted an exploratory data mining 
approach, the contemporary version of exploratory data analysis pro­
pounded by Tukey (Tukey, 1980) and others (De Bie & Spyropoulou, E, 
2013). We did not lay out specific hypotheses to test via traditional null 
hypothesis significance-testing (NHST) but instead relied on broad 
inquiries, visualizations, and semi-quantitative patterns. We, thus, avoid 
the use of the term “statistically significant” when describing the results 
lest it be mis-interpreted as involving NHST. The main variable of in­
terest was science achievement as measured by scale scores in Grade 4 
and Grade 8 general science. The achievement scale scores were 
designed to have a range of 0 to 1000, with mean of 500 and standard 
deviation (SD) of 100. While having similar range, means, and standard 
deviations, these scores were computed from different test items un­
derlying the scores (i.e., Grade 4 booklets were different from Grade 8 
booklets) and absolute scores are not strictly comparable across grades. 
However, TIMSS has been designed to allow comparability across cycles 
within a subject and grade given consistency of the sampling strategy, 
assessment framework, content tested, item pools, and scale score con­
struction. IEA, the lead organization for TIMSS, does extensively use 
trend analysis across cycles, pointing out in 2019 that “trends are re­
ported across the seven TIMSS assessment cycles for countries that have 
comparable data from previous TIMSS assessments” (Mullis et al., 2020, 
p. 3). All 10 countries in our study have been reported by IEA to have 
comparable data across the 5 cycles we examined. Nonetheless, despite 
the strength of the TIMSS design, scores and their distribution may 
become incomparable due to potentially unmeasured/unknown changes 
and we have recognized this as a limitation. Each student was assigned 
just one test booklet out of numerous potential booklets given the dif­
ficulties of testing exhaustively from all content domains. From this one 
booklet, item response theory (IRT) scaling and latent regression were 
used to estimate multiple scores for each student, called plausible values 
(PVs). Five PVs were provided to represent the uncertainty in estimating 
a student’s true (latent) proficiency in science (von Davier, 2020). Here, 
following previous recommended procedures, we performed five inde­
pendent computation, one for each PV, before averaging the final rele­
vant statistic to obtain a more rigorous estimate (Braun & von Davier, 
2017). In addition, to ensure a more nationally representative statistic, 
we used total student weight. All computations of scores were done in R 
programming language using intsvy (version 2.5), a package for pro­
cessing and analysis of large-scale assessment data (Caro & Biecek, 
2017). 
There are different metrics of achievement inequality. We followed a 
previous study that argued for the use of variance, particularly, its 
square root, termed standard deviation (SD) (Ferreira & Gignoux, 2014). 
This is due to its desirable properties in respecting important axioms 
related to inequality, its familiarity in educational research as well as its 
linear decomposability. We filtered away schools with fewer than 10 
students tested, leaving us with a total of 7474 schools from Grade 4 and 
7567 schools from Grade 8 with adequate sample sizes across the five 
TIMSS cycles. To obtain the average scale score for each school, we used 
the instvy.mean.pv function which first generated the weighted mean 
for each of the five PVs before taking the average of the five means for 
each school. The between-school variance was obtained using the 
method proposed by Foy (2004) for PIRLS which is an international 
assessment using a sampling design similar to TIMSS (Joncas & Foy, 
2011). The within-school variance was calculated by subtracting the 
between-school variance from the weighted total variance. We verified 
our decomposition strategy (though for mathematics scores) in one of 
the cycles to be identical to a previous study except for United States 
(Huang, 2009). This discrepancy was due to the school exclusion 
criteria. Furthermore, almost all (over 95%) of the schools across all 
countries and cycles sampled just one or two classes per school. Thus, 
further within-school decomposition was either not possible with just 
one class sampled or likely to be unreliable given just two classes being 
sampled. Thus, we focused on variance between and within schools with 
the caveat that the origins of the variance within the school are unclear. 
Nonetheless, in the Supplementary Material, we performed supple­
mentary analysis of the major results using classes as the unit of analysis 
(instead of schools) to check whether within-school results applied to 
within classes as well. Moreover, by potentially undersampling classes 
that may vary systematically (e.g., within-school tracking), TIMSS

To compare the contribution of different sources of inequality, we 
took the square root of variance to obtain SD, then computed a simple 
ratio of between-school SD and within-school SD, which gives an 
interpretable indication of the source of inequality. A value higher than 
one indicates larger differences between schools than within schools 
while a value below one indicates the opposite. Variance or SD-related 
measures, while argued to be desirable in certain ways, may not cap­
ture subtleties in the data given its dependence on normal distributions. 
Thus, in the Supplementary Material, we computed a widely-used 
alternative measure of inequality based on entropy. To better under­
stand within-school inequality, we generated scatterplots of within- 
school SD against within-school average and further quantified their 
correlation using Pearson’s r. In the Supplementary Material, we per­
formed extensive analysis to rule out estimation issues. 
In addition, we asked how school achievement as measured by 
within-school average related to distributional measures of within- 
school inequality. After ruling out a single-mean explanation in the 
Supplementary Material, in our main analysis, we used Gaussian 
mixture modeling to fit mixtures of different numbers of Gaussians to the 
within-school distribution of achievement scores. This analysis explored 
whether lower school achievement was associated with a mix of sub­
populations with different central tendencies rather than merely skewed 
tails from a single mean. Gaussian mixture modeling involved individ­
ually fitting different numbers of Gaussians (up to four) for each of the 
PVs, finding the best number of Gaussian mixtures, then averaging 
number of Gaussians across the five PVs for each school. The data dis­
tribution modeling was done using the mclustModel function in mclust 
package (version 5.4.7) in R. All scatterplots and line plots were 
generated using the ggplot2 and jtools (Long, 2018) packages in R. 
4. Results 
A total of 221,421 students from 7474 schools at Grade 4 and 
224,062 students from 7567 schools at Grade 8 across 10 countries were 
analyzed from 2003 to 2019. The breakdown of sample sizes of students, 
classes, and schools for each country and year is available in Supple­
mentary Table 1. 
4.1. RO1: Determining the patterns of between-school and within-school 
achievement inequality 
RQ1a, What are the patterns of between-school and within-school 
achievement inequality for a country across five cycles? Fig. 1a 
decomposed achievement inequality as measured by SD to between- 
school and within-school for Grade 4 general science. Japan generally 
had the smallest between-school inequality. There were a few countries 
with some slight trends across cycles in between-school inequality: 
decreasing for Singapore, Russian Federation, and Italy; increasing for 
Hong Kong SAR, with little change for the rest. For within-school 
inequality, there may be a slight downward trend overall with the 
exception of Hong Kong SAR (increasing), suggesting some slight 
equalizing in achievement within schools. For Grade 8, generally, 
Singapore and Japan had the largest and smallest between-school 
inequality respectively. However, there was no clear overall trend in 
between-school inequality with some minor changes in a few countries. 
For within-school inequality, there may be a trend of increase in 2019 
compared to 2015 (Fig. 1b). 
RQ1b, Are schools becoming more unequal over time as indicated 
by increasingly larger between-school achievement inequality? 
Addressing RQ1b, schools across many countries at different grade 
levels do not appear to show a visual trend of becoming more unequal in 
achievement in absolute terms as indicated by the patterns of between- 
school inequality (Figs. 1a, 1b) (see also below for relative terms). 
RQ1c, How do the patterns of achievement inequality compare 
across different countries and across elementary and secondary grade 
levels? Related to RQ1c, the biggest pattern is the much larger within- 
school inequality relative to between-school inequality (Fig. 1). To 
quantify this further, we computed a ratio of between-school to within- 
school inequality, Between:Within ratio using SD (Fig. 2). A ratio higher 
than one would indicate larger differences between schools than within 
schools while a value below one would indicate the opposite. Between: 
Within ratio was much smaller than 1 for all countries at Grade 4 and the 
majority of countries for Grade 8 with Hong Kong SAR and England 
occasionally showing larger between-school inequality than within- 
school. Between:Within ratios for the rest of the countries stayed 
largely flat overall with some minor exceptions (slight downward trends 
for Russian Federation at Grade 4 and same for Italy and Chinese Taipei 
at Grade 8). Together with the results above, the data suggested that 
student inequality within schools is more substantial than inequality 
across schools in both absolute (results above) and relative terms (Be­
tween:Within ratios) and that this pattern has not changed much over 
almost two decades.

RQ2b, What is the statistical nature of the within-school achieve­
ment inequality? We considered different statistical mechanisms 
involving within-school average. One, as proposed by Van de Werfhorst 
and Mijs (2010), is a ceiling effect where the scale scores cannot prac­
tically increase much higher, so schools with high averages are made up 
of higher-achieving students who all are “bunched up” with narrowed 
distributions. However, as can be seen in Fig. 4, even if the analysis is 
limited to the middle distribution where scores can definitely go much 
higher, this negative relationship still holds. Another possibility is that 
lower-achieving schools have extreme lower tails of poorly-performing 
students. Extreme skewness can influence SD measures as confirmed 
in the data in the form of a negative correlation between SD and 
skewness. However, we did not detect any systematic relationship be­
tween within-school skewness and within-school average, with most 
countries hovering around zero Correlation (Supplementary Figure 3). 
This result suggested that lower-achieving schools were not necessarily 
more skewed with longer tails of lower-achieving students. 
To gain further insights, we examined actual distribution of scores 
within-school. We observed that lower-achieving schools seemed to 
display multiple peaks, suggesting bimodality in which one central 
tendency was higher in frequency and value but there was a subpopu­
lation of lower- achieving students forming a second peak lower in 
frequency and value (Fig. 6). To analyze this bimodality more closely, 
we used mixtures of different numbers of Gaussians fitted to the within- 
school distribution of scores. The optimal within-school number of 
Gaussian was then correlated with its within-school average. As shown 
in Fig. 7 for almost all countries in all years, there was an overall 
negative correlation: lower-achieving schools tended to have multiple 
Gaussian distributions compared to higher-achieving schools that were 
more likely to have just one Gaussian distribution. This effect was 
especially clear for Grade 4 but less established for Grade 8 (Fig. 7). 
Examination of the data distribution similar to Fig. 6 suggested that 
these lower- achieving schools had one Gaussian with a higher average 
with at least one other. Gaussian with lower average, indicating pres­
ence of student subpopulations within the same school. Finally, to 
examine trends over time, Fig. 8 plotted the number of Gaussians for all 
countries in all years. For Grade 4, there was no clear trend 2015 and 
prior, but in 2019, there did seem to be a systematic increase in the 
number of Gaussians within a school across all countries (except for 
Singapore). For Grade 8, there was no clear trend. These within-school 
results in Figs. 7 and 8 were generally similarly observed in within- 
class analyses (Supplementary Figures 4 and 5). Overall, there is an 
increased tendency for increased within-school inequality as shown by 
the presence of student subpopulations in recent years, particularly in 
Grade 4. 
5. Discussion 
Using exploratory data mining of international large-scale assess­
ments, we found existence of substantial inequality within a country. 
There were considerable differences between countries in terms of 
within-school and between-school variation. Nonetheless, many of these 
patterns within a country, overall, were quite stable across time, 
particularly how inequality between schools has remained consistent 
across 5 cycles. We further found within-school inequality as a major 
contributor of total achievement inequality. We provided consistent 
evidence of the tendency for higher-achieving schools to be more equal 
in terms of achievement levels within-school than lower-achieving 
schools across almost two decades. Moreover, the latter schools are 
more likely to exhibit bimodality in achievement levels, indicating a 
persistent presence of student subpopulations in lower-achieving 
schools across both elementary and secondary grade levels, with evi­
dence of a rising trend for elementary schools. 
There is concern that educational inequality is increasing across the 
globe in spite of education-related sustainable development goals to 
reduce inequality (Friedman et al., 2020). Moreover, there is evidence 
that the proportion of this inequality is increasingly due to factors such 
as family SES that are unmodifiable by the students nor by schools 
(Chmielewski, 2019). In this study, we decomposed educational 
inequality to ask whether schools were driving total achievement

SES correlates to explain between-school differences. A previous study 
examined this issue and did not find a role of SES in explaining 
between-school differences (Marks, 2006). Thus, it remains to be seen 
whether the stable between-school inequality found here may be 
increasingly due to SES. If it is, then educational systems are evolving 
not by creating new amounts of between-school inequalities (as found in 
this study) but rather re-distributing the drivers of such inequalities. 
Our findings suggest that what happens within a school plays an 
important role in contributing to differences in student achievement. 
This applied even at Grade 8 to countries with early school tracking 
systems such as for Singapore and Hungary. This general finding is, in 
contrast, to conclusions from Van de Werfhorst & Mijs (2010), based on 
more limited data, showing that between-school inequality in 
achievement is relatively high in differentiated systems. What our re­
sults additionally indicate is that greater within-school variability in 
achievement is associated with lower overall achievement. This finding 
suggests that there may be inefficient tracking, that is, students in these 
systems are tracked to different schools depending on their prior 
achievement but the tracking may not be sufficiently strong (or evenly 
applied, see below) to homogenize subsequent achievement levels 
within a school, especially among schools with lower-achieving stu­
dents. Our study further found that a country’s average within-school 
inequality, as plotted in Figs. 1 and 2, can hide substantial variation. 
Within-school inequality varied quite substantially with heavy associa­
tion with school achievement (Figs. 4 and 5). Higher-achieving schools 
tended to have less achievement inequality compared to lower- 
achieving schools. This negative correlation between average achieve­

The findings above have some policy implications on inequality and 
school tracking. First, they suggest that national policies that promote 
more within-school equality in achievement can be associated with 
higher overall achievement. These policies include a focus on helping 
average or lower-performing students which may in turn increase both 
average performance and reduce inequality. Quality and equality out­
comes can be pursued simultaneously. Secondly, our results question the 
effectiveness of tracking given the still large within-school inequality 
relative to between-school inequality even at Grade 8 for tracking sys­
tems (Figs. 2 and 3). In other words, despite the allocated resources and 
political costs (in some countries) of tracking and associated policies 
such as school choice and privatization, students are placed in schools 
may not necessarily homogenously improve student performance. 
Parker et al. (2018) argued that this phenomenon is due to highly 
error-prone student placement that is driven by parents who may not 
make the best placement decisions. To the extent that tracking is still a 
policy means to an educational end, our results motivate the search for 
more efficient ways for policymakers and stakeholders (e.g., parents) to 
place students to take advantage of the perceived benefits of schools that 
can best match the students’ abilities. 
However, placing students in homogeneous environments may not 
necessarily be optimal for youth. Studies show that students in high 
achieving schools often experience pervasive pressures from their 
environment (e.g., parents, teachers, peers) to meet socially sanctioned 
academic and extracurricular expectations (Luthar et al., 2020) This 
pressure to keep up with their peers could explain the homogenization of 
students’ achievement outcomes in higher-achieving schools. However, 
while this homogenization is associated with higher average achieve­
ment levels, such homogenization of students via socialization pressures 
(i.e., pressures of “fitting in”) could have problematic implications for 
their social-emotional well-being (Crosnoe, 2011). High-achieving set­
tings have been consistently linked to increased adjustment problems 
among youth (Luthar, Kumar, & Zillmer, 2020; Tilley & Farver, 2023), 
with social comparison tendencies found to play a role in these problem 
behaviors. Thus, patterns of homogenization within a school may bring 
other challenges for educators and administrators. Nonetheless, it re­
mains to be seen whether it is homogenization that produces high 
achievement or vice-versa and if high achieving settings have spill-over 
homogenization effects on social-emotional functioning. Future studies 
can focus on the direction and implications of such patterns of homog­
enization for overall youth development. 
A new finding of this study is evidence of a pervasive bimodality in 
achievement within lower-achieving schools. Higher levels of inequality 
in lower-achieving schools seemed driven by the presence of distinct 
modes, i.e., Gaussian peaks (Fig. 8), likely representing different student 
subpopulations. The presence of such bimodal peaks in lower-achieving 
schools at Grade 4 may be indicative of pre-existing differences when 
children enroll in elementary schools (e.g., pre-school exposure). 
Schools with more differentiated student populations may be less 
effective at closing learning gaps among students, resulting in lower 
overall school achievement than schools where students have more 
homogeneous backgrounds. It is also possible that lower-achieving 
schools have certain school-based characteristics that make it difficult 
for them to overcome pre-existing heterogeneity by Grade 4, or that 
exacerbate these differences whereas higher-achieving schools may 
have certain institutional characteristics that enable them to equally 
improve student learning and achievement. For Grade 8, additional 
mechanisms via between-school tracking may be at play. Current 
educational systems may be better at identifying students who have 
higher achievement scores and to sort them into higher-performing 
schools; this grouping of high-ability/high-achieving students has the 
effect of uniformly increasing overall school achievement levels. How­
ever, existing tracking systems may be less effective at sorting students 
who have average or lower achievement, leading to these schools having 
distinct subpopulations that persist at secondary level. Additional 
research, preferably involving longitudinal data and specific school 
characteristics, will be needed to determine the nature and direction of 
the mediating mechanisms. 
Regardless of mechanisms, future research on inequality may need to 
examine data distributions more closely to distinguish between simple 
models of inequality via skewed tails versus bimodality in these lower 
achieving schools. These two models may have different educational 
implications. A simple model of long tails may require remediation 
classes or interventions for a small number of students. However, 
bimodality implies distinct subpopulations that require systematic 
changes to how instruction is designed within a class or across classes 
with different performance levels. The presence of subpopulations in 
schools may also threaten school climate and school cohesion, factors 
that are known to contribute to achievement outcomes (Thapa et al., 
2013). Our result of heterogeneity in achievement levels is congruent 
with recent findings of heterogeneity in school climate perceptions using 
different international large-scale assessment data (Rohatgi & Scherer,

The results have policy implications for teacher professional devel­
opment. First, lower-achieving schools not only achieve poorly on 
average (by definition) but further require teachers to manage very 
diverse student needs and abilities. One widely used approach is 
differentiated instruction within a classroom. However, differentiated 
instruction can be challenging to implement effectively (van Geel et al., 
2019). Our results suggest differentiated instruction may need to be 
prioritized and better supported via professional development targeted 
to these lower-achieving schools with large achievement variations. 
Secondly, pervasive achievement inequalities in these lower-achieving 
schools may exacerbate feelings of exclusion and other adverse 
social-emotional 
effects 
in 
students. 
Recently, 
a 
prominent 
social-emotional learning framework by the Collaborative for Academic, 
Social and Emotional Learning (CASEL) was extended to include 
addressing issues of inequalities via the lens of social justice (Jagers 
et al., 2019). Teacher professional development efforts were high­
lighted. One strategy analyzed was the move away from deficit views 
with more affirming views that focus on strengths. Thus, our findings 
support the importance of systemic policy efforts to train teachers from 
lower-achieving schools in identifying, managing, and confronting 
pervasive issues of within-school inequalities. 
Singapore is an interesting case study. While Singapore did exhibit 
above-average levels of within- and between-school inequalities, they 
were not clear outliers, especially in the latest 2019 cycle (Fig. 1). 
However, Singapore consistently has among the highest levels of 
bimodal achievement effects (large negative correlations in Fig. 7), 
meaning within-school achievement inequality is highly uneven with 
highest inequality among lower-achieving schools. This finding adds to 
the growing literature on issues of equality in Singapore schools (Ali, 
2016). Singapore is also known to generally have the highest science 
achievement score globally across many cycles. In sum, Singapore may 
be a case of how a system elevates to very high levels of overall 
achievement while maintaining reasonable amount of overall 
inequality. However, this overall inequality is highly unevenly redis­
tributed across schools, some schools are highly heterogenous in 
achievement while others are extremely homogenous. Whether this is an 
intentional feature or unintended outcome of the Singapore school 
system remains to be seen. 
There are multiple limitations of the study. First, the lack of more 
extensive sampling of classes within a school likely biases our variance 
estimates and prevents us from further decomposing variance within a 
school. For the latter, while we have performed extensive alternative 
analyses (repeating all analysis within a class or for just one randomly 
selected class), we are unable to conclusively attribute within-school 
inequality to differences between or within classes in a school. 
Furthermore, there may be issues of comparability across large time 
periods, thus, our conclusions need to be tempered by potential un­
known/unmeasured changes. Finally, future studies will need to expand 
the analysis to comprehensively uncover correlates of between- and 
within-school inequalities. Moreover, the findings will need to be 
replicated in other academic subjects such as mathematics. 
The current study contributes to the extant literature on inequality in 
student achievement by testing notions of increasing achievement 
inequality across schools, examining a key theoretical model related to 
the efficiency-equality trade-off in education, and quantifying the role 
that schools play in influencing achievement outcomes. We leveraged 
exploratory data mining methods to provide a first step toward identi­
fying patterns of inequality that can inform educational interventions, 
such as those that aid with the homogenization of learning outcomes. 
These policies and practices could ultimately raise overall achievement 
levels across countries in a more uniform manner to help optimize 
educational quality and equality.
