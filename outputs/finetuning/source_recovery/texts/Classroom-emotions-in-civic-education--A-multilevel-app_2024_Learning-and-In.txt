Background: Classroom emotions are major predictors of student learning and academic outcomes. Emotions
might be of particular significance in civic education, where oftentimes highly controversial and heated debates
take place.
Aims: We aimed to examine antecedents and effects of emotions in civic education through the lens of the
control-value theory. Specifically, we investigated the classroom climate during discussions of political and social
issues as an antecedent of students’ enjoyment, shame, anxiety, and boredom, in addition to a possible mediation
effect of these emotions on political knowledge and participation as core outcomes in this domain.
Sample: Participants were 1162 students from vocational schools (grades 10–13).
Methods: Multilevel structural equation modeling was used for the analysis.
Results: We found a positive relation between an open classroom climate and enjoyment and negative relations
with anxiety and boredom. No support was provided for the relation to shame. Enjoyment related positively, and
all negative emotions (shame, anxiety, boredom) negatively to achievement on the knowledge test. All activating
emotions (enjoyment, shame, anxiety) related positively to intended political participation. Furthermore,
enjoyment mediated the association between classroom climate and intended political participation at the student level.
Conclusions: This study strongly supports the theoretical assumptions of the control-value theory. Pedagogically,
the results imply that fostering a classroom context that is open to diverse opinions can prevent the experience of
negative emotions and increase students’ experience of enjoyment.

Based on assumptions of the control-value theory, the study’s aims
are twofold: First, with respect to the antecedents of emotions, we
analyzed whether and how an open classroom climate is related to
discrete emotions experienced in civic education. Second, concerning
the effects of emotions, we tested how discrete emotions are related to
(a) political knowledge and (b) intention to engage in political participation and whether there is an indirect effect between open classroom
climate and outcomes of civic education via these discrete emotions. We
focused on students of Austrian vocational schools who are taught civic
education in a single subject. In contrast to academic track schools,
vocational schools are attended by students part-time next to their
vocational training. Though students at vocational schools are not
representative of all adolescents at this age, they are specifically relevant
for civic education as they are typically less engaged and have less access
to civic education compared to students of other educational tracks
(Sampermans, Claes, & Janmaat, 2021; Schwarzer & Zeglovits, 2013).
Therefore, information about how emotions contribute to political
knowledge and participation in this target group is critical for political
equality. Further, knowledge about contextual antecedents of emotions
can reveal possibilities for civic education teachers to decrease negative
while enhancing positive emotions and further leverage the accomplishment of civic education goals in their classrooms.

emotions and affect as mediators between the classroom climate and

classroom climate (satisfaction with classroom climate, climate among

therefore, seems to be a relevant contextual antecedent of emotions
experienced in the classroom.
Similar to Hamre and Pianta’s (2001) general definition of a positive
classroom climate, open classroom climate, often also termed “open
classroom discussions”, refers to the social environment, specifically
when students discuss political and social issues in class. The openness
thereby describes the extent to which students are encouraged to bring
in political topics, express their own, and respect others’ opinions, and
discuss social and political issues from diverse viewpoints (Campbell,
2019). Throughout the literature on civic education, the significance of
an open classroom climate is the most consistent theme (Campbell,
2019), showing positive relations with civic knowledge (Campbell,
2008; Persson, 2015; Sampermans et al., 2021), political interest
(Kahne, Crow, & Lee, 2013), political efficacy (Manganelli, Lucidi, &
Alivernini, 2014), critical consciousness (Rapa, Bolding, & Jamil, 2022),
political participation (Martens & Gainous, 2013; Quintelier & Hooghe,
2013), and political trust (Dassonneville, Quintelier, Hooghe, & Claes,
2012). More generally, a systematic review by Geboers et al. (2013)
summarizes the broad evidence for the effectiveness of an open and
democratic classroom climate for students’ democratic development.
However, he noted many null findings regarding the relation between
pedagogical climate and civic knowledge, attitudes, and behavior.
1.1.2. Emotions and outcomes of civic education: participation and
knowledge
Further, the control-value theory includes assumptions about associations between emotions and academic outcomes (Pekrun, 2006;
Pekrun et al., 2023). Depending on the valence (e.g., positive versus
negative) and arousal (activating versus deactivating) of emotions,
emotions are thereby assumed to relate positively (e.g.,
positive-activating emotions like enjoyment) or negatively (e.g.,
negative-deactivating emotions like boredom) to learning and learning
outcomes. This is in line with recent research on civic education. For
example, while enjoyment and curiosity positively relate to students’
engagement, motivation, and knowledge, boredom has the opposite
association (Graf, Goetz, Bieleke, & Murano, 2023). Further, students’
feelings of fun about politics are associated with students’ initiation of
civic and political discussions in class (Bayram Özdemir, Stattin, &
Özdemir, 2016). Thus, emotions can be assumed to mediate the associations between distal antecedents (e.g., the classroom climate) and
outcomes of civic education.
Whereas similar associations have been shown in the context of
foreign language learning (Wang et al., 2021), little is known for the
context of civic education. Current evidence revealed students’ efficacy
beliefs as a mediator between open classroom climate and civic learning
(Bayram Özdemir et al., 2016; Manganelli, Lucidi, & Alivernini, 2015),
which is also known as a proximal antecedent of emotions experienced
in class (Graf et al., 2023). An open classroom climate might relate
positively to students’ enjoyment while discussing political and social
issues, which has been shown to relate positively to students’ willingness
to communicate in other domains (Wang et al., 2021). Consequently,
students might bring up issues to discuss themselves, which can foster
their political knowledge and motivation to participate. On the other
hand, a classroom climate that restricts what can be said might create
feelings of shame about saying something wrong or anxiety about
openly expressing one’s opinion. Finally, boredom might be experienced
if the classroom climate does not create a lively discussion, including
multiple perspectives, potentially discouraging students from learning
and participating in politics in the future.

1.1. Antecedents and effects of achievement emotions in civic education
According to the control-value theory (Pekrun, 2006; Pekrun et al.,
2023), which aims to explain antecedents and effects of discrete emotions experienced in achievement situations, students’ cognitive appraisals are central proximal antecedents of emotions experienced in the
classroom. Specifically, students’ perceived control (e.g., their perceived
ability to master a learning task) and the value of the learning activity or
outcome (e.g., the importance of the learning task) are associated with
emotions experienced in this context. Further, distal antecedents located
in the social environment, such as teacher support, instructional quality,
cultural values of the relevance of achievement, and the performance
level of the class, are central predictors of students’ emotions (e.g., Bieg
et al., 2017; Simonton, Garn, & Solmon, 2017). Especially in civic education, discussion-focused and socially interactive teaching approaches
are common and helpful in promoting democratic competencies (Teegelbeckers, Nieuwelink, & Oostdam, 2023). While dealing with inherently controversial and arousing topics, this pedagogical style fosters a
distinctive classroom climate which might be essential for emotions
experienced in civic education.
1.1.1. Classroom climate as an antecedent of emotions in civic education

Hamre and Pianta (2001) characterize a positive classroom climate by
teachers’ regard for students’ perspectives and their encouragement of
students’ in-class participation, as well as sensitivity and interpersonal
warmth (i.e., friendliness and respect). Importantly, and in line with the

2.2.2. Emotions
An adaptation of the short form of the Achievement Emotions
Questionnaire (AEQ-S; Bieleke, Gogol, Goetz, Daniels, & Pekrun, 2021)
was used to measure enjoyment, anxiety, shame, and boredom in civic
education classes. Students were asked to think of a typical civic education class before responding to the items (e.g., “I enjoy being in civic
education class”). Each emotion was measured with four items, which
were rated on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5
(strongly agree).

party do the following politicians belong to?”) from the Austrian national
election study (Kritzinger et al., 2020) with items about policies (e.g.,
“What is regulated at the European level by the so-called “Dublin Procedure”?”; Graf et al., 2023), resulting overall in 15 items (see supplemental
material). Responses were re-coded to dummy variables indicating
correct (=1) and wrong answers or don’t know responses (=0).

into account (Lüdtke, Marsh, Robitzsch, & Trautwein, 2011), and allows
us to clearly separate the variance for each level and avoid conflation in

The hypotheses were tested based on models including random
slopes for the within-level paths between latent variables, thereby
allowing the slopes from open classroom climate to emotions (path a)
and the outcome variable (path cʹ) and the slopes from emotions to the
outcome variable (path b) to vary between classrooms. These random
slopes are usually recommended in multilevel modeling as misspecifications of a random part as fixed results in a smaller standard
error and a higher risk of Type 1 error for these effects (LaHuis, Jenkins,
Hartman, Hakoyama, & Clark, 2020). To estimate the models with
random slopes, we utilized a Bayesian estimator with uninformative
priors using a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation with a
minimum of 10.000 iterations, eight chains, and 0.01 as a convergence
criterion, which was suggested by Hox, van de Schoot, and Matthijsse
(2012) to reduce bias in residual variances at the group level. To check
for convergence, we manually evaluated posterior parameter trace plots.
In addition to estimates for each path, we report values for explained
variance (R2) for each dependent variable and level. At the student level,
R2w-values are calculated using the average of the R2 values for each
cluster (Muthén & Muthén, 1998). For a robustness check, we added
migration background, the frequency of discussions, and topic emotions
as a control variable at the within-level.

2.2.4. Intention to political participation
For political participation, we used seven items from the International Civic and Citizenship Education Study on students’ expected
participation in legal political activities (Köhler et al., 2016). The items
refer to different activities of political participation, such as taking part
in a peaceful march, contacting a member of parliament, or collecting
signatures for a petition. Students were asked to rate the items on a
four-point Likert scale on how likely they will participate in the activities in the future (e.g., “Collect signatures for a petition”), ranging from 1
(I would certainly not do this) to 4 (I would certainly do this).
2.2.5. Controls
In our robustness checks, we include two sets of control variables at
the student level: (1) a dummy for migration background and (2) the
frequency of political discussions and topic emotions. Immigration status is associated with lower civic knowledge in many participating
countries of the ICCS studies (Schulz et al., 2018), and the classroom
climate is often perceived as less open by students with immigrant
background (Alscher, Ludewig, & McElvany, 2022). Migration background was re-coded as a dummy variable, indicating whether the students or at least one of the student’s parents was born in Austria (0) or
both parents were born abroad (1). Second, a dummy variable for the
frequency of discussions (0 = less than at least once a week, 1 = at least
once a week) was used to separate the effect of the classroom climate
from the effects the frequency of discussions might have. Third, topic
emotions – meaning emotions elicited by the topic itself – are specifically relevant in subjects dealing with controversial issues and might
independently contribute to learning (Sinatra, Broughton, & Lombardi,
2014). Students were instructed to report how intensely they experience
emotions when thinking about political and social topics (e.g., “enthusiastic” or “bored”). For each emotion, we use a one-item measure of the
same or most similar emotion from the Epistemically-Related Emotions
Scale – Short Version (EES-Short; Muis et al., 2018; Vogl, Pekrun, &
Muis, 2018; items used: enthusiasm, confusion, anxiety, and boredom).

3. Results
3.1. Missing data and descriptive statistics
Missing data (10.34 % at the item level) were treated using full information maximum likelihood estimation (FIML) in the ML-SEM; only
participants with missing values on all variables were excluded.
Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations are displayed in Tables 2
and 3, respectively. The ICC1 contains the proportion of variance
explained by the grouping in classes, which ranges from 0.032 (i.e.,
almost no variation between classes) to 0.148.
3.2. Measurement models
As a first step, we calculated multilevel confirmatory factor analysis
for each of the included variables and samples (see Table 4). The majority of our models revealed a very good model fit at the within-level,
with values above the cut-off values suggested by Hu and Bentler (CFI
> .95, TLI > 0.95, RMSEA < 0.06, SRMR < 0.08; 1999). The model fit of
the participation and knowledge scales showed an acceptable model fit,
according to Little (2013). While we found no substantial decrease of
model fit indices in constraining loadings to be equal across models, the
model fit for the participation scale decreased when including equality
constraints. To check whether this influenced our results, we repeated
our analysis with and without equality constraints for this scale. At the
between level, model fit was low, most likely due to the low level 2
variance of several items.

2.3. Analysis
The main analysis was conducted in Mplus, Version 8.5 (Muthén &
Muthén, 1998). Our main predictor variable was conceptualized at the
classroom level (students’ ratings of their teachers and peers) but given
that there is often valuable variation within classes (e.g., Lin, 2014), we
were interested in both student and classroom level associations. Since
the school-level explained little variance (between 0% [boredom] and
0.05 % [knowledge]), we used two-level models in our analysis, with
students at level 1 nested in classes at level 2. This allows us to model the
hypothesized relationships on both levels, resulting in information on
how students’ relative values to the classroom mean are associated with
each other, as well as how aggregated class values are associated.
As a first step, we estimated a two-level confirmatory factor analysis
to evaluate the measurement model of each of the latent variables,
holding loadings equal across levels. Second, we used doubly-latent
multilevel SEM (ML-SEM; Preacher, Zyphur, & Zhang, 2010) to simultaneously analyze the proposed relations and mediation on student
(=within) and classroom (=between) levels. Using latent variable
modeling on both levels has the advantage that measurement error at

Emotions accompany the students’ everyday experiences in the
classroom and are important predictors of learning outcomes. For the
context of civic education, some initial evidence on proximal antecedents and associations of emotions to students’ motivation, engagement,
and knowledge exists (Graf et al., 2023), but less is known about distal
antecedents and other outcomes, such as political participation. Using
multilevel structural equation modeling, we tested how an open classroom climate relates to enjoyment, shame, anxiety, and boredom and
whether there is an indirect effect through these emotions on political
knowledge and intention to participate in politics. At the individual
level, we found strong support for positive relations between an open
classroom climate and enjoyment and for negative relations with anxiety and boredom, but no support for a relation with shame. At the
classroom level, we found these effects for enjoyment and boredom. The

mediation (Zhao, Lynch, & Chen, 2010). That is, the more students
perceived an open classroom climate compared to their classroom peers,
the less anxiety they experienced (negative path), which also related to
less motivation to participate in politics in the future (positive path). In
contrast, classroom anxiety was negatively related to political knowledge at the student level, whereas we found positive paths from topic
anxiety to intended participation and knowledge for models with control
variables included. Together, these results highlight two important aspects regarding the functioning of emotions in the context of civic
learning. First, the relevance to distinguish the same emotion by its
object focus (Muis et al., 2018; Sinatra et al., 2014) and the differential
effects related to it. Whereas anxiety triggered by the topic itself can
activate and motivate learning (Muis et al., 2018; Sinatra et al., 2014)
and thus relate to higher achievement, the anxiety experienced in the
classroom might show detrimental effects on knowledge due to
increasing task-irrelevant thinking (Pekrun et al., 2023).
Second, the results reveal differential effects of anxiety depending on
the outcome considered. This is in line with prior studies showing that
specifically for negative activating emotions such as anxiety, anger, and
shame, no uniform pattern regarding relations to learning processes
exists, for example, as shown by differences between relations to
intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (Graf et al., 2023). As we found a
positive path from classroom anxiety and shame to participation and a
negative path to political knowledge, it might be that
negative-activating emotions motivate behavioral outcomes of civic
education rather than cognitive outcomes. This positive association of
anxiety with participation is also in line with studies on political
engagement, identifying negative-activating emotions like fear, anger,
and concern as motivators for low-effort political action (Barrett &
Pachi, 2019; Renström & Bäck, 2022). Shame is also classified as a
negative-activating emotion, including the potential to motivate and
activate coping behavior (Holodynski & Kronast, 2009). Thus, political
action itself could be a coping mechanism used to regulate negative
emotions (Ford & Feinberg, 2020) activated by anxiety and shame

experienced during civic education.
Finally, we expected a negative relation between an open classroom
climate and shame, as in a less open climate, students might experience
shame about saying something wrong. Though the direction of the path
between open classroom climate and shame was as expected, its size was
only half of the one of anxiety at the student level and was not significant. Shame is mainly characterized as a social emotion (Holodynski &
Kronast, 2009) and, therefore, could be influenced by students’ and their
peers’ opinions on the topic. While an open classroom climate might
reduce shame for students who would otherwise not speak up, it might
increase shame for students who feel pressured to say something due to
being encouraged by an open classroom climate. The two opposing effects together might result in non-significant relations shown in our
results.
4.1. Limitations
One limitation of the present study is its’ observational and crosssectional character. Implementation studies on “openness” in civic education that allow causal claims are widely missing (Teegelbeckers
et al., 2023). Future studies could combine randomized controlled
implementation studies with an instrumental variable approach (Cunningham, 2021) to identify possible causal effects involved. Our estimated models are additionally limited by the relatively low model fits
for the between-level models, which are also visible in some convergence problems of paths leading to the outcome variables, and partly
limit our interpretation of the classroom-level effects. Relatedly, we
found some constraints in multilevel measurement models, specifically
differences between levels in loadings of items of the participation scale.
Both might be explained by the low variance of some variables and items
between classes. We found relatively low ICC1 and ICC2 values, indicating relatively low agreement between students within the same class.
Although we are confident that these values and the missing invariance
did not affect our reported level 1 results (for invariance, see robustness
check in the supplemental material), reported results on level 2 should
still be interpreted with caution. As some of these scales are frequently

4.2. Implications for future studies and practice
Surprisingly, we did not find an association between an open classroom climate and political knowledge. Though testing for mediation still
makes sense even if the initial path is not significant (Zhao et al., 2010),
it might explain why we could not find indirect effects through emotions
for this outcome variable. Although there is broad support for this
relation in the literature, there are also several null findings reported
(Geboers, Geijsel, Admiraal, & ten Dam, 2013). The relation between
classroom climate and political knowledge also seemed to be missing in
prior studies using data from Austria (Lin, 2014). In a study based on 38
countries, Lin (2014) found a significant relation between classroom
climate and political knowledge for 25 out of 38 countries, arguing that
an open classroom climate might be particularly important for schools in
countries with lower socio-economic conditions. However, this is not
fully convincing, as they also found these (and comparably stronger)
effects in several countries with high socioeconomic backgrounds (e.g.,
England, the Netherlands). Future studies could investigate how these
discrepancies can be explained.
An intriguing finding of our analysis was the high amount of variation in the perceived climate within classes. Still, it seems that the
variance explained between classes (ICC1) is similar to climate variables
used in other studies (often <0.10; rarely >0.30; Marsh et al., 2012).
Low correlations between students’ perceptions of the same classroom
were also found in prior studies, evoking “skeptics that the results of
classroom climate are anything more than a self-reinforcing perception
of young people who are already engaged” (Campbell, 2019, p. 37). This
opens up the question of what can explain the variance in perception of
classroom climate within classes. Students’ relative position in a classroom (e.g., representing a minority opinion) might be particularly
important in the context of civic education. Whether and how individual
characteristics can explain the variation within classrooms implies a
need for a person-centered, social network (Grunspan, Wiggins, &
Goodreau, 2014), or situative approaches (Nolen, 2023) to deepen our
understanding of the role of classroom climate in civic education.
Regarding our target population of students in vocational schools, we
aimed for representativeness by drawing a random sample of schools
from different regions, thereby including urban and rural areas. Though
we are confident that results also apply to other countries implementing
civic education as a single subject, future studies are encouraged to
replicate and broaden our findings by investigating school types with
different approaches to civic education and countries that differ in their
political context.
Building on the theoretical approach of control-value theory, classroom context – and specifically, in this study, the classroom climate – is
an important antecedent of academic emotions. This is supported by our
results on enjoyment, anxiety, and boredom. The results reveal possibilities for civic education teachers to co-create a classroom climate that
supports a positive emotional experience in class. Schools and teachers
can try to improve the democratic climate during discussions by discussing current events (Siegel-Stechler, 2021), fostering student-teacher
relationships, and students’ collective efficacy for changes at schools
(Maurissen, Claes, & Barber, 2018). Additionally, our results extend
prior evidence on the importance of emotions for civic learning (Graf
et al., 2023) to the outcome of intended political participation. Being
aware of the functioning of these emotions and how they, at times, can
differently relate to outcomes of learning could help civic education
teachers to constructively deal with students’ emotions in this context.
