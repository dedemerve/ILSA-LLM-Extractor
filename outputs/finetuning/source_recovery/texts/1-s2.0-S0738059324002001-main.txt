Educational equity is a fundamental objective in many education systems, yet challenges persist in achieving this
goal, as evidenced by international large-scale assessments like PISA. This study critically examines the relationship between socioeconomic status (SES) and science literacy. Our findings indicate that grouping students
based on academic achievement amplifies the achievement gap and SES disparities between different types of
secondary schools. The academic stratification in secondary schools deepens the issues regarding educational
inequality. Furthermore, considering substantial differences between regions regarding the SES-achievement
relationship, we propose that future research and policy initiatives focusing on SES should address regional
disparities.

1. Introduction
Children from socioeconomically disadvantaged families have only
one opportunity for a successful life, and that hinges on acquiring a
quality education (Schleicher, 2019, p. 19). Therefore, equality in education is considered one of the principal objectives of many countries’
education systems. However, results from international large-scale assessments, such as the Programme for International Student Assessment
(PISA), have revealed significant variations among participating countries regarding the extent to which educational equality is achieved.
Countries like Australia, Canada, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Hong Kong
(China), Japan, Korea, Norway, and the United Kingdom surpassed the
average of Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development
(OECD) member countries in the PISA 2018 implementation and successfully maintained a lower correlation between socioeconomic status
(SES) and achievement than the OECD average (Schleicher, 2019, p. 20).
Nevertheless, in countries such as France, Germany, Hungary, Israel,
Peru, and the Slovak Republic, the score gap between students in the
lowest and highest SES percentiles exceeds 170 points, corresponding to
four years of schooling (Schleicher, 2019, p. 19).
The growing demand for people with science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) skills emphasizes the importance of
teaching science to students. In some European countries, fewer young

people are choosing STEM careers (Kudenko and Gras-Velázquez, 2016),
leading to a shortage of professionals in these fields (Diekman and
Benson-Greenwald, 2018). To understand why some students do better
in science than others, it’s important to study the connection between
their socioeconomic background and their science knowledge. This can
help address the differences in achievement and encourage more students from disadvantaged backgrounds to pursue STEM careers. A society where people understand science is essential for meeting the needs
of the workforce and for helping future generations participate in and
shape technological progress.
We believe that the SES gap is essential to analyze because socioeconomic inequalities have a substantial impact on students’ educational opportunities and outcomes. In their recent studies, Sum and
Bădescu (2023) claim that SES inequality, i.e., heterogeneity in SES, at
both micro and macro levels is inversely related to students’ learning
outcomes including collaborative problem-solving skills. In many
countries, including Türkiye, students from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds often face barriers that prevent them from
reaching the same levels of success as their more affluent peers, thereby
widening the achievement gap and perpetuating cycles of inequality.
This issue is particularly pronounced in Türkiye, where a considerable
proportion of students are socioeconomically disadvantaged, and SES
heterogeneity ranks among the highest in OECD countries.

1.2. Challenges for educational equity in Türkiye
Despite its positive aspects, such as state funding for public schools to
promote educational equality, Türkiye has certain characteristics that
could deepen its educational disparities. Notably, in Türkiye, 64 % of 15year-old students participating in the PISA 2015 assessment are classified as socioeconomically disadvantaged, placing Türkiye in the top
position among OECD countries in this regard (Taş et al., 2016, p. 39). In
addition, in terms of heterogeneity in SES, Türkiye ranks third among
OECD countries (OECD, 2019, p. 51). Furthermore, Türkiye’s gross
domestic product falls below the OECD average, placing it second to last,
just above Mexico (Taş et al., 2016, p. 36). Similarly, when evaluating
per-student expenditure by the state for students aged 6–15, Türkiye
also holds the second-to-last position among OECD countries (Taş et al.,
2016, p. 38).
In addition to the challenge of a comparatively low budget for education, a major concern in Türkiye that could deepen educational
inequality is the significantly high proportion of private (household)
spending per student compared to public expenditure. While this ratio
averages 5.3 % in European countries, it rises to 18.9 % in Türkiye,
making the country an outlier among European nations. By contrast,
countries such as Finland and Norway, which have made significant
progress in educational equality, maintain this ratio at a mere 0.4 %. The
high ratio of private (household) expenditures on education to public
expenditures has the potential to amplify the influence of a family’s
financial resources on student achievement (European Commission/EACEA/Eurydice, 2020, p. 74).
Challenges to educational equality highlight the growing significance of examining the correlation between SES and academic
achievement in Türkiye. Türkiye actively participates in international
large-scale assessments, such as the PISA and TIMSS, where the results
are evaluated in the context of educational equality. National reports
were crafted to thoroughly investigate the association between SES and
academic achievement at the student level, encompassing the entire
sample. However, these analyses require further refinement across
various dimensions.
First, Türkiye demonstrates significant regional disparities in the
cited challenges (Erberber, 2010). According to the socioeconomic
development ranking report from the Ministry of Industry and Technology (Acar et al., 2019), Türkiye exhibits considerable diversity in
terms of socioeconomic indicators. Notably, there are substantial variations in income levels among regions. For instance, according to the
Turkish Statistical Institute (TÜİK), the annual average household per
capita income in the Southeast Anatolia region in 2019 was slightly
above one-third of that in the Istanbul region. This diversity underscores
substantial discrepancies in family income, a pivotal aspect of SES,
across regions.
Furthermore, it is important to highlight that there is considerable
variance between schools in Türkiye due to the grouping of students in

education. These categories encompass various types of schools, such as

1.1. Educational equality
Educational equality comprises two interrelated dimensions: Fairness and Inclusion. Fairness implies that students’ achievements are
independent of their personal and social characteristics, such as SES and
gender. Inclusion ensures that every individual has the right to access
basic education (S. Field et al., 2007). The right to education is
considered a fundamental human right. Article 28 of the United Nations
Convention on the Rights of the Child, adopted by the United Nations
General Assembly in 1989, guarantees every child the right to education, emphasizing equal opportunities for all children.
Ensuring a fair and inclusive education is of utmost importance
because educational equality has both social and financial implications.
Failing to establish a fair education system for all individuals results in
long-term costs to society, as unequal opportunities can hinder social
mobility and economic growth (S. Field et al., 2007). Therefore, nearly
all European countries address educational equality in their educational
policies, with most featuring at least one major initiative to support it.
Yet, relatively better educational equality is achieved in fewer than
one-third of European countries (European Commission/EACEA/Eurydice, 2020, p. 14).
This study focuses on fairness, particularly in relation to SES and
students’ academic achievement. To achieve fairness, students’ success
should not be determined by their SES background, aligning with the
idea of equality of opportunity rather than equality of outcomes
(Ferreira and Gignoux, 2014). In this sense, fairness means that all

The link between SES and academic achievement has been a focal
point in educational equality discussions, with notable studies contributing to our understanding. Coleman et al.’s (1966) report stands out as
one such influential study. Over the years, numerous studies have been
conducted to examine the relationship between SES and students’ academic success. White’s (1982) early meta-analysis, synthesizing these
findings, yielded an average correlation of 0.24. Similarly, Şirin’s (2005)
extensive meta-analysis, drawing on data from 6871 schools and 101,
157 students, reported a correlation coefficient of 0.27.
Hattie (2012, p. 252) took a broader perspective by synthesizing five
separate meta-analysis studies. The comprehensive analysis revealed an
average effect size of 0.52 in terms of Cohen’s d, corresponding to a
correlation of 0.25. In a recent study, Liu et al. (2022) conducted a
thorough analysis, comprising two meta-analyses. The first, based on
data from 326 primary studies, found the average correlation coefficient
between SES and academic achievement to be 0.22. The second, utilizing data from international large-scale assessments, reported an average
correlation coefficient of 0.28. These comprehensive synthesis studies
examining the relationship between family SES and students’ academic
achievements consistently point to an average correlation value ranging
from 0.22 to 0.28. Additionally, “Chmielewski’s (2019) extensive
research, spanning around 50 years and covering approximately 100
countries through international large-scale assessments, identified a
growing trend in the achievement gap between students in the 90th and
10th percentiles of SES in many nations”.
The research exploring the correlation between family SES and a
student’s science achievement also emphasizes the importance of SES
(Çalışkan, 2008; Erberber, 2010; Mullis et al., 2020, p. 288; OECD,
2016a, p. 215; Oral and Mcgiveny, 2014; Ustun, 2023; Ustun et al.,
2022; Üstün, 2021). Çalışkan (2008) employed PISA 2006 data to
explore variables that influence science literacy. Through analyses using
the ESCS index, she concluded that SES affected every aspect of science
literacy. In a recent study, Üstün (2021), utilizing TIMSS 2015 data,
underscored SES as one of the most crucial variables explaining the
science achievement of 8th-grade students in Türkiye. Ustun (2023), in a
study utilizing TIMSS 2019 data, articulated that SES was a significant
variable explaining science achievement in Japan, Norway, and the
United States.

1.3. SES
Various indicators are employed to measure SES, a concept with
diverse definitions in the literature. This diversity arises from the
different approaches used to explain the concept of SES. In PISA, SES is
represented by an index called economic, social, and cultural status
(ESCS). Although the theoretical underpinnings of ESCS are not
explicitly stated in the PISA technical report, Willms and Tramonte
(2015, p. 16) provided two definitions related to SES in their report
aiming to explain the conceptual framework of contextual questions
used in PISA. The first definition, widely cited in the literature and articulated by Mueller and Parcel (1981), refers to SES as the position of a
family or individual within a social hierarchy, which is determined by
their wealth, influence, and authority in comparison to others. The other
definition, recommended by a group of experts for examining SES with
its various dimensions, is as follows: "SES can be broadly defined as
one’s access to financial, social, cultural, and human capital resources"
(Cowan et al., 2012, p. 4).
Building upon the operational definition of ESCS and conceptual
explanations, Avvisati (2020) argued that the SES variable used in PISA
is influenced by materialist and gradient approaches (see for details:
APA Task Force on Socioeconomic Status, 2007). He subsequently proposed the following definition for this variable: “ESCS is a measure of
students’ access to family resources (financial capital, social capital,
cultural capital, and human capital) which determine the social position
of the student’s family/household” (p. 3).
In this context, while debates persist in the literature regarding what
dimensions SES should encompass, Cowan et al. (2012) emphasize in
their report that SES comprises three crucial dimensions. These dimensions, referred to as the "Big Three," include family income, parental
education level, and parental occupational status. The ESCS variable
was constructed to encompass the three recommended dimensions. In
summary, the conceptual framework of the ESCS index used as a metric
for SES in PISA is rooted in materialist and gradient approaches,
covering the three most significant dimensions of SES, commonly known
as the "Big Three."

2. Method

2.2.2. SES
In PISA, SES is represented by the index of ESCS, which is a
continuous variable comprising three key sub-dimensions: parents’ education level, parents’ occupational status, and home resources. These
components align with the triad of education, occupation, and income,
which are critical factors influencing students’ academic performance
and opportunities. Although family income is not directly measured in
PISA, home resources are used as a proxy for family wealth (OECD,
2017, p. 339).
In the PISA 2015 dataset, the ESCS index includes three specific
variables: the PARED variable, which reflects the highest level of
parental education measured in years of schooling; the HISEI variable,
which indicates the highest parental occupational status; and the
HOMEPOS index, which evaluates home possessions. Each of these subdimensions contributes to understanding the SES of students: higher
parental education (PARED) typically correlates with greater academic
support at home; higher occupational status (HISEI) often reflects access
to better resources and networks; and more home possessions (HOMEPOS) generally indicate greater economic stability. Since there is no
direct measure of family income in PISA, home possessions serve as an
important indicator of the family’s wealth, highlighting how socioeconomic factors can influence students’ educational experiences and
outcomes.
The ESCS composite score was constructed using these three variables through Principal Component Analysis. In this analysis, the factor
loadings for Türkiye were 0.82, 0.79, and 0.77 for the HISEI, PARED,
and HOMEPOS variables, respectively. The calculated Cronbach’s Alpha
for the reliability of the ESCS index was 0.68. The ESCS index follows a
distribution with an OECD average of zero and a standard deviation of
one (OECD, 2017, p. 340). The average SES score for schools was
computed using individual ESCS scores.

2.1. Data
2.1.1. Description of PISA data
PISA, organized by the OECD, has been conducted every three years
since 2000, assessing 15-year-old students enrolled in formal education
at or above the 7th grade (OECD, 2016a, p. 22). PISA measures literacy
in three main areas: science, mathematics, and reading. Each cycle
designates one area as the major domain, which takes up half of the test
time and receives greater emphasis in reporting (OECD, 2016a, p. 27).
The major domain rotates every nine years, with science literacy being
the focus in both 2006 and 2015. The most recent PISA results were from
2022, with mathematics as the major domain. PISA 2015 remains the
latest instance where science literacy was the central focus.
Türkiye has participated in PISA since 2003, consistently scoring
below the OECD average in science literacy. Notably, when science literacy was the major domain in 2006 and 2015, Türkiye’s scores were
significantly lower, at 424 and 425, respectively. In PISA, literacy scores
are scaled with a mean of around 500 and a standard deviation of 100
(OECD, 2016a, p. 64).
In PISA assessments, in addition to literacy in core areas, contextual
questionnaires are employed to collect data on students’ affective
characteristics, and school and family environments. This dataset not
only allows for the examination of variables such as student motivation
but also facilitates the analysis of socioeconomic background features
and how these attributes correlate with literacy levels (OECD, 2016a, p.
29).
2.1.2. Population and sample
This study focuses on 15-year-old students attending at least the
seventh grade in formal education in Türkiye as its target population. To
represent this population, a sample of 5895 students from 187 schools
was selected using the stratified random sampling method (OECD,
2016a, pp. 290, 295). The largest subset (72.9 %) of students participating in the PISA 2015 application in Türkiye comprises those in the
10th grade. 9th-grade students (20.7 %) also contribute significantly to
the sample, and these two grades collectively make up the majority of
the sample (OECD, 2016a, p. 296).

2.2.3. Parents’ highest education level
Within the scope of PISA 2015, students were asked open-ended
questions to determine the parents’ education level. Subsequently,
their responses were recoded using the ISCED 1997 classification. Utilizing this classification, two variables were created to represent both
mother’s (MISCED) and father’s education (FISCED) levels. Then, using
these two variables, the HISCED index was constructed to indicate the
highest education level of parents. Finally, the HISCED index was
recoded to create the PARED index, specifying the total years of education for the parent with the highest education level in years (OECD,
2017, p. 298).

2.1.3. Data collection tools
The data used in this study were obtained from the cognitive tests
and student questionnaires administered as part of the PISA 2015. PISA
2015 was implemented in a computer-based format in 57 of 72
participating countries, including Türkiye. The cognitive tests were
conducted in two sessions, each lasting 60 minutes, for a total duration
of two hours. A dedicated 60-minute session was allocated for the major
domain, science literacy. Following a short break, the second 60-minute
session included other cognitive tests. After completing the cognitive
tests, students responded to a 35-minute student questionnaire (OECD,
2016b, p. 28). The data related to the sub-dimensions used in calculating
the ESCS index were obtained from the student questionnaire.

2.2.4. Highest parental occupational status
In this study, the variable measuring the highest parental occupational status, a sub-dimension of SES, is assessed using the HISEI index
derived from PISA 2015 data. To construct the HISEI index, students
were asked open-ended questions about their parents’ occupations. The

representing both mother’s and father’s occupational levels were
created using the International Socioeconomic Index of Occupational
Status (ISEI) corresponding to each code (Ganzeboom and Treiman,
2003). Finally, by utilizing these two variables, the HISEI index was

One of the key advantages of using HLM over ordinary regression is

understanding the extent to which school-level factors contribute to

Before commencing HLM analysis, assumptions of normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity of residuals were checked. Scatter plots
were initially examined for linearity by plotting SES against each

assumption. Subsequently, histograms of standardized residuals and
plots of standardized residuals against standardized predicted values
(zpred-zresid) were created. These plots provided evidence that assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were met (A. Field, 2018).

across schools. This variation is captured by allowing the slope of SES to

school level by employing a means as outcomes model. This approach
allows us to investigate how school-level SES affects the average science
literacy outcomes across schools. For regional analyses, we conducted
the HLM analysis for each region separately.

2.2.5. Household possessions
In PISA 2015, three questions (ST011, ST012, and ST013) were
asked regarding the possessions at home. The first question (ST011)

other words, the index of home possessions comprehensively encompasses these four dimensions. The reliability coefficient for the home
possessions index for the Turkish data was calculated as 0.855 (OECD,
2017, p. 301).

encompassing three types of schools. These school types include basic
education, general secondary education, and vocational and technical
secondary education. While there is a more extensive variety of secondary education school types in Türkiye, the PISA data shared with
researchers lacks a more detailed classification concerning school types.
Therefore, within the scope of this study, the school-type variable was
represented by these three general program types.
2.2.7. Statistical regions
The STRATUM variable has been recoded to create a new variable
that includes the 12 statistical regions in Türkiye. In the context of
Türkiye’s alignment with the European Union in 2002, a three-tiered
classification of statistical regions was established. For this study, the
12 statistical regions falling within the first tier were utilized. These
regions are Istanbul (TR01), West Marmara (TR02), Aegean (TR03), East
Marmara (TR04), West Anatolia (TR05), Mediterranean (TR06), Central
Anatolia (TR07), West Black Sea (TR08), East Black Sea (TR09),
Northeast Anatolia (TR10), Middle East Anatolia (TR11), and Southeast
Anatolia (TR12).
2.3. Methods
In this study, a correlational research method was employed.
Correlational research is used to explain the nature of the relationship
between variables and to predict possible outcomes (Fraenkel et al.,
2012, p. 331). Conducted as a secondary analysis using PISA 2015 data,
this research aims to comprehensively investigate both the nature of the
relationship between family SES and students’ science literacy and the
extent to which SES predicts science literacy at both the individual
student and school levels. Furthermore, the strength of this relationship
is analyzed separately for different school types and regions.
Regarding data analysis, a two-level hierarchical linear model (HLM)
was employed to investigate the relationship between SES and science
literacy using the PISA data, where students are nested within schools.
HLM is particularly well-suited for this type of analysis because it accounts for the hierarchical structure of the data (Tabachnick and Fidell,
2013, p. 787). Students are nested within schools, and the assumption of

4. Discussion
This study examines the relationship between SES and students’
science literacy at both the individual and school levels using the PISA
2015 data. In this regard, we first calculated the mean values for both
the SES and science literacy variables. The findings indicate that the
average SES of families in Türkiye lags significantly behind the OECD
average. Additionally, a high standard deviation value suggests a heterogeneous distribution in terms of SES. This implies substantial variation in SES levels among families in Türkiye. Furthermore, in terms of
science literacy, the findings underscore the fact that the average performance of students in Türkiye falls short of the OECD benchmark.

The correlation coefficient depicting the relationship between SES
and science literacy was calculated as 0.30. This value corresponds to a
moderate effect size (Cohen, 1988) and closely aligns with the 0.28 reported in a comprehensive meta-analysis conducted by Liu et al. (2022)
on international large-scale assessments. Similar findings have been
reported in other meta-analytical studies (Hattie, 2012; Sirin, 2005). In
addition, Yang (2003), in surveying the literature, highlights that many
studies consistently reveal a correlation of around 0.30 between SES and
academic achievement. Moreover, the observed score difference
7

B. Yediyıldız and U. Ustun

International Journal of Educational Development 111 (2024) 103173

between disadvantaged and advantaged students in Türkiye further
underscores the influence of SES on science literacy, revealing a notable
58-point advantage for the latter group.
As Yang (2003) noted, there are a limited number of studies simultaneously examining the relationship between SES and academic
achievement at both student and school levels. In studies where students
cluster within schools, as in this research, multilevel analyses are recommended (Hox, 2002). Therefore, the study progresses with a
two-level regression analysis using the HLM. This analysis found that
more than half of the variance in science literacy could be attributed to
between-school differences (ICC=0.551). This value not only underlines
the necessity of multilevel analysis but also indicates significant disparities among schools in Türkiye.
The OECD (2016a) utilizes the academic inclusion index (100 *
(1-ICC)) as a measure of how similar schools are to one another. The
larger this index, the smaller the between-school achievement differences, indicating that schools are more similar in terms of academic
outcomes within the education system. Applying the ICC value calculated in this study, the academic inclusion index for Türkiye was
determined to be 44.9. This value falls significantly below the OECD
average of 69.9. On the other hand, Finland, which places a high value
on educational equality as a core objective, ranks prominently in this
index with a score of 92.1 (OECD, 2016a, p. 418). According to Broer
et al. (2019, p. 11), two fundamental macro-level features influence
variations in education systems: the extent to which the system is homogeneous or heterogeneous, and the degree of centralization or
decentralization in governance. The relatively low academic inclusion
index in Türkiye indicates substantial differences in academic achievement between schools, signifying a heterogeneous system. Despite
Türkiye having a centralized education system with standardized elements, such as curriculum and teacher assignments, it has not been able
to prevent substantial differences in academic achievement among
schools.
The results of the HLM analyses indicated that SES alone accounted
for almost half (49.4 %) of the between-school variance in science literacy. However, it does not significantly predict science literacy at the
student level. This finding may be attributed to the grouping of students
in Türkiye based on their academic achievement. In the Turkish education system, the allocation of students to secondary schools is determined by centralized exam results. Therefore, students with similar
achievement levels are clustered within schools. This stratification increases between-school variance and decreases within-school variance,
leading to a reduction in academic inclusion. This also amplifies the
differences in SES between schools due to the established link between
SES and academic achievement.
Analyses related to school type also support this finding. At the
secondary education level, there were significant differences between
the two types of schools in terms of SES and science literacy. Although
not explicitly stated in the data shared with the researchers, General
Secondary Education also consists of different subtypes based on students’ achievements, indicating a greater degree of segregation into
more homogeneous subgroups. The higher variation between schools in
the General Secondary Education type is attributed to these subgroups.
The early grouping of students contributes to the deepening of the
educational inequalities rooted in SES. Numerous studies have reached
similar conclusions. For instance, Hanushek and Wößmann (2006), in
their comparative study using data from six international large-scale
assessments, found that early tracking increases inequality in education on a global scale. Similarly, in a review study, Van De Werfhorst and
Mijs (2010) highlighted that national-level grouping contributes to
increased inequality in education. In another systematic review, Zapfe
and Gross (2021) concluded that there is a relationship between
educational systems with high levels of grouping and socioeconomic
inequalities. In a recent meta-analysis, Terrin and Triventi (2023) found
that tracking exacerbated inequality in education. OECD (2016b, p. 5)
has also noted that systems where students choose different schools or

education programs later tend to achieve better equality and exhibit a
weaker relationship between SES and science achievement.
Moreover, studies also present findings indicating that there is no
need for an "equality-efficiency trade-off" (Parker et al., 2018). Essentially, these studies indicate that there is no need to compromise equality
to enhance efficiency. For instance, Terrin and Triventi (2023) found no
significant relationship between tracking and academic achievement in
their meta-analysis. In other words, they concluded that grouping does
not significantly impact academic achievement but contributes to
increased inequality. The presence of countries excelling both in high
achievement and in achieving equality in education in PISA further
supports this perspective. Countries such as Canada, Finland, and Japan,
which stand above average in both academic achievement and
achieving equality in education, serve as examples (Schleicher, 2019, p.
20).
Like the differences observed among school types, there were significant regional variations in SES and science literacy. High levels of
regional disparities pose a significant barrier to achieving educational
equality in Türkiye (Erberber, 2010). The positive relationship between
SES and science literacy persists at the regional level. However, HLM
analyses conducted for each region revealed significant regional variations in the SES-science literacy relationship. The explained variance at
the school level displays considerable variability across regions, reaching 83 % in the West Marmara region (TR02). This highlights the need to
examine indicators related to educational equality, such as the relationship between SES and achievement, at the regional level, particularly in socioeconomically heterogeneous countries, such as Türkiye.
When we examined the relationship between the sub-dimensions
constituting SES and science literacy separately, we observed that the
occupational status and home possessions dimensions stood out. The
educational level sub-dimension, on the other hand, lagged far behind
the other two dimensions in terms of explaining the variance in science
literacy. Within home possessions, variables such as ICT resources and
family wealth explained more variance than educational and cultural
resources.
As previously discussed, Bourdieu’s theory of capital (1986) posits
three primary sources —economic, social, and cultural capital— that
contribute to educational outcomes and academic achievement. Our
findings suggest that economic and social capital may exert a more
pronounced influence on science literacy than cultural capital. The
greater explanatory power of occupational status and home possessions
indicates that access to economic capital, via family wealth and possessions like ICT resources, and social capital, through parental occupational status, plays a pivotal role in shaping students’ educational
outcomes.
Economic capital, represented by home possessions and ICT resources, directly affects access to tools that enhance learning, particularly in science. These resources likely provide students with more
opportunities for out-of-classroom engagement, aligning with Bourdieu’s argument that economic capital enables access to advantageous
educational resources. Additionally, parental occupational status may
reflect both economic and social capital. Parents in higher-status occupations are likely to have broader networks and knowledge that can
benefit their children’s academic success, especially in technical fields
like science. Moreover, a very high percentage of (private) household
funding in Türkiye (18.9 %) (European Commission/EACEA/Eurydice,
2020, p. 74) could be one of the causes for this association between
economic capital and academic achievement.
The lower explanatory power of parents’ educational level, closely
linked to cultural capital, suggests that, in this context, direct access to
educational resources, like ICT tools or wealth, and social networks may
have a more immediate impact on science-related skills than the transmission of cultural capital. This finding could reflect a practical,
resource-driven advantage, where material wealth and access to technology better equip students to succeed in science compared to cultural
knowledge alone. However, conducting more in-depth research on the

of the true nature of the relationship between SES and achievement.

5. Limitations

Funding

This study utilized cross-sectional data obtained from PISA 2015 and
employed a correlational research method. While correlational studies
indicate a possible cause-and-effect relationship, offering insights into
the explanation of relationships between variables, they are not sufficient to establish a direct cause-and-effect link. The robust theoretical
framework established for SES, particularly through Bourdieu’s theory
of capital, along with the existing comprehensive literature provides
insights into the nature of this relationship. However, further research is
needed to investigate the causes of school type and regional variations.
Moreover, this study employs multilevel regression analysis to
investigate the influence of SES on science literacy at both the student
and school levels, considering the clustered nature of the data. This
analysis addresses the statistical issues arising from clustering. However,
a limitation of this study is the relatively low number of schools at the
second level in some regions, particularly in regional analyses.

Conclusions and future research

Acknowledgements

This study examines the relationship between SES and science literacy at both the student and school levels using multilevel regression
analysis. The results indicate that approximately half of the variation in
science literacy across schools can be attributed to the SES of families. In
Türkiye, tracking students in secondary education based on a centralized
exam results in a high ICC value and low academic inclusion. Early
grouping of students exacerbates inequalities within the education system, with socioeconomically disadvantaged students clustering in
schools with lower academic achievement.
At the secondary education level, substantial differences exist between school types concerning both SES and science literacy. Additionally, regional disparities are evident, with the relationship between
SES and science literacy varying across regions. When analyzing the subdimensions of SES, occupational status and household possessions
explain more variance in science literacy than parental educational
level. Investigating the relatively low correlation between parental education, one of the key aspects of SES, and science literacy in Türkiye
presents a valuable area for future research.
In conclusion, this study contributes significantly to the existing
literature by highlighting the pivotal role of SES in shaping science literacy and demonstrating how early grouping practices in Türkiye
exacerbate educational inequalities. The results highlight the strong
influence of socioeconomic disparities on academic achievement and
offer new insights into the regional and institutional dimensions of this
relationship. Notably, the identification of occupational status and
household possessions as stronger predictors of science literacy than
parental education level proposes a nuanced understanding of how
different forms of capital, particularly economic and social capital,
shape educational outcomes. These insights challenge traditional assumptions and underscore the need for further research into the relatively weaker influence of cultural capital, particularly parental
education, in the Turkish context. Overall, this study deepens our understanding of how SES impacts educational achievement and encourages the exploration of policies aimed at reducing these inequalities.
