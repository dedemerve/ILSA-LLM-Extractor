Abstract
We live in a society in which knowing how to manage money is a necessity, since it
is required in our everyday life. In this context, financial literacy is a competence
which people should develop in order to improve their money-related decisions. In
order to analyse the contribution of financial education to this competence, we go
beyond the previous literature in respect to correlation by using student fixedeffects and PISA (Programme for International Student Assessment) 2018 data for
15-year-old students in 20 countries. Our results show that, in most of the countries
under analysis, the financial education that students receive in the subject of mathematics may contribute to the development of their financial literacy competences.
Nevertheless, the lack of an effective subject in economics is still an issue in most of
the educational systems under analysis. (JEL codes: I20, I21, I28, and C10)
Key words: financial education, financial literacy, student fixed-effects, PISA 2018

1. Introduction
We will all be required to have a proper level of financial literacy competence during our
whole life. Almost every day we perform transactions and processes that require a certain
level of financial awareness, for example buying at a store, managing our monthly savings,
calculating interest on mortgages, paying our taxes, etc. The relevance of all these topics
points towards the need for a financial education of quality (as in other competences such
as reading or mathematics), which should be provided to the population by the educational
system, to enable them to make more accurate financial decisions. As indicated by Carpena
et al. (2017), this is because financial education can be translated into financial behaviour.
Besides, financial education has also been found to have a positive influence on objective
financial literacy, subjective financial literacy, desirable financial behaviour, and perceived
financial capability (Xiao and O’Neill 2016). It has also been found to have additional
benefits, such as an increase in credit responsibility (Borden et al. 2008), in the willingness

(Agarwal et al. 2010), a reduction in impulsive decision making (DeHart et al. 2016), and
an increase in satisfaction about financial decisions (Xiao and Porto 2017), among others.
Nevertheless, in spite of the relevance of financial literacy, it was not until the Great
Recession in 2008 and subsequent incorporation of financial literacy competence assessment
to the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2012 (OECD 2014), that we
were able to obtain a general picture of the underdevelopment, in terms of financial literacy,
that most countries are suffering (OECD 2014, 2017, 2020a). Specifically, in the last PISA
cycle (2018), 15% of students did not reach the minimum level of financial literacy, and
only 10% reached the top financial literacy level. In fact, recent research has indicated that
one-in-four persons in the adult population (16–65 year olds) in 31 OECD countries could
not calculate the change in a basic shopping transaction, while half of adults were not able
to interpret basic economic information from a line graph (Bhutoria et al. 2018).
The relevance of financial education for the population has resulted in the appearance of
some out-of-school financial education initiatives (OECD 2020a, pp. 40–2). For instance,
the Talk With Our Kids About Money Day is held in April in Canada, in which students receive lessons about money management. In the case of Portugal, a Financial Education Week
is organized, with 1 day focused on financial education workshops and projects for students.
Similarly, a Financial Education Month is held in Italy, in which many institutions
participate (e.g. schools, universities, professional associations, museums, theatres, etc.).
Furthermore, an Olympics of Economics and Finance is also organized in this country.
The impact of this kind of initiative has been assessed by some research studies. For
example, in the case of Italy, Becchetti and Pisani (2012) analysed the impact of a financial education intervention based on a randomized experiment, finding that it improved
students’ financial literacy at student and class levels. Regarding Spain, a 10-hour financial education programme was taken by 15-year-old students in Madrid, which
improved students’ financial literacy by between 0.25 and 0.33 standard deviations
(Hospido et al. 2015). In the context of Brazilian high-schools, Bruhn et al. (2016) analysed a randomized control trial aimed at analysing the influence of financial education;
these authors found that it improved students’ financial literacy by 0.25 standard deviations, also increasing grade passing rates. Other research studies for the USA have
reported that a seminar format for financial education might be useful for students’ financial knowledge and financial responsibility (Borden et al. 2008). Thus, there are
many examples of this kind of financial education interventions in different modalities
and countries.
Moreover, some meta-analyses have explored this relationship between financial education and financial literacy competences. In particular, some of them have indicated that financial education presents a positive influence. In this sense, Miller et al. (2015) performed
a meta-analysis of 188 papers and found that the interventions aimed at increasing consumers’ financial literacy had a positive influence on their capabilities of saving money and record keeping, but not in other aspects such as credit default. Similarly, Kaiser and
Menkhoff (2017) analysed 126 research studies on financial education impact evaluations,
finding an overall positive influence on financial behaviours and financial literacy; however, this influence was lesser for lower income groups and for low/middle income economies. Likewise, another meta-analysis on 36 research papers by Amagir et al. (2017)
analysed the influence of financial education interventions on children and adolescents;
they found that they had a positive influence on students’ financial literacy competences.

2. Data
The data employed in this research study are from PISA 2018. This programme assesses students’ competences in reading, mathematics and science. In addition, since 2012, students’
financial literacy competence has also been analysed. Furthermore, students took a background questionnaire, while head teachers took a school questionnaire.2

Other authors such as Kaiser and Menkhoff (2020) analysed 37 quasi-experimental research studies and also indicated the existence of a positive influence of financial education
on students’ financial literacy competences (0.37 standard deviations) and on financial
behaviours (0.07 standard deviations). Moreover, Kaiser et al. (2020) analysed 76 randomized controlled trials and also indicated that financial education improved financial
knowledge.
Nevertheless, another group of meta-analyses has pointed towards a negative relationship between financial education and literacy. For instance, Willis (2011) stated that most
research studies did not find strong evidence on the causal relationship between receiving financial education and achieving higher levels of financial literacy. In this sense, Hastings
et al. (2013) reviewed the literature on financial education and indicated that there was a
lack of clear evidence regarding its positive influence on financial literacy competences.
Similarly, Fernandes et al. (2014) studied 168 papers which covered 201 studies related to
the influence of financial education on financial literacy competences; they found that this
financial education explained only 0.1% of the variance of financial behaviours.
To sum up, many financial education projects and interventions have been performed
worldwide; nevertheless, it seems that the empirical evidence regarding their benefits on financial literacy competence is far from conclusive.
The present research study analyses this issue and is novel in at least two ways: first, it
studies the influence of financial education on 15-year-old students’ financial literacy competence for 20 countries which took the financial literacy tests in PISA 2018. Second, a
quasi-experimental methodology (i.e. student fixed-effects) is employed, which enables us
to go beyond correlation and approach a causal relationship. This contrasts with previous
research, to the extent that it used descriptive1 or correlational methodologies to analyse financial literacy data from PISA, since it started to be assessed in 2012 (Lusardi 2015;
Cordero and Pedraja 2019) and also in 2015 (Moreno-Herrero et al. 2018).

3. Methodology
This analysis of the influence of financial education upon students’ financial literacy competences could be studied by employing ordinary least squares regressions. In this sense, the
model to estimate would be:

according to PISA criteria6); SCHj are school observable characteristics; eij is the idiosyncratic error term. However, this methodology does not account for unobservable factors
that may be associated with both receiving financial education and students’ financial literacy competences, so we have to move on to the approach that we propose to avoid this
issue and obtain the influence of financial education upon students’ financial literacy competence: student fixed-effects. Specifically, this approach compares the academic performance of each student in different subjects and, in particular for this research, financial
literacy and mathematics. The assumption of this methodology is that the influence of those

The final model in equation (4) is estimated in a stepwise procedure in Table 1. First, the
base model in equation (3) is obtained, in which we see that financial education does not
seem to contribute to students’ financial literacy competences in any country. Then, additional interaction controls with the financial education variables are added (gender, immigrant status, socio-economic status, and grade retention) and it still seems that financial
education does not influence financial literacy in any country. Finally, the interaction term
between financial education variables and mathematics benchmark level is included to
reach the model in equation (4). In this case, financial education during mathematics classes
seems to positively influence students’ financial literacy competences, this influence ranging
from 0.12 to 0.37 standard deviations, depending on the country. However, there are other
countries such as the Netherlands (whose results have to be taken with caution, as previously indicated), Peru, Serbia, and Slovak Republic, in which financial education during mathematics classes does not seem to influence students’ financial literacy competences.
Students only seem to be positively influenced by having financial education in a subject
other than mathematics in Italy and the Netherlands. In the case of the other two financial
education modalities (one-off lesson or activity in school time from an outside visitor and
extra-curricular activity outside of school time), we see that they do not seem to improve
students’ financial literacy competences in any case.
Interestingly, the inclusion of the interaction term between financial education in mathematics and mathematics benchmark level presents a negative influence. This may indicate
that students with higher level of mathematics knowledge may have financial education in
mathematics to a lesser degree. This situation might happen in countries such as Spain, in
which students can choose between academic mathematics (more related to sciences and
with a higher level of difficulty) and applied mathematics (more related to social sciences
and with a lower level of difficulty) in secondary education (BOE 2013). Thus, students
taking applied mathematics might be those with the lower levels in mathematics, an issue
which the interaction term with mathematics benchmark level might capture.

5. Discussion and Conclusions
In the present research study, we analyse the influence of financial education on students’ financial literacy competence. For this purpose, we employ financial literacy data for the
countries which took the financial literacy assessment in PISA 2018. In particular, the present research study is novel in two ways: first, the influence of financial education on students’ financial literacy is analysed for 15-year-old students in 20 countries; second, a
quasi-experimental methodology (i.e. student fixed-effects) is employed, which allows us to
go beyond correlation and approach a causal relationship. Our results show that financial
education in mathematics lessons seems to improve students’ competences in financial literacy in most of the countries under analysis. However, having this financial education in another class, in a one-off lesson or activity in school time from an outside visitor or in an
extra-curricular activity outside of school time does not seem to improve students’ financial
literacy competence. These results may be even more relevant when taking into account
that PISA analyses students’ competences, which they can use in their everyday life interactions with money.
In view of these results, it appears that effective financial education does not seem to be
provided to students by any other means aside from mathematics problems related to economic issues in the subject of mathematics. In fact, our results show that students may be
selected (on their own or by teachers, schools, institutions, etc.) to have financial education
in mathematics. This may be due, for instance, to the existence of different modalities of
mathematics at school (e.g. more academic or applied mathematics). However, the other financial education modalities do not seem to positively influence students’ financial literacy
competences. Because of that, we suggest the implementation of Economics as a compulsory subject in secondary education, taught by teachers specialized in economics. This subject is necessary because many countries choose a transversal approach in which students
