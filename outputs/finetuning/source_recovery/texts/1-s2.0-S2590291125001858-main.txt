A B S T R A C T
This study investigates the feasibility of improving science teaching quality in middle schools by enhancing 
teachers’ Pedagogical Content Knowledge (PCK) through targeted professional development. In response to the 
growing demand for effective Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) education, the study 
examines the impact of a competency-based STEM training program on teachers’ PCK and science teaching 
competencies. Grounded in a quasi-experimental design, the study involved 48 junior high school teachers in 
Oman, randomly assigned to experimental (n = 24) or control (n = 24) groups. Data were analyzed using t-test 
and MANOVA, with pre- and post-test scores from the Teachers’ PCK of STEM Test (TPCKST), revealing sig­
nificant improvements (p < 0.05) in PCK among teachers in the experimental group compared to the control 
group. These findings demonstrate the potential of targeted, context-specific professional development programs 
in enhancing teaching quality. The study recommends integrating STEM-focused training into teacher education 
and in-service programs to advance STEM education and help science educators develop.
1. Introduction
1.1. Importance of STEM education
The importance of STEM fields in promoting innovation, propelling 
economic progress, and resolving intricate global problems is gaining 
more recognition in the changing educational landscape (Hallstr¨om 
et al., 2023; Shahat, Al Bahri, & Al-Balushi, 2024). As it is elsewhere 
across the globe, strategically developing teachers’ competence in STEM 
courses is crucial in Oman to meet the worldwide need for quality STEM 
education (Shahat et al., 2023). To improve educational outcomes and 
guarantee that students have the knowledge and abilities to succeed in a 
world that is constantly evolving, it is necessary to align science edu­
cation in Oman with a model of best practices (Ambroˇz et al., 2023; 
Shahat & Al Amri, 2023).
1.2. Challenges in STEM education
Despite international recognition of the importance of STEM 
education, Oman faces persistent challenges in this area. The 2023 
Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) 
revealed that Omani students ranked 44th in mathematics and 40th in 
science among participating countries. Alarmingly, only 8 % of Omani 
students achieved proficiency levels in mathematics, and a similar figure 
of 7 % was recorded for science (von Davier et al., 2024). Here, profi­
ciency refers to the minimum level of knowledge, skills, and under­
standing students are expected to achieve, representing the standard 
that indicates mastery of essential concepts and abilities for their grade 
level or age group (von Davier et al., 2024).
These academic challenges are further exacerbated by systemic is­
sues in teacher preparedness. Over 60 % of middle school teachers in 
Oman reported feeling inadequately prepared to implement interdisci­
plinary STEM approaches in their classrooms, highlighting a critical 
need for targeted professional development (Shahat & Al Amri, 2023). 
These challenges are compounded by a lack of national frameworks and 
targeted professional development programs to equip teachers with the 
PCK necessary for effective STEM instruction.

1.3. Importance of PCK for teachers
In addition to STEM competence, the development of teachers’ PCK 
is fundamental to enhancing the quality of science education. PCK, 
which integrates subject matter expertise with effective teaching stra­
tegies, enables educators to teach complex scientific concepts in acces­
sible and engaging ways (Vasquez et al., 2020). However, studies reveal 
that many science teachers in Oman face significant difficulties in 
mastering and applying PCK, stemming from a reliance on traditional 
teaching methods, limited professional development opportunities, and 
inadequate 
exposure 
to 
innovative, 
inquiry-based 
pedagogies 
(Ambusaidi et al., 2021). The absence of sufficient teacher training in 
these areas often results in ineffective science instruction and dimin­
ished student engagement in STEM fields. To address these issues, there 
is a critical need for research focusing on how to enhance teachers’ PCK, 
particularly in contexts like Oman, where cultural and linguistic factors 
play a significant role in shaping educational practices, influencing both 
teaching methods and student learning outcomes. For instance, the 
unique cultural context of Oman may affect how STEM concepts are 
communicated and understood, while linguistic barriers could hinder 
the adoption of interdisciplinary approaches. By identifying and 
addressing the gaps in PCK, this study aims to contribute to the broader 
goal of improving science education and fostering a generation of stu­
dents who are equipped with the skills needed for the 21st century.
1.4. Global and regional perspectives
In Arab and other non-Western contexts, STEM education faces 
similar challenges related to cultural, linguistic, and systemic con­
straints. For instance, studies in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) 
countries highlight issues such as gender disparities in training oppor­
tunities, limited access to interdisciplinary STEM resources, and the 
reliance on traditional, lecture-based teaching methods (Ambusaidi 
et al., 2022). Research from countries like Malaysia and Indonesia 
similarly underscores the need for culturally adapted STEM training 
programs that address local educational priorities while aligning with 
global best practices (Gardner et al., 2019; Loeng & Szalay, 2020). These 
studies emphasize the importance of tailored approaches, which is why 
this study situates Oman’s unique cultural and educational context at 
the forefront of discussions on curricula design.
1.5. Purpose of research
STEM education is globally recognized as essential for preparing 
students to meet the demands of a rapidly evolving world. However, 
research on the effectiveness of national training programs designed to 
enhance teachers’ PCK in STEM education remains limited, particularly 
in the context of Oman (Shahat, Al Balushi, et al., 2024, Shahat, Al 
Bahri, & Al-Balushi, 2024). In Oman, where efforts to advance and 
integrate STEM education are still emerging (Al-Balushi et al., 2022; 
Shahat & Al-Balushi, 2023), there is a pressing need to explore ap­
proaches that align with the country’s educational priorities and cultural 
specificities.
This study aims to address this research gap by evaluating the impact 
of a newly developed national training program designed to improve 
teachers’ STEM-based skills and PCK. By drawing on and extending prior 
studies, such as those by Capobianco and Rupp (2014) and Shahat, Al 
Balushi, et al. (2024), the research adapts existing frameworks to fit 
within Oman’s unique sociocultural landscape. Looking at Western 
contexts, Capobianco and Rupp (2014) promoted the role of profes­
sional development programs that enhance teachers’ PCK through en­
gineering design and inquiry-based learning. Shahat, Al Balushi, et al. 
(2024) explored the effectiveness of a STEM-focused training program in 
improving interdisciplinary teaching skills and PCK within an Arab 
regional setting. Despite their contributions, neither study explicitly 
addresses the cultural and linguistic factors unique to STEM education in 
Oman.
The program integrates interdisciplinary, hands-on, and reflective 
teaching strategies to support teachers’ PCK development. Furthermore, 
this study seeks to position Oman as a model for culturally adapted 
STEM education reform.
Based on the above, the current study sought to answer the following 
three research questions (RQs): 
RQ1: What are the effects of group (Experimental vs. Control) and 
gender (male vs. female) on teachers’ PCK following exposure to a 
STEM-based training program?
RQ2: What is the effect of a STEM training program on teachers’ 
PCK?
RQ3: Is there an effect of gender on teachers’ PCK following exposure 
to the STEM based training program?
2. Theoretical background
2.1. Historical development of adaptive STEM education
Initially focused on science and mathematics, STEM education 
expanded to include engineering and technology, in order to foster 
innovation, problem-solving, and critical thinking (Gardner et al., 2019; 
Hurd, 1975). Key milestones in STEM teacher training include the shift 
to inquiry-based learning and the incorporation of engineering design 
principles, both of which promote active exploration and the practical 
application of theory (Lo, 2021). In addition, integrating reflective 
practices (see Fig. 1.), as proposed by Sch¨on (1983), has enhanced STEM 
training programs and made them more. Moreover, recent technological 
advancements, such as AI and AR, have revolutionized STEM education 
by allowing more personalized and engaging approaches (Ambusaidi 
et al., 2022; Loeng & Szalay, 2020).
Interdisciplinary STEM education has gained global recognition as 
an approach that fosters critical thinking and innovation. Research has 
shown that interdisciplinary instruction enhances students’ ability to 
apply knowledge across different domains, improving problem-solving 
skills and scientific reasoning (Bybee, 2013; English, 2016). This 
aligns with constructivist learning theories, which describe knowledge 
as being actively constructed rather than passively received, reinforcing 
the importance of hands-on, collaborative learning environments over 
traditional lecture based classes. Constructivist STEM education draws 
on Piaget’s (1970) theory of cognitive development and Vygotsky’s 
(1978) social constructivism, emphasizing the role of scaffolding, 
collaboration, and real-world application in knowledge acquisition. 
These perspectives provide the foundation for student-centered 
instructional models, encouraging teachers to facilitate active engage­
ment rather than direct instruction (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007; Taber, 
2010). In countries with highly ranked education systems such as 
Finland and Singapore, STEM teacher training incorporates interdisci­
plinary pedagogies that bridge science, technology, and engineering 
through real-world applications. Examples like these highlight best 
practices that can inform STEm education in Oman (Marginson et al., 
2013).

A key strength of the experiential STEM approach is its emphasis on 
active, inquiry-based learning. While many STEM initiatives prioritize 
theoretical knowledge, this model equips teachers to engage students in 
hands-on activities and real-world applications. The STEM model 
examined in this paper integrates engineering design principles (Shahat 
et al., 2022, 2023; 2024d) with reflective teaching practices (Sch¨on, 
1983). These components ensure the model’s adaptability to diverse 
student and teacher needs in several ways. First, unlike many interna­
tional STEM frameworks that adopt a one-size-fits-all approach, this 
model is designed with built-in flexibility, allowing teachers to tailor 
their instructional strategies to different learning styles, cultural con­
texts, and skill levels. Engineering design principles encourage

problem-solving, creativity, and iterative thinking, which can be 
adapted more effectively to meet students’ specific needs compared to 
rigidly structured curricula. Second, while global STEM models often 
emphasize content knowledge and interdisciplinary integration, they 
frequently overlook cultural and linguistic barriers that impact 
instructional effectiveness in non-Western contexts. This STEM model 
addresses such challenges by incorporating gender-sensitive ap­
proaches, bilingual materials, and reflective practices, ensuring its 
relevance in culturally diverse educational settings. Third, many inter­
national STEM programs focus on pre-defined teaching strategies 
without allowing for adapting to classroom realities. By contrast, this 
model integrates reflective practices that foster ongoing assessment and 
improvement, enabling teachers to refine their instructional methods 
based on student feedback. Additionally, a lack of resources and a reli­
ance on traditional teaching methods remain significant obstacles in 
many regions, particularly in non-Western education systems. This 
model explicitly addresses these limitations by integrating low-cost, 
contextually relevant solutions that do not require extensive infra­
structural changes. By expanding on Capobianco and Rupp’s (2014)
work and integrating inquiry-based learning with engineering design, 
this model moves beyond traditional STEM frameworks to offer a 
transformative, inclusive, and culturally responsive approach to STEM 
education. These advantages position it as a more adaptable and effec­
tive alternative to many international models.
Moreover, constructivist and interdisciplinary STEM approaches 
have been linked to higher student engagement and retention in STEM 
fields. Studies by Johnson and Sondergeld (2015) emphasize that 
interdisciplinary teaching improves STEM identity formation, particu­
larly among underrepresented student groups, making it a crucial 
strategy in culturally diverse regions. This means that in places like 
Oman where STEM identity formation is something lacking (Shahat, Al 
Balushi, et al., 2024).
To enhance STEM integration into existing teacher training frame­
works, this study incorporates principles of adult learning theory 
(Merriam, 2013) alongside interdisciplinary approaches. Adult learners, 
particularly teachers, benefit from training models that are self-directed, 
experiential, and contextually relevant. Hence, the inclusion of collab­
orative, problem-based learning experiences is important to ensure that 
teacher training can help teachers develop the pedagogical expertise 
needed to teach STEM subjects (Kolmos et al., 2014).
According to Shulman (1986) and later expanded by Grossman 
(1990), pedagogical expertise is a critical element of Pedagogical Con­
tent Knowledge (PCK). This concept, combining subject matter 
knowledge, pedagogy, and an understanding of students’ needs, en­
hances teaching quality and student outcomes, particularly in STEM 
(Baumert et al., 2010). Recent studies highlight that high STEM-specific 
PCK allows teachers to be adaptive and modify instruction dynamically 
based on student misconceptions and learning progressions (Park & 
Oliver, 2008). This aligns with the constructivist view that effective 
teaching involves responsive scaffolding and inquiry-based exploration 
rather than static content delivery (Fosnot & Perry, 2005).
2.1.1. Practical implementation and cultural adaptation of STEM in Oman
Practical implementation guidelines from institutions like NASA 
(2015) and the Museum of Science in Museum of Science of Boston 
(2016) lend evidence-based support for inquiry-based STEM approaches 
and offer guidelines for general implementation. However, Oman’s 
unique cultural characteristics means it has specific needs in terms of 
STEM teacher training. For example, gender disparities in STEM edu­
cation present a critical challenge that must be addressed (Moss-Racusin 
et al., 2012). Studies indicate that female students often face systemic 
barriers to STEM engagement, including societal expectations, limited 
access to STEM resources, and fewer female role models in STEM fields 
(UNESCO, 2020). In Oman, these barriers are further compounded by 
gender-segregated schooling. Integrating gender-inclusive STEM 
teaching strategies—such as female mentorship programs, role model 
initiatives, and gender-responsive curricula—can help mitigate these 
disparities and promote equitable STEM participation (Cheryan et al., 
2017). Professional development programs must account for this 
segregation by offering tailored approaches that ensure both groups 
benefit equally (Shahat & Al-Balushi, 2023). Language also presents a 
significant barrier; with Arabic as the primary medium of instruction, 
STEM concepts, which are often rooted in English, require careful 
translation and adaptation to maintain accuracy while aligning with 
cultural nuances (Shahat et al., 2023, 2024a, 2024c, 2024d). Moreover, 
the hierarchical structure of teacher-student relationships in many 
Omani classrooms may hinder the adoption of student-centered, 
inquiry-based teaching methodologies (Shahat, Al Balushi, et al., 2024).
The consequences of inadequate STEM training in Oman extend 
beyond student performance and teacher preparedness, impacting 
higher education, workforce readiness, and innovation at a national 
level. Omani universities report low enrollment rates in STEM programs, 
with many students lacking the foundational STEM skills needed for 
success in higher education. This has led to high dropout rates or a shift 
to non-STEM disciplines (Ambusaidi et al., 2022). This shortage of 
well-prepared graduates is results in employers having difficulty in

that address these challenges, such as fostering collaborative learning 
among teachers, utilizing culturally relevant examples in training ma­
terials, and promoting gradual shifts toward more interactive peda­
gogical practices (Ambusaidi et al., 2022; Gardner et al., 2019). By 
addressing these unique cultural dynamics, STEM teacher training in 
Oman enhances local educational outcomes and serves as a model for 
culturally adapted educational reforms in other non-Western contexts 
(Shahat et al., 2022, 2024a, 2024b, 2024c, 2024d).
The training program developed as part of this study equips teachers 
with strategies for content comprehension and uncovering relationships 
within STEM subjects, thereby enhancing the teaching-learning process 
(Shahat & Al Amri, 2023). Supported by reflective practice frameworks, 
assessment tools, and collaborative learning models, the program aims 
to foster continuous professional development and self-assessment (e.g., 
Thompson, 2021). By adhering to adult learning theory and construc­
tivist principles, and by encouraging teachers to integrate their own 
unique professional experiences into their teaching, the proposed STEM 
model can be flexibly incorporated into Oman’s national training 
framework. As a result, teachers will be empowered to collaborate, 
reflect and engage in self-directed learning over the long term (Loeng & 
Szalay, 2020).
3. Method
3.1. Research design
The study employed a quasi-experimental design, specifically the 
pretest-posttest control group design, to examine the impact of the 
STEM-based training program on teachers’ PCK. This design was 
selected for its ability to provide robust evidence of the program’s 
effectiveness while addressing practical and ethical constraints. Partic­
ipants were randomly assigned to either the experimental (EXP) or 
control (CON) groups, and data collection instruments were adminis­
tered both before and after the implementation of the STEM-based 
training program.
The quasi-experimental design offers several advantages that make it 
particularly suitable for this study. First, it facilitates direct comparisons 
between groups, allowing the effects of the STEM-based training pro­
gram to be isolated and measured while reducing the influence of 
external variables. The inclusion of a control group strengthens causal 
inferences and enhances the reliability of the findings (George & Mal­
lery, 2018). Second, the design takes into consideration logistical and 
ethical challenges that often arise in educational settings. For example, 
randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are difficult to implement in schools 
due to administrative constraints and ethical considerations such as 
withholding potentially beneficial interventions from participants. The 
quasi-experimental approach balances these concerns by maintaining 
methodological rigor while ensuring equitable participation (Smith 
et al., 2024). Third, the design is particularly well-suited to the Omani 
educational context, where classrooms are naturally grouped by schools 
and randomization can be challenging. By using a pretest-posttest 
format, the study captured baseline data and measured changes over 
time, providing a comprehensive understanding of the training pro­
gram’s impact. Previous studies have successfully used this approach to 
evaluate professional development programs and their effect on teacher 
competencies (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Gardner et al., 2019).
3.2. Participants
The research, conducted between November and December 2023, 
received ethical approval (no. 28231038368) from the Research Ethics 
Committee of the Ministry of Education in Oman, ensuring adherence to 
the highest ethical standards. Invitations were sent to middle schools in 
the capital of Oman, specifically those categorized as junior high 
schools, to recruit teachers for the study. Teachers were provided with 
detailed information about the study’s goals and objectives, and the 
authors’ contact information was shared to address any questions. A 
total of 48 teachers responded and provided written consent to partici­
pate. The participants were then randomly assigned into two groups of 
24 each: the experimental group and the control group.
Random assignment into groups was performed using a computer­
ized randomization tool. This process was carefully designed to elimi­
nate selection bias and ensure that both groups were equivalent at the 
outset, increasing the validity of the comparisons between the experi­
mental and control groups. Specifically, the randomization aimed to 
control for potentially confounding variables such as teaching experi­
ence, grade level taught, and prior exposure to STEM-related profes­
sional development. By using a systematic and unbiased method for 
group allocation, the study minimized the risk of systematic differences 
that could have influenced the outcomes.
All participants fulfilled the following inclusion criteria: (a) a mini­
mum of five years of teaching experience, (b) teach in the capital of 
Oman, and (c) work with science students in grades 5–7. Teachers in the 
experimental group were given the opportunity to participate in the 
STEM-based training program that the authors developed in accordance 
with the STEM training framework, whereas teachers in the control 
group continued with their regular professional development activities.
Despite the rigorous selection and randomization process, potential 
biases in participant selection must be acknowledged. First, the study’s 
recruitment was limited to teachers in urban schools within the capital 
of Oman, which may not fully represent teachers’ experiences from rural 
or under-resourced schools. Differences in access to technology, pro­
fessional development opportunities, and classroom infrastructure could 
influence the generalizability of findings to the broader national context. 
Second, participation was voluntary, which may have introduced self- 
selection bias. Teachers who chose to participate might have had a 
stronger interest in STEM education or greater motivation for profes­
sional development compared to their non-participating peers, poten­
tially affecting the study’s external validity. Additionally, although 
random assignment was used to balance prior STEM exposure, indi­
vidual differences in motivation, instructional styles, and teaching phi­
losophy could still have influenced the effectiveness of the training.
It is important to note that all practicing science teachers in Oman 
are required to participate in a professional development program 
divided into two modules. The first module targets beginning teachers, 
while the second focuses on experienced teachers. Both modules cover 
the knowledge and skills required for teaching in general and STEM 
strategies in particular. Randomly assigning teachers further reinforced 
the reliability of the study by ensuring comparable baseline conditions 
for both groups.

3.3. Instrumentation
3.3.1. Teachers’ Pedagogical Content Knowledge of STEM test (TPCKST)
To evaluate the participants’ STEM PCK, the authors developed a 
scenario-based test tailored to the Omani context. The test took local 
educational norms into account, including gender-segregated schools, 
by using neutral scenarios applicable to all teachers and incorporating 
culturally relevant examples tied to daily life in Oman. It was designed 
in Arabic, the primary language of instruction, with careful translation 
of STEM terminology to ensure clarity and accessibility. The content 
aligned with Oman’s grade 5–7 science curriculum, emphasizing inter­
disciplinary approaches and practical relevance. The test assessed 
teachers’ PCK in terms of teaching procedures in general, teaching 
planning, content knowledge, methods of correcting students’ mis­
conceptions, and an understanding of strategies and teaching proced­
ures required to teach science from the perspective of the STEM model 
(Baumert et al., 2010; Bostock, 2019; Grossman, 1990; Hill et al., 2005; 
Shulman, 1986). The TPCKST covers the following STEM model stages 
(see Fig. 1): 1) Task Assignment, 2) planning, 3) building/testing and 
evaluating/redesigning, 4) discussion, and 5) assessment. The stages 
reflect a cyclical process, and the last assessment phase is followed by a 
revaluation of all the steps. The evaluation of teachers’ STEM teaching 
practices is divided into three main categories: (1) definition and 
formulation of objectives related to the STEM Education Model, (2) the 
procedures and steps the teacher implements informed by the model, 
and (3) the skills that the teacher develops through the utilization of the 
model. These categories are closely related to the six main questions in 
the test, each of which contains sub-questions addressing different 
teaching scenarios a teacher may encounter. Q1 (teaching procedures) 
and Q5 (methods of teaching) directly assess the second category, 
evaluating the procedures and steps the teacher uses based on the STEM 
model. Q2 (teaching planning) and Q3 (content knowledge) are related 
to the first category, focusing on the teacher’s ability to define and 
formulate objectives and demonstrate a deep understanding of the STEM 
content. Q4 (methods of correcting students’ misconceptions) and Q6 
(assessment) address the third category, examining the teacher’s skills in 
identifying and addressing student misconceptions and effectively 
assessing student understanding, both of which are crucial for skill 
development using the STEM model. By providing solutions to these 
scenarios, teachers demonstrate their competency in defining objectives, 
implementing procedures, and developing skills, ensuring a compre­
hensive evaluation of their STEM teaching practices. An answer key was 
prepared after presenting the test to a group of experts in science edu­
cation for completion and feedback. The TPCKST scores for each ques­
tion range between 1 and 3. Fig. 2 illustrates an example for one 
scenario.
3.3.2. Treatment
3.3.2.1. Experimental group activities. The experimental group partici­
pated in a three-week training program designed to enhance teachers’ 
competencies in STEM education. The program utilized teaching 
methods aligned with the STEM framework and aimed to equip teachers 
with the skills needed to effectively plan lessons, address students’ 
misconceptions, and implement diverse instructional strategies. These 
strategies included active learning, problem-solving, inquiry-based 
learning, and applying STEM concepts to real-world scenarios. The 
training program was carefully structured into four components sup­
ported by workshops, collaborative sessions, and continuous 
mentorship.
The first component of the program was called Theoretical Foundation 
and consisted of two 3-h sessions. These sessions were led by STEM 
education specialists who provided detailed instruction on advanced 
content knowledge, and the theory behind pedagogical strategies such 
as integrating technology into teaching. Teachers explored the princi­
ples of STEM education, focusing on engineering design, interdisci­
plinary integration, and fostering analytical problem-solving skills. 
Following the STEM education steps (see Table 1), teachers learned the 
theoretical background related to structuring tasks, identifying inte­
gration opportunities, and employing engineering design in lesson 
planning. Interactive lectures, discussions, and case studies ensured 
participants could connect theoretical concepts to classroom 
applications.
The second component, Practical Training Modules, lasted 12 h, 
divided into four 3-h sessions. This stage emphasized hands-on experi­
ences, enabling teachers to design STEM-based lesson plans, create 
instructional materials, and simulate classroom practices. Teachers 
applied the engineering design process through activities such as the 
water filtration challenge (detailed in Table 2), which guided them in 
assigning tasks, planning, and building solutions. Continuous mentor­
ship and coaching provided personalized support to help teachers refine 
their methods. Peer reviews and collaborative projects encouraged 
participants to critically evaluate and improve their instructional 
designs.
The third component, Collaborative Learning Sessions, was covered 
over 6 h in two 3-h sessions. These sessions emphasized teamwork and 
peer learning, creating a dynamic environment where teachers worked 
in groups to solve complex STEM-related challenges. Guided by the 
STEM framework in Table 1, teachers developed creative solutions and 
refined their approaches collaboratively. Activities included brain­
storming sessions, group discussions, and presentations, which pro­
moted teamwork, critical thinking, and the practical application of 
theoretical knowledge.
The final component, Reflection and Feedback, consisted of a 3-h 
session dedicated to analyzing and consolidating learning outcomes. 
Teachers evaluated their progress, discussed challenges, and linked their

3.3.3. Validity and reliability
The validation process for the TPCKST (Technological Pedagogical 
Content Knowledge for STEM Teaching) instrument involved multiple 
rigorous steps to ensure its reliability and relevance. Content validity 
was established through an expert review conducted by seven specialists 
in science education who evaluated the alignment of the test items with 
STEM pedagogy.
The instrument was piloted with 15 teachers, during which revisions 
were made to improve clarity and contextual suitability. Interrater 
reliability, assessed using Cohen’s Kappa (κ), ranged from 0.74 to 0.80, 
reflecting substantial agreement (Field, 2009). Construct validity was 
demonstrated by significant correlations between TPCKST scores and 
observed classroom practices (r = 0.70–0.85), supporting the in­
strument’s ability to measure relevant constructs effectively. Further, a 
reliability analysis confirmed the robustness of the test items. The 
test-retest correlations ranged from r = 0.88 to 0.97 (Cho, 2024), indi­
cating high consistency over time.
The training framework was validated by two professors and five 
teachers specializing in science education and curriculum development. 
These experts thoroughly reviewed the framework’s content, structure, 
and alignment with current educational standards and best practices. 
Intraclass correlations (ICC) were calculated to evaluate the level of 
agreement among reviewers, with values ranging from 0.81 to 0.87, 
indicating strong reliability and consistency (Field, 2009). The collective 
feedback from these experts was used to refine the framework, ensuring 
its effectiveness and applicability in enhancing STEM teaching.
3.4. Data analysis
The data analysis was conducted using IBM® SPSS® Statistics, 
Version 25 (George & Mallery, 2018), to address the research questions 
(RQs). To examine RQ1 (effects of group and gender) and RQ2 (effect of 
the training program on teachers’ PCK), a Two-Way MANOVA was 
performed on the dependent variables: teaching procedures, lesson 
planning, content knowledge, methods for addressing misconceptions, 
teaching strategies, and assessment. MANOVA was chosen to account for 
the interdependence of these variables and identify multivariate effects 
while minimizing Type I error. For RQ3 (effect of gender), interaction 
effects between group and gender were analyzed using the Wilks’ 
Lambda criterion to determine overall significance before conducting 
follow-up tests. Preliminary analyses included a t-test for independent 
samples to confirm baseline equivalence between the experimental and 
control groups.
Post-hoc univariate ANOVAs were conducted for each dependent 
variable when the MANOVA indicated significant main effects. A Bon­
ferroni correction was applied to control for Type I errors arising from 
multiple comparisons, adjusting the significance threshold by dividing 
the conventional alpha level (0.05) by the number of comparisons made. 
This approach ensured a stricter significance criterion, reducing the 
likelihood of false positives while maintaining adequate statistical 
power. Additionally, effect sizes (partial eta-squared, η2) were calcu­
lated for all comparisons to quantify the practical significance of 
observed effects and provide a clearer interpretation of training 
effectiveness.
Assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variance-covariance 
matrices, and absence of multicollinearity were tested prior to con­
ducting the MANOVA. Specifically, normality was assessed through 
skewness and kurtosis tests, alongside visual inspections of histograms 
and Q-Q plots. Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances was used to 
confirm that variance differences did not significantly impact the results 
of individual ANOVAs. Box’s M test was used to assess homogeneity of 
variance-covariance matrices, and the absence of multicollinearity was 
confirmed by ensuring that the dependent variables were not highly 
correlated (correlation coefficients <0.80). These tests verified that the 
assumptions required for MANOVA and the subsequent univariate 
ANOVAs were met, ensuring the validity of the statistical analyses.

methods of teaching, and Q6 = assessment. Specifically, a two-way 
multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was employed to assess 
the main effects of the training and gender and their potential interac­

across various outcome measures. Additionally, by considering the po­

procedures, teaching planning, content knowledge, methods of cor­

between the experimental group and the control group. Therefore, the 
training had a significant impact on the participants’ teaching proced­
ures, teaching planning, content knowledge, methods of correcting 
students’ misconceptions, methods of teaching, and assessment when 
considering them as a set. Specifically, the significant effect of group 
indicates that teachers in the experimental group demonstrated more 
effective teaching procedures, better lesson planning, deeper content 
knowledge, improved strategies for addressing misconceptions, 
enhanced teaching methods, and more effective assessment practices 
compared to their control group counterparts. The lack of gender dif­
ferences confirms that the training program successfully addressed po­
tential disparities in learning outcomes, ensuring equitable benefits for 
all participants.

5. Discussion
With regard to RQ1, this study revealed significant improvements in 
PCK across multiple domains for teachers in the training group, 
including teaching procedures, planning, content knowledge, address­
ing misconceptions, teaching methods, and assessment strategies. These 
findings align with Magnusson et al.’s (1999) PCK model, which in­
tegrates subject matter knowledge, instructional strategies, and stu­
dents’ learning needs. Specifically, Magnusson et al. (1999) concluded 
that effective PCK development requires teachers to not only master 
content but also adapt their teaching approaches to address diverse 
student perspectives and challenges. Notable improvements in teaching 
planning (Q2, η2 = 0.13, which represents a moderate effect size) and 
content knowledge (Q3, η2 = 0.16, a moderate to large effect) highlight 
the importance of sequencing content and making subject matter 
accessible through structured methods. These effect sizes indicate that 
the training program had a meaningful impact on how teachers struc­
tured lessons and deepened their subject knowledge, which are crucial 
components of effective STEM instruction. For instance, teachers 
demonstrated an enhanced ability to organize lessons in a way that 
builds on prior knowledge and scaffolds learning effectively. These re­
sults underscore the program’s success in equipping teachers with 
practical tools to facilitate high-quality STEM instruction, ensuring that 
content is both engaging and comprehensible for students with diverse 
needs.
For RQ2, the strongest impact of the training program was 
addressing misconceptions (Q4, η2 = 0.28, a large effect size), under­
scoring the critical role of diagnosing and remediating student mis­
understandings, as a cornerstone of effective teaching. This substantial 
effect suggests that the training program was highly effective in 
improving teachers’ ability to recognize and correct students’ concep­
tual errors, which is vital for long-term retention and deeper compre­
hension of STEM concepts. By equipping teachers with strategies to 
identify and address misconceptions, the program enabled them to 
create more accurate conceptual frameworks for students, thereby 
fostering deeper understanding and long-term retention of STEM con­
cepts. This highlights the importance of misconception-focused peda­
gogy in improving instructional quality and student outcomes. 
Improvements in teaching methods (Q5, η2 = 0.10, a small to moderate 
effect size) and assessment strategies (Q6, η2 = 0.17, a moderate effect 
size) align with STEM education models that advocate for data-driven 
instructional strategies (Magnusson et al., 1999). While these effect 
sizes are smaller than those for addressing misconceptions, they still 
indicate that the training program had a meaningful impact on teachers’ 
ability to implement structured teaching strategies and use assessments 
to inform their instruction. Specifically, the training program enabled 
teachers to adopt more evidence-based approaches, such as using 
formative assessments to inform their teaching practices and tailoring 
instruction to meet students’ individual needs. These findings reflect 
Magnusson et al.’s emphasis on the integration of subject matter 
knowledge with responsive and adaptive teaching strategies, demon­
strating how data-driven methods can enhance both teaching effec­
tiveness and student learning outcomes in STEM education. Overall, the 
results highlight the program’s success in equipping teachers to imple­
ment evidence-based practices and adapt their instruction to address 
students’ evolving needs.
While the results demonstrate significant improvements, the rela­
tively low percentage of variance explained by the independent vari­
ables (13 %–31 %, indicating a moderate influence of the training 
program) suggests that additional factors likely influence the observed 
outcomes. For instance, contextual elements such as school resources, 
administrative support, and student characteristics might play signifi­
cant roles. Moreover, personal teacher attributes such as intrinsic 
motivation, years of experience, and prior professional development 
could also contribute to the effectiveness of the training. Future studies

For RQ3, the absence of significant gender differences (η2 = 0.06, a 
small effect size) highlights the inclusivity of the training program, 
which is in line with Omani policies promoting gender equity in edu­
cation. This finding aligns with culturally responsive teaching models 
that emphasize tailoring educational initiatives to local sociocultural 
contexts and gender dynamics (Gay, 2018). The program’s design, 
which integrates problem-solving and engineering principles, aligns 
with STEM education frameworks and can hopefully address systemic 
gaps identified in Oman’s STEM education system (Ambusaidi et al., 
2022; Shahat, Al Bahri, & Al-Balushi, 2024). While the primary focus of 
the program is on enhancing pedagogical and content knowledge, its 
inclusive approach ensures that it benefits all participants, regardless of 
gender. This is particularly significant in the Omani context, where 
gender equity in education is a key policy priority. By fostering an 
environment that encourages equal participation and engagement, the 
program supports the broader goal of creating equitable STEM learning 
opportunities for both male and female teachers and students.
Internationally, best practices in STEM education emphasize inter­
disciplinary learning, hands-on application, and continuous professional 
development. Models from Finland, Singapore, and the United States 
demonstrate these principles through structured training programs for 
teachers, government-supported STEM initiatives, and technology- 
integrated classrooms (Finnish National Board of Education, 2016; Na­
tional Institute for STEM Education, 2025; National Institute for STEM 
Education, n.d.). For instance, Finland’s Phenomenon-Based Learning 
approach fosters interdisciplinary connections (Finnish National Board 
of Education, 2016), Singapore’s National Institute of Education pro­
vides continuous STEM-focused teacher training (National Institute for 
STEM Education, 2025), and the United States promotes project-based 
learning through initiatives like STEM-focused charter schools and 
partnerships with industry leaders (National Institute for STEM Educa­
tion, 2025). This study builds on these global frameworks by tailoring 
STEM teacher training to non-Western cultural contexts, incorporating 
bilingual resources and gender-sensitive approaches that many Western 
models lack (Shahat, Al Bahri, & Al-Balushi, 2024). The inclusion of 
engineering design principles aligns with international strategies but 
ensures adaptability to Oman’s educational priorities (Shahat et al., 
2023). Strengthening alignment with these global models through 
further comparative studies could enhance the scalability of this training 
approach.
Grounding these results in established theoretical frameworks can 
help explain the program’s effectiveness while advancing understanding 
of PCK and STEM education models in unique settings. First of all, the 
integration of engineering design principles leading to critical thinking 
and interdisciplinary learning aligns with recent findings (e.g., 
Hallstr¨om et al., 2023; Linh, 2021). Furthermore, this study contributes 
to international research by adding weight to the idea that inquiry-based 
and interdisciplinary training models cause significant improvements in 
teacher confidence and instructional effectiveness (Darling-Hammond 
et al., 2017; Gardner et al., 2019). It also supports other regional studies 
that have demonstrated the impact context-specific adaptations of STEM 
education can have on addressing challenges such as gender disparities 
and resource limitations (Ambusaidi et al., 2022; Shahat et al., 2023).
By considering national training programs, this study addresses gaps 
in the literature and offers practical recommendations for advancing 
STEM education through professional development. One important 
aspect of quality teaching that should be addressed by policymakers and 
educators in professional development programs is PCK (Shulman, 
1986), which has been shown to directly correlate with improved stu­
dent outcomes (Bostock, 2019; Grossman, 1990; Shahat et al., 2023). 
Thus, the creation of a validated metric for evaluating STEM-focused 
PCK in this study represents a significant advancement, enabling an 
investigation in to the impact of training on planning, teaching, and 
assessment.
A significant contribution of this study is that is could be used as the 
basis for intercultural comparisons between Western and Arab coun­
tries. Future research could adapt this model to investigate the stages of 
the STEM approach and PCK in varying educational contexts. The out­
lined framework could be expanded to develop integrated lesson plans 
for varied subjects and assess their impact on student outcomes. 
Extending the model to pre-service teacher training also offers addi­
tional avenues for exploration (Yata et al., 2020). This professional 
training can prepare pre-service teachers for the challenges of rapidly 
changing knowledge (Shahat, Al Balushi, et al., 2024).
Overall, this study underscores the pivotal role of teacher compe­
tence in quality STEM education. Recommendations include integrating 
problem-solving with engineering principles, aligning curricula with 
national objectives, and adopting culturally tailored training frame­
works. Furthermore, professional development must emphasize inclu­
sive teaching practices and equitable STEM education to bridge existing 
gaps and foster sustained educational improvements.
5.1. Global relevance and potential for international collaboration
The findings of this study have practical implications for STEM ed­
ucation globally, particularly in non-Western contexts with similar 
cultural and educational conditions. By addressing cultural and lin­
guistic barriers, gender disparities, and the need for culturally respon­
sive STEM training, this study provides insights for countries in the 
Middle East, North Africa, and Southeast Asia to design tailored STEM 
training programs. The integration of inquiry-based, interdisciplinary, 
and reflective practices within the training aligns with internationally 
recognized best practices that suggest global frameworks be adapted for 
local conditions (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Gardner et al., 2019),.
The potential for international collaboration in STEM training is 
significant. Collaborative efforts can facilitate the exchange of expertise 
and strategies, fostering innovative models to address shared challenges. 
For instance, partnerships between Oman and countries such as Finland, 
Singapore, and the United States could enable joint research, profes­
sional development, and cross-cultural exchanges. These countries were 
chosen for their global leadership in STEM education—Finland for its 
inquiry-based learning, Singapore for its rigorous teacher training, and 
the United States for its innovation-driven STEM industries. These col­
laborations could improve STEM education globally, and thereby 
address challenges such as workforce preparedness and adaptation to 
rapid technological innovation.
6. Conclusion and limitations
This study provides valuable insights into the effectiveness of 
training programs aimed at enhancing STEM competencies and PCK 
among science teachers in Oman. The findings, supported by statistical 
analyses, reveal significant improvements in the experimental group 
compared to the control group. A two-way MANOVA indicated a sub­
stantial main effect of the training program on multiple PCK components 
(Wilks’ Lambda = 0.52, F(6, 39) = 5.99, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.48), 
demonstrating notable gains in teaching procedures, lesson planning, 
content knowledge, methods for addressing misconceptions, teaching 
strategies, and assessment. These results underscore the potential of 
tailored professional development to address challenges in STEM edu­
cation, particularly within unique cultural and linguistic contexts. By 
focusing on a new national training program, the study contributes to 
the literature on culturally adapted STEM initiatives and offers practical 
implications for strengthening teacher competencies and improving 
student outcomes.
However, the study’s quasi-experimental design, involving 48 sci­
ence teachers, limits the generalizability of findings across Oman

7. Future directions
This research opens multiple paths of potential future exploration 
with significant implications for STEM education in Oman and beyond. 
Investigating the program’s impact on teacher attitudes, motivation, 
and self-efficacy, alongside longitudinal studies, could assess the sus­
tainability of PCK improvements and their influence on long-term stu­
dent outcomes.
The competency-based STEM training model addresses challenges 
such as the digital divide in less resourced areas and gender disparities. 
Research into integrating low-cost solutions, such as mobile platforms or 
offline tools, could ensure even more equitable STEM education. 
Training teachers to use available resources effectively would further 
support this goal. In addition, gender equity remains critical, especially 
in gender-segregated schools. Mentoring programs, role models, and 
inclusive teaching practices could address biases and enhance female 
engagement in STEM.
Future training programs should explore the integration of emerging 
technologies, such as Artificial Intelligence (AI), Augmented Reality 
(AR), and Virtual Reality (VR), into STEM education. AI-driven plat­
forms can personalize teacher training, providing real-time feedback 
and adaptive learning experiences. AR and VR applications could offer 
immersive laboratory simulations, allowing teachers to engage with 
virtual STEM experiments before applying them in classrooms. Addi­
tionally, AI-powered analytics could enhance formative assessment 
practices, helping educators identify student misconceptions and adjust 
instructional strategies accordingly. By leveraging these technologies, 
STEM teacher training in Oman could become more interactive, acces­
sible, and aligned with the demands of 21st-century education.
Addressing challenges such as digital divides, gender equity, and 
infrastructure limitations can be achieved through inquiry-based prac­
tices and strategic industry partnerships to bridge resource gaps. The 
integration of emerging technologies, including Artificial Intelligence 
(AI), Augmented Reality (AR), and Virtual Reality (VR), holds signifi­
cant potential in STEM instruction by enabling simulated laboratory 
experiences, personalizing learning paths, and enhancing student 
engagement with interactive content. Conducting comparative studies 
in similar educational contexts and aligning STEM initiatives with global 
frameworks, such as the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), could 
further refine the proposed model. Developing pilot programs that 
incorporate AI-driven instruction and analyzing their impact on teacher 
competency and student outcomes would provide valuable insights into 
the future of technology-enhanced STEM education. This approach has 
the potential to position Oman as a leader in innovative and culturally 
responsive STEM education, setting a benchmark for other non-Western 
educational systems.
