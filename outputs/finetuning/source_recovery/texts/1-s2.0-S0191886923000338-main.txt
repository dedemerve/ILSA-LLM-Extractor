A B S T R A C T  
In the 20th century, a strong increase in IQ test values was observed. However, the results of the last few decades 
point to an end to the FLynn effect or even its reversal. Scientists came to skeptical assumptions for the 21st 
century. There is hardly any research on the possible future development of intelligence. Here we present a 
statistical approach: Results from 24 student achievement surveys (TIMSS, PISA, PIRLS) from 1995 to 2019 serve 
as the data basis. In order to be included in the analysis, at least five measurements per country had to be 
available (N = 79 countries). For statistical estimation, trends (calculated via subtractions between measure­
ments within one student survey approach), linear regressions and nonlinear quadratic regressions (both using 
any given student achievement data) were applied. A correction for outliers smoothed the results. For the sample 
of 79 countries, IQs would increase by about 10 IQ points by 2100 (international mean IQ 101). We compare the 
results with results from theoretical models. The theoretical models came to less optimistic conclusions. The 
results depend on the selected statistical (e.g., linear or nonlinear, outlier correction or not) and theoretical 
assumptions. We recommend that also other authors address this research question.   
1. The FLynn effect in the 20th century 
In the 20th century, there was a steep increase in IQ test scores of 
about dec = 2.31 IQ points (dec: per decade) (Trahan et al., 2014) or dec 
= 2.83 IQ points (Pietschnig & Voracek, 2015). The increase in IQ test 
scores from generation to generation was first observed in soldiers in the 
United States before World War II (Rundquist, 1936). It was later seen 
throughout the developed world, followed by IQ increases in developing 
countries (Flynn, 2012). The American-New Zealand political scientist 
James Flynn's seminal 1984 review “The mean IQ of Americans: Massive 
gains 1932 to 1978” popularized the secular rise of IQ test performance 
in academia and the media. The effect was later labeled the “Flynn ef­
fect”. In the 1980s, Richard Lynn (1982) described the same phenome­
non for Japan. So two researchers, Lynn and Flynn, or Flynn and Lynn, 
rediscovered the secular IQ rise, which is why we call it the “FLynn 
effect”. 
At the outset of research on the FLynn effect, it was doubted whether 
the observed IQ test scores represent true increases in intelligence or just 
IQ inflation (e.g., Flynn, 1987). However, since there are similar de­
velopments in the supporting conditions of intelligence (e.g., in 
education, health and brain size) and in the indicators of cognitive 
abilities (e.g., in share of employees in professional occupations, 
educational degrees acquired and complexity of everyday life), most 
researchers now assume a real increase in cognitive ability (intelligence 
and knowledge). Some even assume that the rise in intelligence has been 
happening for centuries (Oesterdiekhoff, 2014). 
Another issue discussed was whether the gains were in g (general 
factor of intelligence) or in several specific abilities (te Nijenhuis & van 
der Flier, 2013). Despite the observable rise in intelligence test scores, 
other researchers assume that genotypic intelligence has been declining 
for some time.1 The “co-occurrence model” assumes two simultaneous 
processes: an increase in IQ test scores that has nothing to do with the g- 
factor of intelligence and a decrease in genetic intelligence that would be 
on g (Egeland, 2022; Woodley of Menie & Fernandes, 2015). Indicators 
for a more g-related decrease are for example a decline in mental speed 
and other basic cognitive processes or a lessening of important in­
novations and extremely intelligent people (Dutton & Woodley of 
Menie, 2018; Woodley, 2012). 
Nevertheless, results of the last decades point to an end of the FLynn 
effect or even to its reversal in the developed world. In countries with

(Dutton et al., 2016) or dec = −1.66 IQ points (Woodley of Menie et al., 
2018). The FLynn effect seems to be petering out, at least in western 
countries. This skepticism was also expressed in a survey of scientists in 
the field of FLynn effect research (Rindermann et al., 2017, Table 3). In 
addition, the quality of the framework conditions for intelligence 
development can hardly be improved or further improvements (e.g., in 
education and health) are accompanied by diminishing returns. Finally, 
low birth rates among the educated, especially among educated women, 
and immigration from countries with lower levels of education and 
cognitive ability weaken the demographic basis for further FLynn ef­
fects. This is particularly problematic because a further increase in 
cognitive demands in the world of work and life is to be expected (Hunt, 
1995; OECD, 2013). 
While developing countries are cognitively catching up (Meisenberg 
& Lynn, 2023; Meisenberg & Woodley, 2013), gains have reversed, 
particularly in German-speaking countries (see Figs. 1 and 2): Gains in 
cognitive abilities in the past have turned into losses, which nonlinear 
regression models show particularly well. 
In an overall view of the development of student achievement studies 
in Germany, the development looks just as critical: According to 
Woessmann (2021), who evaluated 43 surveys from PISA, TIMSS, IGLU 
and IQB over the last 20 years, ability levels are falling (Fig. 2). Between 
2010 and 2019, there was a decrease of −14 points on the student 
assessment scale (SASQ, M = 500, SD = 100) equivalent to −2.10 IQ in 
the intelligence test scale (IQ, M = 100, SD = 15). If we extrapolate that 
for another 80 years, Germany would be at an IQ of 82 in the year 2100. 
The consequences could be dramatic. They may not be as big as por­
trayed in the 2006 science fiction comedy “Idiocracy”, but given the 
theoretically and empirically well-supported premises, the implications 
are bound to be negative. 
Is this just a temporary decline? Or the tipping point from which 
things go irreversibly downhill (Dutton & Woodley of Menie, 2018)? 
How can we predict future trends? Other researchers such as Lynn 
(2011) or Nyborg (2012) have given general overviews or presented 
individual estimates for individual countries, we try to present results 
for a wide range of countries from developed to developing countries. As 
can already be seen in Fig. 1, two statistical models strive with each 
other: the assumption of linear progress (the straight line) and the 
assumption of a curvilinear relationship (the curve). 
More fundamentally, two competing prediction approaches can be 
distinguished: 
(1) First, the use of theoretical models based on causal theories and the 
use of knowledge (or assumptions) about the development of these de­
terminants. For example, there are assumptions about the development 
of education in 21st century (e.g., Lutz et al., 2014). Education is one of 
the most important determinants of cognitive ability (e.g., Ritchie & 
Tucker-Drob, 2018, 3.39 IQ points per school year). 
Another important factor is migration and the demographic change 
that it causes. One example from the USA: In the US student achieve­
ment study NAEP (National Assessment of Educational Progress), which 
measures reading ability and mathematics, the results in the different 
ethnic groups have increased over the years. However, as the percent­
ages of the relatively higher test-scoring ethnic group (Whites) decrease 
and the relatively lower-testing ethnic group (Hispanics) increase, the 
overall trend has remained flat since the 1970s (Rindermann & 
Thompson, 2013). This means that predictions hardly show any growth 
– but at least no decline either (Rindermann & Pichelmann, 2015). The 
fear of a similar development for Germany was expressed in 2011 by the 
German “PISA pope” Jürgen Baumert (2011): There would be gains in 
PISA, presumably through teaching and school reforms, but these would 
be “cancelled out” by socio-structural change. 
Previous prediction models considered several factors (Rindermann, 
2018; Rindermann & Thompson, 2011): the effects of asymmetric fertility 
(asymmetric birth rates and generation spans), migration (increasing 
shares of immigrants and different ability levels of immigrants), further 
environmental improvements (e.g., in health and education), and different 
limits to further improvements (easier lower-level environmental im­
provements, easier lower-level cognitive improvements, and different 
finishing lines or target points). These factors also interact, for example, 
further environmental improvement and easier cognitive growth at 
lower levels lead to reduced differences between natives and immi­
grants, within societies across generations, and between societies as a 
difference between old residents and new immigrants. 
However, any theory-based prediction is difficult and delicate for 
two reasons: Initially, we have limited knowledge and all models will 
only take into account a limited set of factors. For example, the aging of 
societies has not been taken into account so far, partly because tests are 
usually administered only to younger samples. However, the test per­
formance of middle-aged and older individuals is also relevant for so­
cietal development (e.g., Hanushek et al., 2015). In particular, there are 
also political-ideological problems: If asymmetric child rates and 
immigration are relevant conditional factors, but both are seen as 
politically incorrect (“dysgenics”, “eugenics”, “racism”, “xenophobia”) 
and therefore not addressed, then models become fundamentally wrong. 
(2) Second, an omission of theoretical considerations and a pure use 
of statistical methods of prediction based on an extrapolation of past devel­
opment. The prerequisite for this is the existence of multiple measure­
ment time points, the more the better. This requirement is also a 
disadvantage because for many countries, including very important ones 
(China, India, nearly entire sub-Saharan Africa), such measurements are 
not available in sufficient numbers. In addition, one cannot completely 
dispense with assumptions. First, there is the basic assumption that past 
development can serve as a benchmark for future development. Then 
one also has to make decisions regarding the kind of development, such 
as linear or nonlinear. Finally, plausibility considerations must not be 
ignored. For example, an IQ of 200 or even a negative IQ is to be 
excluded. 
We now attempt to set up such an alternative, theory-reduced model 
of prediction. To do this, we use three different statistical methods: 
Calculating average trends between 1995 and 2018, using linear 
regression (independently done by the first and second author), and 
using nonlinear quadratic regression. These values are adjusted with an 
outlier correction. At the end, the mean corrected statistical prediction 
values are compared with predictions based on theoretical models.

2. Method 
2.1. Large scale student assessment studies as data source for cognitive 
ability 
Student achievement tests are a good indicator of cognitive ability, 
additionally, causes and consequences of student achievement and in­
telligence are similar (Kaufman et al., 2012; Rindermann & Baumeister, 
2015). Similar to intelligence tests, PISA scales show a strong g factor 
(about 70 % of variance; Pokropek et al., 2021). PISA scales are also 
highly correlated with intelligence test scales (the German CogAT, at the 
observed level ro = .47, at the latent level rl = .86; Brunner, 2008, p. 
158). By calculating the mean (across the dimensions of reading, 
mathematics, and science) specific factors are averaged out. We drew on 
the following sources for abilities: 
TIMSS (Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study) 
measures competence in mathematics and science for mostly fourth and 
eighth graders and, depending on school enrolment age, for third and 
seventh graders in some countries. TIMSS focuses on core aspects of 
curricula in different countries, with greater emphasis on the curricula 
of developed countries. Data from 1995 to 2019 were used (1995, 1999, 
2003, 2007, 2011, 2015 and 2019). Data for 4th and 8th grades were 
treated separately. Sources were international reports (e.g., Mullis et al., 
2020). In 1999, only 8th grade students were tested. 
PISA (Programme for International Student Assessment) from 2000, 
2003, 2006, 2009, 2012, 2015 and 2018, 15-year-old students in 
reading, mathematics and science, 2003 also problem solving, di­
mensions (tasks) are not closely related to the content taught in school, 
but represent a more general “literacy”. All student achievement tests 
use scales with a mean of 500 and a standard deviation of 100. Sources 
were international PISA reports (e.g., OECD, 2019). 
PIRLS (Progress in International Reading Literacy Study) from 2001, 
2006, 2011 and 2016 (fourth graders in reading, not close to school 
curricula, “literacy”; e.g., Mullis et al., 2017). 
All student achievement test data were used as corrected, i.e., PISA 
scores were corrected for youth not in the school system (school 
attendance rates), TIMSS and PIRLS scores were similarly corrected for 
youth not in the school system and for age (whether students in grade 4 
or 8 compared to other countries were relatively old or young). For 
several countries for which, for example, only regional data were 
available (such as China or Venezuela) or obviously erroneous data 
(Kazakhstan), further corrections were made (for a detailed description, 
see Rindermann, 2018, Appendix). However, since several measurement 
points were necessary to be included in the analyses and problematic 
data were mostly available from those countries that participated rather 
rarely, the remaining country sample is characterized by relatively 
fewer corrections (China and Venezuela, for example, have too few 
measurement points). 
To be included in the development measurements along time 
(regardless of the specific methods trend, linear or nonlinear regression), 
at least five measurements had to be given per country. The total sample 
of 104 countries with results in the aforementioned student achievement 
studies thus dropped to 79 countries. 
The mean score in the PISA 2018, TIMSS 2019, and PIRLS 2016 
student achievement studies was taken as the starting value. 
2.2. Statistical analysis 
In studies with different dimensions (TIMSS, PISA), the results of the 
different dimensions were first combined into an overall result. 
2.2.1. Trend analysis 
“Trend analysis” means that differences between two measurement 
points were calculated by subtraction. One example: Trend PISA 
2018–2015 is the PISA average 2018 minus the PISA average 2015. 
Because seven measurement time points are available from PISA, 21 
differences could be calculated (e.g., 2015 minus 2012 and 2009 minus 
2000). Each difference was converted to a decadal (dec) 10-year value 
(e.g. ((2009 −
2003) / 6) × 10). At the end, the differences were 
arithmetically averaged. 
Mean differences (unweighted trends) were calculated separately for 
each of the PISA, TIMSS 4th grade, TIMSS 8th grade, and PIRLS studies.

The four trends were then (unweighted) averaged. A maximum of 63 
different trends can be calculated (21 in TIMSS 8, 15 in TIMSS 4, 21 in 
PISA and 6 in PIRLS). In a second variant, an outlier correction was 
applied (see below) within each study (TIMSS 4, TIMSS 8, PISA, PIRLS). 
Values in the student achievement scale (M = 500, SD = 100) were 
arithmetically converted to IQ scores (minus 500, divided by 100, 
multiplied by 15, plus 100) resulting in the usual IQ scale (M = 100, SD 
= 15). As a common benchmark, results were compared with the 
average of British natives that was set to an IQ of 100 (“Greenwich-IQ”). 
Values for years 2030, 2050, and 2100 were obtained simply by 
adding the trends (per decade) from the baseline (set at 2020). This 
analysis was done by the first author. 
2.2.2. Linear regression 
The first step was to bring the 24 measurement time points (6 from 
TIMSS-4, 7 from TIMSS-8, 7 from PISA, 4 from PIRLS) onto a common 
scale. No adjustments were made within the studies (TIMSS 4, TIMSS 8, 
PISA, PIRLS), that is, we assumed constant, comparable scales. How­
ever, adjustments were made between studies (TIMSS 4, TIMSS 8, PISA, 
PIRLS). As far as possible, the same years were taken for finding the 
adjustments. The adjustment formulas were calculated in common 
country samples. 
PIRLS 2001 was adjusted in mean and standard deviation to PISA 
2000 (PISA more countries, older students, more dimensions). The value 
by which PIRLS 2001 was adjusted was adopted for all other PIRLS 
measurement points. 
TIMSS grade 4 2003 was adjusted in mean and standard deviation to 
PISA 2003 (PISA more countries, older students, more dimensions). The 
value by which TIMSS grade 4 2003 was adjusted was adopted for all 
other TIMSS grade 4 measurement points. 
The same was done for TIMSS grade 8: TIMSS grade 8 2003 was 
adjusted in mean and standard deviation to PISA 2003 (PISA more 
countries, more dimensions). The value by which TIMSS grade 8 2003 
was adjusted was adopted for all other TIMSS grade 8 measurement 
points. 
When multiple measurements were available for a year (e.g., from 
PISA and TIMSS 4 in 2003), these values were arithmetically averaged 
(after common scaling). At the end, measurements were available for 14 
years (1995, 1999, 2000, 2001, 2003, 2006, 2007, 2009, 2011, 2012, 
2015, 2016, 2018, 2019). However, values were available for different 
countries at different measurement times. The most measurements were 
available for Bulgaria, Canada, the Czech Republic, Great Britain, Hong 
Kong, Hungary, New Zealand, Russia and the USA (14 each). The fewest 
measurements came from Belarus, Belize, Brunei, El Salvador, 
Honduras, Mauritius, Mongolia, Pakistan and Venezuela (one mea­
surement each). These were all excluded, as were large and important 
countries such as China (4 measurement time points) and India (2 
measurement time points). 79 countries had sufficient data. 
We independently calculated two linear regressions (y = a + bx), the 
first author using SPSS, the second author using R. A separate regression 
formula was created for each country and the coefficients found here 
were used to forecast the mean cognitive abilities of societies on the IQ 
scale for the years 2030, 2050 and 2100. In order to increase the number 
of data points per country and thus improve the accuracy of the linear 
and polynomial regression formulas formed (see next section), the sec­
ond author included for his analysis additional annual results for some 
countries: Whenever a country temporarily dropped out of a study if 
there was only one wave, the mean value was calculated from the pre­
ceding and following waves, e.g. from PISA 2012 and 2018, if PISA 2015 
was missing. Both methods came to very similar results. Estimates for 
2030, 2050, and 2100 were correlated with r = 0.982, 0.989, and 0.995, 
respectively (79-country sample). However, the average difference be­
tween the two estimates was about 3.35 IQ points (first author's values 
higher), even for the year 2030. The reason was that the first author ran 
his estimate from 2020 (2020 was set as the starting value, the general 
student achievement mean 2016 to 2019), while the second author ran 
the estimates from the first available measurement. To arrive at 
consistent values here, for the second author's linear (and nonlinear) 
estimates, the increases from 2020 onward were summed to the speci­
fied starting value in 2020 (the mean of all school performance studies). 
The very high correlations for the years 2030, 2050 and 2100 have even 
increased slightly (r = .999, .998 and .998). The average mean differ­
ence for the years 2030, 2050 and 2100 was now only 0.20 IQ points. 
2.2.3. Nonlinear regression 
The second author additionally set up a nonlinear quadratic regres­
sion (y = a + bx + cx2) for the same data set described above. The 
different formulas here for each country, respectively their coefficients, 
were used to predict the mean IQs for the years 2030, 2050, and 2100. In 
a second variant (suggested by a reviewer), we used a hierarchical 
method: We started with a linear regression for all countries. Then we 
added a nonlinear quadratic regression, but its values were used only for 
those countries when the explained variance increased by 5 % (Δ R2 >
.05). This was the case in 45 % of the countries (nonlinear quadratic). 
Because we later computed an average from the various calculation 
methods, including the linear and nonlinear methods, we did not pursue 
this conditional nonlinear quadratic regression approach any further. 
2.2.4. Outlier correction 
Due to plausibility considerations, an outlier correction was made: 
Trend analysis method: For changes within 10 years equal or greater 
than ±40 SASQ (or d = ±0.40), an outlier correction was applied. 
“SASQ” means student achievement study quotient with a mean of 500 
and a standard deviation of 100. Changes equal to or >40 SASQ but <50 
SASQ were multiplied by a factor of 0.6. So reduction by 40 %. Changes 
equal to or >50 SASQ but <60 SASQ were multiplied by a factor of 0.3. 
So reduction by 70 %. Changes equal to or >60 SASQ were multiplied by 
a factor of 0.15. This results in a reduction of 85 % (of values above 60 
SASQ). At the end, all SASQ scores were converted to IQ scores. 
Regression analysis method: Here we applied a similar outlier correc­
tion (adapted to the IQ scale): Changes equal to or >6 IQ in 10 years but 
<7.5 IQ were multiplied by a factor of 0.6 (reduction by 40 %). Changes 
equal to or >7.5 IQ but <9 IQ were multiplied by a factor of 0.3 
(reduction by 70 %). Changes equal to or >9 IQ were multiplied by a 
factor of 0.15 (reduction of 85 % of values above 9 IQ). 
2.2.5. Regions 
Countries were assigned to regions based on geographical and cul­
tural criteria. For example, Singapore was assigned to East Asia. For each 
region, a sample country was selected and values were reported for that 
country in the results tables. Averages were not weighted by country 
population size, nor was the global mean (at the end of each table). The 
number of countries per region is given in Tables 1 to 3. The most 
countries are found for North Africa and the Middle East (15 countries, 
e.g. Egypt and Iran), then for Southeast Europe (11 countries, e.g. 
Greece, Romania and Israel). Israel and Singapore are examples of 
categorization based not only on geographical but also on cultural 
criteria. The fewest countries are found for Central-South Asia (only 
Kazakhstan, e.g., India has not enough measurements), sub-Sahara Af­
rica (2 countries, Botswana and South Africa), North-English America (2 
countries, Canada and USA) and Australia-New Zealand (both 2 
countries).

Estimates based on the nonlinear formula led to much more extreme 
results (see Table 1). For example, an IQ of 1053 was predicted for the 
year 2100 in South Africa and −1399 for Kazakhstan (see Discussion for 
plausibility considerations). We calculated the mean absolute gains 
(estimate 2100 minus cognitive ability measure of 2020) for each 
method as an indicator of statistical extremity. The values were 19.85 IQ 
points (trend), 24.54 (linear 1), 24.05 (linear 2), 24.29 (linear mean), 
but 309.91 IQ points for the estimation based on the nonlinear model. 
The more data are given for a country in TIMSS, PISA and PIRLS, the less 
extreme were the estimates, correlation between number of measure­
ment points and uncorrected mean gains (mean of the three methods) 
was r = −.38 (N = 79 countries). If one takes the number of possible 
comparisons (subtractions in the trend analysis, e.g. comparison be­
tween PISA 2012 and 2009 and between 2012 and 2006) instead of the 
number of measurement points, the correlation is somewhat higher (r =
−.44, N = 79). From this it can be concluded that more data leads to 
higher reliability of the estimates. 
Looking at the international means, the results of the trend analysis 
and the two linear regression models are quite similar (M = 104.70, 
107.20 and 106.80). The result of the estimation based on the nonlinear 
quadratic regression model deviates greatly from this (M = 229.78, 
similarly the conditional). Results depend strongly on whether a linear 
or nonlinear development is assumed. Likewise, the direction of devel­
opment (whether positive or negative) often differs from each other. 
There is agreement on the direction of development for the following 
regions: increases for sub-Saharan Africa, North Africa and the Middle 
East, for Latin America, East Asia, Eastnorth Europe, Southwest Europe 
and Southeast Europe; and decreases for Trans-Tasman, Western Europe 
and Scandinavia. A positive development is predicted for Central Europe 
using a linear regression, but a negative one using a non-linear 
regression.

Based on past development between 1995 and 2019 for the following 
regions a positive cognitive national development is expected in 21st 
century: sub-Saharan Africa, North Africa and the Middle East, North 
America, Latin America, East Asia, Eastnorth Europe, Southwest Europe 
and Southeast Europe. A negative development is expected for: Trans- 
Tasman, Western Europe and Scandinavia. The development for Cen­
tral Asia is unclear (we only have data from one country, Kazakhstan), 
for Southeast Asia and Central Europe. In all of these regions, outlier 
correction leads to much smaller (absolute) values for the nonlinear 
estimates. Thus, the final uncorrected and corrected estimates differ 
greatly. For Southeast Asia, the (across three methods averaged) 
development is nonlinear (it is at least somewhat nonlinear everywhere, 
but is particularly evident here): The corrected estimate for 2030 shows 
an increase (from 2020 83.12 IQ points to 85.61) but then a decrease 
(2050: 84.49 and 2100 81.69 IQ points). 
Following the corrected statistical estimates the regions with the 
highest IQs worldwide in 2100 would be East Asia (111.65 IQ points), 
Latin America (110.16) and Eastnorth Europe (IQ 104.92). The regions 
with the lowest were Southeast Asia (IQ 81.69), sub-Saharan Africa (IQ 
89.68) and Trans-Tasman (IQ 93.56). 
The worldwide development seems to be positive according to the 
statistical model. According to the corrected estimate, global IQ in­
creases from 91.29 in 2020 to 93.51 in 2030, then reaches 95.69 in 
2050, and finally 101.25 IQ points in 2100. 
Table 3 compares the results of the two former theoretical models 
(presented in 2011 and 2018) based on assumptions (asymmetric 
fertility, migration, environmental improvements, different limits to 
further improvements) and of the current statistical model based on past 
development (between 1995 and 2019, average of the three methods 
trend, linear and nonlinear, corrected). While a slight decrease or only a 
small increase is expected in the theoretical models (from 91.29 in 2020 
to IQ 90.21 or 93.88 in 2100), according to the statistical model an in­
crease of about 10 IQ points is expected worldwide in the next 80 years 
(IQ 101.25). Note that in Table 3 the country samples differ slightly (97 
and 79 countries; for equal country samples, see Table 4). 
The models based on theoretical assumptions also lead in their es­
timates to a convergence of countries. The standard deviations are 
declining (from 8.61 in 2020 to 7.95 or 3.85 in 2100). On the other 
hand, the statistical model leads to an increase in the differences be­
tween countries (SD = 15.72). On closer inspection, this is because, 
according to the theoretical models, developing countries are catching 
up and developed countries can no longer continue to make cognitive 
gains or are even experiencing declines: For example, sub-Saharan Af­
rica, the Middle East, Latin America, and Southeast Asia are catching up, 
while Europe, North America, Trans-Tasman, and East Asia remain 
stable or experience a cognitive decline (particularly Central Europe in 
the 2018 theoretical model, from an IQ of 97.91 in 2020 to 91.71 in 
2100). According to the statistical model, developing countries are also 
catching up here, but some European countries, North America and 
especially East Asia are also showing positive development. The sharp 
increase in Latin American scores (from IQ 84.74 in 2020 to 110.16 in 
2100) further increases country differences. 
Estimates based on theoretical models correlate more closely with IQ 
averages at the beginning of the 21st century (rth2011 = .95, rth2018 = .66, 
rst2022 = .23; N = 79). The order of regions in mean cognitive levels 
hardly changes according to the theoretical models, but it does some­
what according to the statistical model: According to the 2011 theo­
retical model, East Asia, Australia-New Zealand, and North America are 
leading in 2100 (with IQs around 100), similar as in the 2018 theoretical 
model. However, according to the statistical model, East Asia is followed 
by Latin America, East-North Europe, and the Middle East in 2100.

average IQs for the beginning of the 21st century are quite similar

the theoretical models the countries become more similar, but not ac­
cording to the statistical model. In all models, gains are higher for 
countries at lower ability levels (correlation between starting values and 
gains: rth2011 = −.76, rth2018 = −.92, rst2022 = −.31; N = 79). 
4. Discussion 
In this study, we present the results of a statistical prediction of the 
development of intelligence on a societal level in the 21st century. 
“Statistical” means that the past development between 1995 and 2019 is 
used as the basis for the prediction. No explicit theoretical assumptions 
were formulated. Three methods were used: Calculation of average 
gains (trends) by subtracting all possible test time points within a study 
(within TIMSS grade 4, TIMSS grade 8, PISA and PIRLS; a maximum of 
24 measurement points) and then averaging them. The result is the 
average gain over 10 years (dec) converted to an IQ point scale. Second, 
all student assessment scores were converted to a common scale and the 
(maximum) 14 measurement years between 1995 and 2019 were used 
to calculate a linear regression formula for each country (y = a + bx). 
Third, for the same student assessment data a nonlinear regression for­
mula (y = a + bx + cx2) was calculated. (In a variant, we conditionally 
combined the linear with the nonlinear method.) The results were cor­
rected for outliers and averaged. 
According to the average (corrected for outliers) of the three 
methods, worldwide IQ will increase by about 10 IQ points from 2020 
(IQ 91) until 2100 and then reach 101 IQ points (dec = 1.25 IQ points). 
However, the results heavily depend on chosen prediction methods, 
outlier correction and region: Estimates based on the nonlinear formula 
lead to much more extreme results (e.g., IQ 1178 for sub-Saharan Africa 
in 2100 or IQ −1399 for Central Asia). Such a difference depending on 
the linear or nonlinear equation could already be seen in the study with 
data from German-speaking countries (Pietschnig & Gittler, 2015). 
Trend and linear models produce results that are much more similar to 
expert opinions (Rindermann et al., 2017). As intended, the outlier 
correction leads to significantly less extreme results, especially for the

Several results of the predictions contradict expert opinions and 
plausibility considerations, e.g. IQs above 200 or negative values. The 
outlier corrections – based on plausibility considerations – reduced such 
implausible results. But what is plausibility? Plausibility considerations 
here are assumptions about the magnitude of the changes and about the 
pattern of differences between regions. Since there are no individuals (or 
even tests that can measure this) with scores above 200 or below 0, such 
results are also not realistic at the national level. It is therefore simply 
impossible that a linear or nonlinear increase or decrease found between 
1995 and 2019 will continue for decades. The other aspect concerns the 
order of regions and differences between them. Although the absolute 
values are not stable over time (FLynn effect), the patterns are to a 
certain extent stable: Past technological and intellectual development, 
even up to two thousand years back, are positively correlated to per­
formance on student achievement and intelligence tests today (Comin 
et al., 2010; Mokyr, 2005; Murray, 2003; Rindermann, 2018, p. 122). 
Qualitative analyses of cognitive activities and achievements in the past 
and today point in a similar direction (Rindermann, 2018, chapter 
4.4.3). 
However, doubts based on plausibility considerations do not only 
relate to future or predicted increases in intelligence, but also to past 
ones. Can one really assume an increase in intelligence of around 15 to 
30 IQ points in the 20th century, or have only the way of thinking and 
the ability to take tests changed? Further doubts are expressed about the 
extent of international differences and in particular about the low in­
telligence and student test scores in many developing countries. What 
we need here is a collection and analysis of people's everyday thinking, 
especially about people outside the university context. We as scientists 
often only move in a “super-WEIRD” social context with little or no 
contact to thinking outside of this “bubble”. Super-WEIRD does not only 
mean “western, educated, industrialized, rich and democratic” but also 
highly intelligent – the colleagues of scientists, their university students, 
partners, friends and their children (by age comparison), almost 
everyone with whom they spend more than a few seconds and exchange 
two words. In such a lifeworld, thinking and living at a low level of 
intelligence becomes simply unimaginable, completely implausible. 
Some examples: There were trials against animals in Europe up until the 
16th century (Dinzelbacher, 2006): 
“Caterpillars devastated some fields in the region of Lausanne, 
Switzerland, in 1519. An official messenger went to them and or­
dered [them] to appear in court to a scheduled date. The judge held 
some of these animals in his hands and ordered them to leave the 
region within three days.” 
(Oesterdiekhoff, 2009, p. 347f.) 
While that was a glimpse into the past, a look at other regions with 
significantly weaker test results is just as revealing. In the late 1990s, the 
first author asked about 20 adult people in Cuba how many times a day a 
broken analog watch with a dial and hands that stopped working 
showed the correct time. This question relates to daily life in Cuba, as 
many clocks did not work. About 75 % said, “never shows the correct 
time”, 15 % “once” and 10 % “twice” (Rindermann, 2018, p. 115). In 
Nigeria, about 82.5 % of surveyed urban middle-class people believed 
that God can put money in one's purse “if that person really needs money 
at that moment” (73.8 % “highly agreed”; study by Luisa Falkenhayn; 
Rindermann et al., 2014). According to studies by international research 
teams, teachers and students in the same region lack basic skills: “Almost 
one-quarter of the teachers cannot subtract double-digit numbers [in 
Togo] and one-third of the teachers cannot multiply double-digit 
numbers [in various African countries].” (Bold et al., 2017, pp. 192f.) 
“In Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, when grade 3 students were asked 
recently to read a sentence such as ‘The name of the dog is Puppy’, three- 
quarters did not understand what it said.” (World Bank, 2018, p. 3) 
Examples can also be found in the geographical centers of anywheres, at 
international airports, e.g. at Frankfurt Airport: 
“A recorded female voice advised us to board the plane from the front 
if we have a ‘low seat number’, and from the back, in case we had a 
‘high seat number’. I chuckled because this is of course idiotic. No, of 
course it is not idiotic to board the plane that way. However, if you 
do not know how many rows the plane you are about to board has, it 
is quite unclear if your seat number is indeed high. ... Just consider 
how much has to go wrong to make all of this happen. The Frankfurt 
Airport is run by a large organization. ... Yet, nobody of the parties 
involved thought it prudent to point out that a key bit of information 
is missing and that their work is not particularly useful. ... There were 
probably at least five people involved, the guy having the idea, some 
PR people, the person recording the voice track, some empty suits in 
meetings who discuss this project repeatedly. Yet, all of them 
thought that they are about to produce a fine piece of work.” 
(Elias, 2019) 
We do not have to travel that far, we do not have to go back into the 
deep past, we do not have to visit other continents by plane, we just have 
to step outside the door of our Psychological Institute and go to the next 
building, a supermarket. This week, we saw these price tags (Fig. 3): 
There is an advertisement for chocolate. On the left price tag, the 
price is reduced by 33 % from 1.19 euros to 0.79 euros, and on the right 
from 1.15 euros by 52 % to the same 0.79 euros. First of all, it is a bit silly 
to put up two different price tags for the same merchandise. Then a 
glaring calculation error is found on the right. Finally, by hanging both 
signs next to each other, everyone must notice this obvious mistake. 
However, this is not the case. As with the airport example above, 
cognitive errors come together at different levels, in this case from 
management to sales staff. 
Overall, this means that there are enormous cognitive differences 
between the past and the present, between different regions of the 
world; and even in countries that are at the tail end of the FLynn effect of 
one or more centuries, there are striking examples of low cognitive 
ability in everyday life. At the societal level, there is still much room for 
further upward cognitive development, even more in developing coun­
tries. 10 to 20 IQ points would certainly be conceivable. We must not be 
too cautious with our plausibility considerations. 
However, predictions based solely on a statistical model, i.e. pre­
dictions based only on past developments of a few decades and extrap­
olating them into the more distant future, are very likely to be wrong. In 
addition, purely statistical predictions are not possible, because one 
must always decide on a statistical variant, such as additive continuation 
(trend), linear or nonlinear development. Nevertheless, purely statistical 
forecasts are not unreasonable – if they are only used for a short period 
of time and are based on as many different models as possible (e.g. 
trends, linear, nonlinear). 
A combination of both approaches, the statistical one, which con­
tinues the past development, and the theoretical one, which formulates 
explicit assumptions, seems to be optimal: For the first 10 to 20 years to 
predict, one uses a statistical model, one assumes a continuation of the 
previous development. However, the more time progresses, the more 
should be chosen sophisticated theoretical models for a prediction. Over 
the years, the influence of statistical formulas could be tapered off and 
that of theoretical models gradually increased. For short-term statistical 
forecasting, a curvilinear model should only be used if there is clear 
theoretical support for it, such as a change in population composition. 
The trend model that arrives at (positive or negative) mean gains via 
subtractions between as many different measurement points as possible

seems to us to be the most convincing: simple, comprehensible,

The longer the prediction period, the more theoretical models must 
be used. Such models must take into account:  
1. Further opportunities to improve the environmental conditions 
relevant for intelligence of societies, especially at medium and low 
IQ levels (within societies and between societies).  
2. Differential birth rates (better-educated and more intelligent adults 
have fewer children, especially women; asymmetric fertility) show 
environmental (via family, neighborhood, schools etc.) and genetic 
consequences for future generations.  
3. Different possible target points of IQ development. It is plausible to 
assume that in societies with a tradition of intellectual achievement, 
such achievements will also be attainable in the future, and that 
where results are currently rather low, but past achievements have 
been high, particular progress can be expected in the future. 
4. Countries differ in the ability levels of their immigrants and emi­
grants. Europe, in particular, has on average a larger share of 
immigration of less skilled people. Predictions of immigration and 
emigration, as well as where people will come from and where they 
will go, must be taken into account.  
5. There are opportunities for catch-up development for immigrants (e. 
g., te Nijenhuis et al., 2004), but depending on the level of immi­
gration, on culture and on environmental quality.  
6. Intelligence will not only increase or decrease in general, but also 
change due to the increasing average age of the population (aging 
societies).  
7. Older age of parents can also lead to more mutations and better 
health systems to an accumulation of mutations over generations 
(Woodley of Menie & Fernandes, 2016).  
8. Countries learn from each other, and countries with culturally and 
genetically similar populations will continue to be more similar in 
cognitive abilities.  
9. The predictions can be different for different levels of ability – the 
level of ability of the intellectual classes, which are particularly 
important for the development of society (Coyle et al., 2018; Kirke­
gaard & Carl, 2022), could develop less well (Dutton & Woodley of 
Menie, 2018). 
Previous, less complex models need to be refined and improved; in 
particular, the aspect of age-related changes in intelligence should be 
added. All reasonable predictions are based on observations of past 
development (here at the societal level), on empirically well-supported 
evidence and argumentatively supported theories for the causes of 
development, and on equally well-supported assumptions for future 
development. Since different researchers may have different assump­
tions about this, ranging from very optimistic (“The improving state of 
the world”, Goklany, 2007; “It's getting better all the time”; Moore & 
Simon, 2000) to very pessimistic ones (“collapsing West”, Dutton & 
Rayner-Hilles, 2022, Chapter 1; “decay of Western civilization”, Nyborg, 
2012; “eventual collapse”, Weiss, 2007), it would be good if this 
research question were pursued by more researchers. 
On a more technical-statistical level, the following adaptations can 
be made: Trends and regressions could be calculated (and then aver­
aged) not for study means, but for the dimensions literacy, mathematics 
and natural sciences. The regressions themselves can also be calculated 
within studies (e.g., first in TIMSS-4, TIMSS-8, PISA, and PIRLS and then 
averaging the weights) or without averaging across studies if results are 
available from multiple studies in one year (e.g., 2007 from TIMSS 4 and 
TIMSS 8; or 2011 from PIRLS, TIMSS 4, and TIMSS 8; or 2015 from PISA, 
TIMSS 4, and TIMSS 8). The advantage could be that less extreme pre­
dictions are made thanks to the more measurement time points avail­
able. However, the goal is not so much to make dimension- or study-
