An empirical example of capturing the impact of SES on student
achievement using path analysis
Michael R. Harwell *, Qian Zhao
Department of Educational Psychology, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, USA

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
SES
Students’ achievement
Structural equation modelling

Measures of socioeconomic status (SES) are widely used in educational data analyses as control
variables and to increase the statistical power of significance tests. The effectiveness of SES
measures in these analyses depends on moderate correlations between measures and educational
outcomes. However, meta-analyses of the relationship between SES and student achievement
have produced average bivariate correlations in the .03–.32 range, implying these measures offer
modest statistical control and power gains. Author provided an empirical example of using indirect effects in structural equation modeling to better capture the impact of SES analyzing data
from the 2012 Program for International Student Assessment. The results illustrate the potential
of indirect effects to fully capture the impact of SES in educational data analyses.

1. Introduction
Measures of socio-economic status (SES) in educational data analyses are widely used in research in the U.S. (e.g., Alvear, 2019;
Coleman et al., 1966; West et al., 2020), as well as other countries including the United Kingdom, Canada, China, South Africa, and
Malaysia (Chmielewski, 2019; Kim, Cho, & Kim, 2019; Li, Xu, & Xia, 2020; Misran, Sahuri, Arsad, Husain, & Zaki, 2013; Taylor, 2018;
Taylor & Yu, 2009; Webb et al., 2017). Socio-economic status measures are typically used in educational data based on a belief of the
impact of SES summarized by Boocock (1972): “The family characteristic that is the most powerful predictor of school performance is
socioeconomic status (SES) …” (p. 32). There are several definitions of SES but that of Hauser and Warren (1997) is representative.
Socio-economic status represents “… a shorthand expression for variables that enable the placement of persons, families, households
and aggregates such as statistical local areas, communities and cities in some hierarchical order, reflecting their ability to produce and
consume the scarce and valued resources of society” (p. 178).
Similar SES measures appear to be used in western and many non-western countries, especially parental income, educational level,
and occupation (e.g., Chmielewski, 2019; Kim et al., 2019; Tan, 2017; van Ewijk & Sleegers, 2010). Specific characteristics of these
measures vary across countries but all represent access to resources. Correspondingly, SES measures tend to be used in educational data
analyses (especially analyses of achievement) in many countries to control for the effects of SES and to increase statistical power (e.g.,
Anders, 2017; Li et al., 2020; Patten, 2019).

Meta-analyses summarizing the relationship between SES measures and student achievement outcomes have also produced modest
bivariate correlations (Author; Holzberger, Reinhold, Lüdtke, & Seidel, 2020; Kim, 2019; Kim et al., 2019; Sirin, 2005; Tan, 2017). For
example, a meta-analysis of SES–achievement correlations for U.S. studies by Author reported an overall average correlation of 0.22. A
meta-analysis of SES–achievement correlations by Kim et al. (2019) for studies performed in 38 countries reported an average of 0.12,
Holzberger et al. (2020) reported an average of 0.15 for studies from more than a dozen countries, and Ewijk and Sleegers (2010)
reported an average correlation of 0.32 based on data from more than 30 countries. These averages were based on a random-effects
meta-analytic model that assumes SES-achievement correlations vary across studies, implying they represent a random sample from a
population distribution (Borenstein, Hedges, Higgins, & Rothstein, 2009). Some meta-analyses employ a fixed-effects model that
assumes there is one true SES-achievement correlation that every sample correlation is estimating, and many meta-analyses report
results for both random-effects and fixed-effects models (e.g., Author; Kim et al., 2019; Sirin, 2005).
The choice between a random-effects and fixed-effects model is critical for evaluating average SES-achievement correlations
generated in a meta-analysis: Average correlations in fixed-effects models are heavily influenced by studies with large samples,
whereas in random-effects models the impact of large samples on average correlations is diminished (Borenstein et al., 2009). For
example, the above meta-analytic results for SES-achievement correlations based on random-effects models show that if rach,SES = 0.12
then .122 = .014 or 1.4 % of the variance in achievement is attributable to SES, and if rach,SES = 0.32 then .322 = 10.2 % is attributable to
SES. On the other hand, Kim et al. (2019) and Author reported average SES-achievement correlations for a fixed-effects model of 0.03
(.032 = .001 %) and 0.09 (.092 = .008 %).
Modest SES-achievement correlations imply that controlling for SES and increasing the power of significance tests in educational
data analyses is likely problematic. In addition, concerns about what information frequently used SES measures are capturing, such as
student eligibility for a free/reduced-price lunch, number of rooms in a house, and home possessions (e.g., access to a computer and
fast Wi-Fi) have fueled efforts to improve SES measurement (Geverdt & Nixon, 2018; Oakes & Rossi, 2003; U.S. Department of Education, 2012; Webb et al., 2017). These efforts can be roughly categorized into three lines of research. One is focused on identifying
alternative SES measures using existing variables. For example, a U.S. Department of Education (2012) report recommended using
aggregated variables such as the percentage of English language learners and average parent educational level for a school as SES
measures, as well as composite variables reflecting multiple SES measures.
Another U.S. Department of Education (2015) report outlined measures of SES based on the Census Bureau’s Small Area Income
and Poverty Estimates (SAIPE). Similarly, Kurki, Boyle, and Aladjem (2005) examined aggregate measures of school poverty and the
percentage of single parents with children in a neighborhood. A wide range of correlations between measures of school SES and
achievement in multiple countries have been reported, for example, .03–.72 (Goddard, Salloum, & Berebitsky, 2009; Holzberger et al.,
2020; Kurki et al., 2005; van Ewijk & Sleegers, 2010), meaning there is no clear evidence that aggregated SES measures consistently
have higher correlations with achievement than non-aggregated measures. The use of aggregated measures may also be at odds with
research and policy goals focused on student-level outcomes.
A second line of research consists of efforts to construct SES variables in ways that clarify what they measure and enhance their
validity. Oakes and Rossi (2003) highlighted the importance of specifying a conceptual model of SES, which these authors operationalized using a path diagram with latent variables. Oakes and Rossi (2003) then used structural equation modeling (SEM) guided
by the path diagram to construct an SES measure. These authors illustrated this process by initialing drawing on Coleman’s (1988)
theory of social capital to specify three dimensions of SES through the latent variables human capital, material capital, and social
capital. Data from the U.S. National Survey of Families and Households were used to construct an SES measure. Oakes and Rossi (2003)
concluded that using a conceptual model of SES to guide the construction of SES measures via SEM offered evidence of construct
validity not typically available with traditional measures. However, there is no evidence this approach produces SES measures with
larger correlations with educational outcomes.
A third line of research builds on the work of Oakes and Rossi (2003) by proposing indirect effects in SEM be studied. Author
suggested that modest SES-achievement correlations may reflect their failure to fully capture the complex effects of SES. To better
capture the breadth and depth of the impact of SES on achievement, Author suggested using path diagrams to specify and fit a model
characterizing the impact of SES that includes indirect effects. Using SEM to model the impact of SES on other variables is not new (e.g.,
Mueller & Parcel, 1981; Sewell, Haller, & Portes, 1969), but the proposed use of indirect effects to capture the broader impact of SES is.
In SEM total effects are partitioned into direct and indirect effects (Mueller, 1996). Direct effects resemble traditional effects in
regression that are estimated using slopes, whereas indirect effects capture the possibility that direct effects may be mediated by other
variables. For example, if the impact of parent educational level on mathematics achievement represented a direct effect, the extent to
which this effect was amplified by a student’s access to home possessions that promote higher achievement (e.g., access to a computer
and fast Wi-Fi) represents an indirect effect, i.e., home possessions mediates the impact of parental educational level on mathematics
achievement. An indirect effect near zero implies it adds little to capturing the impact of SES and traditional statistics like bivariate
correlations and slopes are likely to be adequate summaries of the SES-achievement relationship; if indirect effects are non-negligible
the inference is the impact of SES is not fully captured by traditional summaries of the SES-achievement relationship. Omission of

2. Rationale for the current study
The mismatch between empirical evidence demonstrating the SES-achievement relationship is typically modest, and the perception
that SES has a strong impact on student achievement shared by many western and non-western educators and educational researchers,
suggests traditional methods for capturing this relationship may be inadequate. Data from the Program for International Student
Assessment (PISA) (2012) are used to empirically assess the ability of indirect effects to provide a more complete characterization of
the impact of SES on student achievement.
The research questions were:
1. Does an SEM approach help to capture the broader impact of SES on student achievement than traditional summaries such as
bivariate correlations as reflected in nonzero indirect
effects?
2. If indirect effects are present how large are they?
If the SEM produces indirect effects near zero and, hence, similar direct and total effects, indirect effects (for the model and data
studied) add little to characterizations of the SES-achievement relationship. On the other hand, indirect effects that differ from zero
suggest (given the model and data studied) traditional summaries underestimate the SES-achievement relationship. The latter would
reinforce the importance of researchers adopting a theoretical model specifying the impact of SES on achievement, using measures
consistent with this model, and including indirect effects when analyzing data. Data not suited to traditional SEM, such as hierarchical
data, can be analyzed with multilevel models that allow mediated effects to be estimated (MacKinnon & Valente, 2014; Zhang, Zyphur,
& Preacher, 2009), or models that combine multilevel models and SEM (Hox, 2013).
3. Method
3.1. Data frame
Data for this study come from the 2012 PISA developed by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development. The
Program for International Student Assessment is an ongoing effort to assess achievement in both traditional (reading, mathematics,
science) and less traditional subjects (e.g., collaborative problem solving, financial literacy) of 15-year-old students across 65 countries. The assessment is administered every three years. Once a country agrees to participate a sample of 15-year-old students in that
country take a test in 7th grade (or their equivalent) or higher. Students take the assessments in the same language used in their regular
classes. We chose to use data from a single country because of concerns the implementation of SES measures may be culturedependent, and selected the U.S. because the educational infrastructure is common across the entire country (e.g., grades 1–12,
teachers must be licensed).
An initial sample of N = 4978 cases was downloaded from https://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/pisa2012database-downloadabledata.
htm but the presence of missing data prompted the SEM software to use listwise deletion, resulting in a smaller sample of N = 2,968.
Missing (incomplete) data can bias inferences and statistical procedures for handling incomplete data are available including imputing
missing values, although valid use of these procedures requires the missing values to be missing at random and multivariate-normal
data (Author). Our purpose of illustrating the use of path diagrams to estimate the impact of SES prompted us to use the available
(complete data) sample in our analyses, which implies our findings must be juxtaposed against the possibility of biased inferences due
to missing data. The same sample was used in all analyses to facilitate comparisons.

Models
Coleman’s (1988) theory of social capital was used to specify an SES model. In this theory, social inequality is the result of differences in interests among individuals and their control over scarce resources and is represented by differential access to material
capital (material and monetary goods), human capital (skills and capabilities), and social capital (strength of beneficial social relationships). Two models were used to examine the magnitude of direct and indirect effects in path analysis of the impact of SES on
mathematics achievement for the PISA data. Fig. 1 represents a relatively simple path model and Fig. 2 a more complex path model
(error terms are omitted in the figures). Path models represent a special case of SEM (Mueller, 1996). A full-blown SEM with latent
variables could be used to assess the impact of SES on achievement via indirect effects, but we limit the analyses to path models (no
latent variables) for two related reasons. First, path models are generally straightforward to specify and interpret and should often be
sufficient to characterize the impact of SES on achievement. Second, an SEM approach filters the impact of SES on achievement
through latent variables, which may be helpful in some instances but adds significant complexity to the modeling. In cases where latent
variables will be used the path models illustrated below provide guidance for specifying a model with latent variables and interpreting
the results.
Specification of the models in Figs. 1 and 2 was guided by theory and prior empirical evidence based on the work of several authors
including Sewell et al. (1969), Lopez-Turley (2002), Lopez-Turley, Gamoran, McCartney, and Fish (2017), and Oakes and Rossi (2003).
The common thread in these models is the prediction that greater access to material capital, human capital, or social capital leads to
improved educational outcomes, and that the impact of SES can be both direct and indirect.
The SES variable reported as a z-score by PISA was added to the home possessions variable after the latter was converted to a zscore, and the resulting variable (hereafter referred to as SESHP) treated as a measure of material capital (positive z-scores represent
above average access to material capital) in Fig. 1. Mother and father’s educational level (0–6 scale, higher values indicate more formal
education) were added together and the resulting variable treated as a measure of human capital. Parent occupation was based on a
0–100 scale in the PISA data with higher values indicating occupations with greater status and served as a measure of human capital. A
variable called teacher support served as our measure of social capital and was computed as a sum of two PISA variables converted to zscores: Teacher support for students and teacher-student relations (higher values imply more support). The outcome variable mathematics achievement was computed as the average of the first three plausible mathematics achievement values of each student. Wu
(2005) described plausible values as representing “… the range of abilities that a student might reasonably have, given the student’s
item responses.” (p. 115).

In Fig. 1 access to human capital (mother and father’s educational level) is posited to impact access to material capital (SESHP), and
both are posited to impact mathematics achievement. Parental occupation (access to human capital) is posited to impact mathematics
achievement indirectly through access to social capital (teacher support). For example, students whose parents have more education
may tend to have higher mathematics achievement, an effect that could be amplified for students from higher SES households. Or
perhaps students from lower SES households tend to have lower mathematics achievement unless they have access to strong teacher
support. Similarly, students whose parents have less education may have lower mathematics achievement unless parents have
prioritized providing support for academic success at home (e.g., access to a computer and fast Wi-Fi); alternatively, students whose
parents have less education may have lower mathematics achievement if support for academic success is not prioritized at home, an
effect that may be exacerbated if students do not have access to strong teacher support. Fig. 2 extends the model in Fig. 1 with four
direct effects and five indirect effects involving mathematics achievement as the outcome. In Fig. 2 measures of material capital (SES,
home possession), human capital (mother and father’s educational level), and social capital (teacher support for students, teacherstudent relations) are treated as separate measures of SES.
4. Results
Table 1 reports summary statistics for the sample. Table 2 reports bivariate correlations among measures of capital and mathematics scores for Fig. 1, and generally reflects the pattern of modest correlations between SES measures and achievement typically
reported in educational research (e.g., Author; Holzberger et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2019). Table 2 reports bivariate correlations among

achievement.
The AMOS software (Arbuckle, 2017) was used to fit the models in Figs. 1 and 2. The chi-square test of fit was statistically significant in both models (p < .01), but the NFI and CFI fit indices equaled (or almost equaled) the recommended minimum of .95 for
both models (.95, .93), and the RMSEA index was less than the recommended value of .05 for the model in Fig. 1 and only slightly
larger than .05 (.08) for the model in Fig. 2. All direct effects in Figs. 1 and 2 were statistically significant using α = .01; indirect effects
are not usually tested and standardized effects are reported to facilitate interpretation.

Examining the indirect effects provides additional evidence of the impact of SES. For example, the indirect effect of. 20SD for
mother and father’s educational level provides evidence this variable impacted mathematics achievement in ways not captured by a
bivariate correlation. The impact of mother and father’s educational level on mathematics achievement is mediated by SESHP, parent
occupation, and teacher support. This finding provides evidence that the strong positive impact of mother and father’s educational
level on mathematics achievement is amplified for student’s living in high SES households whose parents have high status occupations
and who have strong teacher support; alternatively, lower mathematics achievement as a result of lower mother and father’s
educational level may be exacerbated by student’s living in low SES households whose parents have lower status occupations and who
do not have much teacher support. Hence the indirect effect of. 20SD signals that bivariate SES-achievement correlations are likely to
be underestimates of the impact of SES.
The indirect effect of -.06SD signals that the impact of SESHP on mathematics achievement was weakly (negatively) affected by the
absence of strong teacher support. On the other hand, the indirect effect of -.01(SD) implies that teacher support did not mediate the
impact of parent occupation on mathematics achievement, suggesting that the bivariate correlation between parental occupation and
mathematics achievement provides a meaningful summary.
4.2. Path model in Fig. 2
The standardized effects for the model in Fig. 2 are reported in Table 5. The direct effects are similar to those in Table 4. Higher
levels of the PISA SES variable (material capital) are associated with higher mathematics achievement (.32SD), whereas home possessions (.07SD), teacher support (.04SD) and teacher/student relations (.08SD) showed weaker effects. The largest direct effect in
Table 5 is PISA SES on home possessions (1.9SD), meaning that higher (or lower) SES is directly associated with more (or fewer) home
possessions.
Several indirect effects for mathematics achievement as the outcome in Table 5 suggest that bivariate SES-achievement correlations
are likely underestimated. Indirect effects of. 17SD,. 16SD,. 20SD, and .15 SD signal that the influence of the PISA SES variable on
mathematics achievement is not confined to direct effects but is also transmitted indirectly. For example, there is a positive effect on
mathematics achievement of students whose mother has more education, who live in a higher SES household with more home possessions, and who have access to strong teacher support or good teacher/student relations. Similarly, the impact of SES on mathematics
achievement is mediated by teacher support, home possessions, and teacher/student relations, i.e., students living in low SES
households tend to have lower mathematics achievement, an effect that is exacerbated for less teacher support, fewer home possessions, and poorer teacher/student relations.

outcomes, and that the impact of SES can be both direct and indirect. The results of this study suggest an SEM approach can help to

adequately capture the impact of SES.
For example, mother and father’s educational level could serve as a measure of human capital that would be predicted to have a
direct impact on student mathematics achievement, but would also affect achievement indirectly. However the latter is not reflected in
bivariate correlations between SES measures and mathematics achievement reported in meta-analyses or in Table 2. Including the
indirect effect of mother and father’s educational level depicted in Fig. 1 provided evidence of the impact of SES on mathematics
achievement (.20SD) that is broader than that captured with the correlation of .29 in Table 2. Table 5 provided additional evidence of
the value of including both direct and indirect effects.
In sum, examining indirect effects can strengthen the link between theoretical models of the impact of SES on educational outcomes
and inferences from data analyses in two important ways. First, interpretations of indirect effects offer important insights into how SES
impacts outcomes not available with direct effects. For example, Table 4 shows that mother and father’s educational level directly
impacts student mathematics achievement but this relationship is mediated by SESHP, parent occupation, and teacher support. This
finding provides evidence that the strong positive impact of mother and father’s educational level on mathematics achievement is
amplified for student’s living in high SES households whose parents have high status occupations and who have strong teacher support;
alternatively, lower mathematics achievement as a result of lower mother and father’s educational level may be exacerbated by
student’s living in low SES households who do not have much teacher support and whose parents have lower status occupations. This
indirect effect complements the interpretation of the direct effect that students whose parents have higher educational levels tend to
demonstrate higher mathematics achievement. Second, SES measures typically serve as control variables in data analyses that reflect
direct effects, such as using mother and father’s educational level to adjust (partial out) the impact of SES on mathematics achievement. A failure to include indirect effects in a statistical model can bias findings and inferences.
Our results suggest that SEM models like those in Figs. 1 and 2 should be considered for many educational data analyses involving
SES and educational outcomes such as student achievement. Data not suited to path models like those in Figs. 1 and 2, such as hierarchical data, can be analyzed with multilevel models that include mediation effects. The case in which hierarchical data include SES
measures at student and/or group (e.g., schools) levels is a standard analytic tool in educational research. Recent work has illustrated
how mediated effects can be added to multilevel models at one or more levels of the model.

multilevel effects. Zhang et al. (2009) used an empirical example to show that the relationship between job satisfaction and work
performance was either underestimated or overestimated unless indirect effects were included. Specifying the mediation mechanism
involving SES at one or more levels of the model provides a way to more broadly capture the impact of SES, but also makes the analyses
more complex.
In short, educational researcher should routinely look to fit statistical models that offer a broad characterization of SES consistent
with theories of the impact of this factor on educational outcomes, whether through path models with no latent variables, SEM with
latent variables, or statistical procedures that have incorporated indirect effects such as multilevel models. Doing so should enrich the
interpretation of the impact of SES and enhance statistical control of SES which should strengthen inferences.
