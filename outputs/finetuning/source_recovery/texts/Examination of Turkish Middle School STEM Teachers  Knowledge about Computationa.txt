ABSTRACT

Over the past two decades, the integration of computational
thinking, as well as information and communications technology within educational programs, has gained importance. A
primary obstacle to integrating computational thinking into
teaching and learning is the level of teachers’ knowledge,
skills, and perceptions related to this topic. This study aimed
to determine the views of science, technology, engineering,
and math (STEM) teachers regarding using information and
communications technology within their teaching, emphasizing computational thinking within lessons, and conceptually
developing levels of computational thinking skills. Another
aim was to examine whether these variables differed according to educational discipline, teaching experience, and graduation factors. Data collection was conducted via an online
questionnaire completed by participating teachers. One hundred twenty-one (121) teachers from 38 middle schools (34
public and 4 private) in seven different cities in the Republic
of Turkey participated. An F test was used to determine
whether selected scales, translated from an ICILS 2018 Teacher
Questionnaire, differentiated according to educational discipline, teaching experience, and/or graduation factors. Pearson
correlation coefficients were calculated to determine relationships between variables used in data collection. Results indicated that teachers need a sufficient level of education and
guidance regarding incorporating information and communications technology into their lessons as well as how to consistently use these tools within their classrooms.

students to develop proficiency at being computational thinkers, creative
communicators, knowledge constructors, innovative designers, empowered
learners, global collaborators, and digital citizens. Similarly, it was highlighted by the Royal Society in the United Kingdom that every child should
have an opportunity to learn concepts and principles related to computer
science and information technology from the beginning of their primary
education (The Royal Society, 2012). As a result, the push to educate students about information and communications technology has occurred on
a worldwide basis. In an effort to learn more, the International Association
for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) has worked to evaluate the computer and information literacy (CIL) levels and computational
thinking (CT) skills of eighth-grade students through computer-based
assessments within the scope of the 2018 International Computer and
Information Literacy Study (ICILS; Fraillon et al., 2019a).
According to ICILS, the computer and information literacy of students
refers to their ability to use computer technologies to not only collect and
manage information but also produce and change information as well
(Fraillon et al., 2013; Fraillon et al., 2019a). In addition, CT has been
defined as “the type of thinking used when programming on a computer
or developing an application for another type of digital device” (Fraillon
et al., 2019a, p. 3). Wing (2008), one of the researchers who started these
discussions by taking CT out of computer science, defined CT as “solving
problems, designing systems and understanding human behaviour that
draws on concepts fundamental to computing” (p. 3717). Wing also argued
that “computational thinking is a fundamental skill for everyone, not just
for computer scientists. To reading, writing, and arithmetic, we should add
computational thinking to every child’s analytical ability” (p. 33). As a
result, there is a considerable consensus in the literature regarding the
importance of providing CT skills at all levels of education, even as early as
kindergarten (Fessakis et al., 2013; Sullivan & Bers, 2016).
The importance of CT has gained traction because of an increased
emphasis on integrating CT into more and more educational programs as
well as on improving teachers’ knowledge and skills in this area. Examples
of this can be seen in the literature when it is emphasized that teachers
should be well prepared to integrate CT concepts into the learning-teaching
processes (Blank et al., 2003; NRC, 2010). In this vein, the National Science
Foundation (NSF) project, Leveraging Thought Leadership for
Computational Thinking in the PK–12 Curriculum, stands out as the first
comprehensive large-scale study carried out for this purpose (Barr et al.,
2011). The project aimed to make computational thinking concepts accessible through a common conceptualization as well as to provide computational thinking examples that are suitable according to students’ age

characteristics as well as the goals of educational programs (Barr et al.,
2011). In another study, Bower et al. (2015) researched teachers from different fields in Australia to examine their unique CT perceptions. As a
result, it was revealed that many teachers lack sufficient knowledge and
skills for developing their students’ CT skills. In a study where teachers’
thoughts regarding CT were examined, Corradini et al. (2017) found that

found that teachers see stronger connections between CT and math classes

result of time constraints and a lack of content knowledge. It can be understood from these results that one of the basic obstacles to integrating CT
into the learning-teaching process is a deficiency in teachers’ knowledge,
skills, and perceptions regarding CT. Although the importance of developing the CT skills of students has been demonstrated, there are still questions that need to be answered with regard to the kinds of support teachers
need to integrate CT skills into their teaching. In particular, what kind of
framework should be prepared for in-service training of STEM teachers
(Rich et al., 2019)? Within the scope of this study, the aim was to determine the views of teachers regarding the use of information and communications technology (ICT) within their instructional processes, the emphasis
placed on computational thinking skills within lessons, tendencies to
cooperate in using ICT, and the conceptual development levels of their
computational thinking skills. Also, another aim was to provide recommendations for teacher education policies by examining whether these variables
differed according to educational discipline, teaching experience, and/or
graduation factors as well as any relationships that may have occurred
between these stated variables.
Background of the research

According to Binkley et al. (2012), ICT literacy includes the ability to access,
evaluate, manage, and use information as well as effectively implement technology. However, it was revealed in the findings of an ICILS 2018 study that
solely providing ICT such as computers, laptops, smart boards, and internet
connection to students and/or teachers is not sufficient to develop their
digital skills (Fraillon et al., 2019a). One primary reason for this shortcoming may be the teachers’ and students’ limited knowledge and experience
regarding ICT. For example, in a study with 972 primary school teachers,

Corradini et al. (2017) found that many teachers do not have a conceptual
command of computational thinking, and as a result, often do not feel
ready to develop their students’ ICT competencies. In another study with
Saudi Arabian computer teachers, it was determined that most computer
science teachers have a low level of conceptual development regarding
computational thinking, and some teachers also have false perceptions
about the nature of computational thinking. As a result, it is recognized
that computer science teachers often need more detailed and extensive
training on what computational thinking means as well as how to appropriately and effectively teach it (Alfayez & Lambert, 2019).
However, it has been revealed in other studies that such deleterious
outcomes can be eliminated with the use of appropriate and adequate
intervention programs. For example, Blum and Cortina (2007) stated that
teachers’ opinions about using computer science in many areas such as
problem solving, algorithms, and data organization changed throughout a
workshop conducted for teachers to improve their knowledge and skills
regarding computer science. Similarly, teachers who have self-confidence
in ICT use, have positive opinions about ICT in general, and believe that
their schools promote ICT use in education are usually more inclined to
include ICT and CT as part of their teaching (Fraillon et al., 2019a; GilFlores et al., 2017). Unfortunately, it has been documented in several
studies that access to technological resources, internet facilities, and professional development opportunities is limited in many rural schools
(Aduwa-Pgiegbaen & Iyamu, 2009; Wood & Howley, 2012). Therefore,
Guzdial (2016) emphasized the fact that in comparison to teachers and
students from well-funded and/or urban schools, the teachers and students in poorly funded and/or rural schools often lack sufficient opportunities for developing their CT and ICT skills. In addition, Kale et al.
(2018) revealed that in general teachers working in urban areas have
higher levels of CT knowledge and ICT skills than their teaching counterparts in rural areas. As a result, it is important to better determine the
state of CT and ICT use in schools throughout the world in both urban
and rural environments, in any teaching discipline and at any grade level.
There also appears to be a deleterious gap in the literature regarding the
extent to which STEM teachers within the Republic of Turkey have
access to ICT within their schools and how they integrate ICT and CT
skills into their classrooms, lessons, and teaching.
Research problem

Based on the assumption that the ways in which ICT is used and its
frequency of use in schools affects the CT and ICT skills of students

(Fraillon et al., 2019b), this study aimed to determine the views of teachers
with regard to using ICT in their teaching, the emphasis they place on
computational thinking skills in their lessons, the tendencies for cooperation in ICT use that occur, and the conceptual development levels of their
computational thinking skills. Also, another aim was to examine whether
these variables differed according to educational discipline, teaching experience, and/or graduation factors as well as to determine any relationships

this study follow:
1. Do STEM teachers’ opinions regarding the use of ICT in teaching, their

development levels differ according to their educational discipline,
teaching experience, and/or graduation factors?
2. Is there a significant relationship between STEM teachers’ opinions
regarding the use of ICT in teaching, their emphasis on computational
thinking skills, cooperation related to ICT use in schools, and personal
computational thinking skills and conceptual development levels?
Method

To collect data in this study, a descriptive survey model was used as the
quantitative research methodology because of its following advantages: (a)
simultaneous comparison of a variety of factors, (b) accessibility to
participant groups, and (c) allowing for short-term data collection with following: interfering and/or deleteriously affecting the research environment
(Fraenkel et al., 2011).

Instrumentation and data analysis

The data collection included not only participants’ demographic information (e.g., graduation factors, educational discipline, and years of teaching
experience) but also consisted of teachers’ positive and negative views
regarding ICT use in schools, teachers’ emphasis on teaching approaches
related to computational thinking, teachers’ perceptions regarding collaboration in ICT use, and also results of a CT conceptual development test.
Furthermore, as part of data collection, participating teachers were asked to
score on a scale of 1 to 10 the level of ICT in their schools as well as the
use of ICT in their classes: 0 ¼ no ICT and 10 ¼ ICT is very good. Also, on
the scale the teachers rated their effectiveness at using ICT, a value of 0
was labeled “I do not use effectively at all” and a value of 10 was labeled “I
use very effectively.” It was important that participating teachers responded
to each question on the online survey in order to gather adequate and
detailed information regarding the study aims.
In order to determine teachers’ CT conceptual development levels, the
Questionnaire of Computational Thinking (QCT), developed by Alfayez
and Lambert (2019), was translated into Turkish for use in this current
study. The QCT test consists of 22 multiple choice questions regarding
computational thinking. Following language translation of the QCT into
Turkish it was determined that two items did not work well for analysis.
In addition, three other questions were not used in the online questionnaire because they did not fit the scope of the research. Alfayez and
Lambert (2019) reported a Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient for the
QCT at .70, yet the reliability coefficient for the 17-question test used in
this study was determined to be .60. To examine the participating teachers’
views regarding the use of ICT and CT, a set of scales were selected from
the ICILS 2018 Teacher Questionnaire and translated into Turkish. In
doing so, the following information was sought: (a) teachers’ positive views
regarding the use of ICT in schools, (b) teachers’ negative views regarding
the use of ICT in schools, (c) teachers’ emphasis on teaching approaches
related to computational thinking, and (d) teachers’ perceptions of collaboration in ICT use. The first, second and fourth scales used contained the
following 4-point Likert type responses: strongly disagree, disagree, agree, or
strongly agree. The third scale contained the four-point Likert type
responses: strong emphasis, some emphasis, little emphasis, or no emphasis.
The reliability and descriptive statistics values for all scales are presented in

Kline (2016) recommended reporting a 90% confidence interval for
RMSEA, v2, degree of freedom (df), significance value, and CFI values in
studies where DFA is conducted. Anderson and Gerbing (1984) stated
that the value of v2/df less than 5 is an acceptable criterion for model fit
in DFA studies. Within the scope of this study, the v2/df value was found
to be 2.34. However, some researchers have concluded that it would be
incorrect to use this value as a criterion for model fit (Kline, 2016;
Wheaton, 1987). As a result, a RMSEA value at 90% confidence interval
was also examined and the statistics obtained were determined to range
from 0.0952 to 0.115. MacCallum et al. (2001) stated that RMSEA values
ranging between 0.08  0.10 indicate a mediocre level of compliance.
Finally, the CFI value of the scale used in this study was also examined,
and it was determined to be 0.871. Hu and Bentler (1998, 1999) stated
that the CFI value for a scale should not be less than 0.90. Considering all
this information, the factor structure for the scale created for use in this
study was determined to be appropriate within the scope of the
research aims.
For data collected with adapted tests, MANOVA was carried out to
determine whether these scales differentiated according to items such
as educational discipline, teaching experience, and graduation factors. It
was examined whether the assumptions for MANOVA such as independent
observations and homogeneity of covariances were met. Because teachers
completed the scale and questionnaire independently, the assumption
of independent observations was met. Box’s M statistic (Box’s M ¼ 132.34)
of equality of covariance matrices was not significant (p ¼ .744), indicating
that the assumption of equality covariances was met. To designate the
multivariate predictive ability of independent variables, the Wilks’ lambda
(k) statistics were reported. Also, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to better determine relationships between the variables used in
data collection.

Results for research question two

Pearson correlation analysis was carried out to determine relationships
between teachers’ perceptions of collaboration in ICT use, the emphasis
placed on teaching approaches for CT, the CT conceptual development
scores, and teachers’ positive and negative views regarding the use
of ICT in schools. Results from this analysis are presented in Table 6.
When the data analysis results presented in Table 6 were examined,
a significant relationship (r ¼ .501, p < .01) was determined between
teachers’ perceptions of collaboration regarding ICT use within their
teaching as well as how much emphasis was placed on teaching approaches
related to CT skills within lessons. In this regard, this relationship was
interpreted as a fact that, when schools offered sufficient ICT to teachers
and supported collaboration among teachers, an increase in ICT use within
teachers’ lessons and the integration of CT skills into lessons occurred. As
a result, the strong correlation (r ¼ .489, p < .01) between teachers’ positive
views regarding ICT use in schools and teachers’ tendency to integrate CT

skills into their lessons supports the finding by Fraillon et al. (2019b) that
effective use of ICT at school ultimately leads to improvements in students’
CT skills in general.
Discussion

Investigating whether teachers’ views regarding the use of ICT in instructional processes, the emphasis placed on teaching approaches to CT, perceptions about collaboration in ICT use, and the CT conceptual development
level of teachers differ in terms of their educational discipline, teaching
experience, and/or graduation factors was considered to be an important
part of teacher education policy development. Analyses in the context of
these variables revealed that there was no significant difference, consistent
with the findings of Korkmaz and Demir (2012). On the other hand, a significant difference was found between teachers’ negative views regarding ICT
use and the level of ICT in their schools—in particular, with negative views
toward schools with low levels of ICT. Consistent with this finding, it was
also determined that teachers tended to use more ICT in their instructional
process in schools with high levels of ICT. In addition, it was found that as
teaching experience increased, teachers’ negative opinions about the use of
ICT in the classroom also increased, and this tended to be most significant
among teachers with 25 years or more of teaching experience. It can be
speculated by this finding that the teachers with a high level of teaching
experience were reluctant to learn about and/or apply newer forms of technology and/or teaching methodologies. In parallel to the increase in teachers’
teaching experience, their decrease in technology use and computer literacy
as well as the increase in the rate of alienation to technology was also
revealed in the literature (Scherer et al., 2015; Tezci, 2010). This general
trend may sometimes differ based on a teacher’s educational discipline. For
example, in Daşdemir et al. (2012) teachers queried about their support of
tablet PC use indicated that science teachers positively supported tablet PC
use while teachers from other disciplines had negative opinions in this
regard. In the same research, science teachers expressed that using a tablet
PC makes the science lessons more enjoyable and suggests that teachers’
views regarding the use of ICT may change in a positive direction if they are
provided effective content and guidance regarding ICT and CT use.
For teachers to guide students to better use thinking strategies such as
CT skills and to do so effectively, teachers need to be trained and prepared
to integrate CT and ICT into their instruction (NRC, 2010). Teachers who
have a low level of conceptual development in terms of CT and/or ineffectively use CT and ICT within their lessons pose an obstacle to students CT
skill development (Blum & Cortina, 2007; Bower et al., 2017; Curzon et al.,

COMPUTERS IN THE SCHOOLS

69

2014). Therefore, determining teachers’ views regarding the use of CT in
schools is an extremely important issue because it is ultimately the first
step in overcoming this obstacle. In addition, based on this study as well as
relevant literature, it can be stated that teachers’ views as they relate to CT
and/or ICT are affected by a variety of demographic features such as their
educational discipline, teaching experience, and/or graduation factors.
Furthermore, based on the results of this study and the research literature,
the same can be stated with regard to the effect of these demographic features on teachers’ opinions about IT and/or ICT (Suarez et al., 2012,
Tondeur et al., 2008, Wong & Li, 2008).
Dexter (2008) argued that to develop teachers’ ICT skills, a learning
environment that fosters and promotes ICT should be created within
schools. However, according to Fraillon et al. (2019b) solely providing ICT
to students and/or teachers is not sufficient for developing their digital
skills. It is recognized in the literature that efforts regarding in-service
teacher training focused on the development of teachers’ computational
thinking skills have become more prevalent in recent years (Lee et al.,
2011). However, there appears to be no effective understanding of how to
best train teacher candidates regarding this matter (Yadav et al., 2014). In
this context, it has been stated that pre-service teachers should first
improve their CT skills, then work to develop their knowledge and skills in
regard to how they can improve their students’ CT skills (Yadav et al.,
2017). This is important because as previously highlighted, solely providing
ICT to teachers and students is not sufficient for developing their digital
skills (Fraillon et al., 2019a). Instead, teachers need education and guidance
regarding how to effectively and consistently use ICT within their lessons.
In line with this view, Gil-Flores et al. (2017) stated that teachers use less
ICT in lessons despite the high level of ICT within Spanish schools in comparison to other countries throughout Europe. Consequently, early exposure of teachers and prospective teachers to CT concepts will support their
understanding of the importance of CT within the learning process (Yadav
et al., 2014). Thus, there will be a domino effect where teachers’ fully grasp
CT at a conceptual level, feel more competent and prepared to develop
their students’ CT competencies (Corradini et al., 2017), and ultimately
overcome any hesitation to integrate CT into their lessons (Rich et al.,
2019). An example of this can be seen in Tasir et al. (2012) who reported
that in Malaysia there is a strong correlation (r ¼ .749) between teachers’
ICT competencies and their confidence in using ICT.
According to Kale et al. (2018), in schools where teachers have at least
some basic knowledge regarding technology as well as an ability to use it,
their students will become more apt at acquiring CT skills. Similarly, according to Fraillon et al. (2019b), the degree to which ICT is used in schools as

70

K. BATI AND M. _I. YETIŞIR

well as how it is used does ultimately affect the development of students’ ICT
and CT skills. The finding obtained in this current study about the relationship between teachers’ cooperation with other teachers regarding the use of
ICT in their instruction as well as the emphasis placed on CT skills within
lessons (r ¼ .501, p < .01) suggested that if schools provide teachers with sufficient ICT and support collaboration among teachers, it ultimately increases
the teachers’ ability to integrate CT skills into their lessons.
Conclusions

Within the scope of this study, data were collected regarding the level of ICT
within the schools of participating teachers. This was carried out in order to
examine the use of ICT in teachers’ instructional processes as well as their
inclusion of CT within their teaching. It was indicated from the findings
obtained that approximately 58% of the teachers queried about the ICT in
their schools rated it at the middle or upper level, while 5.8% of teachers
stated not having ICT in their schools and 5.8% of teachers stated having
very good ICT in their schools. In parallel, 65.2% of teachers rated the use of
ICT in their lessons as effective at a rate of medium or above. The rate of
7.4% for those who stated that they never effectively used ICT and 9.1% for
those who stated that they used it very effectively is in line with the claim
from Guzdial (2016) that teachers’ ICT use is naturally affected by the level
of ICT within their schools. However, teachers are more likely to include
CIL and CT in their instructional processes if they have self-confidence in
ICT use and have positive opinions about ICT as well as work at schools that
encourage collaboration in regard to using ICT within instruction (Fraillon
et al., 2019b). More than half of the teachers who participated in this current
study claimed that they include CT skills in their lessons (M ¼ 30.46,
SD ¼ 4.12). Voogt (2010) revealed that there is a positive relationship
between the frequency of ICT use of teachers and their participation in ICT
competencies and professional development activities. In light of these findings, it can be argued that the level of ICT in schools as well as the amount
of guidance provided about how to use it effectively plays a key role in the
overall improvement of students’ CT skills.
