Keywords:

Increasing investment in educational ICT resources is a strategy adopted worldwide to improve student performance in the digital era. Nevertheless, whether the investment is effective and how to realize its positive effect
remains unclear. To address these limitations, the dataset of PISA 2018 in five countries and regions with superior academic performance was used, and multilevel structural equation modelling was applied. The results
indicated that, first, educational ICT resources are negatively associated with student academic performance.
Second, educational ICT resources positively correlate with cognitive-motivational, curriculum-based engagement, and non-curricular engagement. School and home educational ICT resources exhibit strong associations
with curriculum-based and cognitive-motivational engagement, respectively. Third, student engagement mediates the relationship between educational ICT resources and academic performance, and this mediating role is
different at the school level and the student level. Last, the above relationships show heterogeneities across
research countries and regions. Practical implications for governments, schools, and teachers were discussed.

1. Introduction
The rapid development of ICT has fostered a new generation of
learners in the information era with unique ways to receive, process, and
exchange information (Jones, 2010). The application of ICT in education
leads to profound changes in both traditional educational philosophy
and practical teaching and learning approaches (Lim et al. (2016)). To
meet the new demands of talent training in the information era, many
countries and regions have issued plans and policies to stress the
importance of education digitalization for national and regional development. According to the Office of Educational Technology of the
United States (2017), technology infrastructure is essential for student
success since it offers unique opportunities for creating, designing, and
exploring effectively and supports new types of engagement. Under this
guideline, the National Educational Technology Plan (NETP) has been
carried out for more than 10 years; however, the government has
admitted that the affordability of digital devices continues to be a primary obstacle for many schools, especially in rural areas. European
countries have also addressed the importance of improving ICT-based
education, encouraging teachers to use educational ICT resources to

design courses, develop students’ digital skills, and help students better
adapt to the future society (European Commission, 2015). For instance,
in Finland, the Comprehensive School of the Digital Era Program was
designed to optimize learning environments by providing more digital
resources for teaching and learning (Saarinen et al., 2021).
Aside from the United States and European countries, Asian countries are also actively making preparations for the world of digital education. For instance, in China, The Fourth Strategy on IT in Education
(ITE4) was introduced by the Education Bureau of Hong Kong in 2016 to
enhance students’ learning capabilities with ICT. More concretely, the
need to strengthen school ICT infrastructure and raise the standard of elearning resources is highlighted in ITE4. Additionally, this initiative
invites parents, stakeholders, and community members to participate in
the advancement of the ICT environment (Education Bureau, 2015). In
Korea, The Ministry of Education (2022) announced a five-year plan to
provide more digital education opportunities to Korean people. According to the roadmap, digital curricula are put in a leading position
and receive more funding to equip ICT infrastructure; students will
continuously receive the support of tablets or laptops for their digital
education. Moreover, computer language (coding) will become a

Home educational ICT resources are more convenient, autonomous,
and open than school educational ICT resources (Wellington (2001)).
Therefore, home educational ICT resources are also widely considered to
be the key contributors to student learning and development. Previous
research has reached controversial conclusions on the relationship between home educational ICT resources and student academic performance. Some scholars have affirmed that more sufficient home
educational ICT resources often correlate to higher academic performance. According to Li et al. (2020), students perform better in science
when home educational ICT resources are more accessible. Almerich
et al. (2021) pointed out that using information technology tools to carry
out academic activities at home helps improve students’ higher-order
thinking ability. However, other scholars have held different opinions.
For example, Gonzalez (2021) surveyed more than 2000 secondary
school students and found that home educational ICT resources are
mostly used for non-learning purposes, resulting in a detrimental effect
on student academic performance. Naumann and Salzer (2017) found
that the availability of home information resources has a considerable
negative association with German students’ reading literacy based on
PISA 2012 data. The negative relationship is largely attributable to
students’ entertainment-related misuse of home educational ICT resources. In summary, the relationships between school and home
educational ICT resources and student academic performance have not
been demonstrated clearly by previous studies. Given this, this study
proposes the following competitive hypotheses:

H1a2. : More home educational ICT resources show stronger positive

2. Literature review
2.1. Educational ICT resources and student academic performance

H1b1. : Less school educational ICT resources show stronger positive
correlations with student academic performance than more school
educational ICT resources.

Educational ICT resources are generally acknowledged to be the
foundation and impetus of ICT-based education. The relationship between educational ICT resources used in schools and at home and student academic performance has been thoroughly investigated from two
different viewpoints. Regarding school educational ICT resources, some
scholars consider these resources as an important foundation of student
academic performance due to the high correlation between them. For
example, Zhong (2011) examined the relationship between the accessibility of school educational ICT resources and student digital literacy
and confirmed a positive correlation between them based on PISA 2003
and 2006 survey data. (Naik et al., 2020) conducted a randomized
controlled trial on information technology-assisted teaching in more
than 1800 Indian schools. The results revealed that educational ICT
resources consistently exhibit a significant positive effect on student
academic performance even under the conditions of lower computer
ownership per student and less competent teachers. Some scholars,
however, argued that increasing educational ICT resources is ineffective
and may even adversely impact student academic performance. For
instance, Angrist and Lavy (2002) investigated a computer aid program
and noted that although teachers who received the aid used computers
more frequently in the classroom, the academic performance of their
students did not improve. Li and Wang (2022) examined the connections
between student ICT use, metacognition, and reading literacy. They

2.2. Educational ICT resources and student engagement
It is noted that educational ICT resources can enhance students’
learning motivation and engagement (Khlaif et al., 2021) and support
"student-centred" instructional practices (Akhmed-Zaki et al. (2019)).
Zylka et al. (2015) proposed the concept of “ICT engagement” based on
self-determination theory in response to the ongoing advancement of
ICT and the strengthening of student engagement research. They defined
ICT engagement as a crucial individual component for developing and
adapting ICT skills in a self-regulated way, which supports the acquisition of new knowledge and skills throughout the lifespan in both formal
and informal ways using ICT. ICT interest, perceived ICT competence,
perceived autonomy related to ICT use, and ICT as a topic in social
interaction are the four dimensions of ICT engagement. These four dimensions are also utilized in the PISA test to evaluate student engagement with educational ICT resources from a cognitive-motivational
dimension (Kunina-Habenicht, & Goldhammer, 2020). Two studies

3. Research Design

2.3. The mediating role of student engagement between educational ICT
resources and academic performance

Based on the above literature review, Biggs’s (1987) "Presage-Process-Product" 3 P model was harnessed to build the conceptual
model in this study (Fig. 1). The “Presage” dimension includes characteristics of students and the learning environment; the “Process”
dimension refers to motivation and strategies that students have and use
in a specific institution, which can be measured by a holistic concept
such as student engagement; and the “product” dimension represents
learning outcomes, which are frequently measured by academic tests.
Returning to the present study, school and home educational ICT resources belong to the "Presage" dimension, and their effectiveness is the
main focus of the present research. In addition to including educational
ICT resources in the “Presage” dimension, control variables at the student level and school level are also considered. “Process” is measured by
student engagement based on ICT devices. Martin (2009) proposed that
student engagement can be divided into cognitive-motivational
engagement and behavioural engagement, emphasizing internal motivation and external behaviours, respectively. The concept of ICT
engagement
in
PISA
has
similar
connotations
to
the
cognitive-motivational concept proposed by Martin and is utilized to
measure internal engagement, such as interest in ICT (Kunina-Habenicht, & Goldhammer, 2020). The measurement of behavioural
engagement was from two dimensions, namely, curriculum-based
engagement and non-curricular engagement. Finally, the academic
performance of the “product” dimension is measured by students’ scores
in reading, mathematics, and science.

3.1. Conceptual model

Existing research has rarely examined the whole relationship between educational ICT resources, student engagement, and their academic performance. However, previous discussions on student
engagement and academic performance in the context of ICT-based
education provide cornerstones for this research. For example, Srijamdee and Pholphirul (2020) discovered that using ICT resources to
carry out learning-related activities positively correlates to student academic performance, but carrying out activities unrelated to learning
exhibits negative associations with student academic performance in
Thailand. Ilomaki and Rantanen (2007) conducted a three-year follow-up survey on 18 middle school students who were given laptops to
be used at school and home. The results revealed that students who use
computers more frequently have higher self-reported ICT skills. Another
study by Hu et al. (2018) indicated that academic engagement with ICT
is negatively associated with academic performance, while entertainment engagement with ICT is positively associated with academic performance. Furthermore, considering that ICT engagement is a
subordinate concept to student engagement and that student engagement is confirmed to be a robust predictor of academic performance in
many studies (Lei et al., 2018; Fung et al., 2018), this study proposes the
following hypotheses:
H3. : Student engagement significantly and positively mediates the
relationship between educational ICT resources and student academic
performance.

3.2. Participants

H3a. : Student engagement significantly and positively mediates the
relationship between school educational ICT resources and student academic performance.

The data used in this study come from the Program for International
Student Assessment (PISA) 2018, which was conducted by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD). In comparison to traditional tests of academic performance, PISA pays more
attention to the capabilities of applying and creating knowledge. Students’ reading, mathematical, and scientific literacy are the three main
focuses of the test. In response to the growing demand for applying ICT
in education, PISA developed the ICT Familiarity Questionnaire and
began to investigate students’ accessibility, use frequency, attitudes, and
abilities towards educational ICT resources in 2003. Participants in this
study were 15-year-old secondary school students from Finland, Hong
Kong, South Korea, Singapore, and the United States. Participants were
selected for two reasons in this study: first, students in the five representative countries and regions received high scores on the PISA tests
(OECD, 2019b); second, the five research countries and regions are
actively reforming ICT-based education in recent years and increasing
the investment of educational ICT resources is an important action of
their reforms (see introduction). Nevertheless, few studies have been

cognitive-motivational, curriculum-based, and non-curricular engagement. The cognitive-motivational engagement dimension is in line with
the concept of ICT engagement in PISA, consisting of interest in ICT
(INTICT), perceived competence in ICT (COMPICT), perceived autonomy related to ICT use (AUTICT), and ICT as a topic in social interaction
(SOIAICT) (Habenicht & Goldhamme, 2020). Curriculum-based
engagement evaluates students’ use of ICT in and out of school to
learn subject-related knowledge through ICTCLASS and ICTOUTSIDE.
Non-curricular engagement evaluates students’ use of ICT to send
emails, download audio, chat online, and other behaviours not directly
related to a study by ENTUSE (using ICT for entertainment outside the
classroom), HOMESCH (using ICT for extracurricular activities) and
USESCH (using ICT in school to complete daily affairs). INTICT, COMPICT, AUTICT, SOIAICT, ICTCLASS, ICTOUTSIDE, ENTUSE, HOMESCH,
and USESCH variables are measured by Likert scales first and then
transformed through item response theory. Sample items of these variables are shown in the Appendix.
Additionally, to ensure the accuracy of estimation, the present study
carefully selects the control variables based on existing studies from the
student and school levels (Zhao et al. (2018); Hu et al., 2018; Meng
et al., 2018) to eliminate the bias of omitted variables. The student-level
control variables include gender (female/male), grade (below the model
grade in the country, model grade in the country, above model grade in
the country), and indicators of economic, social, and cultural status
(ESCS). The school-level control variables consist of school type (public/private independent or private government-dependent), student-teacher ratio (STRATIO), school size (SCHSIZE), and teacher
qualification ratio (PROATCE).

3.3. Measures
The dependent variable of this research is student academic performance, consisting of reading, mathematical, and scientific literacy.
Reading literacy refers to students’ abilities to understand, use, evaluate,
reflect, and participate in texts to achieve their goals and develop their
intellectual potential; mathematical literacy indicates students’ abilities
to represent, apply, and explain mathematical concepts, procedures,
facts, and tools in a variety of contexts; and scientific literacy represents
students’ capabilities to understand, engage, and critically analyse
relevant scientific phenomena, evaluate and design scientific inquiry,
and scientifically interpret data and evidence (OECD, 2018). PISA 2018
provides 10 plausible values (PVs) for each student’s reading literacy,
mathematical literacy, and scientific literacy levels. In this study, we
estimated each of the ten data sets (i.e., one data set for each PV and
each literacy), and then combined all parameters with Rubin’s combination rules (OECD, 2019a; Rubin, 1987; Laukaityte and Wiberg, 2017).
Furthermore, we reduced the dependent variables by 100 times to
accelerate the estimation process. Therefore, all estimating results
related to dependent variables should be enlarged by 100 times to understand their real meanings.
Independent variables are school educational ICT resources and
home educational ICT resources. Educational ICT resources are the
material basis for students’ learning, and the effectiveness of educational ICT resources is tightly correlated with formulating policies and
allocating funds by governments and schools. PISA defines educational
ICT resources as hardware, software, networks, and other ICT infrastructure that can provide digital resources, such as desktop computers,
portable laptops, and interactive whiteboards (OECD, 2010). PISA 2018

4.2. The relationship between educational ICT resources, student
engagement, and academic performance
The present research examined the relationship between educational
ICT resources and student academic performance and further analysed
the role of student engagement in the relationship. For the 1–1–1
multilevel model, the mediating effect is estimated at the within level
(student level) and the between level (school level) separately. The
mediating effect at the student level reflects how an individual’s
engagement and academic performance change when he/she gets
higher/lower accessibility to educational ICT resources. The mediating
effect at the school level reflects how average student engagement and
average academic performance change when students tend on average
to have higher/lower accessibility to educational ICT resources. The
results indicated that (Fig. 2 and Table 3) school and home educational
ICT resources show significant and negative correlations with student
academic performance at the student level, while these direct correlations are not always significant at the school level. Hence, H1b is
partially supported. Second, school and home educational ICT resources
represent significant associations with student engagement, but these
correlations are different at two levels. More concretely, at the student
level, school educational ICT resources positively relate to curriculumbased engagement but show no significant correlation with cognitivemotivational engagement. Home educational ICT resources significantly correlate with student cognitive-motivational engagement but
show no significant correlation with curriculum-based engagement.

theory in an ICT-based learning context. Given that the positive mediating effect of cognitive-motivational and curriculum-based engagement
is less than the negative mediating effect of non-curricular engagement
at the student level, student engagement represents a negative mediating effect in general. At the school level, the positive mediating effect
of curriculum-based engagement between home educational ICT resources and academic performance is more necessary than other mediating effects. Thus, a significant positive mediating effect is shown at the
school level.

5. Discussion
The goal of this research was to examine the relationship between
educational ICT resources, student engagement, and student academic
performance. Multilevel structural equation analysis was conducted
with PISA 2018 data from Finland, Hong Kong, South Korea, Singapore,
and the United States. Before reaching conclusions, there are several
research findings worth noting and discussing.
First and foremost, the results indicated the relationship between
educational ICT resources and student academic performance is significantly negative in general. Two possible reasons are proposed to
explain this finding. On the one hand, even if many schools are equipped
with relatively adequate information hardware equipment, the accompanying software resources are still insufficient (Esfijani & Zamani,
2020). On the other hand, the implementation of educational ICT resources is constrained by the level of professionalization of teachers.
Many studies have noted that teachers rarely use educational ICT resources in the classroom (Chisalita & Cretu, 2015; Esfijani & Zamani,
2020; Masingila et al., 2019), and some of them encounter difficulties in
combining conventional teaching strategies with ICT (Hernandez et al.
(2017)). Moreover, the working memory burden of students may increase in courses with improper ICT integration and design, resulting in
decreased efficiency of those resources (Saarinen et al., 2021). The
negative associations between home educational ICT resources and
student academic performance may be brought about by the incapability
and negligence of parents. Some parents may have trouble selecting
appropriate educational ICT resources for their children, and some
parents may even neglect their parental responsibilities to supervise
their children’s use of educational ICT resources, which could lead to
children’s misuse of ICT resources (Stevenson, 2011).
Second, this research discovered that the relationship between
educational ICT resources and student engagement should be understood from both positive and negative perspectives. As expected, more
educational ICT resources show stronger correlations with students’
cognitive-motivational engagement and curriculum-based engagement.
Unexpectedly, more educational ICT resources also exhibit stronger
associations with students’ non-curricular engagement. This finding is in
line with some recent studies assessing the digital literacy of students.
For example, some researchers pointed out that a large percentage of
students are unable to select and use ICT resources effectively, and their

implies that school educational ICT resources could become an important driving force for curriculum-based engagement. However, the
misuse of school educational ICT resources at the student level may
consequently create a negative atmosphere that hinders average student
engagement at the school level. Different from school educational ICT
resources, home educational ICT resources show their unique value in
cognitive-motivational engagement. This may be because students who
are born in an information era develop their interests in ICT from a quite
young age, and parents may be their first teachers in ICT-based learning.
Overall, the above findings depict a clear pattern of how different
educational ICT resources relate to different types of student

engagement, uncovering two significant relationships: school educational ICT resources and curriculum-based engagement, and home
educational ICT resources and cognitive-motivational engagement.
Third, the study found that student engagement mediates the relationship between educational ICT resources and academic performance.
In particular, this relationship is adversely mediated by non-curricular
engagement, while positively mediated by cognitive-motivational
engagement and curriculum-based engagement at the student level. At
the school level, average curriculum-based engagement plays the
dominant mediating role between average educational ICT resources
and academic performance. Average cognitive-motivational engagement also shows a positive correlation with student academic engagement, but the average accessibility of educational ICT resources in each
school does not show a significant link to average student cognitivemotivational engagement. One possible reason for this is that secondary students, who are born in an information age, have almost developed
their cognition and motivation in ICT before entering secondary schools
through diverse ways, such as social media, parents, etc. Therefore,

increasing educational ICT resources in each school may have a negligible impact on their average cognitive-motivational engagement. This
also inspires that secondary schools should pay more attention to student engagement with ICT in curricula rather than focusing on developing students’ cognitive-motivational engagement with ICT. At the
student level, in contrast with other significant mediating effects of
student engagement between educational ICT resources and academic
performance, unexpectedly, the mediating effect of curriculum-based
engagement between educational ICT resources and mathematical literacy is not significant. The unexpected finding may have several explanations, one of which is that compared to other subjects,
mathematics may be harder for a larger proportion of students globally,
resulting in high math anxiety among them (Luttenberger, et al., 2018).
Students’ working memory may be disturbed by math anxiety, and
reduced metacognitive ability is observed for math compared to science
and literature (Erickson & Heit, 2015). This implies that secondary
students’ engagement in mathematics courses with educational ICT resources is worthy of more guidance from teachers, otherwise students’
high anxiety and reduced metacognition abilities may hinder the
effectiveness of educational ICT resources. The significant and positive
mediating effect of curriculum-based engagement in the relationship
between educational ICT resources and mathematical literacy at the
school level also supports this viewpoint, highlighting the importance of
teachers in helping students apply educational ICT resources during
learning.
In addition, there are some interesting findings when comparing the
relationship between educational ICT resources, student engagement,
and academic performance across five research countries and regions.
First, in South Korea, a significant correlation is identified between
school educational ICT resources and student engagement rather than
home educational ICT resources and student engagement. This exceptional phenomenon may be attributed to the relatively insufficient home
educational ICT resources in South Korea (Table 2). On such an occasion, school educational ICT resources take the responsibility of
intriguing students’ interest and motivation in ICT-based learning.
Second, in the United States, the indirect effect of student engagement is
significantly positive while the direct effect between educational ICT
resources and academic performance is significantly negative at the
school level. This indicates that on the one hand, the usage of educational ICT resources in American schools is generally effective; on the
other hand, the misuse of educational ICT resources among American
students is also prevalent, revealing a relatively loose regulation. The
third interesting finding is the strong and positive association between
home educational ICT resources and academic performance among
Singaporean students at the school level. One possible interpretation for
this is, on the one hand, Singaporean students have relatively lower
accessibility to school educational ICT resources (Table 2), thus home
educational ICT resources may compensate for the lack of educational
ICT resources in curricula at school. On the other hand, according to
Singapore’s EdTech Plan (2019), parents are highly encouraged to
engage in children’s ICT-based learning. Parents are provided with
relevant resources and suggestions to ensure their children’s effective
and safe learning with technology at home, which helps improve students’ capabilities in using educational ICT resources. Therefore, when
students use home educational ICT resources (such as iPads) at school,
they can be more self-regulated, less likely to use those ICT resources
inappropriately and finally exhibit higher academic performance.

6.1. Implications
In light of the results, some suggestions are proposed for governments, schools, and teachers. First and foremost, governments should
pay more attention to the effectiveness of educational ICT resources.
Although the investment in educational ICT resources has been continuously expanding in five research countries and regions during the past
decade, the positive relationship between educational ICT resources and
academic performance has not been proved in this study. According to
the research findings, governments are suggested to take different
measures to realize the potential positive effect of educational ICT resources. Specifically, in Finland, Hong Kong, and South Korea, more
attention should be paid to the supply of educational ICT resources for
each student to strengthen the positive link between educational ICT
resources, cognitive-motivational and curriculum-based engagement,
and academic performance. In Singapore, the value of home educational
resources at the school level should not be overlooked. Utilizing these
resources properly may help reduce governments’ burden of supplying a
growing number of educational ICT resources at school. In the United
States, a strong mediating effect of curriculum-based engagement was
represented. Therefore, American governments are suggested to shift
investment from infrastructure to teacher training to some extent in
order to enlarge the benefits of ICT-based curricula at the school level.
Second, schools are suggested to adjust their focus when utilizing
educational ICT resources. Typically, South Korean schools should
emphasize the curriculum construction and the development of students’ interest and motivation in ICT simultaneously. Schools in Finland,
Hong Kong, Singapore, and the United States are recommended to focus
more on curriculum optimization, since students’ interest and motivation in ICT may be developed more effectively or earlier in their families.
Typically, how to realize the positive potential of educational ICT resources in mathematical courses remains to be explored. In addition,
schools could organize various forms of professional development activities to raise teachers’ awareness of ICT-based teaching and enhance
their capabilities to incorporate ICT resources into curricula. Building an
evaluation system for teachers’ ICT-based teaching skills may also be
helpful because teachers can be regularly monitored or self-monitored
and receive feedback to promote their instructions continuously.
Teachers are suggested to strengthen the management of students’
non-curricular engagement and guide students’ usage of ICT resources
in school, and this issue should be particularly addressed in the United
States. Teachers are also encouraged to carry out activities to improve
students’ abilities to select and judge different educational ICT resources. In addition, teachers are recommended to broaden the path of
home-school cooperation and help parents support students’ learning
process with educational ICT resources. In this way, schools and homes
could form a joint force and create a new model of home-school cooperation in the digital era. Here, the experience of active parent
engagement in Singapore might be helpful and inspiring.

6. Conclusions
This study first explored the relationship between educational ICT
resources and student academic performance at the school and the
student level using MSEM; then, the mediating role of student engagement in the above relationship was examined; and finally, heterogeneities in the relationship across different countries and regions were
discussed. This study extends Biggs’ 3 P model in the context of ICT10

6.2. Limitations and future study

CRediT authorship contribution statement

This study has the following limitations. First, the research findings
are based on five countries and regions that have higher PISA scores.
Therefore, whether the findings are valid in other contexts remains to be
assessed. Second, deeper research on the heterogeneous relationships
between core variables in different countries and regions is required.
Policy text analysis and case studies are recommended to explore the
underlying mechanisms of the relationship. Third, the research mainly
examines the correlation between educational ICT resources, student
engagement, and academic performance using cross-sectional data.
Causal relationships among these variables are expected to be examined
with longitudinal data or experimental design in the future.
