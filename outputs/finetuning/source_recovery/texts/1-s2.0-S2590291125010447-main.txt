A B S T R A C T
The present study examines the technological preparedness of schools, teachers, and students for the pandemic in 
US schools using PISA 2018 data (N = 4838). Results showed that (a) the school technology policy strongly 
affects schools’ capacity for teaching-learning and collaboration; (b) the location of school had effect on tech­
nological preparedness; (c) teacher gender had mixed effect on technological preparedness; and (d) female 
students had higher correlations for student reported constructs. These findings support the contention that the 
school’s technology policy is a major factor for the technological preparedness for equitable learning by creating 
opportunities for digital resources, promoting collaboration, and improved pedagogical practices. The findings of 
this study contribute to the questions of to what extent schools, teachers, and students were prepared for global 
pandemic.
Educators have been trying to achieve the goals of meaningful 
technology integration in the K-12 classrooms for the last several de­
cades (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2013). However, the efforts to 
integrate Information and Communication Technology (ICT) to its full 
extent have not been achieved (Davies & West, 2014; U.S. Department of 
Education, 2010). In fact, issues of ineffective ICT usage in American 
classrooms were visible during the pandemic (Ikeda, 2020). More pre­
cisely, during the pandemic, the educational system relied on the use of 
technology to learn, live, and stay connected (Goldschmidt, 2020); 
however, the past efforts did not prepare the U.S. schools for equitable 
online learning (Ikeda, 2020).
The diffusion of ICT in society has affected education in myriad ways 
resulting in a wider adoption and implementation of ICT policies (Siddiq 
et al., 2016). Research suggests that ICT skills are relevant and necessary 
to achieve economic and social development of the nation (Van Laar 
et al., 2017). Additionally, it is evident that to grow the nation’s econ­
omy, students need to attain skills of collaboration, communication, 
digital literacy, citizenship, problem-solving, critical thinking, crea­
tivity, and productive abilities (Voogt & Roblin, 2012). Thus, to achieve 
economic prosperity, twenty-first-century technological advancement 
and the focus on digital problem-solving skills in K12 schools are crucial 
and mandated (Ferrari, 2013; Siddiq et al., 2016; Van Laar et al., 2017). 
By framing the importance of digital technologies, the school leaders 
and policymakers are promoting teachers’ use of technology in class­
rooms (Office of Ed Tech, 2016).
As students and teachers are the major actors in the education sys­
tem, their attitudes for ICT acceptance and adoption are essential for 
equitable learning (Scherer et al., 2018). The effort to promote tech­
nology in public education has been initiated through several federal 
policies such as the National Education Technology Plan (NETP) and the 
No Child Left Behind Act of 2001 (NCLB, 2001; NETP, 1996; NETP, 
2017).To attain the goals of fully technology-enabled learning, the focus 
should be on helping the teachers to integrate technology in the class­
rooms (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2013; Ikeda, 2020).
ICT is the use of computer-based classroom instructional technolo­
gies and processes for teaching and learning (Ghavifekr & Rosdy, 2015). 
It supports the process of knowledge transfer and acquisition while 
incorporating new skills of teaching and learning for the potential users 
(Grani´c & Maranguni´c, 2019). Additionally, teachers’ professional 
competencies in integrating ICT in the classroom is significant for 
teachers to enter into the workforce (Scherer et al., 2018; Van Laar et al., 
2017). However, teacher’s digital readiness has been an issue for the full 
implementation of ICT (OECD, 2020). Thus, to meet the challenges of 
digital classrooms competencies, school districts should include the 
Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis, 1986, 1989) in their 
technology policy.

Previous studies have explored the use of technology in the class­
room and suggest that it provides an interactive learning environment 
with better student attention compared to traditional ways of learning 
(Pazilah et al., 2019). Additionally, technology improves teacher’s 
digital fluency while reforming their knowledge and promotes creativity 
(Wang et al., 2013). Thus, the importance lies in expecting teachers to 
have mastery of the technology usage for effective teaching and learning 
(Beauchamp et al., 2015; Jimoyiannis, 2010; Tweed, 2013; Inan & 
Lowther, 2009). To achieve the goals of well-versed technology usage in 
classrooms, effective and well-designed professional development ac­
tivities are necessary for teacher development (Yurtseven et al., 2019).
1. Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK)
This study draws conceptual tools from the Technological Peda­
gogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) framework (Mishra & Koehler, 
2006) to examine the technological preparedness of the U.S. education 
system for the pandemic. The Technological Pedagogical Content 
Knowledge (TPACK) framework is the intersection of Content Knowl­
edge (CK), Pedagogical Knowledge (PK), and Technological Knowledge 
(TK) to provide knowledge, skills, and abilities for classroom teaching 
and learning (Mishra & Koehler, 2006). In TPACK, all the three domains 
such as content, pedagogy, and technology play equal roles in driving 
teaching-learning, and they are interdependent with each other. Harris 
et al. (2009) describe that TPACK focuses on the connections among 
technologies, curriculum content, specific pedagogical approaches, and 
how it is affecting and improving teachers’ understandings of technol­
ogy, pedagogy, and content to teach disciplines with educational 
technologies.
Educational technology creates a rich student-centered learning 
environment with exceptional opportunities (DenBeste, 2003). While 
designing the course, teachers should consider how technology will 
integrate with content and pedagogy to support the learning objectives 
using the technological tools and abilities to enhance and support stu­
dent learning. TPACK manifests teachers with a variety of skills such as 
ability to evaluate and select the right tool to meet the pedagogical need, 
finding the appropriate digital resources for subject/grade, and how it 
operates effectively to create best learning opportunities for students. 
Archambault and Crippen (2009) describe that the TPACK framework is 
a useful instrument in providing “an analytical structure for researching 
what teachers should know and be able to do and highlighting the 
importance of content knowledge when incorporating the use of tech­
nology” (p. 83). Thus, the TPACK extends to understanding how stu­
dents engage with the elements of instructional technology in 
conjunction with content and pedagogy.
In online teaching, TPACK presents a systematic way to organize 
high quality instruction while incorporating technology (Archambault & 
Crippen, 2009). The essence of technology in education creates an op­
portunity for teachers to explore innovative ways of learning; however, 
the educators need to understand how and when it works effectively. 
Integration of technology in classroom precedes with the ability to apply 
the technological knowledge so that “instead of applying technological 
tools to every content area uniformly, teachers should come to under­
stand that the various affordances and constraints of technology differ 
by curricular subject-matter content or pedagogical approach” (Koehler 
& Mishra, 2008, p. 22). Thus, the importance lies in teachers knowing 
TPACK to construct, facilitate, and prepare learning environments to use 
ICT technologies including artificial intelligence (Kim et al., 2021).
Although from a policy perspective, schools’ technology policy, 
location of schools, and teacher and student gender are important for 
technological preparedness for the pandemic in providing equitable 
learning opportunities, yet research on its effectiveness is limited. Thus, 
the importance lies in examining two major notions for the nation’s 
public schools: 1) the empirical evidence of schools’ technological in­
fluence on teachers’ and students’ technological preparedness, and 2) 
how teachers’ technological preparedness helped students’ technology 
readiness for equitable online learning for the pandemic. Thus, to 
quantitatively examine the technological preparedness of U.S. schools 
for the pandemic, the study asks two questions. 
Research Question 1: To what degree did school districts’ techno­
logical policies and preparedness influence teachers’ and students’ 
technological preparedness for an equitable learning environment 
for the pandemic?
Research Question 2: To what degree does teacher technology 
usage in the classroom help students to use technology at school and 
home?
To answer research questions, six hypotheses had been tested using 
Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) by drawing the data from the 
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2018 as pre­
sented in Table 3. For the study, the PISA 2018 dataset was carefully 
selected as it is the nationally representative data sample of US schools 
and provided publicly available dataset just before the pandemic. PISA 
2018 is the best dataset for this study as it collected data on student 
demographics, students and teachers’ attitude towards technology, 
schools’ learning environment, teachers’ teaching experiences, and 
teachers’ views on schools’ policies and evaluation (Kastberg et al., 
2021). Equitable learning environment is essential for 21st-century 
learning goals and digital technologies have shown as a powerful in­
strument that can help improve education and set new directions for 
future policy measures in promoting educational, economic, and social 
growth (Haleem et al., 2022). The current study fills the gap by exam­
ining the technological preparedness of schools, teachers, and students, 
and what matters most in providing an equitable learning environment 
to all students.
2. Background on technology Reforms in US schools
The NETP was introduced with a vision to reduce the achievement 
gaps among students through equity and inclusion which allowed 
schools to reset their priorities to improve educational opportunities 
(NETP, 2017). The NCLB mandated similar efforts to integrate tech­
nology in K12 education and created a policy window for advocates to 
push for increased funding and more accountability in reducing 
achievement gaps. The U.S. federal and state governments made avail­
able roughly anywhere from $26 billion to $41 billion per year for 
schools to invest in educational technology (The digital access divide, 
2024). These investments are being made on the belief that 
technology-mediated learning environments will provide an enhanced 
learning 
environment 
where 
students 
will 
learn 
skills 
of 
problem-solving, analyzing and integrating information, collaboration, 
and communication which will help them with twenty-first-century 
workforce needs (Lim et al., 2013).
During the pandemic, technological resources in the nation’s public 
schools were not sufficient to meet the technological challenges. To 
combat that deficit, the technology stabilization fund was established 
under the Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security Act to help 
schools establish several grant programs (CARES Act, 2020). To address 
schools’ technological needs, the Coronavirus Response and Relief 
Supplemental Appropriations Act was signed into law to provide an 
additional $81.9 billion to the Education Stabilization Fund (CRRSA, 
2021).

efficacy, schools’ vision should be integrating pedagogy which supports

4. Location of schools
The decision of how to use technology for instructional purposes 
depends on the teachers’ beliefs on technology (Ertmer, 2005); however, 
the location of schools plays an important role in it. In their research 
Shambare and Jita (2024) reported that the constant evolution and 
change in technology requires ongoing updates and adaptations of 
technology-related knowledge resulting in a delay in teachers’ knowl­
edge for technology integration which elucidate comparatively lower 
knowledge of technology for teachers in rural schools. Thus, it un­
derscores the importance of continuous professional development for 
effective technology integration in schools (Shambare & Jita, 2024). 
Teachers recognize and understand the importance of integrating tech­
nology; however, limitations impose barriers in technology imple­
mentation (Hew & Brush, 2006). Technology in the classroom not only 
has significance in student learning, it provides an interesting and 
engaging classroom environment (Pazilah et al., 2019). The following 
hypothesis was tested to examine how location of schools imposed on 
technological preparedness. 
Hypothesis 2.
The location of the school has a significant effect on the 
technological preparedness of schools, teachers, and students.
5. Teacher quality
Several factors such as class size, curriculum revamping, reorganiz­
ing class schedules, and investment in technology impact student 
achievements; however, teacher quality (TQ) is the most important 
factor (Goldring et al., 2014; Hanushek, 2011; Tyler et al., 2010). 
Excellent teachers have much higher long-term student achievement 
gains than average teachers (Hanushek, 2011). More precisely, teachers 
who possess knowledge of the TPACK framework are more comfortable 
and satisfied with technology integration in their classrooms (Raygan & 
Moradkhani, 2020). In addition, knowledge of the TPACK framework is 
vital for the successful implementation of digital technologies such as 
computer-assisted language learning (CALL) and mobile-assisted lan­
guage learning (MALL) in English as a foreign language (EFL) classroom 
(Hsu, 2016).
Technology has been used as a tool to remove achievement gaps; 
however, effects heavily depend on how, where, and when it has been 
used. Students’ perspectives on the teachers’ use of technological tools 
are essential for overall students’ performance as they thrive on teach­
ers’ use of technology for group work (Hoffmann & Ramirez, 2018). 
Using a similar lens, I examined whether teachers’ use of technology has 
an impact on students’ technological preparedness. 
Hypothesis 3.
If the teachers are using technology in classrooms, it 
motivates students to use technology for academic learning purposes.
6. Teacher gender for technological preparedness
Teachers are the strongest factor in student learning because it 
matters what teachers know, do, and care (Hattie, 2003) and teacher 
gender plays an important role in student learning. Gender differences 
had played a role in classrooms where male teachers are more confident 
in content knowledge (CK) and less in pedagogical knowledge (PK) 
(Jang & Chang, 2016; Liu et al., 2015). Thus, enhancing male teachers’ 
pedagogical knowledge (PK) strategies may be helpful to development 
their TPACK (Cheng, 2017). On another note, female teachers perceive 
higher self-confidence in PK (Lin et al., 2013). Additionally, female 
teachers generally perceive lower self-confidence in technological 
knowledge (TK) redundency with regard to technology application (TK) 
(Koh et al., 2014; Roig-Vila et al., 2015). Considering role of teacher 
gender in education, gender showed mediated effects on relationship 
between teachers’ age and their perceived TPACK knowledge where 
highly experienced female teachers had more confidence in their ability 
to guide students using their pedagogical knowledge (PK) then male 
teachers (Cheng, 2017). In other words, teaching experiences showed 
high significance for PK in female teachers’ but no significance for male 
teachers. Additionally, the competence in TK is an essential factor in 
promoting female teachers’ ability to teach native language such as 
Hakka language with an appropriate combination of subject content, 
technologies, and instructional strategies exploring the TPACK frame­
work (Cheng, 2017).
Considering teacher gender as a characteristic affecting student 
learning with the use of technology, the study tested the hypothesis of 
the importance of teacher gender in technology-embedded classrooms. 
Hypothesis 4.
Teacher gender will predict students, teachers, and 
schools’ technological preparedness.
7. Student learning in technology embedded classroom
The TPACK describes a deeper level of understanding of how to 
provide instruction so that students are actively participating in the 
learning process as an intersection of pedagogy, content, and technology 
(Mishra & Koehler, 2006). Results suggest that students’ academic 
self-efficacy is the most important characteristic that influences their 
academic achievement and teachers’ TPACK levels add value to it. More 
precisely, teachers’ TPACK levels have higher impact than students’ 
social, and emotional self-efficacy (Akturk & Saka Ozturk, 2019). In 
other words, to achieve learning goals, it is extremely importance for 
teachers to have high skills to integrate technology into coursework and 
students’ need self-efficacy to succeed.
Bray and Tangney (2015) suggest that mobile technology has the 
potential to transform the traditional classroom into an inclusive and 
collaborative 
learning 
environment 
with

for 
problem-solving and data sharing activities; however, educators need to 
re-imagine teaching and learning with enhanced learning experiences 
for students (Heflin et al., 2017). Technology helps students to use their

styles (Zmuda, 2009). Some research suggests that technology has 
negative effects because of student multitasking during classroom in­
struction, which results in lower overall grades, less time spent on 
studying, and lack of any makeup from students (Bellur et al., 2015). 
Therefore, educators should carefully consider such situations while 
allowing students to use technology in the classroom and explain the 
consequences of technology misuse to the students.

To summarize, the general belief is that the issues of equity and in­
clusion can be addressed with the effective use of technology, but 
schools’ capacity was lacking during the pandemic. Despite the growing 
efforts to embed technology in education, the entire U.S. educational 
system struggled to provide uninterrupted learning experiences to all 
students. Previous literature has shed light on different factors such as 
effective use of digital technology, teachers’ attitude towards the use of 
ICT, barriers in the ICT implementation, and teacher professional 
development for ICT. However, we know little about what the most 
relevant technology is and how the association works for ICT in the 
classrooms. In addition, some researchers have examined the techno­
logical preparedness of schools, teachers, and students during the 
pandemic but what was the actual preparedness is debatable. To answer 
the research questions, I tested the hypotheses to fill the gap in research 
by examining the technological preparedness of schools, teachers, and 
students by latent variable approach while using the tools of Structural 
Equation Modeling (SEM).
8. Research design and Methodology
The study used SEM to examine the relationships between observed 
and unobserved variables (Byrne, 2012). To test the hypotheses, model 
fit, and individual parameter estimation was performed (Kline, 2015) by 
examining Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) using optimal parceling 
scheme. The R programming was used for the analyses.
8.1. Data selection
For this study, PISA 2018 is the most relevant publicly available data 
before pandemic. PISA is a two-stage stratified systematic sample with 
sampling probabilities proportional to the estimated number of 15-year- 
old students in the grade level. The three main outcomes are: 1) Basic 
indicators - profile of knowledge and skills, 2) Indicators from ques­
tionnaires shows how skills are related to demographic, social, eco­
nomic, and educational variables, and 3) Indicators to show the 
relationship between student-level, school-level, and system-level vari­
ables (OECD, 2019).
8.2. Data analysis scheme
A four-step statistical process was performed to determine if the data 
is sufficient to conduct the SEM and the steps are: 1) test of the statistical 
assumptions for the model, 2) CFA, 3) parceled-level CFA, and 4) SEM. 
While analyzing the PISA 2018 dataset, measure weight has not been 
considered because of the following two recommendations: a) the study 
uses perception data of only US schools, teachers, and students, and no 
pupils’ proficiency scores (“plausible values”) were used (Jerrim et al., 
2017), and b) no two educational systems (countries) were compared for 
the technological preparedness. Considering these recommendations, 
this study does not require to use measure weight.
First, the test of statistical assumptions is performed by creating the 
Latent Constructs (LC). Secondly, I established the CFA model to test the 
ten-factor model of the technology usage provided by schools, teachers, 
and students. Thirdly, after establishing the CFA model, items were 
parceled to achieve a three-item model for higher reliability, greater 
commonality, higher common-to-unique factor variance, reduced 
distributional variations, and better or equal intervals (Little, 2013). In 
other words, parceling is done to achieve a better model fit by reducing 
the errors of the overall model. Lastly, I used the techniques of SEM to 
evaluate measurement invariance, test of latent mean, variance and 
covariance, phantom constructs, and latent regression analysis.
8.3. Descriptive statistics
For the study, a total of 4838 students containing 2462 boys (50.9 %) 
and 2376 girls (49.1 %) participated from 162 US schools. A total of 167 
questions from school, teacher, and student questionnaires were used. 
The missing value ranges from 0 % to 14.72 % for the dataset. The means 
of the variables range from .50 to 3.10 and the standard deviations range 
from .16 to 1.32 as presented in Appendix table.

8.4.2. Teacher technology constructs
Two teacher technology constructs were created using 22 survey 
questions. The teacher’s technology constructs are as follows: 1) Teacher 
Utility of Technology (TUT), and 2) Teacher Digital Reading Activity 
(TDA). The survey questions are presented in Appendix A and the 
description of the questions are in Table 1.
Teacher Utility of Technology. The construct TUT was created using 
fourteen questions about teachers’ use of technology, software, digital 
tools, social media, and ICT resources. Responses are on a four-point 
scale (1 = never, 2 = in some lessons, 3 = in most lessons, 4 = in 
every or almost every lesson).
Teacher Digital Reading Activity. The construct TDA was created 
using six questions about the teachers’ use of digital devices for reading 
email, chatting online, reading online news, searching online informa­
tion on particular topics, and discussions on online forums. Responses 
are on a five-point scale (1 = min, 5 = max).
8.4.3. Student technology constructs
Four student technology constructs were created using 97 survey 
questions. The student technology constructs are as follows: 1) Student 
Technology Availability (STA), 2) Student Digital Device use for 
Learning (STL), 3) Student Time Spent on Digital Devices (STD), and 4) 
Student Experience with Digital Device (SED). The survey questions are 
presented in Appendix A and the description of the questions are in 
Table 1.
Student Technology Availability. The construct STA was created 
using twenty questions about the availability of technological devices 
such as desktops, laptops, tablets, internet connection, video games, 
printers, cell phones, etc. for student use at school and home. Responses 
are on a three-point scale (0 = no, 1 = yes, but I do not use it, 2 = yes, 
and I use it).
Student Digital Device use for Learning. The construct STL was 
created using twenty-seven questions about students’ use of digital de­
vices for lessons in science, mathematics, social science, foreign lan­
guage, sports, visual arts, etc. during the classroom lessons, outside the 
classroom lessons, and for teaching or learning during the lessons. Re­
sponses are on a five-point scale (0 = min, 4 = max).
Student Time Spent on Digital Devices. The construct STD was 
created using thirty-four questions about students’ use of digital devices 
for games, chatting, social networking, reading news, listening to music, 
email communication, doing homework, etc. at school and outside of 
school. Responses are on a five-point scale (1 = min, 5 = max).
Student Experience with Digital Device. The construct SED was 
created using fifteen questions about students’ experiences and comfort 
with digital devices and digital media, and ability to help others with 
digital devices and media. Responses are on a four-point scale (1 =
strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = agree, 4 = strongly agree).

Next, I examined the latent mean difference between two groups

variance partial.

significantly different. By constraining, △χ2 = 1.18, Δdf = 4, △CFI =
.000, p = .9779 > .05, which implies that the mean difference test passed 
for the constrained model and there is a statistically significant differ­

numerically negative for female students.

10. Discussion
10.1. Characteristics of schools’ technological preparedness
The schools play a dominant role in implementing digital technolo­
gies in providing 21st-century learning skills for equitable learning (Van 
Laar et al., 2017). Digital technologies are continuing to transform ed­
ucation and how we work (OECD, 2020). Technology enhances equi­
table access to information; however, the quality of information is 
accessed is not a guarantee (Lim et al., 2013). Thus, the importance lies 
in developing a system for quality information access and the imple­
mentation of technology in classrooms through TK is most prominent 
among all technology-related components (Shambare & Jita, 2024).
10.1.1. Schools’ technology policies and usage
The findings of the study confirm and extend on previous literature 
indicating that the school technology policy has a strong influence on 
technology usage for learning processes. More precisely, the schools’ 
technology policy (SCP) carries an appropriate technology practice in 
school (SCS). As Raygan and Moradkhani (2020) stated, the teachers’ 
attitude towards use of technology is created by school climate and is 
supported by institutions policy toward technology integration. It is 
perceived that schools with written digital technology policy for 
teaching and learning have alignment with teachers’ intentions, atti­
tude, and collaboration with respect to integration of technology in 
classrooms. In general, when the schools have their own written state­
ment and policies for digital device usages for pedagogical purposes, it 
translates to responsible internet behavior and equitable learning op­
portunities. In other words, the schools with written digital technology

and city. In other words, schools located in higher population areas were 
better prepared than low population areas. In our study, student gender 
played no role in the overall technological preparedness of the schools 
based on location of the schools.
10.2. Characteristics of teachers’ technological preparedness
10.2.1. Teachers’ technology usage for academic purposes
TPACK and teacher attitude have a significant relationship with 
positive technology integration in classrooms. In other words, teachers’ 
higher TPACK level and positive attitude predicts higher technology 
integration (Raygan & Moradkhani, 2020). In fact, Archambault and 
Crippen (2009) suggested that TCK is the most challenging area for the 
teachers. Thus, teachers with higher level of TPACK implicates higher 
technology usage for teaching and learning. The current research shows 
similar results and suggests that the teachers’ use of technology is 
correlated to their self-efficacy, perceived computer skills, and their 
technology support and access (Li et al., 2015). In other words, the 
higher the teacher’s use of technology, the higher the digital technology 
skills they bring to the classroom teaching. The study found evidence 
suggesting that there is a statistically moderate correlation between 
teachers’ digital reading activities (TDA) and teachers’ use of technol­
ogy (TUT) for teaching and learning which implies that teachers who 
were reading information digitally are inclined towards using digital 
technology for in-class teaching and learning. In other words, the 
teachers who are searching for information online through emails, news, 
on specific topics, discussion, and chatting are inclined to use different 
software etc. more often in their lessons.
Prior research suggests that teachers have a high variance in stu­
dents’ academic achievement (Hattie, 2003, 2008) which indicated 
higher technology usage by teachers in the classroom influences higher 
student technology usage for academic learning purposes. We examined 
the effects of teacher usage of technology in classroom instruction, and 
how it influences students to use technology for academic learning at 
school as well as home. The study did not reveal any direct correlation 
between the teachers’ use of digital technology in the classroom for 
teaching and students’ use of digital technology for academic learning. 
The use of technology in classroom requires training, practicing, 
exploring technology, and teacher’s willingness to learn (Vannatta & 
Fordham, 2004). Similar results are found in this study. In other words, 
teachers who are using technological tools daily reported that they often 
use technological tools for teaching and learning.
10.2.2. Role of Teacher gender
Teacher gender matters for the use of technology in classroom in­
struction. In fact, teachers’ gender has significant relationship with 
teacher’s content knowledge and pedagogical content knowledge (Luo 
et al., 2022). Another research suggests that the adoption of educational 
technology in higher education suggests that male teachers have greater 
expertise and feel more confident than females; however, female 
teachers are more student-centric (Zhou & Xu, 2007). In a similar study, 
Cheng (2017) suggest that the male teachers showed more confidence in 
content knowledge (CK) and technology knowledge (TK) than the fe­
male teachers. Considering the significance of teacher gender, we 
examined the role of teacher gender in the technological preparedness of 
schools and compared the technological preparedness of male and the 
female teachers. No effect of teacher gender on availability of digital 
devices and students’ use of digital technology in school/home was 
found. Cheng (2017) suggest that TPACK of game-based learning 
revealed that males tended to learn digital games more easily and have 
more effective technical skills to play digital games (TK) than females 
which implicate that male teachers have higher aptitude of TK. The 
study has similar findings suggest that SCL is higher for male teachers 
compared to female. On the other hand, we found that male teacher had 
negative effect on schools’ written technology policy, use of digital de­
vices on teaching and learning, culture for technology usage in schools, 
and use of technology for personal purposes.
In summary, male teachers perceive lower for schools’ technology 
policy, digital device usage, learning culture using technology, and 
person digital technology usage. In other words, it is suggested that the 
female teachers are more influenced by the school’s technology policy, 
use of technology for teaching, and personal uses. It was also evident 
that teacher gender has no influence on the students’ use of digital 
media and digital devices of teaching and learning.
10.3. Students’ technology usage and preparedness
10.3.1. Technology usage on student gender
Schindler et al. (2017) suggests that digital technologies such as 
digital games, web-conferencing software, and social media (Facebook) 
have an influence on student engagement (Schindler et al., 2017). 
Research suggests that technology usage has a positive relationship with 
student engagement; however, it has a negative and insignificant rela­
tionship with academic performance (Rashid & Asghar, 2016). Mims- 
Word (2012) suggests that both genders of students can navigate com­
puters equally; however, gender differences appear in relation to some 
specific computer usage activities. Similar effect has seen in this study 
where female students have higher association between how much time 
they spent on digital devices (STL) and how often they use digital de­
vices for learning activities (STD) compared to male students.
Research suggests that students’ home environments are an impor­
tant factor in developing an interest in computer technology (Margolis & 
Fisher, 2002). On a similar note, we found that digital environment 
matters where female has higher associate between how often they use 
digital devices for learning activities their experiences with digital de­
vices (STD) and their experiences with digital devices and media (SED) 
compared to male students. Next, results suggests that female students 
have higher association between how much time they spent on digital 
devices (STL) and their experiences with digital devices and media 
(SED) compared to male students. Thus, while designing the courses, 
designers should emphasize the friendliness of mobile-assisted and 
computer-assisted materials and their affordances of various mobile 
device content transformation and pedagogy (Hsu, 2016). We recom­
mend that it is extremely important for students to use digital devices for 
learning as digital experiences provide better experiences for all stu­
dents including male as well as female.
10.3.2. Latent mean difference
Lastly, we examined the latent mean difference of female students 
compared to male students and revealed that the latent mean for the 
constructs SCT, SCP, SCS, SCL, TUT, and TDA are the same for both male 
and female students. Interestingly, for female students, latent mean for 
the constructs STA, STL, STD, and SED is lower than the male students. 
In other words, the female students rated lower for constructs STA, STL, 
STD, and SED than the male students which implies that female students 
rated lower for these constructs comparing to male students.

The present study investigates the technological preparedness of 
schools, teachers, and students for the pandemic using data (N = 4838)

policies, availability of resources, and learning environment.
11.1. Implications, limitations, and future research
There are two implications from the present study. First, there needs 
to be a greater focus on the written technology policy for schools. The 
schools’ written technology policy provides a foundational direction for 
schools, teachers, and students and ensures promoting proper resources 
for ICT embedded teaching and learning. If schools truly want to impact 
and maximize student learning, they should consider the technology 
embedded learning environment through various angles through tech­
nology initiatives and policy measures (Higgins, Huscroft-D’Angelo, & 
Crawford, 2017). Second, technology is a powerful tool to improve 
student achievement where school can devise strategies to implement 
technology in creative ways to remove achievement gaps and create 
equitable learning for all. For the students’ overall success, it is para­
mount to design teaching and learning activities that help students 
achieve the intended learning goals (Kuhn & Rundle-Thiele, 2009).
As with all studies, the results of the present study must be viewed in 
light as there are some notable limitations. First, the study used PISA 
2018 survey-based data that was collected a few years before pandemic 
and presents a sampling limitation to examine the actual technological 
preparedness of schools, teachers, and students. Although various 
sensitivity analyses were performed but the Post hoc analysis performed 
might represent some limitations and generalization of the results.
Second, this study represents an in-depth analysis of schools, 
teachers, and students’ technological preparedness and contributes 
theoretically and empirically in understanding the technological pre­
paredness of all stakeholders, but readers should aware that these 
finding are not generalized for the entire educational system and does 
not represent all the schools, teachers, and students.
Third, the analysis shows the issues of reliability for some constructs. 
Two constructs such as SCP and SCS have lower reliability (ω, mc 
Donald’s omega) which presents some issues with the consistency of the 
measures. In other words, the internal consistency of the items of the 
constructs SCP and SCS possess some issues and presents lower factor 
loadings (higher unexplained variances).
Fourth, the research finding was inconclusive to establish the direct 
correlation between the teachers’ digital technology usage in the 
classroom and students’ digital technology usage; however, it was 
evident that students were given more opportunities to use digital 
technology in schools ar e encouraged to use technology for their own 
learning.
The findings of this study contribute to the questions of technological 
preparedness of schools, teachers, and students for global pandemic; 
however, more research is needed to establish what resources matter 
most for technological preparedness in education system. Another 
recommendation would be to replicate this study to measure techno­
logical preparedness of schools, teachers, and students after pandemic 
and how these stakeholders have evolved to avoid such situations.
