A B S T R A C T  
Teacher innovation has increasingly received the attention of researchers from various disciplines. A total of 109 
articles published between 1971 and 2023 were selected and qualified for inclusion in this review study. Our 
study emphasized the imperative for enhanced conceptual precision to refine the understanding of teacher 
innovation. Additionally, we underscore the necessity for methodological advancements that bolster the rigor of 
empirical investigations. Furthermore, we advocate for theory-driven research to deepen insights into the un­
derlying mechanisms of teacher innovation processes. Lastly, we emphasize to acknowledge both the positive 
outcomes and possible drawbacks, or the “dark side,” of teacher innovation.   
1. Introduction 
In the past decade, scholarly consensus has been reached on the idea 
that effective education reforms hinge on fostering conducive school 
environments that inspire and empower teacher innovation (Bl¨omeke 
et al., 2021). Such environments are characterized by teachers devel­
oping competencies to design personalized, learner-centered, and 
cognitively engaging learning experiences to enhance student perfor­
mance (Bl¨omeke et al., 2021; OECD, 2019). Consequently, there has 
been a surge in studying teacher innovation which has been defined as a 
deliberate process encompassing idea generation, development, imple­
mentation, evaluation, modification, dissemination, continuation, and 
ultimately, the realization of anticipated benefits (Chand, 2014; Kuril 
et al., 2023; Lukes & Stephan, 2017; Moore, 2005). Researchers have 
argued that teacher innovation plays a pivotal role in enhancing the 
educational process and outcomes through the cross-disciplinary 
teaching skills, personalized and collaborative learning practices, 
problem-based learning approaches, heightened cognitive activation, 
and formative feedback to students (Andiliou & Murphy, 2010; Bl¨omeke 
et al., 2021; Buske, 2018; OECD, 2019; Thurlings et al., 2015). Schools 
characterized by high levels of teacher innovation are associated with 
increased student enjoyment of learning, enhanced academic 
self-efficacy, and improved academic outcomes (Maun et al., 2023; 
Vincent-Lancrin et al., 2019). Furthermore, scholars have delved into 
the structural supports and personal factors that contribute to successful 
teacher innovation (Buske, 2018; Johari et al., 2021; Nguyen et al., 
2021; Noviyanti et al., 2021; Rais & Rubini, 2022). 
Despite the growing recognition of the pivotal role of teacher inno­
vation, the current understanding of this issue remains fragmented and 
lacks clarity, hindering knowledge development in this area (Da’as, 
2023). Persistent issues include ambiguous definitions (Mullet et al., 
2016), insufficient theoretical grounding, inconsistent research findings 
(Lambriex-Schmitz et al., 2020), and enduring knowledge gaps (Huang 
et al., 2019; Thurlings et al., 2015). 
We identified eight comprehensive studies on teacher innovation. 
Table 1 encapsulates these reviews, delineating their respective time­
frames and research foci. More than a decade ago, Andiliou and Murphy 
(2010) laid a foundation by conducting the first review, scrutinizing 17 
articles to elucidate teachers’ conceptualizations of creativity. Subse­
quently, Reilly et al. (2011) provided a review of 12 Canadian case 
studies on creative teaching, and elaborated two themes of these studies, 
namely, creative person and community. Following this, Bereczki and 
K´arp´ati (2018) synthesized findings from 53 articles, investigating the 
interplay between teachers’ beliefs about creativity and their practical 
manifestations, alongside the influencing factors therein. Furthermore, 
Mullet et al. (2016) delved into the perceptions of creativity among

to our comprehension of teacher innovation research, addressing diverse

without limitations, as detailed below. 
First, many of these reviews predate 2017 and thus fail to incorporate 
the latest advancements in the field, and analyzed at most 37 articles. 
Given the burgeoning interest in this research issue, it is crucial to 
include recent studies to provide a more up-to-date overview. Second, 
existing reviews tend to focus on specific, albeit fragmented, aspects of 
teacher innovation, such as influencing factors (Pazin Fadzil et al., 2022) 
or particular dimensions like teachers’ perceptions of creativity, creative 
teaching, and beliefs about creativity (Andiliou & Murphy, 2010; 
Bereczki & K´arp´ati, 2018; Pazin Fadzil et al., 2022; Thurlings et al., 
2015; Zainal & Matore, 2019). This fragmented approach impedes a 
cohesive understanding of teacher innovation. Third, important aspects 
crucial for researching teacher innovation, including theoretical foun­
dations, research methodologies, influencing mechanisms, and so on, 
are often overlooked, hampering a holistic understanding of this topic. 
Our research aimed to investigate the definitions, instruments, 
methodologies, and theories used in existing studies of teacher innova­
tion. Additionally, we endeavored to integrate the antecedents, pro­
cesses, and outcomes of teacher innovation into a structured framework, 
thus providing a roadmap for future research endeavors. This review 
may contribute to the literature on teacher innovation in three key ways. 
First, amidst the increasing interest in teacher innovation, there is a lack 
of clarity regarding its definition. Our study sought to address this 
conceptual confusion by elucidating the distinctions between teacher 
innovation and related concepts such as teacher creativity and innova­
tive behavior, and hence to establish conceptual clarity. Second, in line 
with the call for the development of robust measurement instruments for 
teacher innovation (Thurlings et al., 2015), our study recognizes the 
importance of accurately capturing the multifaceted nature of this 
construct. Such measurement tools can help identify innovative teachers 
and design effective professional development interventions, thereby 
fostering a culture of innovation in schools (Kuril et al., 2023). Third, 
while prior research has predominantly focused on the factors influ­
encing teacher innovation and its outcomes (Holdsworth & Maynes, 
2017; Thurlings et al., 2015; Zainal & Matore, 2019), there is limited 
understanding of the mediating mechanisms and boundary conditions 
shaping the relationships. Our study sought to bridge this gap by con­
structing a more comprehensive conceptual framework that elucidates 
the dynamics of teacher innovation (Potoˇcnik & Anderson, 2016). 
2. Materials and methods 
To ensure the rigor and comprehensiveness of our literature review, 
we adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and 
Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, a recognized framework for con­
ducting systematic reviews (Moher et al., 2009). 
2.1. Strategies for the literature search 
The process of searching for and identifying relevant research is 
paramount in ensuring the integrity of a systematic review, as sampling 
bias can significantly impact its credibility. To mitigate this risk and 
maximize inclusivity, we employed a multifaceted approach, integrating 
multiple search methods, including online databases, manual reference 
list screening, and expert consultation. The specific procedure and se­
lection criteria employed in our systematic review are detailed below. 
First, to minimize the likelihood of overlooking pertinent articles, we 
employed a comprehensive set of search terms and their derivatives, 
including “teacher innovation,” “teacher innovative*,” “teacher crea­
tivity,” and “teacher creative*,” to systematically screen titles, abstracts, 
and keywords. We conducted searches across seven databases, namely 
the Web of Science, Education Resources Information Centre, Science 
Direct, Taylor & Francis, Emerald, Sage, and Springer. Second, to 
augment our search efforts, we implemented a snowballing technique, 
wherein we scrutinized the reference lists of the eight existing reviews of 
teacher innovation outlined in Table 1. This approach allowed us to 
identify any potentially overlooked studies and expand the scope of our 
review. Third, to further ensure the comprehensiveness of our literature 
selection, we reached out to two international experts renowned in the 
field of teacher innovation research via email. We provided them with 
our list of identified publications and sought their input to verify the 
inclusion of relevant studies and potentially identify additional sources 
that might have been overlooked. 
By meticulously employing these methodologies, we aimed to 
construct a robust and exhaustive corpus of literature on teacher inno­
vation, thus laying the groundwork for a comprehensive analysis and 
synthesis of existing research in this domain. The search process 
concluded on December 31, 2023. In order to encompass all potentially 
relevant studies, no restriction was imposed on the start time of the 
literature search. Consequently, all included publications spanned the 
period from 1971 to 2023. 
2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria 
This literature review adopted the following inclusion criteria.  
1. Date: Studies published prior to December 31, 2023. 
2. Study Design: All research designs were considered eligible. Empir­
ical studies were incorporated to synthesize empirically validated 
evidence, while conceptual studies were included to capture schol­
arly insights. 
3. Document Type: Only articles published in peer-reviewed interna­
tional journals were deemed eligible.  
4. Language: Publications written in English were exclusively included.

2.3. Selection process for including articles 
Through the initial literature search across seven electronic data­
bases, we retrieved 1354 results. Following the removal of duplicates, 
863 unique hits remained. Subsequently, we conducted an eligibility 
assessment based on the predefined exclusion criteria by reviewing the 
article titles and abstracts. This initial screening, independently con­
ducted by the third author, resulted in the exclusion of 661 articles that 
did not meet the outlined criteria: (1) not pertaining to teacher inno­
vation, (2) not published in a peer-reviewed international journal; (3) 
not in English; or (4) involving participants other than in-service 
teachers in primary, secondary, or vocational schools. 
Consequently, 202 studies were identified for full text analysis. 
During this phase, two members of our research team (the first and third 
authors) independently evaluated the full texts. Any discrepancies were 
resolved through consultation with the second author, engaging in dis­
cussions until consensus was reached. Ultimately, 101 articles were 
excluded due to the following reasons: (1) teacher innovation was not 
treated as the primary focused concept or was referred to in a marginal 
way in the full-text version of the article; (2) participants were pre- 
service teachers or tertiary teachers. This step of screening resulted in 
a set of 101 relevant articles. 
In addition, we added 18 articles through the snowballing search 
method based on the reference lists of the eight existing teacher inno­
vation reviews and expert consultation. This comprehensive screening 
process resulted in a corpus of 119 publications for further assessment of 
research quality. 
Table 2 illustrates the research quality assessment, conducted ac­
cording to 11 assessment criteria across five dimensions, as proposed by 
Petticrew and Roberts (2006). Each study was evaluated on a 3-point 
scale (i.e., 1, 0.5, or 0). Articles scoring a minimum total of 5.5 were 
deemed eligible for inclusion. To ensure transparency and reliability, the 
first and third authors independently rated each study, and the average 
of their scores was considered. Consequently, 10 articles were excluded 
for failing to meet the quality assessment criteria, resulting in a final 
selection of 109 articles for the systematic review. The entire process of 
selecting relevant publications for this study is presented in Fig. 1. 
2.4. Information retrieval and analysis process 
Initially, we established an Excel table to systematically code the 
selected articles, facilitating the organization of our findings in a 
consistent manner. To address the three research questions, we extrac­
ted pertinent data from each publication included in the review. These 
data encompassed various aspects, including author(s), publication 
year, research questions, terminology along with corresponding defini­
tions, theoretical underpinnings (if applicable), demographic details of 
the sample, methods of data collection and analysis, presence of a 
research model, utilization of measurement instruments, and key find­
ings. The third author carefully read through eligible studies and 
extracted relevant data utilizing a predefined checklist. Subsequently, 
the first author oversaw the eligibility-checking process and imple­
mented any necessary adjustments. Regular online meetings of the 
entire research team were conducted to deliberate upon discrepancies 
and uncertainties arising during the data extraction process. These 
collaborative discussions ensured consensus and rigor in the extraction 
and interpretation of data from the selected articles. 
3. Results 
Fig. 2 presents an overview of the volume of research on teacher 
innovation published annually. Historically, the exploration of teacher 
innovation received limited scholar attention, evidenced by only 36 
papers (approximately 33%) published between 1971 and 2017. How­
ever, a notable increase occurred in 2018, with 73 papers (approxi­
mately 67%) published on this topic between 2018 and 2023. This trend 
underscores the relatively nascent status of teacher innovation research, 
highlighting a substantial increase in publications over the last 5 years. 
3.1. How is teacher innovation conceptualized? 
Clear conceptual definitions play a vital role in fostering the devel­
opment of robust theory (Podsakoff et al., 2016). When definitions of 
teacher innovation lack necessary precision, this will engender disparate 
conceptualizations with overlapping domains and insufficient oper­
ationalization, and will hinder the comparability of the research out­
comes. Thus, it has become imperative to establish a coherent definition. 
Despite the burgeoning research on teacher innovation in recent years, 
the absence of a consensus among researchers regarding its definition 
has impeded operationalization, thwarted cumulative theoretical 
advancement, and made it difficult to differentiate teacher innovation 
from related constructs such as teacher creativity, teacher innovative 
work behavior, and teacher entrepreneurial behavior. Indeed, terms 
associated with teacher innovation are often employed interchangeably, 
resulting in ambiguity in the definition (Scott & Bruce, 1994; Thurlings 
et al., 2015). Hence, a detailed examination of various constructs related 
to teacher innovation helps avoid conceptual ambiguities and bolsters 
the discriminant validity of the construct. The subsequent sections will 
illustrate the similarities and distinctions among these constructs (see

Teacher creativity serves as a fundamental underpinning and catalyst 
for innovative work behavior, and both constructs exhibit some degree of 
overlap (Potoˇcnik & Anderson, 2018). While teacher innovative work 
behavior emphasizes the stage of idea promotion and implementation, 
teacher creativity encompasses the cognitive and behavioral process, 
often focusing on the initial generation of wholly novel ideas (Hughes 
et al., 2018; Lu, 2019; Potoˇcnik & Anderson, 2018; West & Farr, 1989). 
Notably, teacher creativity does not necessarily translate into teacher 
innovation outcomes. 
Teacher innovative work behavior is rooted in the theory of innovation 
process, encompassing behaviors across all stages leading to innovation. 
Driven by the pursuit of social value creation and a learning orientation 
(Chand, 2014), teacher innovative work behavior involves the intentional 
generation and promotion of relatively novel ideas (adopted or adapted 
from others) to enhance work performance or teaching methods

Teacher entrepreneurial behavior entails managerial competencies 
such as coordinating efforts, resource acquisition, advocacy for inno­
vation, and personal attributes like risk-taking propensity, humility- 
confidence balance, as well as a sense of ownership and enthusiasm. 
These attributes enable teachers to identify innovative opportunities, 
reflect on them, and scale them up within schools, prioritizing social 
values (Chand, 2014; Liu & Hallinger, 2021). While entrepreneurial 
teachers may not always be highly inventive themselves, they play 
pivotal roles as adopters and facilitators of novel initiatives (Chand 
et al., 2023), suggesting a convergence between teacher entrepreneurial 
behavior and teacher innovative work behavior, both integral to fostering 
teacher innovation (Chand, 2014; Lukes & Stephan, 2017). 
In the corpus of papers analyzed, excluding eight review articles, the 
examination revealed that 77 papers (76.3%) provided definitions of 
teacher innovation. It is noteworthy that 24 articles (23.7%) did not 
provide a specific definition of teacher innovation. The most commonly 
adopted definition among researchers characterizes teacher innovation 
as a “process” (n = 50, 49.5%), involving the generation and imple­
mentation of new ideas crucial for school improvement (Anderson et al., 
2014). Although this definition clarifies the dynamic process of teacher 
innovation, it closely resembles the construct of teacher innovative work 
behavior. Teacher innovation, however, is a broader concept than teacher 
innovative work behavior and is characterized by its result-dependent 
nature (Chand et al., 2021). Some scholars defined teacher innovation 
as a “capability” (n = 9, 8.9%) to collaborate within a complex social 
system to produce novel and useful ideas (Eva et al., 2020). This defi­
nition predominantly draws from the perspective of organizational 
creativity (Lee & Choi, 2003), without adequately distinguishing be­
tween innovation and creativity. Chand (2014) defined teacher inno­
vation as a novel and/or unique response to a problem or need, 
involving a stage of collective generation or adaptation of novel ideas 
from external resources, followed by communication, implementation, 
evaluation, modification, dissemination, continuation of novel and 
useful ideas, and ultimately the achievement of improvement in 
educational goals (Chand et al., 2023; Lu, 2019). Another approach 
seeks to address the unique professional challenges and demands 
teachers encounter in school settings, including interactions with stu­
dents and parents, collaboration with colleagues, and cooperation with 
principals. For example, Cai and Tang (2022) defined teacher innova­
tion as teachers exploring and adopting new and effective teaching 
strategies to cultivate a student-centered, creativity-nurturing learning 
environment. However, questions regarding what qualifies as an inno­
vative teaching strategy or the distinctions between innovative and 
non-innovative teaching strategies remain largely unanswered. 
Though the definitions presented above vary, they seem to point to 
four features widely applicable to teacher innovation. First, the defini­
tions underscore the importance of interpersonal social processes within 
the workplace for generating and implementing novel ideas to solve 
context-relevant problems (Chand et al., 2023; West & Farr, 1989), 
which has also been acknowledged in broader innovation studies (West 
& Farr, 1989). Second, teacher innovation does not necessitate the 
creation of entirely new inventions or discoveries by teachers to address 
a problem. Instead, it involves the competences (e.g., divergent thinking 
ability, imaginative ability, and the ability to take risks) to adapt useful 
strategies from external sources (Anderson et al., 2022; Erdem & Cag 
Adiguzel, 2019; So & Hu, 2019; West & Farr, 1989). Third, teacher 
innovation focuses not only on introducing an innovation but also on 
subsequent phases such as experimentation, implementation, modifi­
cation, and scaling-up. Fourth, teacher innovation, as a goal-directed 
effort, is contingent upon identifiable improvement; whether some­
thing is labeled as an innovation depends on its impact (Lukes & Ste­
phan, 2017). 
3.2. Measurements of teacher innovation 
We identified two approaches to measuring teacher innovation, 
namely the latent construct approach and the task-driven approach. The 
widely used latent construct approach adopts an “indirect” method by 
utilizing characteristics indicative of teacher innovative work behavior 
for measurement purposes. Researchers usually employ scales relying on 
self-reports from teachers or other-rated evaluations from their super­
visors to gauge the degree to which teachers are innovative. In contrast, 
the task-driven approach entails peers’ and experts’ evaluations of 
teachers’ actual work, and then categorizes teachers into innovative and 
non-innovative subsets. 
Our corpus found 16 articles that used scales to measure teacher 
innovative work behavior (De Jong & Den Hartog, 2010; Gkontelos 
et al., 2022; Messmann & Mulder, 2014) (see Table 3). We evaluated 
each of these scales according to their theoretical and methodological 
rigor in the development and validation stages. To achieve this, we 
traced the development and validation processes of each measure, 
subsequently examining the psychometric qualities against the scale 
development guidelines. 
Hinkin et al. (1997) outlined seven steps for scale development and 
validation: item generation, content adequacy assessment, question­
naire administration, factor analysis, internal consistency assessment, 
construct validity, and replication. A meticulous approach to item 
generation (Step 1) and content adequacy assessment (Step 2) ensures 
that the measurement items have strong and clear linkages with the 
theoretical domains of teacher innovation. However, five studies did not 
report the process of item generation (Nemerˇzitski et al., 2013; Nguyen 
et al., 2021; Runhaar et al., 2016; Schacter et al., 2006; Suyudi et al., 
2022). Some researchers adopted a deductive approach to item gener­
ation based on the theoretical assumptions or literature review (Hurt 
et al., 1977; Scott & Bruce, 1994). Only two scales considered the 
interview texts when developing scale items (Messmann & Mulder, 
2014; Welbourne et al., 1998). Furthermore, there was a prevalent lack 
of content adequacy assessment, with only one-third (n = 6) of the 
studies inviting experts to agree on the scale’s content (Gkontelos et al., 
2022; Kuril et al., 2023; Messmann & Mulder, 2014; Suyudi et al., 2022; 
Welbourne et al., 1998; Zainal & Matore, 2019). 
Regarding questionnaire administration (Step 3), all scales were 
distributed and tested. Notably, seven studies conducted factor analysis 
(Step 4) (De Jong & Den Hartog, 2010; Gkontelos et al., 2022; Kuril 
et al., 2023; Messmann & Mulder, 2014; Runhaar et al., 2016; Suyudi 
et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2013), but only two performed both exploratory 
factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) (De Jong & 
Den Hartog, 2010; Gkontelos et al., 2022)). While most studies (11 of 16 
measures) assessed the internal consistency (Step 5) of the scales using 
Cronbach’s α or McDonald’s ω (Hurt et al., 1977; Scott & Bruce, 1994), 
only three tested the construct validity (Step 6) of the scales, including 
discriminant validity, convergent validity, or criterion-related validity 
(Kuril et al., 2023; Messmann & Mulder, 2014; Scott & Bruce, 1994). 
Replication (Step 7) is essential for enhancing the confidence in the 
reliability and validity of psychometrically valid instruments for future 
research. Among the scales, 11 were cited at least twice, contributing to 
the robustness of their reliability and validity (i.e., De Jong & Den 
Hartog, 2010; Hurt et al., 1977; Janssen, 2000; Scott & Bruce, 1994). 
A rigorous measure must adhere to stringent criteria during both 
scale development and validation processes. Our analyses led us to 
recommend four measures of teacher innovative work behavior that 
have undergone thorough development and validation processes (De 
Jong & Den Hartog, 2010; Kuril et al., 2023; Lambriex-Schmitz et al., 
2020; Messmann & Mulder, 2014). This indicates that many scales lack 
adequate validation through repeated applications to various contexts.

efficient questionnaire evaluating teacher innovative work behavior

view teacher innovative work behavior as multidimensional. For 
instance, Messmann and Mulder (2014) devised a 20-item scale that 
measures teacher innovative work behavior as a second-order factor 
comprising four first-order factors: exploration, generation, promotion, 
and reflection of ideas. Distinguishing itself from other scales, Mess­
mann and Mulder (2014) instrument incorporates “reflection,” denoting 
the process of reflecting on work experiences and evaluating progress 
during an innovation. Lambriex-Schmitz et al. (2020) constructed and 
validated a 44-item teacher innovative work behavior scale (IWB-44), 
featuring five dimensions: opportunity exploration, idea generation, 
idea promotion, idea realization (comprising criterion-based imple­
mentation and learning-based communication), and idea sustainability 
(encompassing external dissemination and internal embedding). 
Notably, the inclusion of the idea sustainability dimension is pivotal, as 
it emphasizes the long-term success of innovations, addressing both 
internal embedding and external dissemination of innovative ideas. The 
dimension holds significance given the transient nature of many in­
novations, which often fail to exert a lasting impact on school effec­
tiveness. Rasch analyses and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) 
demonstrated the IWB-44’s strong construct validity (Lambriex-Schmitz 
et al., 2020). Kuril et al. (2023) extended the IWB-20 scale originally 
developed by Lukes and Stephan (2017) and tailored it to educational 
contexts. They adapted a six-dimensional teacher innovative work 
behavior inventory that significantly contributes to increasing research 
outputs on teacher innovation. The IWB-20 scale, with the dimensions of 
idea generation, idea search, idea communication, implementation ac­
tivities, involvement of others, and overcoming obstacles, offers a 
fundamental assessment of teacher innovative work behavior. 
Our review shows considerable variations in the measures employed 
in research on teacher innovative work behavior. We contend that the 
IWB-20 scale stands out as a concise, comprehensive, and rigorously 
designed instrument for three reasons. First, it covers the diverse facets 
constituting teacher innovative work behavior. This approach is vital for 
comprehensively capturing teacher innovative work behavior and 
discerning which dimensions are more effective (Lukes & Stephan, 
2017). Second, it integrates teacher entrepreneurial behavior into its 
measurement, emphasizing that for meaningful changes to occur, 
teachers must exhibit high levels of ownership, enthusiasm in 
resource-seeking, risk-taking, effective communication of vision, and 
the ability to overcome obstacles (Liu & Hallinger, 2021). These quali­
ties are pivotal for the success of teacher innovative work behavior. 
Third, it was developed within educational contexts, involving large 
research samples and undergoing cross-cultural validations (Kuril et al., 
2023; Lukes & Stephan, 2017). 
While self-report questionnaires have been widely used to measure 
teacher innovative work behavior, they often face criticisms due to 
susceptibility to biases such as social desirability and false memories. 
Therefore, Chand (2012) introduced a task-based assessment of teacher 
innovation, relying on peer and expert ratings of teachers’ actual work. 
The consensual technique was employed to assign innovation scores 
according to criteria such as novelty and uniqueness, context and 
impact, scope of the activity, complexity, and the spread effect of the 
work. This approach proves powerful in measuring teacher innovation 
by concurrently considering both innovative processes and outcome 
data.

3.3. Methodologies and theories adopted by the studies on teacher 
innovation 
We categorized the included articles into three categories: empirical 
studies (n = 96, 88.1%), review articles (n = 8, 7.3%), and theoretical 
papers (n = 5, 4.6%). Empirical studies were broadly defined as those 
studies in which data were collected, analyzed, and reported (Hallinger 
& Bridges, 2017). They were further delineated into quantitative (n =
69, 63.3%), qualitative (n = 22, 20.2%), or mixed-methods studies (n =
5, 4.6%). 
3.3.1. Reported data collection methods 
We initially examined whether the 69 quantitative studies employed 
a cross-sectional design or a longitudinal design. As depicted in Table 5, 
the results indicated that the majority of the studies (n = 66, 95.6%) 
were cross-sectional, while only three (4.4%) adopted a longitudinal 
design involving two or more rounds of data collection. The overreliance 
on cross-sectional designs limits the capacity of existing teacher inno­
vation research to establish causal relationships. 
Quantitative studies employed various data sources, with 64 studies 
(92.7%) relying solely on teachers’ self-reports, while only five (7.3%) 
collected data from participants other than teachers (e.g., researchers, 
students, and school leaders). The overreliance on teachers’ self-report 
data may increase the likelihood of common method bias. 
Among the 22 qualitative studies, 12 (54.5%) employed a single data 
collection method (e.g., journal entries or interviews only), while 10 
(45.5%) utilized a combination of methods (e.g., interviews and docu­
ments). Thus, researchers in teacher innovation are increasingly recog­
nizing the importance of employing multiple data sources to enhance 
the credibility of their research findings. 
Four out of the five mixed-methods studies (80%) utilized interviews 
to collect qualitative data and self-reported surveys to collect quantita­
tive data, while the remaining study employed other means to collect 
qualitative data (i.e., open-ended questions). 
3.3.2. Reported data analysis methods of quantitative research 
Bridges (1982) introduced a classification scheme comprising four 
levels of statistical approaches to represent data analysis techniques. 
Based on the sophisticated levels of statistical methods proposed by 
Bridges (1982), we categorized quantitative studies on teacher innova­
tion into the following levels.  
1. Level 1 – Descriptive and factor analysis: This level is utilized to 
describe the central tendencies, difference between treatments, and 
variable structures within a specific setting.  
2. Level 2 – Correlational study: It involves describing the association 
between two variables.  
3. Level 3 – Single/multiple causal factor(s): This level explores the 
varying effects of multiple sources of influences on a particular 
variable, with or without controls.  
4. Level 4 – Advanced modeling: Advanced modeling encompasses 
research aimed at exploring mediating and moderating effects or 
utilizing multilevel models. 
Our findings indicated that advanced modeling at Level 4 (e.g., 
multilevel modeling, hierarchical regression, mediating, moderating 
analysis) was predominantly utilized in the majority (n = 43, 62.3%) of 
the 69 quantitative studies. This level of data analysis enables the 
exploration of relationships among multiple dependent and independent 
variables, facilitating the detection of theoretical mechanisms or 
boundary conditions related to teacher innovation. Statistics related to 
single/multiple causal factor(s) at Level 3 accounted for 21.7% (n = 15) 
of the quantitative research. In contrast, only a small number of studies 
primarily employed descriptive and factor analysis at Level 1 (n = 7, 
10.2%) or correlational analysis at Level 2 (n = 4, 5.8%) (see Table 5). 
3.3.3. Reported samples 
This section presents an analysis of the composition and size of the 
research samples in the 96 empirical studies. Regarding sample 
composition, we distinguished the following categories: studies 
involving only primary school teachers (n = 14, 14.6%), those including 
only secondary school teachers (n = 28, 29.2%), those with solely 
vocational school teachers (n = 8, 8.3%), and studies with a mixed 
sample (e.g., including primary, secondary, and/or vocational school 
teachers) (n = 46, 47.9%). In other words, in the 46 studies with a mixed 
sample, researchers did not clearly differentiate between teachers at 
different school levels. 
Regarding sample size, three studies did not report related infor­
mation (Jeffrey & Troman, 2013; Selkrig & Keamy, 2017), and thus were 
excluded from the subsequent descriptive statistics. Six quantitative 
studies utilized data from the Teaching and Learning International 
Survey (TALIS) with large samples, which may potentially skew the 
results. Therefore, these six studies were treated separately from others 
that collected their own data. Consequently, the final sample consisted 
of 87 articles (62 quantitative, 20 qualitative, 5 mixed-methods). To 
address the significant variations in sample sizes, both the arithmetic 
mean and the median are reported. 
As depicted in Table 6, the 87 studies employing primary data (i.e., 
collecting their own data) included an average of 904 cases, totaling 
78,667 cases. Quantitative studies with primary data resulted in a total 
dataset of 73,006 cases (with an average of 1177). More than half (n =
33) of the 62 quantitative studies included 400 or fewer cases (median 
= 239). Seventeen studies included 400-1000 cases (median = 507). The 
remaining 12 studies had a sample size exceeding 1000 (median =
1718). The six studies utilizing secondary data (i.e., using TALIS data) 
had an average sample size of 109,779. Additionally, the 20 qualitative 
studies had a total dataset of 442 cases (with an average of 22), while the 
five mixed-method studies had a total of 5219 cases (with an average of 
1043). 
It important to note that despite the large average sample size, there 
was considerable variation in sample size among the studies, particu­
larly in quantitative studies, which may impact the generalizability of 
the findings.

3.3.4. Theories used to inform teacher innovation research 
Theory serves as a critical framework for providing logical justifi­
cations to describe, explain, and predict various phenomena (Whetten, 
1989). However, out of the 109 studies reviewed, a significant majority, 
92.6% (n = 101), demonstrated inadequate attention to existing theories 
that could inform their research. Only a small proportion, 7.3% (n = 8), 
explicitly integrated theories from psychology and communication (e.g., 
self-determination theory, social cognitive theory, social constructivist 
framework, cultural-historical activity theory, and innovation diffusion 
theory) to conceptualize the research topics, guide data collection and 
analysis, and discuss their findings. 
Among these studies, seven utilized theories to offer causal expla­
nations for the factors influencing teacher innovation. For instance, self- 
determination theory was frequently employed to explore the motiva­
tional processes underlying teachers’ development of innovative 
teaching practices in their professional contexts (Fix et al., 2021; 
Klaeijsen et al., 2018). Social cognitive theory was utilized to elucidate 
how environmental support predicted teachers’ information literacy, 
technostress, and innovative behaviors (Wu et al., 2022). Another one 
utilized cultural-historical activity theory to analyze the interactions 
among various stakeholders involved in educational innovation to 
reveal how and what teachers learn from innovating their own educa­
tional practices (Stam et al., 2014). 
3.4. The antecedents, processes, and outcomes of teacher innovation 
We synthesized the findings from the 96 empirical articles into a 
research matrix and proposed a comprehensive framework to integrate 
previous insights into teacher innovation. These matrices served to 
organize our findings into a coherent whole, while the multi- 
dimensional framework condenses them into an overarching structure, 
providing scholars with a conceptual tool to analyze teacher innovation. 
The matrices were composed of the three aforementioned categories: 
antecedents, mechanisms, and outcomes. To enhance clarity in report­
ing these findings, we present them in Figs. 4 and 5, respectively. Fig. 4 
delineates various types of antecedents of teacher innovation, while 
Fig. 5 delineates the outcomes of teacher innovation along with the 
mediating or moderating mechanisms. In the subsequent sections, we 
elaborate on the details of each category, including its sub-categories 
and key themes. 
3.4.1. Antecedents of teacher innovation 
Given the significance of teacher innovation, its antecedents have 
emerged as one of the most extensively researched areas in the litera­
ture, garnering substantial attention from researchers. We categorize the 
antecedents of teacher innovation into three main types: teacher char­
acteristics and behaviors, school characteristics, and impeding factors. 
Teacher characteristics and behaviors encompass individual factors

Various demographic variables have been identified to be relevant to 
teacher innovation. Research indicates a positive correlation between 
teaching experience and age with teacher innovation (Koziko˘glu & 
Küçük, 2020; Lambriex-Schmitz et al., 2020; Loogma et al., 2012). 
Experienced teachers tend to possess more integrated knowledge 
structures and a better understanding of their students’ learning needs, 
facilitating creative problem-solving (Lambriex-Schmitz et al., 2020b). 
Teacher innovation often aligns with specific personality traits. For 
instance, teachers exhibiting higher tendencies for lifelong learning 
(Eks¸i et al., 2021), a survey thinking style (Sadeghi Boroujerdi & Hasani, 
2014), a proactive personality (Li et al., 2017), multicultural experience 
(Wei et al., 2022), or a sense of humor (Johari et al., 2021) are more 
prone to demonstrate greater levels of innovation. 
Educational, technological, and social competencies are deemed 
essential factors fostering teacher innovation. Scholars have argued that 
new educational beliefs, subject knowledge, teaching strategies, and 
cognitive activation are pivotal for innovative teaching (Loogma et al., 
2012; Zhu et al., 2013). Teachers integrating ICT into their pedagogy 
can create additional opportunities for innovative teaching approaches 
(Loogma et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2013). Furthermore, 
teachers’ social competencies in fostering constructive interactions with 
colleagues and students enable them to share insights and actively 
participate in professional learning communities, thereby further moti­
vating teacher innovation (Zhu et al., 2013). 
Similarly, teachers exhibiting positive work behaviors, such as high 
work involvement (Nemerˇzitski et al., 2013), reflective practices 
(Messmann & Mulder, 2015), teacher leadership (Nguyen et al., 2022), 
and engagement in professional and informal learning (Nguyen et al., 
2021), are more inclined to explore novel teaching approaches and 
identify areas for enhancing their teaching practices. For instance, by 
transcending conventional educational practice, surmounting resources 
and facility barriers, engaging in negotiations with various stakeholders 
to gain legitimacy for teaching innovations, and collaborating with 
parents, teachers demonstrating leadership qualities exhibit higher 
levels of pedagogical innovation (Nguyen et al., 2022). 
Some psychological characteristics, such as satisfaction of 
psychological needs (Gorozidis & Papaioannou, 2014; Klaeijsen et al., 
2018; Messmann et al., 2022) and achievement motivation (Noviyanti 
et al., 2021), serve as significant drivers of teacher innovation. When 
teachers perceive support for and recognition of their work, they 
become more open to new ideas and are willing to take risks when 
implementing innovative teaching strategies (Messmann & Mulder, 
2014). Additionally, teachers’ perceptions of the value and worthiness 
of innovation directly influence their willingness to adopt innovative 
teaching approaches (Morris, 1985). 
School organizational determinants play a crucial role in fostering 
teacher innovation. Studies in this category indicate that teacher inno­
vation is not an isolated phenomenon but is deeply influenced by the 
organizational environment of the school. When teachers perceive that 
they are supported, respected, and trusted by various stakeholders such 
as principals, students, and colleagues, they are more inclined to embark 
on innovation to enhance their teaching practice. 
Among the different leadership styles, research has underscored the 
significance of transformational leadership, empowering leadership, 
and distributed leadership in catalyzing teacher innovation. Trans­
formational leadership, through fostering an innovative school culture 
and promoting intellectual development, encourages teachers to think 
creatively and take bold risks for change, thereby directly facilitating 
teacher innovation (Buske, 2018; Zainal & Mohd Matore, 2021). 
Empowering principals foster teacher innovation by offering greater 
autonomy, involving teachers in decision-making processes, and 
demonstrating confidence in their capabilities (Gkorezis, 2016; Zhu 
et al., 2019). Distributed leadership, characterized by collaborative 
interaction among principals, teachers, and the school context, em­
powers teachers’ greater work autonomy, thereby motivating them to 
embrace innovative teaching practices (Buyukgoze et al., 2022; Lin, 
2022). Additionally, principals’ technology leadership provides oppor­
tunities for teachers to enhance the technology integration and inno­
vation in their teaching (Mert, 2021). In addition to principals, school 
middle leaders’ values also influence teachers’ engagement in creativity 
(Da’as, 2023). 
Professional supports from peers and students also play a significant 
role in shaping teacher innovation. Studies have explored the correla­
tion between teachers’ interaction with colleagues and teacher inno­
vation. For instance, Liu et al. (2022) found that the establishment of 
professional learning communities in schools facilitated teachers’

Factors impeding teacher innovation encompass both contextual factors 
(i.e., institutional contexts, educational policies, and national curricu­

to how teachers formulate a rationale for developing innovative

creative anxiety, and fixed mindsets toward uncertainty (Anderson 
et al., 2022; Morris, 1985) tend to deter teachers’ receptivity and 
inclination toward innovation. 
3.4.2. Outcomes and influencing mechanisms of teacher innovation 
Our investigation indicates that outcomes such as students’ intrinsic 
goal orientation, learning performance, and teachers’ teaching practice 
(i.e., technology acceptance, responsible teaching) are commonly 
examined in the literature on teacher innovation. As illustrated in Fig. 4, 
the findings suggest that teachers who employ creative teaching 
methods to foster cross-curricular skills may lead to students’ intrinsic 
goal orientation (Maun et al., 2023) and learning satisfaction (Suyudi 
et al., 2022), which has been linked to their achievement gains (Sali & 
Akyol, 2015; Schacter et al., 2006). The second category of outcome 
pertains to teachers’ teaching practice. Orakcı et al. (2020) explored the 
direct relationship between teacher innovation and responsible teach­
ing. Akar (2019) discovered that the connection between teachers’ 
innovativeness and behavioral intention to use technology was medi­
ated by their perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and subjective 
norms. Sodergren et al. (2023), on the other hand, found that team-level 
innovations by teachers can enhance their self-efficacy for teaching, 
classroom management, and student engagement. 
Fig. 4 also depicts the mechanisms and boundary conditions through 
which teacher innovation influences its outcomes. To delve deeper into 
how school conditions impact teacher innovation, a promising avenue of 
inquiry has been launched to unpack this black box. Drawing on the 
social cognitive theory (Rubenstein et al., 2018) and self-determination 
theory (Vermeulen et al., 2022), teachers’ efficacy (Cai & Tang, 2021; 
Klaeijsen et al., 2018; Liu & Hallinger, 2021; Runhaar et al., 2016), 
psychological empowerment (Zhu et al., 2019), autonomy (Lin, 2022; 
Nemerˇzitski et al., 2013), psychological need satisfaction (Cai & Tang, 
2022; Messmann et al., 2022), commitment (Kılınç et al., 2024), job 
satisfaction (Buyukgoze et al., 2022) and intrinsic motivation (Klaeijsen 
et al., 2018) serve as mediators in the relationship between school 
conditions and teacher innovation. Some researchers have also explored 
the mediating role of interpersonal interactions, such as professional 
collaboration (Buyukgoze et al., 2022; Lin, 2022), knowledge sharing 
(Xi & Li, 2023), employee empowerment (Cobanoglu, 2021), and 
leader-member exchange (Vermeulen et al., 2022). Other organizational 
or team-level mediators include leader and organizational ambidex­
terity (Dedering & Pietsch, 2023), team diversity and knowledge 
self-efficacy (Da’as, 2023). 
Furthermore, scholars have suggested that psychological safety (Zhu 
et al., 2019), perceived organizational support (Imamoglu et al., 2023), 
innovative climate (Liu et al., 2022), teacher work stress (Li & Zhu, 
2022), and teacher trust (Cai & Tang, 2021) act as the moderators or 
critical boundary conditions for promoting teacher innovation. 
4. A research agenda for future research on teacher innovation 
4.1. Advancing conceptualization clarity of teacher innovation 
Advancing the conceptual clarity of teacher innovation is the 
fundamental step in any research endeavor. In line with previous 
research, we have synthesized previous conceptualizations of teacher 
innovation. Our analysis unveiled the utilization of various terms (i.e., 
teacher creativity, teacher innovative work behavior, and teacher entrepre­
neurial behavior) to signify teacher innovation. These terms were often 
poorly defined and interchangeably used in previous studies. This lack of 
conceptual clarity poses challenges in assessing whether these terms can 
capture the essence of teacher innovation, thereby hindering re­
searchers’ ability to draw cohesive conclusions from existing literature. 
To mitigate this limitation, our review advocates for a more rigorous, 
consistent use of terminology to establish the conceptual clarity of 
teacher innovation. 
Second, while the definition proposed by West and Farr (1989) for 
general work innovation has been widely adopted in teacher innovation 
research, it falls short in terms of fully capturing the characteristics of 
teacher innovation. The process perspective of teacher innovation 
typically implies a linear progression from one stage to the next. How­
ever, Chand et al. (2023) recently suggested a more nuanced concep­
tualization. Specifically, they proposed that teacher innovation involves 
a creative re-combination of resources to establish new means-ends re­
lationships and the goal-oriented conversion of ideas into practice, while 
overcoming obstacles. This definition can be contextualized within the 
Input-Mediator-Output-Input (IMOI) model (Ilgen et al., 2005). First, 
definitions of teacher innovation encompass the concept as an input, 
acknowledging that both idea generation and entrepreneurial behaviors 
are vital skills and resources for teacher innovation (Liu & Hallinger, 
2021). Second, teacher innovation pertains to the dynamic interaction 
among educators that transforms inputs into outcomes. Instead of 
following a singular linear path from one stage to another, teacher 
innovation manifests as a nonlinear process involving the development 
of novel means to recombine resources and establish new relationships 
(Chand et al., 2023). Third, a broad spectrum of outputs stemming from 
teacher innovation is anticipated (Chand et al., 2021), including 
increased satisfaction, enhanced performance, heightened commitment, 
improved interpersonal communication, and the sustainable develop­
ment of professional learning communities (West & Farr, 1989). Finally, 
teacher innovation is not an isolated event or one-off implantation of 
new ideas, but rather a continuous process of adaptation and enhance­
ment. The IMOI model emphasizes the feedback loop of teacher inno­
vation where some outputs of teacher innovation serve as inputs for 
future innovations. 
This model enhances our understanding of the complex and dynamic 
nature of teacher innovation, yet contextual factors have not received 
enough attention. Organizational aspects (e.g., private or public, size, 
structure, power distribution, task interdependence) significantly

influence how teacher innovation is conceived, nurtured, disseminated, 
and sustained. Future research on teacher innovation should pay 
attention to the role of contextual factors in its development and 
enactment. We endorse the utilization of more inductive, qualitative 
techniques (Hinkin et al., 1997), including case studies, interviews, 
dialog, diaries, and direct observation of principals or teachers, to 
elucidate the intricacies of teacher innovation and to provide a more 
detailed understanding of the dynamic processes (Podsakoff et al., 
2016). 
4.2. Improving the measurement rigor of teacher innovation 
In this review, it was found that researchers have introduced 15 
scales for measuring teacher innovative work behavior (De Jong & Den 
Hartog, 2010; Gkontelos et al., 2022; Messmann & Mulder, 2014), 
significantly contributing to the growing body of research on teacher 
innovation in recent years. However, existing measurements encounter 
several challenges. 
First, measuring teacher innovation presents significant challenges 
as many reviewed studies primarily focused on assessing teacher inno­
vative work behavior without adequately considering the broader 
concept of teacher innovation. Teacher innovative work behavior en­
compasses various stages, from generating novel ideas to implementa­
tion, dissemination, and ultimately innovation. However, teacher 
innovation also entails various shifts in educational objectives and 
diverse facets of the innovative process, making it a complex construct to 
measure. These challenges stem from several factors. First, the definition 
of teacher innovation is often ambiguous or overly broad, lacking pre­
cision, thereby impeding measurement and hindering further inquiry. 
Second, while many teachers engage in developing innovative ideas, the 
stages of implementation and dissemination of innovation may signifi­
cantly depend on the supportive climate of educational organizations. 
Lastly, the limitation of data availability often prompts scholars to 
narrowly focus on teacher innovative work behavior. Given the result- 
dependent nature of teacher innovation, establishing shared criteria 
for assessing teacher innovation across different contexts proves 
challenging. 
Second, about two thirds of the scales did not adhere to or report the 
process of scale development. Although researchers have examined the 
inter-item consistency of most scales through reliability analysis and 
inter-factor relationships through correlation analysis, they still lacked 
evidence of construct validity. This deficiency indicates the questionable 
psychometric qualities of these scales. Therefore, future studies should 
rigorously follow and report the procedures of scale development and 
validation when developing any measurement of teacher innovation. 
Third, measures of teacher innovation have mainly used surveys of 
the responses from either teachers themselves or their colleagues, which 
may lead to potential problems such as informant bias and over-reliance 
on self-report data. Colleagues may underestimate or overrate teachers’ 
actual extent of innovation depending on their teaching performance, 
while self-report surveys are susceptible to social desirability bias. To 
address these issues, we propose to incorporate multiple data sources to 
enhance the validity of assessment, including teachers, colleagues, stu­
dents, and parents in the data collection to measure teacher innovation. 
Additionally, given that assessment is a process of reasoning from evi­
dence to evaluate specific capabilities (Vincent-Lancrin et al., 2019), we 
encourage using objective data from relevant sources to measure the 
actual extent of teacher innovation in order to increase the objectivity of 
teacher innovation measurements. In fact, some scholars have con­
ducted relevant research to improve the measurement rigor of teacher 
innovation (Schacter et al., 2006). 
4.3. Upgrading methodologies for teacher innovation research 
In our review, we identified 96 empirical studies in our corpus, 
comprising 69 quantitative, 22 qualitative, and five mixed-method 
studies, indicating a predominant preference for quantitative research 
methods in the existing literature on teacher innovation. Further ex­
amination of the 69 quantitative studies revealed that a significant 
majority employed advanced statistical methods in the data analysis, 
with Level 3 and Level 4 statistical analyses representing 84.0% of these 
quantitative studies. However, it was evident that these quantitative 
studies heavily relied on single-source, one-time, self-reported survey 
designs, which are insufficient for capturing the dynamic nature of 
teacher innovation. Notably, we found a lack of longitudinal studies 
employing three or more time points to assess changes in teacher 
innovation over time. This scarcity of longitudinal studies presents 
challenges for establishing causality, thereby limiting the capacity of 
existing studies to make meaningful contributions to knowledge build­
ing (Thurlings et al., 2015). Hence, researchers of teacher innovation are 
strongly encouraged to employ a wider array of research designs, 
including controlled experiments, to obtain accurate causal inferences. 
Considering the inherent strengths and weaknesses of each research 
method, there is a compelling need to advocate for more applications of 
qualitative and mixed-methods designs. These approaches can provide 
in-depth information on nurturing and disseminating teacher innova­
tion. Given the complexities and uncertainties associated with teacher 
innovation, often entailing stress and risk, qualitative or mixed-method 
approaches are instrumental in exploring the multifaceted dynamics of 
the innovative process. They frequently yield valuable insights into how 
teachers navigate obstacles and constraints to foster, sustain, and diffuse 
innovations in schools, ultimately driving significant improvements 
(Chand et al., 2023; Moore, 2005). Additionally, future research en­
deavors could amplify students’ voices to enrich our understanding of 
their experiences of teacher innovation. 
Our study revealed that the existing research on teacher innovation 
generally lacked theoretical underpinning, as only 7.3% (n = 8) of the 
studies in our corpus clearly incorporated theories to frame and inform 
the research. The majority of the empirical studies were under- 
theorized, failing to articulate the theoretical foundation or assump­
tions guiding the research. One explanation for this under-theorization 
may stem from the context-specific nature of teacher innovation and 
the practice-oriented focus of research in this area, which often em­
phasizes strategies and the effectiveness of teacher innovation (Thurl­
ings et al., 2015). The absence of a suitable theoretical framework and 
an overreliance on an inductive approach to research have led to a 
notable limitation: our knowledge remains predominantly descriptive, 
lacking in-depth analysis of the underlying mechanisms driving different 
components or relationships within the teacher innovation phenomenon 
(Thurlings et al., 2015). Consequently, researchers frequently obtain 
similar results across different studies, hindering the accumulation of 
knowledge and the generalizability of research findings (Zainal & 
Matore, 2019). Therefore, researchers are encouraged to utilize estab­
lished theories to enrich the theoretical grounding of teacher innovation 
studies or to develop new middle-range theories that delineate the 
influencing or causal mechanisms underlying teacher innovation pro­
cesses, thus facilitating the advancement of knowledge in this field. 
4.4. Moving toward a more integrative theoretical framework for teacher 
innovation 
Firstly, this review highlights that the majority of the examined 
literature concentrates on identifying the principal antecedents of 
teacher innovation. However, an underdeveloped aspect exists con­
cerning the intermediate psychological processes through which various 
individual and contextual antecedents influence teacher innovation. 
Additionally, there is a critical need for future research to delve deeper 
into understanding the mechanisms by which teacher innovation im­
pacts student performance and teacher-related outcomes, such as work 
motivation, job satisfaction, and sustainability of professional learning 
communities (Maun et al., 2023; Thurlings et al., 2015). Given that 
non-cognitive outcomes serve as robust indicators for students’ future

Teaching and Teacher Education 145 (2024) 104611
12
success, further investigations are expected to explore how innovative 
teachers may foster student creativity through social modelling and 
innovative teaching strategies. Moreover, considering the value-laden 
nature of the teacher innovation process, the overarching objective of 
teacher innovation should encompass the generation of social value, 
especially in resource-constrained contexts (Chand et al., 2023). 
Second, it is commonly assumed that teacher innovation is inher­
ently beneficial, and thus, more teacher innovation is desirable. How­
ever, teacher innovation processes can be unpredictable and 
contentious, potentially leading to increased workload, role dilemmas, 
feelings of tension and anxiety, and envy among colleagues (Cheng, 
2010). To date, there is limited or even no research on the negative 
effects of teacher innovation, leading to an obvious publication bias. 
Therefore, to achieve a more comprehensive understanding, future 
studies should delve into the “dark side” of teacher innovation, uncov­
ering its potential dysfunctional consequences. 
Third, the current understanding of the determinants of teacher 
innovation is predominantly based on isolated subfields and various 
theories, resulting in inconsistencies in the research findings. The lack of 
a unified framework to guide theory development and research actions 
hinders knowledge building. Moreover, most existing studies have 
focused on the school- or teacher-level factors influencing teacher 
innovation. In contrast, research on the antecedents of teacher innova­
tion at the macro level (e.g., policy, culture, society, etc.) is scant in the 
literature. Teacher innovation is embedded in a complex social, insti­
tutional, and political system (Thurlings et al., 2015). The macro-level 
antecedents of teacher innovation should be brought to the forefront 
of empirical studies when investigating the relationships, causal mech­
anisms, and effect sizes. Specifically, national or regional policies 
regarding curricula, assessments, and incentives require more attention 
in future research, as these factors can significantly influence teachers’ 
motivation and capacity to innovate their teaching by offering guidance, 
support, or pressure for change. Unfortunately, these issues are 
marginalized in existing studies (Dobbins, 2009; Holdsworth & Maynes, 
2017). 
Furthermore, researchers have suggested that contextual factors 
such as the educational system, curriculum structure, supportive 
resource, and enabling organizational structure are associated with 
teacher innovation (Andiliou & Murphy, 2010; Buske, 2018; Dobbins, 
2009; Leikin et al., 2013; Thurlings et al., 2015). Despite the entrenched 
sociocultural traditions that shape organizational culture and teachers’ 
behavior, research on teacher innovation has predominantly dis­
regarded the roles of sociocultural traditions (Andiliou & Murphy, 2010; 
Lukes & Stephan, 2017). A prevailing notion suggests that Asian cul­
tures, in particular, may not foster an environment conducive to teacher 
innovation (Cheng, 2010; Mullet et al., 2016; Tan & Ng, 2021). For 
instance, scholars have observed that the word “cautious” holds higher 
regard in Chinese society, and significantly shapes teachers’ attitudes 
toward innovation (Mullet et al., 2016). Rooted in the Chinese proverb 
“Shooting the bird that sticks its head out,” there exists a common 
apprehension among Chinese educators that innovations often attract 
criticism, envy or opposition from colleagues. This underscores the 
profound link between cultural norms and teacher innovation. However, 
there remains a paucity of cross-national or cross-cultural research 
exploring how and why social or organizational contexts impact the 
processes and outcomes of teacher innovation (Andiliou & Murphy, 
2010; Liu et al., 2022; Liu & Hallinger, 2021; Nguyen et al., 2021). To 
cultivate a more context-sensitive understanding of teacher innovation 
across diverse cultural settings, future research endeavors should take 
into consideration the roles of cultural norms in teacher innovation. 
Researchers are encouraged to undertake more comparative studies to 
demonstrate how contextual factors may influence the antecedents, 
enactment, and outcomes of teacher innovation. 
4.5. Limitations 
While our study has yielded some interesting findings, we recognize 
that, like all research endeavors, it is not without its limitations. Firstly, 
concerning data collection, we opted to include only published journal 
articles to prevent the duplication of data (i.e., unpublished dissertations 
may later appear in peer-reviewed journals). While the exclusion of 
sources beyond journal articles is standard practice in review studies, it 
may have resulted in overlooking insights presented in book chapters 
and unpublished dissertations. Second, due to the language proficiency 
of our research team, we restricted our selection to articles written in 
English. Consequently, studies published in other languages are under­
represented in our research, despite English being the predominant 
language in international academic discourse. Third, despite our efforts 
to meticulously adhere to search and analysis procedures, it remains 
possible that we overlooked some pertinent articles. Moreover, the 
coding process for articles in our corpus was conducted manually. 
Human coding is susceptible to biases, notwithstanding our attempts to 
mitigate this by employing two or more coders and conducting multiple 
rounds of checks. Given these limitations, we encourage future research 
to validate, refine, and extend upon our findings by incorporating 
diverse literature sources or analytical methodologies. 
5. Conclusion 
Through this systematic review of 109 articles, we have accom­
plished several key objectives. First, we have provided a comprehensive 
clarification of the concept of teacher innovation and critically evalu­
ated the measures used in its assessment, noting that Chand et al. (2023) 
went through a rigorous process of scale development and validation. 
Second, we synthesized and appraised the research methods and theo­
retical frameworks employed in existing teacher innovation research. 
Third, we summarized the collective insights concerning the anteced­
ents, processes, and outcomes of teacher innovation, offering scholars an 
overarching framework of the current state of research in this area. 
Lastly, we outlined a research agenda aimed at enhancing, deepening, 
and expanding the scope of studies on teacher innovation. 
Our review has underscored that despite the rapid expansion of 
teacher innovation research in recent years, much work remains to be 
done. First, we identified a lack of clarity in the definitions and oper­
ationalizations of teacher innovation, resulting in considerable variation 
across studies. Future research should strive to establish a more precise 
definition and develop a unified measure to ensure consistency in 
conceptualization and measurement. Second, there is a pressing need for 
both large-scale quantitative investigations and in-depth qualitative 
inquiries into teacher innovation. Additionally, the integration of 
mixed-methods approaches utilizing diverse data sources would enrich 
the understanding of this complex phenomenon. Moreover, the dearth of 
robust theoretical frameworks in existing research constrains knowledge 
advancement in the field. Future studies should endeavor to incorporate 
solid theoretical underpinnings to facilitate theory building and refine­
ment. Furthermore, while numerous school organizational or individual 
factors influencing teacher innovation have been identified, there re­
mains a need for exploration of macro-level environmental influences. 
Researchers should investigate whether additional conditions can either 
foster or impede the relationships between teacher innovation and other 
variables. Finally, there is a call for more extensive research on the 
outcomes of teacher innovation beyond student and teacher perfor­
mance, including an examination of potential negative consequences. 
Understanding the mechanism through which teacher innovation may 
lead to adverse outcomes holds both theoretical and practical signifi­
cance. By highlighting these areas for improvement and fostering 
collaboration among scholars, we aspire to propel the field of teacher 
innovation research forward, enabling it to achieve greater depth and 
breadth in its exploration of this vital educational phenomenon.
