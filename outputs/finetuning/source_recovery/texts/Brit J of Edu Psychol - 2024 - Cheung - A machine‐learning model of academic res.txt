Abstract
Background: Given that students from socio-­economically
disadvantaged family backgrounds are more likely to suffer
from low academic performance, there is an interest in identifying features of academic resilience, which may mitigate
the relationship between disadvantaged socio-­economic status and academic performance.
Aims: This study sought to combine machine learning
and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) technique to
identify key features of academic resilience in mathematics
learning during COVID-­19.
Materials and Methods: Based on PISA 2022 data in 79
countries/economies, the random forest model coupled
with Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) value technique
not only uncovered the key features of academic resilience
but also examined the contributions of each key feature.
Results: Findings indicated that 35 features were identified in the classification of academically resilient and non-academically resilient students, which largely validated the
previous academic resilient framework. Notably, gender
differences were shown in the distribution of some key
features. Research findings also indicated that resilient students tended to have a stable emotional state, high levels
of self-­efficacy, low levels of truancy and positive future
aspirations.
Discussion: This study has established a research paradigm essentially methodological in nature to bridge the gap
between psychological theories and big data in the field of
educational psychology.

I N T RODUC T ION
In PISA 2022, socio-­economically disadvantaged students from OECD countries/economies underperformed 93 points in mathematical literacy than their advantaged counterparts on average
(OECD, 2023a). That is, those advantaged students gained four to five more years of schooling than
their disadvantaged peers in mathematics learning. Notably, students from low-­income families were
more likely to have difficulties in mathematics learning, which might bring challenges to their career
development ( Jordan & Levine, 2009). It is widely examined that socioeconomic status has a significant impact on students' mathematics literacy across Western and Eastern cultures (Kalaycioglu, 2015;
Kang & Cogan, 2022; Li et al., 2021). Surprisingly, a group of low socio-­economically students were
strongly motivated, and they aspired to learn mathematics despite their disadvantaged family background (Ng, 2020). Moreover, a portion of socio-­economically disadvantaged students could reach high
levels of mathematics literacy (Aydın & Erdem, 2023). In this sense, students' mathematics literacy is
not fully explained by their socioeconomic status. Of note, disadvantaged individuals who beat against
the odds and achieve academic success are called academically resilient students. There is an urgent need
to identify key factors contributing to academically resilient students. On one hand, the proportion of
academically resilient students reflects the degree of educational equity (Agasisti et al., 2017). On the
other hand, key factors of academic resilience play a key role in developing educational quality (Clavel
et al., 2022).
There is a growing interest in academic resilience for international large-­scale assessment (ILSA)
research. Since OECD released a pioneer report called Against the Odds: disadvantaged students who succeed
in school in 2011, an increasing number of research studies shared common interests in identifying key
factors of academic resilience based on ILSA (e.g., Agasisti et al., 2021; Cheung et al., 2014; García-Crespo et al., 2019; Sandoval-­Hernández & Białowolski, 2016; Zheng et al., 2024a). Of note, ILSA has
a great impact on educational policy, such as enhancing educational reform and debate (Domínguez
et al., 2012; Santos & Centeno, 2021). From a global perspective, key factors of academic resilience in
ILSA contribute to education equity for the participating countries/economies, which further narrow
the achievement gap between socio-­economically advantaged and disadvantaged students.

L I T ER AT U R E R E V I E W
Mathematical literacy
In PISA 2022, mathematical literacy is defined as ‘an individual's capacity to reason mathematically and
to formulate, employ, and interpret mathematics to solve problems in a variety of real-­world contexts. It
includes concepts, procedures, facts and tools to describe, explain, and predict phenomena. It assists individuals to know the role that mathematics plays in the world and to make the well-­founded judgments
and decisions needed by constructive, engaged, and reflective 21st-­century citizens' (OECD, 2023c,
p.22). Therefore, mathematical reasoning and problem-­solving competence are two crucial components
of mathematical literacy in the latest PISA cycle.

Academic resilience
Resilience is an umbrella concept in human development. It has been widely applied to multiple fields
(e.g., psychology, ecology and sociology) (Adger, 2000; Gunderson, 2000; Song et al., 2019). In the field
of education, academic resilience is defined as ‘the heightened likelihood of success in school and other
life accomplishments, despite environmental adversities brought about by early traits, conditions, and
experiences’ (Wang et al., 1994, p.137). Thus, academic resilience is composed of two dimensions: adversity and positive adaptation (Windle, 2011).
Academic resilience has been studied in different school subjects, such as language (Namaziandost
et al., 2023), science (Wang et al., 2024), art (Winters, 2022) and physical education (Tudor et al., 2020).
Up to now, there is a growing interest in academic resilience within the field of mathematics (e.g.,
Borman & Overman, 2004; Ricketts et al., 2017; Sandoval-­Hernández & Białowolski, 2016). Since
there is no consensus on the definition of academic resilience in mathematical literacy (Xenofontos
& Mouroutsou, 2023), from an international perspective in PISA, our study conceptualized it as ‘students whose economic, social and cultural status (ESCS) locate in the bottom 25% within their own
country/economy but mathematics performance locate in the top 25% across countries/economies’
(OECD, 2011, 2023a).

Factors contributing to academic resilience in mathematical literacy
Up to now, an increasing number of studies have identified factors of academic resilience in mathematical literacy. Key factors contributing to mathematical resilience have been categorized into two major
domains: psychological factors and social/environmental factors (Xenofontos & Mouroutsou, 2023).
On one side, a number of prior studies indicated that familiarity with the mathematical concepts,
strong engagement in mathematics activities, confidence in mathematics, positive self-­esteem, as well
as high levels of mathematics self-­efficacy and academic expectations were protective factors for the
socio-­economically disadvantaged students to achieve high mathematics performance (e.g., Aydın &
Erdem, 2023; Borman & Overman, 2004; Cheung, 2017; García-­Crespo et al., 2022; Salvo-­Garrido
et al., 2020). To summarize, psychological factors are divided into four aspects: cognitive skills, behavioural engagement, emotional styles and motivation.
On the other side, social/environmental factors include family support and atmosphere, school contextual and process factors, as well as community-­and system-­level factors. To be specific, high quality
of parent–child relationships played a protective role for socio-­economically disadvantaged students
(Whitten & Weaver, 2010). Teacher support, especially teaching strategies based on cognitive activation
was associated with academic resilience in mathematics (Alivernini et al., 2016). In addition, a positive
school climate, such as positive student-­teacher relationships, low levels of bullying at school, and orderly disciplinary climate in mathematics class compensated the effects of low socioeconomic status on
students' mathematics achievement (Teng, 2020). Regarding the system-­level factors, education expenditure and teacher salary were considered as key factors associated with academic resilience (Vicente

Combining machine learning with explainable artificial intelligence in search
for key features of academic resilience
During the early research agenda of academic resilience, most studies adopted conventional statistical approaches to identify key features (e.g., Cheung, 2017; Cheung et al., 2014; Sandoval-­Hernández
& Białowolski, 2016). However, those studies were limited to a particular cohort of variables depending on the theoretical background (Zheng et al., 2024b). In contrast, machine learning has a
key advantage in working with a massive group of correlated features (Senaviratna & Cooray, 2019;
Zheng et al., 2024a). As a branch of artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning enables a machine
to automatically learn from the big data. In simple terms, machine learning enables algorithms to
imitate the way that human learn. Since our application of machine learning is exploratory in approach based on big data with interpretable results, it has the potential to lay a solid foundation
for further confirmatory work. In the latest stage of academic resilience research, most studies
commonly utilized machine-­learning approaches to identify key features associated with academic
resilience (e.g., Wang et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2024a). However, machine-­learning approach has
weaknesses in unfolding relationships between different features, which further fails to examine the
effect size of each feature (Bu & Chen, 2023; Bzdok et al., 2018). In other words, machine learning
is commonly a black box that brings challenges for human to interpret the output of models under
exploration.
Up to now, a growing number of educational research combined machine-­learning approaches with
explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) to identify key features (e.g., Lezhnina & Kismihók, 2022; Tan
et al., 2024; Zhai et al., 2024). Compared with machine learning, XAI is a completely different concept
that allow human to comprehensively understand the results of machine learning models. Taking one
step further, XAI focuses on the reasoning, which is primarily inductive behind the classifications, predictions and decisions made by machine learning models. XAI shows advantages in twofold. First, it
enables humans to comprehend the ‘black box’ of machine learning models in an intuitive way (Dwivedi
et al., 2023). Second, it avoids the multicollinearity problems brought by massively correlated features
(Al-­Najjar et al., 2023). Originated from the game theory, Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) value
is an XAI technique that visualizes the contributions of each variable and considers all combinations
of correlated variables with multicollinearity issues (Lundberg & Lee, 2017; Maxwell et al., 2021).
SHAP attempts to measure the relative contribution of each player to the overall outcome or effect.
Mathematically, the Shapley value is one way to distribute the overall outcome or effect fairly to the
players, assuming that they all collaborate to produce the overall outcome or effect. For example, there
are three key players (features A, B and C) identified in the resilience classification model. To calculate
the SHAP values of each key feature, an empty coalition is initially developed. Then, the three features
are randomly permutated with the coalition being formed one feature at a time while demanding their
contribution as a fair compensation. Finally, the mean SHAP values, that is, the average of these contributions over the possible different permutations in which the coalition can be formed, are assigned for
the features A, B and C, respectively.
However, it is challenging for traditional statistical analysis (e.g., regression-­based techniques) to
interpret the impact of a large number of correlated variables on the dependent variable in an intuitive
way. Therefore, the current study sought to deploy a machine learning technique (i.e., random forest)
coupled with an XAI method (i.e., SHAP value) to identify key features of academic resilience in mathematics learning during the times of the COVID-­19 pandemic.

The current study
To gain a better understanding of resilience, Ungar and Theron (2020) considered it as a multisystemic
concept that involved various levels of analysis. According to Bronfenbrenner's (1979) ecological theory, a conceptual framework of academic resilience has been proposed by a systematic review (Zheng
et al., 2024b). There are five layers of protective factors contributing to academic resilience, including
demographic factors, personal experience, family support and atmosphere, school contextual and process factors, as well as community and system-­level factors (see Figure 1).
Combining machine learning and XAI approach based on PISA 2022 data from a global perspective,
two research questions are addressed in the current study:
Research question 1 (RQ1): What are the key features of academic resilience in mathematical literacy? To
what extent do these key features validate the conceptual framework of academic resilience?
Research question 2 (RQ2): To what extent does each key feature contribute to academic resilience
in mathematical literacy? What are the similarities and differences of key features broken down by
gender?

M E T HOD
Data
The PISA 2022 dataset was utilized in this study, which can be derived from the OECD's official website (https://​w ww.​oecd.​org/​pisa/​data/​2022d​ataba​se/​). PISA is an international large-­scale assessment
initiated by OECD in 2000, which focuses on 15-­year-­old students' 21st-­century life-­long learning skills
and literacies in contemporary society. Due to school lockdown caused by the COVID-­19 pandemic, the
latest data collection cycle has been postponed 1 year later to 2022. The major domain of PISA 2022 referred to mathematics, whereas reading, science and creative thinking were assessed as minor domains.
Since PISA is undertaken by the OECD, it strictly monitors the data cleaning procedure thus the noise is
under control. A total of 607,631 students from 79 countries/economies are involved at the initial stage.
Although the definition of academic resilience is well established, there is no consensus on its measurement (Rudd et al., 2021). Over a decade ago, OECD proposed the first operational definition of
academic resilience in the report called Against the Odds: Disadvantaged Students Who Succeed in School. It
lays a solid foundation for academic resilience research in large-­scale assessment. According to the
definition of academic resilience, it is composed of two dimensions: adversity and positive adaptation (Windle, 2011). Concerning the operational definition of academic resilience, adversity refers to a
relative student's disadvantaged background within each country/economy. This is attributed to the
differences in the meaning of capital (e.g., owning a car) between the developed and developing entities
(Ye et al., 2021). In addition, positive adaptation refers to students' academic success. Of note, OECD
has developed two approaches to measure students' academic success: (1) an international perspective
and (2) a within-­country perspective (OECD, 2011). From an international perspective, researchers
tend to utilize a fixed academic success across countries/economies. This operational definition (i.e.,
relative adversity, fixed positive adaptation) indicates the proportion of resilient students at the system
level, which yields insights into education systems with a low proportion of resilient students. From a
within-­country perspective, researchers tend to adopt relative academic success within a specific education system. This operational definition (i.e., relative adversity, relative positive adaptation) provides
implications for stakeholders to foster academic resilience within their education system, which aims to
develop country-­specific educational policies.
Since the current study utilized a globally comparable research sample (i.e., 79 countries/economies),
we followed the OECD's operational definition from an international perspective to determine an
academically resilient student in three steps (OECD, 2011, 2023a). First, we screened disadvantaged
students who were in the bottom quarter of ESCS within their country/economy (i.e., relative adversity). Second, of these ESCS-­d isadvantaged students, we further selected high performers whose
mathematics performance achieved the top quarter across 79 countries/economies (i.e., fixed positive
adaptation). The selected individuals in this step are defined as academically resilient students (ARS)
and the remaining students are defined as non-­academically resilient students (NRS). Finally, ARS was
coded as 1, and NRS was coded as 0.
Overall, the final sample of this study involved 147,658 ESCS-­d isadvantaged students, which were
further classified into 18,500 ARS and 129,158 NRS. In other words, the globally comparable research
sample involves 12.53% ARS and 87.47% NRS. The supplementary materials describe the percentages
of ARS and NRS in each country/economy (see Appendix S1).

Analytical strategy
The data analysis plan comprised three step-­by-­step procedures (i.e., data preprocessing, primary analysis and model evaluation), which were implemented by the Python 3.10.6 program. The coding documentation libraries are freely and publicly available on the website of scikit-­learn (https://​sciki​t-­​learn.​org/​
stable/​) and imbalanced-­learn (https://​imbal​anced​-­​learn.​org/​dev/​).

Data preprocessing
We employed an equal distribution technique to regroup the imbalanced data in the first procedure of
data preprocessing. The overarching goal of this procedure is to provide a more fair and randomized
method to reduce the selection bias during data preprocessing. As a first step of regrouping of the imbalanced data, the raw data were categorized into ARS and NRS. To facilitate the subsequent 10-­fold
cross-­validation technique, the dataset was regrouped into 10 modules proportionally. In other words,
the ratio of the ARS and NRS is equal in each data module. This step ensures the effectiveness of 10-fold cross-­validation in the primary analysis [i.e., eight modules as a training dataset (80%) and the remaining two modules as a test dataset (20%)]. Furthermore, each module was shuffled at random. This
data regrouping procedure not only ensures the independence of each data module and prevents data
information leakage but also accelerates the computational speed of data preprocessing and increases
the model performance compared to data preprocessing without an equal distribution technique.
After the data regrouping procedure, three specific steps should be followed to preprocess each
data module independently. First, the k-­nearest neighbour (kNN) imputation was utilized to handle
the missing data as suggested by previous large-­scale assessment research through machine learning
techniques (e.g., Chen et al., 2022; Gorostiaga & Rojo-­Á lvarez, 2016; Hu et al., 2022). Second, normalization was conducted to standardize the data ranging from −1 to 1. Finally, a hybrid resampling
technique (i.e., SMOTETomek) (e.g., Jonathan et al., 2020; Sanguanmak & Hanskunatai, 2016) was
implemented to address the issue of imbalanced data in our study. SMOTETomek is a combination of
the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) and Tomek Links (Batista et al., 2003), which
involves two major steps. First, SMOTE generated artificial data samples of the ARS, which is based on
the Euclidean distance between the random data and its k-­nearest neighbours. Of note, the Euclidean
distance refers to the length of the shortest line between two data points. Second, Tomek Links detects

Primary analysis
Since 10 data modules were preprocessed independently, we further combined eight modules as a training dataset (80%) and the remaining two modules as a test dataset (20%) to implement the primary
analysis. The primary analysis involves four steps: (1) train the random forest model; (2) rank the optimal subset of features; (3) determine the optimal subset; and (4) calculate the relative contribution of the
optimal subset through SHAP values (Marcílio & Eler, 2020).

Random forest
Random forest is a tree-based ensemble learning approach that contains multiple decision trees
(Breiman, 2001). The majority voting of each independent tree results in the output of random forest
model (Kulkarni & Sinha, 2013). Random forest not only shows superiority in handling imbalanced
and high-­d imensional datasets (More & Rana, 2017) but also proves its high classification accuracy and
good generalization ability (Breiman, 2001). Compared with the decision tree, random forest is superior to avoiding overfitting problems (Liaw & Wiener, 2002). Overfitting occurs when the algorithm
fits closely to the training data since the model might learn the noise (i.e., irrelevant information). In
this sense, the overfitting model fails to generalize to a new dataset, which is unable to implement the
prediction and classification tasks. Compared with XGBoost, random forest is more robust to noisy
data because it applies the bagging technique (Parmar et al., 2019). Bagging technique trains each base
learner in parallel, which aims to deal with different data subsets. Since the final prediction is based on
the average of base learners, the effects of individual errors are reduced by the aggregation of predictions. Therefore, random forest is less sensitive to the noise than XGBoost.
Feature selection aims to determine the most relevant features of the machine-­learning model and
reduce the number of input variables. In this sense, key features were determined by the algorithm of
recursive feature elimination (RFE). It is a wrapper-­type feature selection technique, which aims to
determine the optimal subset of features (i.e., key features contributing to academic resilience in this
study). In simpler terms, this technique ‘wrap’ around the machine learning models and evaluate different combinations of feature subset iteratively. Random forest with an RFE approach involves three
major procedures in the current study. Primarily, we trained a random forest model in the classification
of ARS and NRS. Furthermore, the relative feature importance was ranked, and the least important
feature was removed. Using the remaining features, a new random forest model was re-­t rained and the
former working process was repeated. Finally, the optimal subset of features was determined if there
was only one feature left. The working process of random forest with RFE is presented in Figure 2.
Shapley additive explanations values
Rooted in the game theoretical approach, SHAP values aim to calculate the contribution of each key
feature (Marcílio & Eler, 2020). In a machine learning ‘game’, each feature is regarded as a ‘player’. In
other words, SHAP values are used to measure the relative contribution of each player to the overall
outcome (Merrick & Taly, 2020). To be specific, SHAP values are iteratively calculated by comparing the
model's predictions for the presence or absence of a particular feature. For each prediction, SHAP values assign an importance value to each feature by considering all possible combinations. Of note, there
is no single agreed measure of importance and different measures have different properties (Nathans
et al., 2012).

Model evaluation
To ensure the stability of the overall model, k-­fold cross-­validation was adopted to fairly evaluate the
model. According to Kuhn and Johnson (2013), it might be suitable to adopt k as 10 because a larger k
leads to a less biased result. Overall, five evaluation metrics of the model were obtained from a 10-­fold
cross-­validation, including accuracy, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC), recall, precision and F-­measure.
First, accuracy is the most basic evaluation metric measuring the overall correctness of a model
prediction. Nevertheless, it overlooks the influence of the minority class (Kulkarni et al., 2020),
which, therefore, is not a fair evaluation metric in an extremely imbalanced dataset (e.g., ARS/
NRS = 1/6.98 in the current study). Second, ROC-­AUC aims to discriminate positive instances from
negative samples. In addition, ROC-­AUC is a probability curve ranging from 0 to 1, with .5 indicating a random guess and 1 indicating a perfect prediction (Huang & Ling, 2005). According to
Juba and Le (2019), recall and precision are more suitable for evaluating the imbalanced model. On
one side, recall aims to evaluate the possibility of the model to identify all positive instances. On
the other side, precision aims to measure the correctness of positive predictions. Finally, F-­measure
is the harmonic average of recall and precision, which is used to evaluate the model from a holistic
perspective (Bekkar et al., 2013). To be specific, their mathematical formulae are listed in the supplementary materials (see Appendix S3).

R E SU LT S
Classification performance of the random forest model
In our study, the random forest model has a good classification performance since five evaluation
metrics (i.e., accuracy = 90.54%, ROC-­AUC = 96.79%, precision = 93.13%, recall = 87.58% and F-measure = 90.26%) are above 85% on average (Xia et al., 2013) (see Table 1).
Four elements in the confusion matrix need to be explained to clarify evaluation metrics, including
True Positive (TP), False Positive (FP), True Negative (TN) and False Negative (FN). The confusion
matrix represents the numbers of predicted and actual values: (1) TP reflects ARS is correctly predicted
as ARS; (2) FP reflects NRS is incorrectly predicted as ARS; (3) TN means NRS is correctly predicted
as NRS; and (4) FN means ARS is incorrectly predicted as NRS.
First, accuracy is used to measure the overall correctness of TP and TN. A high accuracy indicates
a high percentage of correct predictions of ARS and NRS made by the random forest model (accuracy = 90.54%). Second, ROC-­AUC is related to FN and FP. A very high ROC-­AUC near to 100%
(ROC-­AUC = 96.79%) indicates that the number of students wrongly predicted as ARS and wrongly
predicted as NRS in our study are very low simultaneously. Third, recall is highly correlated to FN. A
smaller FN means ARS is less likely to be wrongly predicted as NRS, which further decreases the underestimation of talents needed for cultivation. Fourth, precision is associated with FP. A smaller FP means
NRS is less likely to be wrongly predicted as ARS, which further reduces the overestimation of talents.
In our study, recall indicates that less than 15% of talents are lost (recall = 87.58%), whereas precision
means less than 10% of talents are overrated (precision = 93.13%). Since F-­measure is the harmonic
average of recall, a high F-­measure indicates a high model performance from a holistic perspective
(F-­measure = 90.26%).

Gender differences in the optimal subset of features
Figure 5 reveals the contribution of the optimal features by gender. Overall, the impacts of each optimal
feature on academic resilience were similar for females and males. To be specific, there are four main
findings in gender differences. First, at the very low end of the familiarity with mathematics concept in
the bee-­swarm plots, the majority of the females compared with the males were having slightly higher
SHAP values. This subtle observation indicates that females with low familiarity with mathematics
concept were more prone to be NRS than males. However, students of both gender at other higher level
of familiarity with mathematics concept were having SHAP values that were comparable in magnitude.
Second, for those who frequently arrived late for school, females were more likely to be classified as
NRS than that of males. Although both gender who never arrived late for school were largely classified
as ARS, the number of those females are slightly more than that of the males. Third, although both
gender who repeated a grade were more prone to be NRS, males who ever experienced a grade repetition tended to have a larger number of NRS compared with females. Finally, comparing between males
and females, males studying in schools with low responsibility for the curriculum were having not only
more students in number but also were more prone to be classified as NRS. Some males may be classified with ARS but the number was smaller than that of the females.

DIS C US SION
Overall, the top 35 features identified in the random forest model were categorized into six domains.
Specifically, six domains identified in the current research cover four layers of the conceptual framework of academic resilience proposed by Zheng et al. (2024b). First, in the layer of demographic factors, gender showed a significant effect on academic resilience, which is consistent with the previous
research (Ricketts et al., 2017; Yavuz & Kutlu, 2016). Second, in the layer of personal experience, our
study validated four sub-­domains of the conceptual framework of academic resilience (i.e., cognitive
skills, emotional styles, motivation and behavioural engagement). To summarize, our research findings
indicated that ARS tended to have a stable emotional state, high levels of self-­efficacy, low levels of
truancy and positive future aspirations. This crucial finding is in line with prior studies on academic
resilience (e.g., Agasisti et al., 2021; Gizir & Aydin, 2009; Tamannaeifar & Shahmirzaei, 2019; Wang
et al., 2022). The self-­determination theory might contribute to explaining the reasons behind this

The results showed that familiarity of math concepts and grade repetition were important features of resilient students, which was consistent with Cheung's (2017) findings. The greater learning difficulties experienced by disadvantaged females with a similar lack of familiarity of mathematics concepts could be
contributed by the existence of gender stereotypes and biases. Girls may internalize the societal/cultural
beliefs that mathematics is a ‘male subject’ and thus develop a low self-­concept in their math-­related
competencies. Fostering a growth mindset and providing equitable access to educational resources and
support for all students to engage in activities, regardless of gender, may help address this issue (Mak
et al., 2017). In addition, repeating a grade may result in lower levels of psychological dispositions and
self-­regulatory learning skills (Cheung et al., 2021; Sit et al., 2015) and chronic lateness to school can disrupt learning and hinder the development of important skills. Due to societal expectations and gender
norm, males with grade repetition experience may receive greater pressure of achieving mathematical
milestones, while females may have greater stress and inadequacy if they cannot meet the punctuality
standard. Further studies will be needed to identify the mechanisms between those features and resilience as well as the targeted interventions narrowing the gender gap of resilience.
Overall, the current study makes significant contributions to academic resilience research in the field
of educational psychology. From the perspective of methodological development, the current study adopted a collaborative approach to combine machine learning and the XAI technique, which established
a new paradigm for education research from a big data perspective. To be specific, key features were
initially selected by machine learning models and further explained by the XAI technique in a comprehensive way. In this sense, traditional regression-­based models (e.g., structural equation modelling,
mediation and path analysis) can be developed in line with the machine learning model interpretation.
This methodological paradigm can bridge the gap between big data and educational theories (Kitto
et al., 2023). Theoretically speaking, this study involved a wide range of variables from a global perspective, which adopted a multisystemic perspective to understand academic resilience. By examining the
interactions across different systems (Ungar & Theron, 2020), this study illuminates the development
of academic resilience research.

With respect to the practical contributions, there are two main aspects. First, this study emphasizes the importance of self-­efficacy in fostering academic resilience from a global perspective.

pay close attention to the differences in psychological states between girls and boys when fostering

L I M I TAT IONS A N D F U T U R E DI R EC T IONS
Although the current study provides crucial implications, several limitations should be addressed.
First, this study merely adopted an international perspective to operationalize academic resilience.
Since the understanding of academic resilience varies in different cultural contexts, culture plays a
key role in the development of academic resilience from a cross-­c ultural perspective (Ungar, 2011).
Therefore, key features contributing to academic resilience are culturally relevant. It is necessary to
take a deep look at academic resilience in each country/economy that is rooted in different cultural
contexts. Future work should utilize a within-­country perspective and pay close attention to a specific country/economy.
Second, due to a low response rate of the teacher questionnaire in most education systems, the
data source is constrained to student and school questionnaires in this study. Likewise, parent questionnaire involves a high rate of missing data, which might directly influence the machine learning model output. However, parent-­related items were involved in the student questionnaire and
teacher-­related items were involved in the school questionnaire. By involving a massive group of
features, the classification performance of this machine learning is above 85% on average. Further
studies could focus on specific education systems with a low rate of missing data in teacher and
parent questionnaires.
Third, this study is limited to utilizing a random forest model. Researchers are suggested to adopt
other machine learning methods, such as Decision Trees, Support Vector Machine and XGBoost. By
comparing different machine learning techniques, a methodological guideline could be developed according to their preprocessed techniques, algorithms and adjusted parameters.
Fourth, machine-­learning results, which are based grossly on inductive logic should be interpreted
with caution since they might deviate from educational theories. Therefore, traditional statistical analyses (e.g., multilevel regression, hierarchical linear modelling and structural equation modelling) that
are based more on the deductive logic should be utilized to validate the machine learning output in the
future studies.
Finally, PISA follows a cross-­sectional research design, which could not generalize to a causal relationship. In addition to adopting the longitudinal dataset, future studies could try to develop causal
graphs based on observational data, for example by causal structural learning.

C ONC LUSION
Combining machine learning and the XAI technique, the current study identified 35 key features and
examined their contributions to academic resilience. By identifying key features of academic resilience,
this study paves a new way to narrow the achievement gap between ESCS-­d isadvantaged and advantaged students from a big data perspective. The current research not only facilitates the theoretical
development of academic resilience but also establishes a methodological paradigm for researchers to
understand academic resilience in a multisystemic way. To summarize, our study provides fresh insights
into the issues of academic resilience from a global perspective in the times of the COVID-­19 pandemic.
