The Effect of Inquiry‑Based Practices on Scientific Literacy:
the Mediating Role of Science Attitudes
Yue Ma1
Received: 27 October 2021 / Accepted: 31 October 2022 / Published online: 28 November 2022
© National Science and Technology Council, Taiwan 2022

Abstract
Inquiry-based practices have important impacts on student science learning outcomes, yet the psychological mechanisms behind the relationships are less clear.
This study aimed to explore the mediating effects of science attitudes in the relationship between student-perceived inquiry-based practices and scientific literacy using
data from eight countries in the 2015 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA). Results of multilevel structural equation modeling (MSEM) suggested that (1) the three science attitudinal factors, i.e., interest in science, science
self-efficacy, and instrumental motivation of learning science, served as significant
mediators between perceived inquiry-based practices and scientific literacy; (2) the
direct effect of perceived inquiry-based practices on scientific literacy was consistently negative across the countries, yet consistently positive on the three attitudinal
factors; and (3) the indirect effects of perceived inquiry-based practices on scientific
literacy via interest in science and science self-efficacy were consistently positive
across the countries while negative via instrumental motivation for six out of the
eight countries. The results may provide deeper insights into inquiry-based practices
and suggest ways for science educators to improve student science attitudes and science learning outcomes.
Keywords Perceived inquiry-based science practices · Science attitudes · Scientific
literacy

attitudes toward science, and scientific literacy differ across different countries/cultures. Notably, attitudes toward science were specified in terms of three factors in

Inquiry‑Based Practices and Science Achievement
Inquiry-based practices have been acknowledged as an effective way to foster student scientific literacy (Liou, 2021). Different from traditional teacher-centered
instruction, inquiry-based practices emphasize involving students into the processes
of scientific activities by employing different instruction techniques, e.g., raising
questions, conducting explorations, collecting, analyzing and interpreting data, proposing solutions, justifications, and predictions (NRC, 2012).
Many researchers have explored the relationships between inquiry-based practices and science achievement, yet the findings are inconsistent in the literature.
Some researchers (e.g. Furtak et al., 2012; Liou & Ho, 2018) indicated that inquiry
instruction is an important element of science education, and they exert positive
influences on science achievement. For instance, Minner et al. (2010) explored
how inquiry instruction might affect K-12 student science learning by analyzing the
numerical and text data in 138 prior studies from 1984 to 2002. Results suggested
that inquiry instruction was beneficial for student learning, as it could stimulate student active thinking and help students draw evidence-based conclusions.
However, some researchers (e.g. Aditomo & Klieme, 2020; Hwang et al., 2018)
suggested negative relationships. For instance, Cairns and Areepattamannil (2019)
explored the relations of inquiry-based practices to 15-year-old students’ scientific
literacy using data from 54 countries in PISA 2006. Results indicated that inquirybased practices were a significantly negative predictor of scientific literacy, but it
was a significantly positive predictor of science attitudes. Researchers supporting the negative relationships argued that many inquiry-based practices are in fact
minimally guided, leaving students with insufficient support while learning sciencerelated knowledge and skills. Moreover, inquiry-based practices require students
to deal with demanding scientific tasks as scientists and engineers, which may go
beyond students’ capability, overload their working memory, and thus prevent them
from efficient learning (Rosenshine, 2012).
Furthermore, some researchers even found that the impacts of inquiry-based
practices might vary across different inquiry activities. For example, Areepattamannil et al. (2011) explored how science motivational beliefs and inquiry-based practices might relate with 15-year-old student science performance in Canada. Results
suggested that motivational beliefs positively predicted student science performance, yet interest in science was a negative predictor. In addition, hands-on activities seemed to increase science performance, while investigations tended to lower
the performance. Gee and Wong (2012) explored how four types of inquiry-based
practices (i.e. models/applications, hands-on activities, interaction, and independent
investigations) might relate to 15-year-old student scientific literacy in eight countries in PISA 2006. Results indicated that scientific literacy would be promoted if
models or applications were used more frequently, while decreased if independent
investigations were used more frequently.

Inquiry‑Based Practices, Science Attitudes, and Science Achievement
Science attitudes are closely associated with inquiry-based practices and science
learning outcomes. First, the associations between science attitudes and sciencerelated outcomes are found to be significant (Osborne et al., 2003). Possessing positive science attitudes may facilitate the engagement in scientific activities, deepen
the learning of complicated science concepts and knowledge, and improve the performance on science-related tasks.
Furthermore, science attitudes (e.g. self-efficacy, interest, instrumental motivation) are also closely associated with inquiry-based practices. Specifically, Liou
(2021) suggested that “human functioning is a reciprocal interaction among environmental, behavioral, and personal factors.” One important personal attitudinal factor
that can predict an individual’s behaviors is self-efficacy, which reflects the level at
which an individual believes he/she can successfully accomplish certain tasks (Bandura, 1986). An individual’s self-efficacy can be improved if he/she is familiar with
the tasks and have more mastery experiences. As such, inquiry-based practices are
likely to enhance an individual’s self-efficacy, as the practices may provide him/her
with more chances to experience similar tasks, get over difficulties involved in the
tasks, and feel the joy of success in accomplishing the tasks (Schunk, 1987).
Interest in science and instrumental motivation are also two major components of
science attitudes, which correspond to “intrinsic motivation” and “extrinsic motivation” respectively in the self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2009). To be more
specific, interest in science suggests that an individual takes an active role in certain
scientific activities because of his/her interest or curiosity. Interest in science can
be enhanced if an individual has more options or freedom (Zukerman et al., 1978).
In this sense, inquiry-based practices are beneficial because they allow students to
explore the world freely and make their own decisions (Hmelo-Silver, 2004). Instrumental motivation (or extrinsic motivation) reflects the level at which an individual
views learning science as vital and useful (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2016). Inquiry-based practices tend to situate students into authentic or real-world scenarios, through which students may have better
chance to know the utility or practical application of learning science in real life.
As stated above, many researchers have theoretically discussed the relationships
among inquiry-based practices, science attitudes, and science learning outcomes.
However, so far, little empirical evidence has been provided, particularly on how science attitudes might mediate the relationships between inquiry-based practices and
science achievement in a multi-cultural context. For instance, Liou and Ho (2018)
explored the relationships between inquiry instruction, motivation beliefs, and science performance among eighth-graders in Taiwan based on TIMSS 2007. Results
suggested that inquiry-based instruction was a significant moderator between intrinsic motivation and science performance, yet it was not clear whether it was a mediator or not. Liou (2021) explored how inquiry-based practices, science attitudes, and
scientific literacy might relate with one another using the PISA 2015 Taiwan data.
However, the study did not consider the nested structure of the PISA 2015 data, and
the results may not generalize well to other cultural contexts.

Cultural Context
The efficacy of inquiry-based practices can be affected by cultural context (Tang
& Zhang, 2020). In fact, many scholars (e.g. Forbes et al., 2020; Oliver et al.,
2019) have investigated inquiry-based practices in a multi-cultural context. For
example, Tang et al. (2020) explored potential variables affecting inquiry-based
instruction across One Belt One Road (OBOR) countries/regions (e.g. Mainland
China, Korea, Italy, United Arab Emirates, Czech Republic) using data from
PISA 2015. Results indicated that teachers’ collaborative experiences and beliefs
were positive predictors of inquiry-based instruction across OBOR countries, yet
mixed result was found for school location and science-specific resources across
the countries. Oliver et al. (2019) compared the effectiveness of three kinds of
classroom instructions (i.e. inquiry-based instruction, adaptive instruction, and
teacher-directed instruction) on student scientific literacy across six countries
(e.g. New Zealand, USA, Australia, UK) in PISA 2015. Results showed that students who were taught using inquiry instruction tended to exhibit worse scientific
literacy than those taught under the adaptive and teacher-directed instruction.
In the present study, four Eastern countries (i.e. China, Japan, Korea, Singapore) and four Western countries (i.e. the USA, Germany, Australia, Finland)
were chosen for investigation. The reasons were as follows: (1) Collective culture
is emphasized in Eastern countries, in which such characteristics as self-sacrifice, generosity, and dependence are valued. In contrast, individualistic culture
prevails in Western countries, in which independence, autonomy, and confidence
are highlighted (Hofstede et al., 2010). These cultural differences may shape the
idea of education differently across the countries (Tang & Zhang, 2020). (2) Similar policies on inquiry-based science practices have been launched in the eight
countries. For example, the Ministry of Education (MOE) in China recommended
inquiry-based instruction in the curriculum standards of compulsory education
in 2001 and 2011. In 2019, the Curriculum, Assessment and Reporting Authority (CARA) in Australia regarded inquiry in science to be one important pillar
of science curriculum. The United States Research Council (2012) emphasized
the use of inquiry approaches in science education to aid students in exploring
the world. In Europe, many inquiry-based learning projects such as Mathematics
and Science for Life (MASCIL) and Strategies for Assessment of Inquiry Learning in Science (SAILS) have been initiated by the European Union (EU), and (3)
student-reported frequencies of inquiry-based practices carried out in science
class were different across the countries (see Table 1). For instance, on average,
students in the USA (index score = 0.337) and Australia (= 0.183) reported higher
frequencies of inquiry-based instruction than the mean of the OECD countries
(= 0); students in China (= − 0.146), Japan (= − 0.630), Korea (= − 0.617) and
Finland (− 0.305) reported lower frequencies of inquiry-based instruction than
the mean of the OECD countries (= 0), and the reported frequencies of inquirybased instruction of students in Singapore (= − 0.004) and Germany (= 0.065)
were roughly the same with the mean of the OECD countries.

The Present Study
This study intended to explore the mediating effects of science attitudes in the relationship between perceived inquiry-based practices and scientific literacy across
eight countries (i.e. China, Japan, Korea, Singapore, USA, Germany, Australia, Finland) in PISA 2015. Figure 1 presents the hypothesized model.
As seen in Fig. 1, student demographic information, including gender and ESCS,
were statistically controlled by incorporating them into the student-level model as
covariates. Similarly, school mean ESCS and school type were also statistically controlled by incorporating them into the school-level model as covariates.
It is hypothesized that perceived inquiry-based practices exert direct and positive effects on science attitudes and scientific literacy, and they exert positive and
indirect effects on scientific literacy via the three attitudinal factors, i.e., interest in
science, science self-efficacy, and instrumental motivation.
Methods
Participants
Data from eight countries were retrieved from the 2015 Programme for International
Student Assessment (PISA) website (https://​www.​oecd.​org/​pisa/​data/​2015d​ataba​
se/): China (Beijing-Shanghai-Jiangsu-Zhejiang, B-S-J-Z) with 9841 students in 268
schools; Japan with 6647 students in 198 schools; Korea with 5581 students in 168
schools; Singapore with 6115 students in 177 schools; the USA with 5712 students
in 176 schools; Germany with 6504 students in 256 schools; Australia with 14,530
students in 758 schools; and Finland with 5882 students in 168 schools.
Measures
Student‑Level Measures
Student-level measures refer to the variables that were recorded at the individual
level. Seven student-level measures were included in this study.
Gender Girls and boys were coded as 0 and 1 respectively.
ESCS Economic, Social, and Cultural Status (ESCS) measures student socioeconomic status in PISA 2015, which was an index score calculated based on the following variables: “parents’ highest occupational status,” “parents’ highest level of
education,” and “home possessions” (OECD, 2017).

Scientific Literacy In PISA 2015, science is a major assessment subject in which
student abilities to “explain phenomena scientifically,” “evaluate and design scientific enquiry,” and “interpret data and evidence scientifically” are evaluated (OECD,
2017). PISA 2015 generated ten plausible values for each student using item
response theory. Following prior studies (e.g. Tang & Zhang, 2020), the mean score
of the ten plausible values was used for student scientific literacy in this study.
Perceived Inquiry‑Based Science Instruction Practices Perceived inquiry-based practices reflect students’ reported frequency of inquiry activities in science class. Nine
items related to specific inquiry activities (e.g. do practical experiments, explain
ideas) were included in this scale (see Appendix A for complete items). For each
item, students responded with “In all lessons,” “In most lessons,” “In some lessons,”
“Never or hardly ever.” Higher scores indicated higher reported inquiry instruction frequencies in class. Based on the nine items, PISA 2015 computed an index
or composite score of perceived inquiry-based practices using item response theory.
To evaluate the reliability of the scale, McDonald’s 𝜔 coefficient was used
instead of Cronbach’s 𝛼 , as 𝜔 takes the correlations between items into account, corrects the bias of 𝛼 , and thus generates more accurate results (Trizano-Hermosilla
& Alvarado, 2016). The McDonald’s 𝜔 coefficient for the scale ranged from 0.963
to 0.978 (> 0.70, Nunnally, 1978) across the countries, which indicated high reliability. According to the results of an Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), the nine
scale items loaded on one latent factor, and the loadings ranged from 0.604 to 0.799.
The nine items together accounted for 52.09% of the total variation, which indicated
acceptable construct validity.
Interest in Science This variable reflects students’ intrinsic motivation to engage in
science-related activities. There were five items in this scale (see Appendix B for
complete items). For each item, students responded with “Strongly disagree,” “Disagree,” “Agree,” and “Strongly agree.” Students with higher scores indicated that
they are more interested in engaging with these science activities. Based on the five
items, PISA 2015 computed an index or composite score of interest in science using
item response theory. The McDonald’s 𝜔 coefficient for the scale ranged from 0.960
to 0.983 (> 0.70, Nunnally, 1978) across the countries, which indicated high reliability. According to the EFA results, the five scale items loaded on one latent factor,
and the loadings ranged from 0.900 to 0.928. The five items together accounted for
84.16% of the total variation, which indicated acceptable construct validity.
Science Self‑Efficacy This variable indicates one’s perceptions of his/her capability
to deal with scientific tasks. In PISA 2015, there were eight items in this scale (see
Appendix B for complete items). For each item, students responded with “I could do
this easily,” “I could do this with a bit of effort,” “I would struggle to do this on my
own,” and “I couldn’t do this.” Reverse coding was adopted for student responses,
and students with higher scores indicated that they have more confidence in successfully accomplishing the tasks. Based on the eight items, PISA 2015 computed an

index or composite score of self-efficacy using item response theory. The McDonald’s 𝜔 coefficient for the scale ranged from 0.947 to 0.969 (> 0.70, Nunnally, 1978)

Instrumental Motivation of Learning Science Instrumental motivation reflects students’ extrinsic motivation to learn science. Four items were included in this scale

School‑Level Measures
School-level measures refer to variables that were recorded at the school level. In
this study, there were two school-level measures.
School Type Schools were dichotomously classified, with 0 = private school and
1 = public school.
School Mean ESCS This variable was to reflect the mean school socioeconomic status, which was calculated by summing up students’ ESCS scores at the school level.
Data Analysis
In this study, data were analyzed in SPSS 28.0 and Mplus 8.3. First, descriptive
statistics were computed for selected variables in SPSS 28.0. To locate significant
mean differences between countries, pairwise comparison tests using Bonferroni
correction were also performed in SPSS 28.0.
In order to explore if science attitudes serve as mediators between perceived
inquiry-based practices and scientific literacy, Pearson’s correlation analyses were
first performed in SPSS 28.0 to establish significant relationships among variables.
Then, a hypothesized mediation model (see Fig. 1) was tested in Mplus 8.3 using
multilevel structural equation modeling (MSEM; student at level-1, and school at
level-2; Muthen & Muthen, 1998–2019). Perceived inquiry-based practices and the
three attitudinal factors (i.e. interest in science, science self-efficacy, and instrumental motivation) were entered into the hypothesized model as latent variables with

respective items introduced in the “Measures” section as indicators. Scientific literacy, gender, ESCS, school mean ESCS, and school type were entered into the model
as observed variables.
Two steps of multilevel structural equation modeling were performed:
1. To examine the validity of the measurement model via confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). In addition to standardized factor loadings, average variance extracted

2. To explore the validity of the structural model via MSEM. Several test statistics
were utilized to assess the model fit, including chi-square test statistic, root mean
squared error of approximation (RMSEA, < 0.10 acceptable fit, < 0.08 good fit),
comparative fit index (CFI, > 0.90 acceptable fit, > 0.95 good fit), and TuckerLewis Index (TLI, > 0.90 acceptable fit, > 0.95 good fit; Hu & Bentler, 1999)
In PISA 2015, schools were first sampled from each specific country, and then
students sampled from each specific school. In order to achieve higher accuracy,
“sampling weight” was considered in the analyses. “999” was utilized to code missing values for the variables used in this study. Pairwise deletion was utilized for
descriptive statistics and correlation analyses. For MSEM analyses, cases with missing values were omitted because they cannot be processed in Mplus 8.3 (Tang &
Zhang, 2020).

Discussion
This study intended to explore if science attitudes act as mediating variables between
perceived inquiry–based science practices and scientific literacy using 15-year-old
student data from eight countries in PISA 2015.
The Direct Effects of Perceived Inquiry–Based Practices on Science Attitudes
and Scientific Literacy
First, it was found that perceived inquiry–based practices showed consistently positive associations with the three attitudinal factors (i.e. interest in science, science
self-efficacy, and instrumental motivation) across the eight countries. In particular,
the relation of perceived inquiry-based practices to self-efficacy was stronger than
that of interest in science and instrumental motivation. The results are consistent
with many previous researches (e.g. McConney et al., 2014).
Furthermore, results indicated that perceived inquiry–based practices were negatively associated with scientific literacy across the countries. The results are consistent with some prior studies (e.g. Liou, 2021), yet contradictory with some others
(e.g. Jiang & McComas, 2015). Why inquiry-based practices do not seem to positively associate with students’ scientific literacy in this study? First, inquiry-based
practices in PISA 2015 reflected the student-perceived frequency instead of the
quality of inquiry activities occurred in science class. According to Kaya and Rice
(2010), “Even though students reported high frequencies of experiencing certain
instructional methods, it does not necessarily equate to how effectively or appropriately the methods were being utilized.” Moreover, previous studies have shown
that it is the cognitive aspect rather than the behavioral or procedural aspect of
inquiry instruction that may support student science learning (Furtak et al., 2012).
The cognitive aspect of inquiry learning emphasizes the processes of making evidence-based conclusions rather than following procedures such as planning investigations and collecting data (Oliver et al., 2019). In inquiry-based learning, students
are expected to be able to associate practical investigative findings to abstract science concepts. However, the nature of inquiry-based practices items in PISA 2015

13

2062

Y. Ma

seemed to be more social (e.g. debate in science class, argue about science questions) and procedural (e.g. design experiments, explain ideas) rather than cognitive. Thus, reported inquiry-based practices were found to not be positively associated with student science learning in this study. Finally, Sweller’s (1988) cognitive
load theory (CLT) suggested that new information is processed in working memory. However, the capacity of one’s working memory is limited, and learning will
be blocked if the memory is overloaded. In CLT, two kinds of cognitive load are
specified, i.e., intrinsic cognitive load and extraneous cognitive load. Specifically,
intrinsic cognitive load indicates the inherent complexity involved in certain tasks
or materials (Sweller et al., 2011). If some materials are in nature more challenging
to learn than others, then they can trigger intrinsic cognitive load. Extraneous cognitive load is the kind of load that depends on the way how the materials are presented
to learners. If materials are presented in a unistructural or unproductive way, then
learners may have difficulty in understanding the materials effectively, and thus, an
extraneous cognitive load is created (Sweller et al., 2011). In this study, it is likely
that the 15-year-old students’ working memory was overloaded intrinsically by the
demanding scientific tasks. More importantly, the minimally guided inquiry instruction may also have caused extraneous cognitive load for students (Hanze & Berger,
2007). The results may suggest that appropriate guidance, cues, or prompts to minimize extraneous load in the process of inquiry are needed.
The Mediating Effects of Science Attitudes in the Relationship Between Perceived
Inquiry–Based Practices and Scientific Literacy
In this study, results supported the mediating effects of the three attitudinal factors
(i.e. interest in science, science self-efficacy, and instrumental motivation) in the
relationship between perceived inquiry–based approaches and scientific literacy. The
indirect effects of perceived inquiry–based practices on scientific literacy via interest
in science and self-efficacy were consistently positive across the countries, yet negative via instrumental motivation with China and Finland as exceptions.
Interest is a vital driving force for science learning, which can intrinsically inspire
students to have persistent scientific pursuits (Tang & Zhang, 2020). By providing students with more chances to explore the world and make their own explanations, inquiry-based practices are likely to stimulate student active learning, and thus
improve their learning outcomes (Hmelo-Silver, 2004).
Self-efficacy is also an important impetus that can inspire individuals to make
efforts (Bandura, 1986). One prominent origin of self-efficacy is mastery experience
(Britner & Pajares, 2006). Students engaged in inquiry-based practices may have
more chances to overcome task obstacles and get the mastery experiences while
making scientific inquiries. Thus, they might show higher levels of self-efficacy and
achieve better scientific literacy, as compared to those who are not taught by inquirybased instruction (Jansen et al., 2015).
Instrumental motivation is extrinsic in nature, which can exert differential influences on student learning depending on the level of autonomy (Ma & Corter,
2019). Instrumental motivation is more likely to be beneficial for student academic

performance if it is with more autonomy (e.g. expression of oneself, sense of importance), while it may not or even negatively affect academic performance if less
autonomy is involved (e.g. incentives, external pressure from parents). In the present
study, a negative mediating effect was found for instrumental motivation, which may
occur due to the fact that the instrumental motivation items in PISA 2015 (e.g. get a
job, improve career prospects) seemed to target less autonomous types of instrumental motivation.
In addition, it was found that the magnitude of the mediating effect of interest in
science was larger than that of self-efficacy and instrumental motivation (except for
Finland), which highlighted the importance of interest in science in this relationship.
This might occur because inquiry-based practices are often novel, interesting, and
attractive. Students like having new experiences, and thus, they can get enjoyment
from the inquiry activities. In addition, interest is at the core of motivation theory,
and greater interest is likely to lead to better science performance.
Implications and Limitations
Theoretically, the current study has explored the mediating effects of science attitudes on the relationship between perceived inquiry–based practices and scientific
literacy, and empirically validated a two-level mediation model using data from eight
countries in PISA 2015. The two-level mediation model may provide deeper insights
into the psychological mechanisms of the impacts of perceived inquiry–based practices in a multi-cultural context. In particular, it may offer a new perspective to
understand the gap between the endorsement of science inquiry instruction and its
negative impacts on scientific literacy as shown in previous research (e.g. Cairns
& Areepattamannil, 2019). That is, although the direct effect of perceived inquirybased practices on scientific literacy tended to be negative, its indirect effects via
science attitudes were positive. Thus, the total negative effect of perceived inquirybased practices was in fact mitigated. Moreover, this study drew upon a large-scale
and representative sample including 60,812 15-year-old students in eight different
countries. Thus, the results may have better ecological validity, as compared to some
prior studies using small-scale samples or laboratory experiments.
In terms of practical implications, first, the results revealed that the direct effects
of perceived inquiry-based practices on science attitudes were consistently positive
across the countries. The results, if true, are promising because promoting student
science attitudes is also an important objective in science education. Given that
students are showing decreasing interest in chasing science-related education and
careers (Osborne et al., 2003), inquiry-based practices should be given more credit.
Furthermore, the negative association between perceived inquiry-based practices
and scientific literacy suggested that educators around the world should not overuse inquiry methods in class. It is the quality rather than the quantity of inquirybased instruction that might determine student learning outcomes. Teachers should
take effective measures to reduce student cognitive load and improve the efficacy of
inquiry instruction, for example, by lowering the task difficulties and offering cues
or prompts to guide student to go toward the right direction.

This study has some limitations. First, following many prior studies (e.g. Oliver
et al., 2019; Tang & Zhang, 2020), this study has taken student self-reported frequencies of inquiry-based practices as a proxy for the actual inquiry-based learning
in class. This might incur some problems. On one hand, the data might suffer from
self-report bias and cause inaccuracies, especially considering asking 15-year-old
students for their general and broad impressions of inquiry-based instruction while
they have experienced a number of science courses over the school years. Second, reported frequencies of inquiry-based practices might not necessarily indicate
its effectiveness in class. It would be desirable to develop some quality measures
(e.g. observation rubrics) in addition to the quantitative measures. Third, similar to
many prior studies (e.g. Tang & Zhang, 2020), eight items related to specific science topics were arguably used to assess student overall self-efficacy in this study.
It would be more precise to utilize some general questions with broad science scenarios. Fourth, instrumental motivation indicators in PISA 2015 (e.g. improve career
prospects) may focus more on the extrinsic motivation with less autonomy. Future
studies could further examine how more autonomous types of extrinsic motivation
(e.g. express oneself) may influence the relationships between inquiry-based practices and scientific literacy. Finally, the model results from this study should be
interpreted as correlations rather than causal effects, as the data from the PISA 2015
assessment was cross-sectional in nature.
