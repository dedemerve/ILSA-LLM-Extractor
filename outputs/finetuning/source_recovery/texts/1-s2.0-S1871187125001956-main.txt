A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Creativity

This study investigates the direct and indirect relationships between creative learning environments, mathematics self-efficacy, and mathematics achievement by using the Programme for
International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022 data sets from Korea, Canada, and Türkiye. The
study sample consists of 6454, 23 073, and 7250 participants from Korea, Canada, and Türkiye,
respectively. The structural equation model was built on the group socialization development
theory and the social cognitive theory. The results for all countries reveal that creative learning
environments positively and significantly affect mathematics self-efficacy. Mathematics selfefficacy, in turn, has a positive and significant effect on mathematics achievement. In each
country, the direct effect of creative peers and family environment on mathematics achievement
is positive, low, and significant, while the direct effect of creative school and class environment is
found to be negative, low, and significant. Furthermore, creative learning environments indirectly
contribute to mathematics achievement through mathematics self-efficacy. Creative learning
environments directly predict mathematics self-efficacy at a moderate level, whereas they indirectly predict mathematics achievement at a low level. Mathematics self-efficacy consistently
emerges as a moderate predictor of mathematics achievement across all three countries, and it
plays a partial mediating role in the relationship between creative learning environments and
mathematics achievement.

1. Introduction
Creativity is widely recognized as a critical 21st-century skill by policymakers, researchers, and educators (Bicer, 2021; Henriksen
et al., 2019; Van Harpen & Sriraman, 2013) and is viewed as a set of competencies that all learners can develop (Kaufman & Beghetto,
2009). It plays a vital role in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics education by preparing students to innovate and
tackle complex global challenges (Neumann, 2007; Sriraman, 2005, 2009; Bicer et al., 2021). Defined as the ability to produce new and
meaningful changes in ideas, products, or behaviors (Beghetto, 2016a; Sternberg, 1999), creativity is closely linked to learning and
academic achievement (Alexander et al., 2009; Greiff et al., 2013). It involves cognitive processes such as deductive and inductive
reasoning and problem-solving strategies (Vartanian et al., 2003; Weisberg, 2006; Beghetto, 2016b; Sternberg, 1998) and is shaped by

both individual and environmental factors (Leikin, 2009; Bicer et al., 2021). Given these connections, it is reasonable to expect an
association between creativity and mathematics achievement. In addition, some other studies have particularly revealed the relationship between the learning environments, such as school and classroom (e.g., Davies et al., 2013; Karwowski, 2022; Sebanc et al.,
2016), family (e.g., Crosnoe & Cooper, 2010; Crosnoe et al., 2010; Moon et al., 2022; Zhao & Zhao, 2022), and peer (Roseth et al.,
2008; Vit et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2023) and academic achievement. This perspective raises the question of whether creative learning
environments might affect students’ mathematics achievement.
In the literature, another factor associated with mathematics achievement is mathematics self-efficacy. The belief in one’s cognitive
ability to learn anything or complete a task successfully indicates self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997). From here, mathematics self-efficacy is
an indicator of one’s positive beliefs about the ability to do mathematical operations, solve mathematical problems, and perform
mathematical tasks successfully (Betz & Hackett, 1993). It affects students’ learning performance, engagement, and achievement in
mathematics (Chang, 2012; Pajares & Miller, 2014). Higher math self-efficacy increases students’ cognitive performance, persistence
in the face of challenges, and willingness to study math. As a result, they have higher mathematics achievement (Watson, 2015).
Educators and policymakers may develop targeted interventions and instructional strategies that increase students’ confidence and
mathematical competency by understanding the factors that affect mathematics self-efficacy and achievement, such as creativity
(Pajares & Miller, 2014).
Taheri et al., 2020 investigated the role of creativity and mathematics self-efficacy in predicting mathematics achievement through
a fuzzy regression model and found a positive correlation between the variables. When considering the effects of both creativity and
mathematics self-efficacy on mathematics achievement, the question of whether there might be a relationship between creativity and
mathematics self-efficacy comes to mind. The four elements stated by Bandura (2007) to increase self-efficacy are as follows: (i)
mastery experiences, (ii) vicarious experiences, (iii) social persuasion, and (iv) emotional states. Thus, it is possible to assume that both
creative school and class environments offering mastery and vicarious experiences and creative peers and family environments
providing social persuasion and emotional states would strengthen students’ self-efficacy in mathematics. Mastery and vicarious
experiences generated by designing and implementing tasks supporting creativity in school and class environments require completing
a series of learning processes, each advancing cognitive skills (Puozzo & Audrin, 2021). Bandura (2007) addresses such situations as
“the most influential source of information about efficacy” (p.125). In parallel, it is possible to argue that creative school and class
environments providing mathematics-specific complex tasks that lead students to produce innovative solutions would be the source of
mathematics self-efficacy. According to the social cognitive theory of Bandura (1986), self-efficacy is organized around three interrelated factors: the environment, the individual, and the individual’s behavior. Therefore, in addition to the creative classroom and
school environment, creative peers and family environments can also be seen as a factor that can affect self-efficacy. Furthermore,
according to Harris (1995) group socialization development theory, two environments—one inside the family (parents, family
members) and one outside the family (peers, school, class)—have an impact on children’s physical and mental development as well as
their academic achievement. On the other hand, the mathematics self-efficacy addressed in this study is mathematics self-efficacy in
mathematical reasoning and 21st-century mathematics, referred to as MATHEF21 in PISA. Mathematics self-efficacy (MATHEF21),
which measures how confident students felt about having to do a range of mathematical reasoning and 21st-century mathematics
tasks, was specially chosen since creativity is a crucial 21st-century skill. The role of mathematics self-efficacy has been investigated in
much research. However, there is little study on mathematics self-efficacy in mathematical reasoning and 21st-century mathematics
because it is a relatively new concept. This study brings new details regarding the interactions among creative learning environments,
mathematics self-efficacy, and mathematics achievement, thereby yielding theoretical and practical implications.
According to Gajda (2016), existing research reports on the relationship between creativity and mathematics achievement are
generally based on small samples and localized results (e.g., Palaniappan & Persekutuan, 2008; Vijetha & Jangaiah, 2010). Therefore,
their generalizability may be limited. Furthermore, they are conducted without controlling for moderators or analyzing the effects of
mediating variables, which may lack reflection of the relations (i.e., Naderi et al., 2009, 2010). Considering these deficiencies, our
study aims to display the relationship between mathematics achievement and creative learning environments, which are “creative
school and class environment” and “creative peers and family environment” and examine the mediating effect of “mathematics
self-efficacy: mathematical reasoning and 21st-century mathematics” through big data from PISA that can be broadly generalized. The
importance of developing students’ creativity throughout their education is becoming more widely acknowledged (Chien & Hui,
2010). However, this particular need is ignored by educational systems and curricula, which concentrate on helping students acquire
more specialized and limited skills (Wilson, 2008). The study of the relationship between creative learning environments and
mathematics achievement, combined with mediating variable, (1) can broaden the existing literature in the field and (2) can
strengthen students’ creativity and mathematics self-efficacy by enhancing educational methods. Research on the creativity and
achievement relationship indicates the potential factors influencing this link, such as the type of measurement (Freund & Holling,
2008; Gralewski & Karwowski, 2012) and the geographic region/culture (Kaufman & Sternberg, 2006; Rudowicz, 2003). Considering
these, PISA is one of the most comprehensive international large-scale assessments that enables the reflection and comparison of
various cognitive and affective features along with the mathematics achievements of students from many countries. It includes a
variety of questionnaires that present latent constructs that cannot be observed directly (e.g., a student’s mathematics self-efficacy,
creativity, or economic, social, and cultural status).
To sum up, although prior studies have explored the individual effects of creativity and mathematics self-efficacy on academic
achievement, much of this research has relied on small, local samples, limiting the generalizability of findings (e.g., Gajda, 2016).
Moreover, existing studies have rarely examined how creativity-enabling environments—specifically school, class, peer, and family
contexts—jointly influence mathematics achievement through the mediating role of mathematics self-efficacy. The construct of
mathematics self-efficacy in mathematical reasoning and 21st-century mathematics (MATHEF21), as defined in PISA, is a relatively

new measure, and research investigating its relationship with creativity or learning environments remains scarce—particularly in the
context of the newly released PISA 2022 dataset, which has yet to be fully explored in this regard. Furthermore, previous research often
overlooks potential cultural and systemic differences across educational contexts. To address these research gaps, this study employs

First, mathematics is a fundamental domain in the PISA framework and a key indicator of students’ preparedness for future academic
and career pathways. Second, prior literature suggests that mathematics achievement is closely tied to 21st-century competencies such
as reasoning, problem-solving, and perseverance (Watson, 2015; Pajares & Miller, 2014). Finally, among the three core PISA
domains—mathematics, reading, and science—mathematics offers the strongest theoretical and empirical justification for examining
the influence of self-efficacy and learning environment, particularly concerning cognitive demands and standardized curricular
structures.
Based on PISA 2022 data sets for Korea, Canada, and Türkiye, our study aims to investigate the direct and indirect relationships
between creative learning environments, mathematics self-efficacy, and mathematics achievement by using a structural equation
model built on the social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1986) and the group socialization development theory (Harris, 1995). This model
examines the direct and indirect effects of creative learning environments (peers, family, school, and class) on mathematics
self-efficacy and mathematics achievement, as well as the mediator effects of mathematics self-efficacy. According to the PISA 2022
results, the mathematics achievement scores of the countries are 527, 497, and 453 for Korea, Canada, and Türkiye, respectively. The
selection of Korea and Canada was based on their constant high performance in large-scale international assessments. Therefore, they
represent countries with high mathematics achievement in the PISA. On the other hand, compared to these countries, Türkiye has a
lower mathematics achievement score and represents an average-achieving country. The differentiation of these countries from a
cultural perspective is another reason for selection, as well as the diversity of their achievement scores. Consistent with the aim of the
study, a structural equation modeling was generated to investigate the structural relationship among these variables and to answer the
following research question:
RQ: How is the interaction among creative school and class environment, creative peers and family environment, mathematics selfefficacy in mathematical reasoning and 21st-century mathematics, and mathematics achievement, according to PISA 2022 data sets
of Korea, Canada, and Türkiye?
2. Theoretical background
2.1. Creativity and mathematics achievement
Creativity is influenced by both environmental and individual factors due to its context dependency (Deng et al., 2016). The
research has revealed that family environment and school education (Fasko, 2001; Wright & Wright, 1986), as well as culture
(Csikszenmihalyi, 1988; Ng, 2003; Sternberg & Lubart, 1995), are crucial for the development of creativity. Research has revealed a
positive relationship between students’ creative thinking and parental participation in the learning process (Liu et al., 2013), as well as
between students’ creativity and parental support and family exposure to media like literature and art (Robinson et al., 2013).
Moreover, a correlation between students’ creativity and parenting styles was found. For instance, compared to other parenting styles,
the authoritarian approach resulted in less creativity and originality in students (e.g., Fearon et al., 2013; Miller et al., 2012).
A social aspect of developing creativity includes peer interaction that provides students’ learning from and with each other in both
formal and informal ways (Boud et al., 2001). The studies stress that the quality of friendships (Sebanc et al., 2016), the gain and loss of
friendships (Lessard & Juvonen, 2018), and peer personalities (Golsteyn et al., 2021) all have an impact on student’s academic
engagement or learning performance (Zhao & Zhao, 2022). Similarly, the studies point out that collaboration, open communication,
and healthy relationships among peers are all essential for fostering creativity (Chen & Agrawal, 2017; Songkram & Chootongchai,
2020). School and class environments, including teachers’ attitudes toward creative activities, their teaching methods, the atmosphere
in the classroom, and the educational system and activities, affect the development of creativity (Deng et al., 2016; Fleith, 2000). The
results of many studies show that the creative climate in schools and classrooms based on cooperation and teacher-student interaction
providing creative learning is crucial to stimulating creative thinking and increasing creativity (Chang et al., 2013; West et al., 2012;
Wu et al., 2014). Thus, it is necessary to take potential peers, family, class, and school factors into consideration to better understand
the effects of creativity in these environments on mathematics achievement.
Mathematics educators and mathematical psychologists have long explored the relationship between creativity and mathematics
achievement, recognizing that both constructs play a critical role in students’ cognitive and academic development. Numerous
empirical studies have demonstrated a statistically significant positive relationship—ranging from moderate to strong—between
creativity and mathematics achievement (e.g., Bahar & Maker, 2011; Ho, 2006; Livne & Milgram, 2006; Mann, 2009; Sternberg,
2007). These findings suggest that students who exhibit creative thinking skills often perform better in mathematics, likely due to their
enhanced problem-solving abilities, cognitive flexibility, and capacity for abstract reasoning. However, as Bicer et al. (2021) note, the
existing literature is highly fragmented. Research varies considerably in terms of definitions of creativity, measurement tools, and
contextual factors, leading to a lack of coherence and generalizability across studies.
Moreover, while a substantial body of work focuses on individual creativity, relatively few studies have examined how creative

learning environments—which refer to the social, pedagogical, and cultural contexts that encourage creative expression—relate to

what students know, but how they can apply knowledge in novel and meaningful ways. By leveraging large-scale international data
such as PISA, this study aims to provide more generalizable insights into the relationship between creative learning environments and
mathematics achievement. In doing so, it shifts the focus from individual traits to contextual supports, offering a valuable perspective
for both researchers and educators interested in designing classrooms that foster creativity and improve learning outcomes in
mathematics.
2.1.1. Creative peers and family environment (CREATFAM)
Ecological (e.g., Bronfenbrenner, 1979) and sociocultural theories (e.g., Vygotsky, 1978; Wong & Hughes, 2006) emphasize the
need to consider the contexts in which children grow and the broader social environment while examining students’ learning and
achievement. Consistently, we conceptualize students’ mathematics achievement as influenced by their peers, families, classes, and
schools and by creativity in the environments that include interactions among these people. Family environments for children include
various experiences, interactions, and actions, such as parents’ support for homework and parents’ expectations for their children’s
academic achievement. A cognitively stimulating family environment predicts students’ academic achievement (e.g., Crosnoe &
Cooper, 2010; Crosnoe et al., 2010; Moon et al., 2022; Zhao & Zhao, 2022). In parallel, a creative family environment may provide
many opportunities for the acquisition of creative and mathematics skills. Peer interaction is also an important factor that enables
students to highly quality questioning, helps with knowledge construction, provides higher-level thinking and learning from other
peers (King et al., 1998), and develops cognitive skills by providing group learning (Anderson et al., 2001; Schwartz et al., 2003). Han
et al. (2013) stress that peers can influence students’ creative problem-solving and thinking abilities and point out that previous
theories and empirical research also indicated the positive effect of peer interaction on students’ academic achievement (e.g., Krnjajić,
2002; Roseth et al., 2008; Vit et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2023). Research results show that creative students can solve real-life problems
and fulfill the requirements to succeed academically (Rubenstein et al., 2020; Sebastian & Huang, 2016).
The results of studies indicate that positive peer relationships support students’ self-efficacy, emotional well-being, and social
competence (e.g., Green et al., 2024; Rubin et al., 2008; Wentzel, 2017). Peer interactions provide social support that promotes
students’ self-efficacy, academic motivation, and good social skills (Wentzel, 2017). Although the relationship between peer interaction and academic achievement has been searched, the role of self-efficacy in this display remains to be investigated (Li et al., 2020).
In particular, considering the significant effect of peers on mathematics achievement (Carman & Zhang, 2012), the role of mathematics
self-efficacy in the relationship between peers and mathematics achievement needs to be explored. Parents and peers are the main
objects for students in their process of individual lives (Zhao & Zhao 2022). Some research on the family environment, peer interaction,
and students’ mathematics achievement addresses the effects of both on students’ mathematics achievement (e.g., Lessard & Juvonen,
2018; Long & Pang, 2016; Sebanc et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2021). In light of the results of the studies in the literature, since the focus of
this study is to examine the effect of creative learning environments on mathematics achievement.
2.1.2. Creative school and class environment (CREATSCH)
Fostering creativity in the school context has been given priority in many countries (Castillo-Vergara et al., 2018) since creativity
provides the advancement of science and technology (Rubenstein et al., 2013). School and class environment play a role in the
development of creativity as well as education (Cropley, 2006) and curriculum (Eisner, 2002). Many factors in a school environment,
such as classroom atmosphere, teachers’ attitudes toward creativity (Anderson et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2021), and classmate relationships (Day et al., 2020), are also related to supporting creativity. For example, it is stated that positive classmate relationships
foster creativity by promoting collaborative knowledge sharing and working creatively in teams (Guo et al., 2020; Songkram and
Chootongchai, 2020), while negative ones hinder creativity by decreasing motivation and communication (Lavasani & Khandan,
2011) and negatively affect academic achievement (Sebanc et al., 2016). According to Karwowski (2022), creativity-relevant mental
processes support learning, indicating that schools fostering creativity can positively affect mathematics achievement. Similarly, a
systematic literature review, including 210 educational research, stresses the positive impact of creative learning environments on
students’ academic achievement (Davies et al., 2013).
Many studies have revealed that school and classroom environments are associated with both self-efficacy and mathematics
achievement (Danielsen et al., 2010; Eshel & Kohavi, 2003). Fast et al. (2010) addressed that students with perceptions about their
mathematics classrooms as challenging and mastery-oriented have high mathematics self-efficacy. Furthermore, Tosto et al. (2016)
investigated the relationships between classroom environment and mathematics achievement through mediators, including mathematics self-efficacy, maths interest, and academic self-concept. The researchers found that mathematics achievement was predicted by
environmental factors and mathematics self-efficacy partially mediated the relationship between classroom environment and mathematics achievement. Thus, it is believed that creativity in the school and classroom environment will affect their mathematics
self-efficacy in mathematical reasoning and 21st-century mathematics which in turn will affect mathematics achievement.
2.2. Mathematics self-efficacy (MATHEF21) and mathematics achievement
Mathematics self-efficacy, in which under this title, refers to a person’s belief in their capabilities in mathematical reasoning and
21st-century mathematics. Mathematics efficacy is an important concept strongly related to mathematics achievement (e.g., Hoffman
& Schraw, 2009; Liu et al., 2024; Pietsch et al., 2003; Yang et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024). From the perspective of cognitive theory, this

relation is bidirectional: mathematics achievement can boost one’s mathematics self-efficacy, which can then lead to better
achievement in mathematics (Marsh & Craven, 2006; Williams & Williams, 2010). Although there are many studies examining the
relationship between mathematics self-efficacy and mathematics achievement, it is remarkable that there are few studies that address
MATHEF21, and these studies associate this concept with different variables such as mathematics literacy (Zeng, 2024; Huang et al.,
2024), financial literacy (Ilgaz, 2024). When these studies dealing with MATHEF21 are examined, it is seen that this concept has
recently started to be studied, and there is a need for further research. Furthermore, considering creativity as one of the important
21st-century skills, it is also thought that mathematics self-efficacy in mathematical reasoning and 21st-century mathematics will be a
meaningful mediator for examining the relationship between creative learning environments and mathematics achievement.
2.3. The education systems in Korea, Canada, and Türkiye
In the central education system of Korea, students start free and compulsory education at the age of 6 and follow a national
curriculum (Sang et al., 2016). Korea has an education system including 6 years of elementary school, 3 years of middle school, 3 years
of high school, and 4 years of college or university (Kim, 2008). The Korean education system is standardized with the state controlling
the curriculum, recruitment, teacher training, and instructional speed (Park et al., 2011). Its national curriculum is periodically
updated to meet the new demands of society, which is evolving quickly (Geesa et al., 2019). The national mathematics curriculum aims
at the acquisition of key skills such as self-management competency, creative thinking skills, aesthetic-emotional competency, and
communication skills (Korean Ministry of Education, 2017). Graduating from the best educational institutions in Korea can have a
great effect on one’s career success and influence social, political, and economic conditions (Lee, 2006). Families resort to extra ways in
education, such as out-of-school tutoring, private cram schools, and online courses, since this situation creates a competitive examination system in the transition to higher education in Korea (Choi & Cho, 2016; Kim & Park, 2010).
The education system in Canada is characterized by its decentralized structure, with each province and territory responsible for its
own curriculum and policies. This system emphasizes inclusivity, equity, and high academic standards, as noted by OECD reports,
which consistently rank Canada among the top-performing countries in reading, mathematics, and science (OECD, 2020). Education
that is under provincial/territorial responsibility in Canada leads to significant diversity in the structure and organization of
elementary and secondary school systems across regions. These differences reflect the unique culture, history, and geography of each
province or territory. Despite this diversity, some general features can be identified: most children attend kindergarten at age four or
five voluntarily, and all are required to start compulsory elementary schooling in Grade 1 at age six. Secondary education extends to
Grade 11 or 12, depending on the province. Each province or territory’s ministry of education oversees schooling through local boards
or districts. While most students attend public schools, a small number are home-schooled or attend private schools, both of which
must adhere to provincial/territorial guidelines (Volante & Ben Jaafar, 2008). Canada has a comprehensive school system for
elementary (grades 1–6) and secondary (grades 7–12) education, with no academic selection between school levels. However, high
school math and English are divided into “university-level” and “applied” tracks. Although students receive a single high school
diploma, university admission may depend on the number and type of university-level courses taken. Post-secondary education in
Canada includes universities, colleges, and vocational programs, which are offered concurrently (and not consecutively) with flexible
learning paths. The Canadian educational system is primarily grounded in academic principles, with vocational principles playing a
minimal role (Imdorf et al., 2015). The Canadian mathematics curriculum focuses mainly on cognitive skills but also on valuing affective abilities. Along with goals like problem-solving, mathematical reasoning, communications, and mental mathematics, which
reflect the student’s internalization and application of knowledge, it also includes affective goals that emphasize understanding the
societal role of mathematics and fostering a positive attitude toward the subject (Serçe & Acar, 2021).
In the central education system in Türkiye, students begin their 12-year, free, and compulsory schooling at age 6. The Turkish
educational system is structured around 4-year periods: 4 years for elementary school, 4 years for middle school, and 4 years for high
school (Ministry of National Education [MoNE], 2013). Students take university entrance exams to begin their higher education after
graduating from high school. The government controls curriculum, recruitment, teacher training, and instructional speed (Mullis et al.,
2012). The main objectives of the national mathematics curriculum are the development of students’ foundational mathematical skills,
as well as their ability to solve problems, reason, use mathematical terms and models, and use instructional tools, information, and
communication technologies (MoNE, 2024). In Turkish society, having a university graduation is crucial for gaining respect, landing a
decent career, and earning a high income. The Turkish educational system’s high-stakes exams push competitiveness among students
and lead parents to provide children with private tutoring (e.g., cram school or a private instructor at home) to raise their accomplishment scores (Topçu et al., 2016). The socioeconomic status of the family affects the achievement of children due to the opportunities they can provide (Alacacı & Erbaş, 2010; Berberoğlu et al., 2003).
In terms of family expectations, meaning attributed to attending university, and additional support for achievement, Korea and
Türkiye are similar. Canada, on the other hand, does not have a competitive examination environment in transition to university like
these countries and does not need additional paths to support success. The cultural and mathematical achievement disparities between
these countries will enhance the research results and the recommendations to be presented for education in this context.

achievement (MATHACH) through mathematics self-efficacy (MATHEF21).
3. Method
Structural equation modeling was used to examine the interactions among creative learning environments (peers, family, school,
and class), mathematics self-efficacy, and mathematics achievement for students from Korea, Canada, and Türkiye based on PISA 2022
data.
3.1. Participants
PISA is a triennial survey of 15-year-old students around the world that assesses the extent to which they have acquired key
knowledge and skills essential for full participation in social and economic life. PISA assessments do not just ascertain whether students
near the end of their compulsory education can reproduce what they have learned; they also examine how well students can
extrapolate from what they have learned and apply their knowledge in unfamiliar settings, both in and outside of school. In 2022, 81
countries participated in PISA. The main subject in 2022 was mathematics, as it was in 2012 and 2003. In PISA 2022, where the
average mathematics score is 472, Korea ranks third with 527 points, Canada sixth with 497 points, and Türkiye 36th with 453 points
(OECD, 2023). Sample characteristics for each country are shown in Table 1.
3.2. Measures
3.2.1. Creative peers and family environment (CREATFAM)
Students’ ratings of their agreement with statements about the degree to which creative thinking is fostered and supported by their
peers and family environment (e.g., “My friends are open to new ideas.”, “At home, I am encouraged to use my imagination.”) in
question ST336 were scaled into the index of “Creative peers and family environment”. Each of the six items included in this scale had
four response options (“Strongly disagree”, “Disagree”, “Agree”, “Strongly agree”) (OECD, 2024, p.404).
3.2.2. Creative school and class environment (CREATSCH)
Students’ ratings of their agreement with statements about the degree to which creative thinking is fostered and supported in their
school and class environment (e.g., “My teachers value students’ creativity.”, “At school, I am given a chance to express my ideas.”) in
question ST335 were scaled into the index of “Creative school and class environment”. Each of the six items included in this scale had
four response options (“Strongly disagree”, “Disagree”, “Agree”, “Strongly agree”) (OECD, 2024, p.404).

Students’ ratings of how confident they felt about having to do a range of mathematical reasoning and 21st century mathematics

3.3. Statistical analyses
Data analysis was carried out in four steps: preliminary analyses, measurement model analyses, Multiple-Group CFA, and SEM
analyses. Data cleaning and assumption checks were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 24), while the structural equation
modeling was conducted through AMOS. In preliminary analyses, the entire dataset was employed. To address the issue of missing
data, we employed two widely used approaches: regression-based imputation and Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML)
estimation. The comparison between these methods was conducted to ensure the robustness and stability of model results across three
countries: Korea, Canada, and Türkiye. Mardia’s kurtosis coefficient and the critical ratio values were examined for multivariate
normality. After the preliminary analyses, configural invariance, metric invariance, scalar invariance, and residual invariance were
examined sequentially using the Multiple-Group CFA (MG-CFA) method (Meredith, 1993). The chi-square difference test is not
practical for studies with large sample sizes, as increasing the sample size raises the likelihood of obtaining a statistically significant pvalue (Kline, 2023). Cheung and Rensvold (2002) emphasized that, in large samples, the difference between fit indices should be
examined and stated that a change of 0.010 or less in the CFI value serves as evidence against rejecting the constrained invariance
hypothesis. Similarly, Chen (2007) suggested that the simultaneous use of ΔCFI ≤ 0.010 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 criteria may yield more
accurate results in certain cases. In this study, both ΔCFI and ΔRMSEA were considered together in the analysis.
Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) and SEM analyses were performed using AMOS (Version 24). The goodness-of-fit for both
measurement and structural models was evaluated based on several fit indices, including the chi-square (χ²) value, degrees of freedom,
CFI, TLI, IFI, NFI, RFI, RMSEA, and SRMR (Kline, 2023). Acceptable and good model fit thresholds were set at 0.90 for CFI, TLI, IFI,
NFI, and RFI; 0.08 for RMSEA; and 0.05 for SRMR (Hair et al., 2017; Hu & Bentler, 1999). However, because the chi-square value is
highly sensitive to sample size, it tends to be inflated in larger samples, making it a less reliable measure of model fit in such cases
(Kline, 2023). Research involving large sample sizes has reported high χ²/df ratios, highlighting the importance of considering
alternative fit indices (Marsh et al., 2018; Ustun, 2024). Specifically, the use of CFI, RMSEA, and TLI is strongly recommended for
assessing model fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999).
To assess construct validity, both convergent and discriminant validity were examined. Convergent validity was evaluated by
ensuring that all standardized factor loadings exceeded 0.5 (Hair et al., 2017). The threshold values were taken as 0.7 for CR and 0.5 for
AVE (Hair et al., 2017). Discriminant validity was assessed using both the Fornell-Larcker Criterion (Fornell & Larcker, 1981) and the
heterotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT) approach (Henseler et al., 2015). According to the Fornell-Larcker Criterion, the square root of
AVE for each construct should be higher than its correlations with other constructs (Hair et al., 2017). HTMT values were also checked,
ensuring they did not exceed the recommended thresholds of 0.85 or 0.90 (Raza, Umer & Shah, 2017). Internal consistency was
evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, with a minimum acceptable threshold of 0.70 (Raza, Qazi, & Yousufi, 2021).
To address potential violations of multivariate normality, bias-corrected bootstrapping was applied, generating 95 % confidence
intervals (Stevens, 2009; Westfall & Henning, 2013). These intervals refer to the standardized beta coefficients. The analysis involved
resampling the dataset 5000 times with replacement to assess the significance of mediation effects. Mediation was considered significant if the bias-corrected confidence interval for the indirect effect did not include zero (Hayes, 2013).
To determine the type and presence of mediation effects in the model, we followed the decision-making framework proposed by
Zhao et al. (2010), which provides a more refined classification of mediation beyond the traditional Baron and Kenny (1986) approach.
This framework evaluates the significance of the indirect effect (i.e., the product of path coefficients p₁ × p₂) and the direct effect (p₃) to
categorize mediation into one of five distinct types. Specifically, the mediation type was determined as follows: If the indirect effect (p₁
× p₂) was significant and the direct effect (p₃) was also significant, we then assessed whether p₁ × p₂ + p₃ was positive or negative. A
positive product indicated complementary partial mediation, while a negative product indicated competitive partial mediation. If only
7

Thinking Skills and Creativity 58 (2025) 101946

S.Ö. Bütüner et al.

the indirect effect (p₁ × p₂) was significant and the direct effect (p₃) was not, the result was classified as indirect-only mediation (full
mediation). If the direct effect (p₃) was significant but the indirect effect was not, it was considered direct-only (no mediation). When
neither the indirect nor the direct effect was significant, this indicated no effect (no mediation). This analytical procedure allowed us to
clearly distinguish between full mediation, partial mediation (complementary or competitive), and non-mediation scenarios based on
the statistical relationships among the involved variables. Furthermore, direct and indirect relationships among variables were
examined in alignment with the study’s hypotheses. Effect sizes were assessed using standardized coefficients (beta weights). Acock
(2014) claims that the magnitude of beta weights can be interpreted similarly to correlations.
Common method bias (CMB) reflects the extent to which the observed relationships between constructs are distorted due to
common method variance (CMV), which can inflate correlations beyond their true values. As a result, CMV poses a significant threat to
the validity and reliability of research findings (Spector et al., 2019). In the current study, CMV was assessed using Harman’s
single-factor test and the correlation matrix procedure (Damanik & Widodo, 2025; Widodo et al., 2022). To examine the potential
impact of common method bias (CMB), both Harman’s single-factor test and the correlation matrix approach were employed for the
datasets from Korea, Canada, and Türkiye. Results indicated that the variance explained by a single factor was below the recommended
threshold of 50 % for all three countries. Additionally, inter-construct correlations (e.g., between academic achievement and
self-efficacy) remained well below the critical value of 0.90, further supporting the absence of CMB. The results confirm that the
dataset is not subject to common method variance (bias), ensuring the validity and reliability of the observed relationships among
constructs.

5. Discussion
This study examined the direct and indirect relationships between creative learning environments, mathematics self-efficacy, and
mathematics achievement using PISA 2022 data sets of 36,777 students from Korea, Canada, and Türkiye and tested the model built on
group socialization development theory and social cognitive theory. The structural equation model was found to fit the data very well
for all countries. The results show that, for all countries, (1) the direct effect of creative learning environments on mathematics-selfefficacy is positive, and significant, (2) the direct effect of mathematics-self-efficacy on mathematics achievement is positive and
significant, (3) the direct effect of creative peers and family environment on mathematics achievement is positive, low, and significant,
while the direct effect of creative school and class environment on mathematics achievement is found to be negative, low, and significant, and (4) the indirect effect of creative learning environments is positive and significant on mathematics achievement through
mathematics self-efficacy. Creative learning environments directly predict mathematics self-efficacy at a moderate level, whereas they
indirectly predict mathematics achievement at a low level. Mathematics self-efficacy is consistently the moderate predictor of
mathematics achievement for all three countries. Besides, mathematics self-efficacy has a partial mediating effect for each country.
5.1. Structural relationships
Creative peers and family environment were found to have a direct effect on mathematics self-efficacy (H1). Ecological (e.g.,
Bronfenbrenner, 1979) and sociocultural theories (e.g., Vygotsky, 1978; Wong & Hughes, 2006) also emphasize the positive impact of
the family and social environment in which the child grows on self-efficacy, learning, and achievement. Similarly, our results show that
creative learning environments, which promote the development of creative skills such as creative thinking, creative problem-solving,
and creative learning in children and young people (Davies et al., 2013), increase children’s mathematics self-efficacy. Usher (2009)
asserts that parents and peers are two important sources on which children frequently rely for evaluative feedback, judgments, and
appraisals. Tang et al. (2022) argue that highly creative parents provide more flexible and communicative environments for their
children. Miller et al. (2012) drew attention to the negative effects of authoritarian and perfectionist families on students and stated
that creative families are more permissive, and this contributes to the child both cognitively and psychologically. Besides, parenting
styles and students’ creativity were found to be associated. For example, students with authoritarian parents are less creative and
original students (e.g., Fearon et al., 2013; Miller et al., 2012). According to research, there is a positive correlation between students’
creative thinking and parental involvement in their education (Liu et al., 2013), as well as between students’ creativity and parental
support and exposure to creative activities in the family (Robinson et al., 2013). Self-efficacy, which is the belief in one’s ability to
reach specific performance levels, is linked to creativity (Markman et al., 2002; Prabhu et al., 2008). Various studies have indicated
that the social environment of the students (e.g., parents, peers) not only directly influences self-efficacy (Kontaş & Özcan, 2022) but
also indirectly affects it through creative self-efficacy (e.g., Ma et al., 2024). Thus, a creative family environment increases creative
self-efficacy, that is, confidence in one’s own creativity (Meinel et al., 2019) and helps children’s beliefs in their capabilities in
mathematical reasoning and 21st-century mathematics, which refers to MATHEF21. Considering the relationship of creativity as an
important 21st century skill with mathematical reasoning and the influence of parents, who are the main educators that shape children’s learning on their mathematics self-efficacy (Wang et al., 2023), it is an expected result that the creative family environment has
a significant and positive effect on mathematics self-efficacy, even if it has a moderate effect. On the other hand, the results of studies
reveal that peer interactions enable social support to foster students’ self-efficacy, emotional well-being, social competence (Rubin
et al., 2008; Wentzel, 2017), academic motivation, and social skills (Wentzel, 2017). Green et al. (2024) highlighted that creative peers
affect the self-efficacy beliefs of their mates. In other words, engaging in collaborative learning with creative peers has the potential to
enhance students’ mathematics self-efficacy. Indeed, working together on creative projects can increase students’ confidence in their
abilities by allowing them to share ideas and receive constructive feedback.
Creative peers and family environment, on the other hand, showed an indirect effect on mathematics achievement through
mathematics self-efficacy (H6). To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have integrated both creative peers and family
environment and mathematics self-efficacy into a model to examine whether their intertwined relationships predict mathematics
achievement. As reported above, creative peers (e.g., Green et al., 2024) and family environment (e.g., Kontaş & Özcan, 2022) have a
significant effect on students’ self-efficacy, as highlighted by other scholars. The existence of the indirect effect of creative peers and
family environment on mathematics achievement seems to support the presumption that this environment may influence students’
cognitive outcomes through affective or social support (Tomul et al., 2021). According to Lee (2024), a creative home environment
significantly influences students’ creative achievement intentions. In another study, Ma et al. (2024) found that parental support,
while not directly affecting student creativity, positively impacts it through mediators like creative self-efficacy, particularly in
younger students. Similarly, Gu et al. (2009) highlighted that social preference fully mediates the relationship between social

creativity and academic achievement, suggesting that children with creative peers may experience enhanced academic outcomes.
Accordingly, one’s positive beliefs in the ability to carry out mathematical operations, solve mathematics problems, and do mathematical activities successfully are reflected in one’s mathematics self-efficacy (Betz & Hackett, 1993). Thus, the creative peer and
family environment influences children’s general, creative, and mathematics self-efficacy, which increases students’ engagement,
learning performance, and mathematics achievement (Chang, 2012; Pajares & Miller, 2014). The more children with a high sense of
mathematics self-efficacy, the more they will come up with creative solutions, persist in the face of challenges, and be willing to study
math. Thus, they have higher mathematics achievement (Watson, 2015). On the other hand, Beghetto (2016a) defines learning as the
outcome of a creative process, pointing to the theoretical connection between creativity and academic achievement. Both deductive
and inductive reasoning are components of creativity (Vartanian, Martindale & Kwiatkowski, 2003; Weisberg, 2006), as is the capacity
to use certain problem-solving techniques to provide original solutions to problems (Beghetto, 2016b; Sternberg, 1998). The results of
studies point out a significant positive relationship between creativity and achievement in mathematics (e.g., Bahar & Maker, 2011;
Bicer et al., 2020; Livne & Milgram, 2006; Mann, 2009; Sternberg, 2007) and the effect of both the family environment and peer
interaction on students’ mathematics achievement (e.g., Lessard & Juvonen, 2018; Long & Pang, 2016; Sebanc et al., 2016; Wang
et al., 2021). From this point, we can also suggest that creative peers and family environment affect mathematics achievement by
strengthening creativity. Our research results reveal that the creative peer and family environment is an important factor affecting
directly mathematics self-efficacy and indirectly mathematics achievement. Therefore, a family environment in which parents and
family members develop children’s creative skills and increase their self-efficacy in the process of learning mathematics will help raise
individuals who are successful in mathematics.
This paper also reveal that creative school and class environments have a direct effect on mathematics self-efficacy (H2). A large
body of literature reported both school and class environments affect student mathematics self-efficacy (e.g., Claiborne, 2001; Dangur-Levy et al., 2023; Fast et al., 2010). For instance, Claiborne (2001) reported empirical connections between students’ classroom
learning environments and the strength of their self-efficacy beliefs in eighth-grade mathematics. Similarly, Yin et al. (2020) explored
the relationship between mathematics self-efficacy and the school and class environment, highlighting that a positive classroom atmosphere can enhance students’ self-efficacy beliefs. According to the social cognitive theory of Bandura (2007), an efficient source of
self-efficacy is the mastery and vicarious experiences generated by designing and carrying out creative tasks in classroom and school
environments. Likewise, it is likely to contend that mathematics self-efficacy develops through creative learning environments in
schools and classrooms that offer challenging assignments unique to mathematics and encourage students to come up with creative
solutions. In parallel, Fast et al. (2010) indicate that students who believe their mathematics classes are challenging and focused on
mastery have high levels of mathematics self-efficacy. The results obtained from the present study also confirm these findings. Students
will be equipped to lead the country in innovation and handle the complex problems of the 21st century if they have educational
experiences that strengthen their creativity in the classroom and encourage them to apply their knowledge in new ways (Bicer, 2021,
2021). Therefore, a teacher needs to provide a creative learning environment in a class where students can enhance their creativity. In
addition, the role of creative school culture, which has been emphasized before, was revealed in this study.
As another result, creative school and class environments were found to have an indirect effect on mathematics achievement
through mathematics self-efficacy (H7). According to the group socialization development theory of Harris (1995), children’s academic achievement and mental development are influenced by two environments: one outside the family (peers, school, class) and one
inside the family (parents, family members). As mentioned, it is also known that these environments have effects on mathematics
self-efficacy. A substantial body of literature has established consistent results regarding students’ academic achievement or
self-efficacy achievement (Alt, 2015; Danielsen et al., 2010; Eshel & Kohavi, 2003; Gilman et al., 2024), which has been affected by
learning environments in class and school. Some other researchers explained this complex relationship through moderators. For
instance, Tosto et al. (2016) examined the relationships between classroom environment and mathematics achievement through
mediators, including mathematics self-efficacy, and found that mathematics achievement was predicted by environmental factors and
mathematics self-efficacy partially mediated the relationship between classroom environment and mathematics achievement. In
another study, Afari and Eksail (2022) focused on learning environments in terms of involvement components and concluded that
self-efficacy could play a mediating role in the relationship between student involvement and mathematics achievement. It can thus be
expected that generating a creative learning environment in the classroom and school that boosts students’ mathematics self-efficacy
may improve their mathematics achievement. On the other hand, it is known that creative school and class environments stimulate
creative thinking and increase creativity (Chang, Hsu & Chen, 2013; West et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2014). For instance, while negative
relationships with classmates hinder creativity by lowering motivation and communication (Lavasani & Khandan, 2011) and so
negatively affect academic achievement (Sebanc et al., 2016), positive relationships are reported to enhance creativity by encouraging
collaborative knowledge sharing and creative teamwork (Gou et al., 2020; Songkram and Chootongchai, 2020). A systematic literature
review by Davies et al. (2013) also stresses the positive impact of creative learning environments on students’ academic achievement
(Davies et al., 2013). Considering these results and the relation of creativity with achievement in mathematics (e.g., Bahar & Maker,
2011; Bicer et al., 2020; Livne & Milgram, 2006; Mann, 2009; Sternberg, 2007), we may also infer that a creative school and class
environment influence mathematics achievement by fostering creativity. Our research results reveal that the creative school and class
environments are important factors affecting directly mathematics self-efficacy and indirectly mathematics achievement. Creative
learning environments are crucial for developing well-rounded students who are engaged, motivated, and prepared for the challenges
of the 21st century. By fostering collaboration, innovation, and social-emotional growth, these environments enhance both academic
achievement and personal development. Thus, policymakers, educators, and teachers should consider the importance of creative
learning environments (peers, family, class, and school) in designing and shaping educational contents and processes.
Mathematics self-efficacy has been found to have a direct effect on mathematics achievement (H3). Numerous studies have

demonstrated that mathematics self-efficacy is significantly related to students’ mathematics achievement (e.g., Hoffman & Schraw,
2009; Liu et al., 2024; Pietsch et al., 2003; Yang et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024). For instance, in their large-scale study considering NAEP
results, Yang et al. (2024) demonstrated the relationship between mathematics self-efficacy and mathematics achievement and
highlighted how enhancing students’ self-efficacy in mathematics is crucial in reducing achievement disparities. Similarly, Liu et al.
(2024) reported a reciprocal relationship between mathematics self-efficacy and student mathematics achievement among high school
students. Zhu et al. (2024) also found that mathematics self-efficacy positively predicts mathematics achievement. Therefore, practices
that will increase students’ mathematics self-efficacy should be taken into consideration by teachers. On the other hand, despite
numerous research, few of them have addressed mathematics self-efficacy: mathematical reasoning and 21st-century mathematics
(MATHEF21), and these studies associate this concept with different variables, such as mathematics literacy (Zeng, 2024; Huang et al.,
2024) and financial literacy (Ilgaz, 2024). Our research not only supports previous studies demonstrating a relationship between
mathematics self-efficacy and mathematics achievement, but it also reveals a significant relationship between MATHEF21 and
mathematics achievement. Based on the results of the present study on the effects of creative learning environments, teachers, educators, and policymakers can provide a creation of learning environments that consider account the factors that influence mathematics
self-efficacy and achievement, such as creativity, which is one of the important 21st-century skills, and generate targeted interventions
and instructional strategies that boost students’ confidence and mathematical competency (Pajares & Miller, 2014).
For each country, the direct effect of creative peers and family environment on mathematics achievement is positive, low, and
significant (H4), while the direct effect of creative school and class environment on mathematics achievement is found to be negative,
low, and significant (H5). The results regarding the direct effect of creative peers and family environment on mathematics achievement
in Korea, Canada, and Türkiye align with existing research emphasizing the role of social and familial contexts in shaping academic
outcomes. Creative peers play a critical role in enhancing collaborative problem-solving skills and fostering motivation, while a
supportive family environment contributes essential resources and active engagement in mathematics. Thus, both peers and family
environment contribute to mathematics learning and academic achievement (e.g., Lessard & Juvonen, 2018; Long & Pang, 2016;
Sebanc et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2021). However, the negative and significant effect of the creative school and classroom environment
on mathematics achievement in these countries raises the possibility that the design or nature of creative educational practices in the
classroom may not be in line with the specific needs of students in math. Creative teaching methods may not necessarily immediately
improve mathematics achievement unless they are properly adapted to the subject matter, even while they help develop critical
thinking and general cognitive skills. The negative effect may stem from a potential disconnect between creative teaching methods and
the structured, assessment-focused nature of mathematics education. While creativity promotes innovation and critical thinking, it
may fall short in developing the foundational mathematical skills required for standardized assessments. Although it is designed to
foster creativity, the school environment, which also encompasses various factors such as class size, teaching strategies, and peer
dynamics, may not always support diverse learning styles and individual needs in mathematics. These situations could explain why the
creative school and classroom environment has been demonstrated to exert a negative effect on mathematics achievement, particularly
in countries such as Korea, Canada, and Türkiye, where there is an emphasis on academic standards and structured learning to
mathematics instruction. Given the unexpected negative relationship between creative school and class environments and mathematics achievement, it is essential for educators to align creativity-enhancing practices with subject-specific goals. Teachers can design
mathematically rich tasks that invite creative exploration without sacrificing conceptual rigor. Empirical studies suggest that integrating structured scaffolding within open-ended tasks (e.g., Kwon et al., 2006) helps balance creativity and content mastery.
Furthermore, professional development programs for teachers should emphasize how to create cognitively demanding yet creative
tasks tailored to mathematics (Boaler, 2016). Schools may also benefit from fostering a school culture where creativity is encouraged
not as a separate pedagogical aim, but as an embedded part of problem-solving and reasoning in mathematics instruction. Involving
parents and peer groups in out-of-school creative mathematics activities (e.g., math clubs or design challenges) can further reinforce
self-efficacy and achievement. By applying these targeted strategies, educators can better harness the benefits of creative environments
while mitigating unintended outcomes in mathematics learning.
As a result, it is known that there are many factors affecting students’ mathematics achievement. Our study focused on only a few of
them and found direct or indirect effects of the components it focused on. When the models are analyzed in terms of variances, the
results obtained indicate that other important variables are also effective in achievement. The results demonstrate that the variation in
mathematics self-efficacy accounted for by creative learning environments is close (11 % to 17 %) across all countries. On the other
hand, the variation in mathematics achievement accounted for by creative learning environments together with mathematics selfefficacy varies across countries. The results show that the effect of these variables together on mathematics achievement is highest
in Korea (26 %), followed by Canada (22 %), and lowest in Türkiye (10 %). Our study also reveals that mathematics self-efficacy
partially mediates the relationship between creative learning environments and mathematics achievement for each country. The
educational system in countries like Korea may place a higher priority on challenging, structured learning environments that include
creativity in instruction, promoting both self-efficacy and mathematical achievement. Meanwhile, countries like Türkiye might have
more traditional or rote learning environments that do not foster creativity and self-efficacy as much, resulting in a lower overall effect
of these variables. The study’s findings reflect how educational environments, self-efficacy, and national contexts interact to influence
student outcomes. Understanding these nuances is crucial for tailoring educational strategies that promote both creative learning and
self-efficacy, ultimately leading to improved mathematics achievement in diverse contexts.
Recent studies have continued to emphasize the importance of creative learning environments and mathematics self-efficacy in
shaping students’ academic outcomes. For instance, it has been reported that creative environments positively influence student
achievement (e.g., Lessard & Juvonen, 2018; Wang et al., 2021), while Puozzo and Audrin (2021)) proposed a framework linking
creativity, well-being, and learning environments. Additionally, Liu et al. (2024) and Zhu et al. (2024) provided empirical evidence

5.2. Cultural aspects
Among the countries compared, the effects of creative peers and family environment on students’ mathematics self-efficacy were
found to be moderate in Korea, Canada, and Türkiye. According to Bandura (2002), while beliefs about efficacy have a broadly
functional value, their development, organization, application, and intended purposes may differ across cultures. A person’s culture
and the experiences it offers influence their belief systems, values, and identities, leading to variations in how self-efficacy beliefs
operate across cultures (Klassen, 2004). Culturally, Korea, Canada, and Türkiye share a strong emphasis on academic achievement and
the value of family support in education. In Korea and Türkiye, education is deeply valued and often reinforced by family, while in
Canada, education is seen as a key to social mobility, with families playing a significant role in supporting academic achievement. All
three countries feature educational systems that integrate formal instruction with peer and family influence. Creative peers encourage
collaboration and motivation, while supportive family environments enhance students’ belief in their mathematical abilities.
According to Bandura (1995), the more students trust in their knowledge and skills, the more effort and persistence they
demonstrate in completing tasks, including highly complex ones. Because self-efficacy is an intra-individual factor (Puozzo & Audrin,
2021), it is not only shaped by personal experiences but also physiological and emotional states (Usher & Morris, 2023). In other
words, self-efficacy may be influenced by many external factors such as teacher support, parental involvement, peer support, school or
classroom environment (Laka & Suryanto, 2024) as well as internal processes such as motivation, behavior, and emotional responses.
This result can be interpreted as creative school and classroom environments alone having a limited effect on self-efficacy, regardless of
the country, and affective factors should support such environments.
On the other hand, the effect of mathematics self-efficacy on mathematics achievement has been found to have a significant but
moderate effect. The relationship between mathematics self-efficacy and mathematics achievement is a highly controversial topic in
educational research. For instance, although various studies demonstrated a linear relationship between these two phenomena, students’ mathematics self-efficacy may also change over time (Liu et al., 2024). According to Mangos and Steele-Johnson (2001), as the
difficulty of mathematics increases, the consistent relationships between mathematics self-efficacy and mathematics achievement
deteriorate over time. The present study shows that this may be the case regardless of the culture. When PISA age group students are
considered, it is thought that the abstractions made in mathematics increase, and this, as claimed, differentiates the relationship
between students’ mathematics self-efficacy and mathematics achievement.
The positive and significant effect of a creative peer and family environment on mathematics achievement suggests that these
variables have high potential to contribute to students’ mathematics learning. However, the relatively low impact indicates the need
for greater involvement of peers and parents in the learning process. While these countries value education, the role of creative peers
and family support may not be as strongly emphasized in the context of mathematics achievement. In many educational systems,
including those in Korea, Canada, and Türkiye, there tends to be a stronger focus on traditional teaching methods and structured
learning, particularly in subjects like mathematics, where precision and logical thinking are prioritized over creativity. Moreover, the
educational systems in these countries may place greater emphasis on teacher-led instruction and formal assessments, potentially
limiting the direct impact of creative peers and family environments on students’ mathematics achievement and leading to the
observed low effects across these countries.
Cultural and contextual factors may further clarify the negative and significant effects of the creative school and classroom
environment on mathematics achievement. Countries like Korea and Türkiye, the focus on high-stakes testing often favors traditional,
structured learning over creative methods, reducing the benefits of creative school environments for mathematics achievement
(Leung, 2001). Similarly, in Canada, varying educational policies and cultural attitudes toward creativity affect how creative teaching
is implemented and its impact on student outcomes. These highlight the importance of aligning creative teaching strategies with both
the demands of mathematics education and the cultural expectations of each country.
6. Limitations and future directions
While interpreting the results of the study, some certain limitations should be considered. First, because the paper adopts a crosssectional design, the results present associational rather than causal relationships. Although the hypothesized assumptions relied on
robust theories, longitudinal designs should be used to provide further empirical evidence for causal relationships, as recommended in
the literature (e.g., Ustun, 2024). Second, the data of this study is restricted to Korea, Canada, and Türkiye. Analyses of data from
different countries can be included to make generalizations more obvious, even though some countries in the study share similar
cultural characteristics. Lastly, since the variables that affect mathematics achievement are limited in the current study, analyses that
include other variables can help us to identify which other variables affect mathematics achievement.
