ABSTRACT

International large-scale assessments (ILSAs) like TIMSS, PIRLS, and
PISA use planned missingness completely at random (MCAR) but suffer from non-response bias with missing data not at random (MNAR),
especially in home questionnaires where parental omissions may correlate with item content, compromising cross-country comparisons.
This study evaluated whether PIRLS 2021’s Home Socioeconomic
Status (HSES) could be reliably imputed using Multivariate Imputation
by Chained Equations (MICE) based on student and school data. Six
methods—Predictive Mean Matching (PMM), Weighted PMM, CART,
Random Forests, Bayesian Linear Regression, and Lasso—were evaluated on 5,500 students with 10–60% simulated missingness.
Imputation accuracy declined from 36% explained variance (correlation ~0.6) at 10% missingness to 16% (correlation <0.4) at 60% missingness. PMM performed best but exhibited shrinkage, overestimating
low HSES and underestimating high HSES. Results underscore the
critical need to address MNAR in ILSAs to ensure equitable and reliable international educational comparisons.

Introduction
Socioeconomic status (SES) and related constructs, such as home resources for learning,
have consistently been identified as critical predictors of reading achievement in assessments like PIRLS (Marôco, 2021; Mullis et al., 2017) and PISA (Michael & Kyriakides,
2023; Schleicher, 2014) in multiple countries. Therefore, accurate SES measures are vital
for explaining variations in student achievement within countries and enhancing the
validity and reliability of reading (as well as mathematics and science) achievement
obtained from complex test designs with planned missingness. Regrettably, low-stakes
tests and studies, like PIRLS or PISA, often experience missing responses, particularly
in the supplementary questionnaires probing contextual factors and learning attitudes.
This missingness can stem from the noncompliance of participating countries or variations in participants’ motivation and willingness to answer questions.

Prevailing analytical practice tends to downplay the issue of unplanned missing
data. A review by van Buuren (2012) outlined common approaches to address unplanned
missing data, ranging from straightforward deletion of observations with missing data
to multiple imputation methods that can lead to varying degrees of misleading conclusions. Naturally, the most effective way to avoid the effects of missing data is to
prevent their occurrence altogether (Orchard & Woodbury, 1972). Hence, research
project coordinators for large-scale, low-stakes assessments should strive to minimize
unplanned missing responses since no imputation method can guarantee the estimation
of “true” responses and their derived measures.
There are various possibilities for dealing with missing data, such as pairwise and
listwise deletion of cases, or imputation of missing responses. Imputation may use
mean, median, linear regression, maximum likelihood, and Bayesian methods. Initially
championed by Donald Rubin, multiple imputation methods have become the standard
in statistical treatment of missing data (Rubin, 1996). Initially, due to the limitations
in computational power, the recommended number of imputations was small, with
typically only five imputations being recommended three to four decades ago. However,
with the advent of ubiquitous computing power, many authors now recommend and
use 20 or more imputations (Bodner, 2008; Graham et al., 2007), as smaller numbers
of imputations lead to larger confidence intervals and lower degrees of freedom (Rubin
& Schenker, 1986; von Davier, 2025). A common rule of thumb suggests performing
as many imputations per missing variable as the percentage of missing data across the
database (e.g. Gorst-Rasmussen, 2021; Royston, 2004) This helps ensure more precise
and reliable statistical estimates by utilizing today’s readily available increased computing capabilities.
Common ILSAs such as PIRLS, TIMSS, and PISA rely on simpler approaches such
as dummy coding, or mean substitution, that do not fully model uncertainty in missing
data (Avvisati, 2020; Bezirhan, Foy, & von Davier, 2023).
In this study, we employed Fully Conditional specified models for imputation of
missing HSES data to assess whether unobserved HSES in PIRLS 2021 could be imputed
with acceptable reliability and efficiency. There are various methods for conducting
imputation within conditionally specified models. One specific example is outlined in
Algorithm 4.3 of van Buuren (2012, P. 120), which is the MICE algorithm introduced
by Van Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn in 2000 and later refined (van Buuren &
Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). This algorithm begins by selecting a random set of values
from the observed data and fills in the missing data one variable at a time, once at
a time. In the next step, a conditional variable distribution uses all the imputed data
on the remaining variables. The algorithm then draws values from the empirical condition distribution of the following variable to be imputed. Each cycle goes through
all variables and constitutes one iteration of the MICE algorithm. The MICE algorithm
generates multiple imputations by running this algorithm concurrently m times in
parallel until convergence. The MICE algorithm operates as a Gibbs sampler, which
is a Bayesian simulation technique that draws samples from conditional distributions
to construct samples from the overall joint distribution, and, in the case of the MICE
algorithm, the user only directly controls the conditional distributions, meaning that
the joint distribution is only indirectly understood and may not necessarily exist. While
this might raise concerns from a theoretical standpoint, as we lack knowledge of the

specific joint distribution to which the algorithm converges, in practice, this does not
appear to impede the algorithm’s usefulness in various applications (van Buuren, 2012).
Thus, for this study, we defined three research questions (RQ):
RQ1: Can missing HSES be estimated from complete data records from student and school
questionnaires?
RQ2: Can missing HSES be estimated from student and school data with random missing
patterns?
RQ3: How efficient is the MICE algorithm found to be the best in RQ1 and RQ2 in reproducing missing data in HSES from the PIRLS 2021 countries and territories that took part
in the 2021 PIRLS assessment?

These research questions are crucial for advancing methodological rigor in large-scale
educational assessments. RQ1 explores whether socioeconomic status indicators, which
are often incomplete, can be reliably recovered from existing information sources,
addressing a long-standing issue in educational data quality. RQ2 extends this inquiry
by evaluating the robustness of imputation models under simulated missingness, thereby
assessing their generalizability to less ideal, real-world datasets. RQ3 evaluates how
well the best-performing MICE approach transfers across diverse national contexts
within PIRLS 2021, providing evidence on the external validity and scalability of the
imputation methods.
By answering these research questions, our study contributes to both methodological
and applied domains. It systematically compares multiple advanced MICE-based methods under simulated and empirical conditions, offering guidance for researchers and
policymakers on how to manage missing socioeconomic data more effectively. In doing
so, it clarifies the performance tradeoffs and practical usability of different imputation
techniques, particularly in high-missingness scenarios and datasets with non-normal
distributions, which are common in international educational surveys.

Methods
Data and data preparation
All the data employed in the simulations can be accessed through the TIMSS and
PIRLS International Study Center at Boston College (available at https://pirls2021.org/
data/). The Home Socioeconomic Status (HSES) is an IRT-derived scale, generated
from parents’ responses to four ordinal items, encompassing: 1) the number of books
at home; 2) the highest educational level attained by either parent; 3) the highest
occupational level of either parent; and 4) the number of children’s books at home
(Yin & Reynolds, 2023), which are commonly used variables to define SES (Avvisati,
2020). In the analysis, Rasch scores for reading achievement and all items from the
PIRLS Student questionnaire, excluding Gender and Age, were included. In this analysis, we used only Rasch scores rather than PIRLS estimates of reading achievement
in the form of plausible values (PVs), which are five imputations of the latent reading
proficiency. PVs cannot be used to impute SES measures, as PVs require context data,
and their inclusion may lead to circularity that cannot be resolved. Additionally, all
items from the schools’ questionnaires were incorporated into the imputation process

(for a comprehensive description of the student and school questionnaires, refer to
https://pirls2021.org/questionnaires).
For the MICE simulations, 141 context items and each student’s Rasch score estimates of reading literacy were used as predictors of HSES. We randomly selected a
sample of 5,500 students with complete data on all variables from the extensive pool
of 367,575 students in the PIRLS 2021 database. This sample size was chosen to replicate the recommended sample size for countries participating in International
Large-Scale Assessments. To answer RQ1, missing data completely at random was
introduced in the HSES variable, keeping the complete data from student and school
questionnaires. However, Home, student, and school questionnaires in PIRLS 2021
exhibited varying degrees of unplanned missing data, and thus, the complete information scenario described in RQ1 is unrealistic. Thus, for RQ2, we seeded missing
data completely at random on HSES and students and schools’ variables and assessed
different MICE algorithms in recovering the seeded missing on HSES only. However,
unlike simulations, the pattern of unplanned missing data in PIRLS 2021 exhibited
significant variability between and within countries, making it unclear whether it was
missing completely at random, missing at random, or not missing at random, and
potentially correlated with parental SES and educational qualifications. For RQ3, we
leveraged information from students’ sampling weights to borrow missing data patterns
and simulated missingness for HSES data using the observed patterns of missing and
observed variables from the student and school questionnaires. RQ3 can be considered
the case where the pattern of missing responses is modeled after the real PIRLS field
data and is not necessarily missing completely at random.
Multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE)
In all our simulations, we utilized the MICE 3.0 package (van Buuren &
Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011) and the miceadds package (Robitzsch & Grund, 2023)
within the R 4.1.2 Statistical System (R Core Team, 2017).
The MICE multivariate imputation model is defined separately for each variable,
employing a set of conditional density functions, with one function assigned to each
incomplete variable. Imputations are generated iteratively, drawing from these conditional density regressions, starting from an initial imputation. Typically, a modest
number of iterations, such as 5-10 or at maximum 20-40, are sufficient to achieve
estimation convergence, and following past recommendations (Van Buuren &
Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011), we use only five imputations for the initial comparisons
of multiple models to obtain estimates of means and variances of imputed values with
20 iterations (Mera-Gaona et al., 2021; van Buuren, 2012). However, once a successful
model and imputation method have been found, higher numbers of imputations should
be considered to limit the effect of small sets of imputations on confidence intervals
and degrees of freedom.
The MICE 3.0 package offers various methods for data imputation. The following,
each producing five imputations, were employed (for a detailed description of these
algorithms, see van Buuren, 2012): These methods were selected to reflect diverse
theoretical properties—PMM and weighted PMM preserve distributional shape, CART
and RF handle non-linearities, and Bayesian and Lasso regression offer stabilization

Predictive Mean Matching (pmm): This semi-parametric imputation method preserves the observed data distribution more effectively than fully parametric approaches.
The algorithm first fits a linear regression model on the observed (non-missing) values of the variable to be imputed, using the available predictors. It then calculates
predicted values for both observed and missing cases. For each missing value, the
algorithm identifies a small set of observed cases (in our simulations, the five nearest
neighbors by default) whose predicted values are closest to the missing case. One
donor is randomly selected from this set, and the corresponding observed value is
imputed. This method ensures that only plausible values from the observed data are
used, avoiding unrealistic imputations.

Weighted Predictive Mean Matching (midastouch): This method extends standard
predictive mean matching by assigning weights to potential donor cases based on
their similarity to the case with missing data. The closer the predicted value of
a donor to that of the recipient, the higher its probability of being selected.
Unlike pmm, which uses a fixed number of nearest neighbors, midastouch considers all observed cases as potential donors, weighted by a kernel function of
the distance between donor and recipient. This makes it theoretically more efficient and flexible, but computationally more intensive, particularly in large datasets or complex models with many predictors. The algorithm may converge
slowly in complex problems with large sample sizes, such as those in this study.
Both PMM and weighted PMM offer robustness to non-normality while maintaining realistic imputed values.
Classification and Regression Trees (cart): This algorithm uses decision tree algorithms to predict missing values by recursively splitting the data into homogeneous subgroups based on predictor variables. A regression tree is fitted using
the complete cases. Then, for each case with missing data, the algorithm determines the terminal node (leaf) to which it would be assigned based on its predictor values. An observed value from another case in that same terminal node
is then randomly drawn and used as the imputation. This non-parametric
approach captures complex nonlinear relationships and interactions among predictors but can be sensitive to overfitting if not finely tuned.
Random Forest (rf): This algorithm builds on the idea of CART but uses an
ensemble of trees to improve stability and predictive accuracy. It fits multiple
regression trees using bootstrap samples of the complete cases and aggregates
the predictions (mean for regression) to generate the expected value. For each
missing value, the algorithm predicts a set of values using these trees and
identifies donor observations whose predicted values are closest to the missing
one (like pmm). One of these donors is then randomly selected, and its
observed value is used to impute the missing value. This approach benefits
from the robustness and flexibility of random forests, especially when managing high-dimensional or nonlinear data. Both CART and Random Forests can
model complex, non-linear relationships and interactions (Shah et al., 2014).
Bayesian Linear Regression (norm): This method assumes a standard linear
regression model for the imputation but incorporates uncertainty by adopting a

Bayesian framework. Specifically, it uses non-informative priors for the regression
coefficients and error variance. At each iteration, regression parameters are sampled from their posterior distribution, and missing values are then imputed by
drawing from the posterior predictive distribution. This approach propagates
model uncertainty into the imputed values, resulting in more valid statistical
inferences after imputation.
Lasso Regression (lasso): This algorithm estimates regression parameters using
the LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator), which applies L1
regularization to perform variable selection and shrinkage. The model is fitted on
multiple bootstrap samples of the dataset containing missing values. Each bootstrap yields a set of regression coefficients, treated as draws from the posterior
distribution. These coefficients are then used with the original (non-bootstrap)
data to draw imputed values from the corresponding predictive distribution. This
method is particularly effective in high-dimensional settings where traditional
regression models may overfit or fail to converge. Both Bayesian Linear Regression
and Lasso provide parametric imputation approaches, with Lasso offering variable
shrinkage, useful in high-dimensional data (Bartlett et al., 2015).
Student Weighted Predictive Mean Matching (stdwpmm): This algorithm, implemented in the miceadds package, is a weighted variant of pmm that integrates
complex survey design into the imputation process. Each student is weighted
according to the PIRLS 2021 Senate Weights when simulating the full international dataset (5,500 students), or by their total student weight when imputing at
the country level. These weights influence both the model-fitting and
donor-selection stages, ensuring that the imputed data respects the original sampling design and yields unbiased estimates in weighted analyses. This method is
particularly suited for large-scale educational assessments with complex stratified
sampling designs. The rationale for choosing stdwpmm is grounded in methodological research highlighting the risks of ignoring survey weights in multiple
imputation, particularly in stratified or clustered sampling designs common to
studies like PIRLS and PISA (Foy et al., 2012). Studies have shown that unweighted
imputations can lead to biased results and underestimated variances, particularly
when the probability of missingness correlates with design variables (Kim, 2001;
Madley-Dowd et al. 2019). Therefore, stdwpmm is particularly suited for international educational assessments where preserving the representativeness and inferential validity of imputed data is critical.

Simulations were conducted using the described MICE 3.0 and miceadds algorithms,
introducing 10% to 60% missing completely at random (MCAR) in the HSES for RQ1,
for HSES, and students and schools’ variables for RQ2 simulations. For RQ3 with country
data, we retained the naturally occurring missing data in student and school questionnaires
and introduced additional MCAR missingness (10% to 60%) in the HSES variable only.
The selection of variables for MICE imputation was done using the “quickpred”
function from MICE 3.0. This function selects the predictors for the MICE models
based on a pairwise threshold correlation of 0.1 between the predictor and the criterion.
Estimation efficiency, defined as the degree of agreement between the original HSES
and the imputed HSES generated by the MICE algorithm, was assessed using Pearson

sets. High correlations indicate strong agreement and accurate imputation, while low
or no correlation suggests invalid estimations. This reflects the algorithm’s ability to

participating countries and territories that utilized the home questionnaire in the PIRLS
2021 edition. Meanwhile, Figure 2 displays the percentage of missing HSES data for each

ranged from 0.5% (Bulgaria) to 67% (Lithuania). The average percentage of missing data
across these countries was 17% (Median = 9%, Standard Deviation = 18%). A multiple
unweighted OLS regression using all 141 predictor variables and complete-case data
yielded an overall correlation of 0.68 with HSES. This indicates that the predictors collectively explained approximately 46% of the variance in HSES, setting the baseline for
our imputations.

Research question 3: MICE imputation for countries and territories

decrease as the MCAR percentage increased, as observed in the case of the complete

Discussion
Missing data is a common feature of large educational datasets, especially in international large-scale student assessments. These assessments intentionally include planned
missingness in their test designs to reduce the testing burden and keep the time on
task appropriate for the assessed age groups. However, unplanned missing data is also
prevalent and occurs in varying frequencies within context questionnaires administered
to students, parents, teachers, and schools. These context variables, such as home
socioeconomic status, have been proven to be important predictors of students’ achievement, particularly in reading literacy (Jerrim & Micklewright, 2014; Marôco, 2021;
Michael & Kyriakides, 2023; Mullis et al., 2017; Schleicher, 2014).
In this research paper, we employed the MICE 3.0 package (van Buuren &
Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011) and the miceadds package (Robitzsch & Grund, 2023)
within the R 4.1.2 Statistical System (R Core Team, 2022) to generate imputations
under the Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) mechanism. We applied seven
different algorithms that are available in these packages. Simulations were conducted

International Journal of Testing

49

on randomly selected samples of 5500 students with complete records for student and
school questionnaires and their reading literacy Rasch scores. Unlike ILSAs, which
commonly use simpler dummy coding or mean replacements to treat missing data,
our use of MICE enables richer, non-parametric modeling of missingness.
At 10% MCAR percentages, all methods except random forests produced correlations
between the original and imputed HSES scale scores in the 0.55-0.60 range. Considering
that the multiple correlation (R) between the HSES and the 141 predictors in the complete dataset was 0.68, (indicating that these predictors explain about 46% of the variance
in HSES) an observed Pearson correlation of ~0.6 between imputed and true HSES values
(explaining around 36% of variance) represents a substantial recovery of the underlying
structure given the uncertainty introduced by imputation. This level of recovery is consistent with what other simulation studies have found to be acceptable or even strong
under similar MCAR conditions (e.g., Mera-Gaona et al., 2021; Shin et al., 2017). As the
MCAR percentage increased from 10% to 60%, the original and imputed HSES correlations were stable for the complete students’ and schools’ data (RQ1). However, for the
scenario with randomly seeded students and schools’ data (RQ2), the correlations decreased
to around 0.45 for most algorithms as the percentage of seed MCAR data increased to
60%. The exception was random forests with a 0.32(SE = 0.008) correlation. This trend
aligns with both theoretical simulations (Little & Rubin, 2002) and empirical findings in
MICE simulations elsewhere, such as with cancer data (Mera-Gaona et al., 2021), where
increasing MCAR percentages led to decreased reliability in imputing missing data. Taken
together, these results show that higher levels of missingness reduce the amount of
information available, making it more difficult to estimate the missing values accurately.
Additionally, all algorithms exhibited shrinkage, overestimating missing values at
lower HSES levels and underestimating them at higher HSES levels compared to the
observed HSES. Post-imputation calibration methods such as residual adjustment (Slade
& Naylor, 2020), or hybrid models like Random Forest donor selection (Shah et al.,
2014), may mitigate these biases.
We also conducted simulations for MCAR imputations on complete HSES data at
the country level, using real country student and school questionnaires missingness
patterns (RQ3). This approach retained the naturally occurring missing data in student
and school questionnaires, and we, thereafter, introduced additional MCAR missingness
(10% to 60%) in the HSES variable only, applying the pmm and stdwpmm algorithms
to recover the missing HSES data. Both algorithms struggled to predict imputed missing
values accurately for countries with asymmetrical, non-Gaussian-like HSES distributions
and high missingness in student and school questionnaires, such as Sweden, Morocco,
or Montenegro. In contrast, imputations displayed higher quality (with correlations
between 0.6 and 0.7) for countries like the Slovak Republic, Dubai, or Canada’s British
Columbia, where HSES exhibited a more symmetrical and Gaussian shape with a lower
percentage of missing data in students and schools’ questionnaires.
Weighted imputation using students’ sampling weights improved the correlation
between original and imputed HSES for all countries and participants. The pattern of
decreasing correlation with an increasing percentage of MCAR observed in the RQ2
simulations with the complete dataset of 5500 students, was now evident in the stdwpmm method heat map, contrary to what was observed in the pmm method. The
PIRLS student total weights consider not only the probability of selecting each student

in the target population but also the students who did not participate in the test. This
may lead to a statistical association that students with larger weights being those that
can be expected to produce more complete data and data of higher quality, which is
reflected in the imputations. Furthermore, the observed range of correlations (from
<0.2 to ~0.7) reflects both varying levels of initial missingness and the non-normality
and skewness in the HSES distributions across countries.
In this study, we show that the MICE framework offers flexibility by accounting
for different types of variables (ordinal, categorical, continuous), supporting non-linear
relationships (via CART and random forests), and generating multiple plausible datasets
that reflect estimation uncertainty (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). This
MICE flexibility may improve the missing estimation—either by design or non-planned—
current strategies used in ILSA.

Conclusions and practical implications
Missing data is a persistent challenge in large educational datasets, particularly in
international student assessments. This study highlights that the reliability of imputations declines as the percentage of missing data increases. Most algorithms performed
adequately for low levels of missingness (up to 10%), but their reliability diminished
as missingness rose to 60%, with random forests being notably less effective. These
findings underscore the importance of minimizing data loss in assessments to maintain
the quality of analyses and ensure meaningful imputations.
The study also reveals that the distributional characteristics of the data heavily
influence imputation accuracy. Countries with asymmetrical or non-Gaussian HSES
distributions, such as Sweden, Morocco, and Montenegro, faced challenges in achieving
accurate imputations. In contrast, participants with more symmetrical, Gaussian-like
distributions, such as the Slovak Republic and Dubai, displayed better results. This
underscores the need for researchers to carefully evaluate the statistical properties of
their data before selecting and applying imputation methods.
One important finding is the role of weighted imputations in enhancing the quality
of imputations. Using students’ total sampling weights improved correlations between
original and imputed HSES across all countries. This highlights the importance of
integrating weight-based methods when dealing with uneven distributions or important
levels of missing data.
Finally, predictive mean matching emerged as an effective and efficient method for
inputting missing HSES values, particularly for datasets with low to moderate missingness and symmetrical distributions. However, researchers should prioritize adjustments, such as weighted imputation, when dealing with higher missingness rates or
asymmetrical data. These findings emphasize the need for careful planning, data
preparation, and method selection in large-scale assessments to ensure robust, reliable
insights and minimize biases in subsequent analyses.
This study contributes to the ILSA literature by systematically comparing several
advanced MICE-based imputation methods under both simulated and real-world conditions. It highlights the performance tradeoffs and the practical applicability of each
approach, particularly in contexts with elevated levels of missing data and skewed
variable distributions. Our simulations suggest that weighted predictive mean matching
can improve ILSA missing data imputation.

Limitations
This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, while the MICE
algorithms demonstrated reasonable performance under MCAR conditions, these simulations may not fully capture the complex missing data mechanisms present in PIRLS
2021, which include Missing at Random (MAR) or Not Missing at Random (NMAR)
patterns. For instance, missing data may occur more frequently in specific countries,
schools, or among children with certain family characteristics, such as those from
low-income families. If non-MCAR mechanisms are plausible, MICE estimates may be
biased, leading to inaccuracies in imputed values and, consequently, in the correlation
estimates (Little and Rubin, 2002). This heterogeneity in missing data patterns is evident in Simulation 3, where performance with full-country datasets was poor for several
countries. Future work will extend simulations to include MAR and MNAR conditions
using logistic models, as recommended by Collins et al. (2001), to better approximate
realistic nonresponse mechanisms. Other post-imputation calibration can be used. For
example, using regression residuals as well as hybrid models combining pmm with
random forest donor selection (see e.g. Razzak & Heumann, 2019) or pre-processing,
like z-scores transformation, of target data to address distributional asymmetry and
outliers (e.g. Zhao et al. 2023) may overcome some of the above-stated limitations.
Secondly, the imputations were more accurate in countries with symmetrical distributions of Home Socioeconomic Status (HSES) and lower rates of missing data. The
MICE approach assumes that partially observed variables are multivariate normal, functioning as a Gibbs sampler under these conditions. However, this assumption is not
guaranteed when multivariate normality is absent (Bartlett et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2014).
Deviations from multivariate normality may limit the generalizability of the results,
particularly in contexts with important levels of missingness or skewed distributions.
Additionally, the study identified systematic biases in imputations, with overestimation at lower HSES levels and underestimation at higher levels. These biases could
impact the interpretation of SES-related findings. Finally, HSES exhibits variability
across three levels—within schools, between schools, and between countries. Despite
including student sampling weights in some scenarios, the variation in performance
across countries and methods underscores the challenges of achieving robust imputations in diverse international contexts.
