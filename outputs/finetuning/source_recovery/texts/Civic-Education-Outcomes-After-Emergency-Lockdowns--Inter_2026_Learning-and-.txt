ABSTRACT
Background: In early 2020, nations around the globe responded to the rapidly developing COVID-19 pandemic
by implementing emergency containment measures to slow the spread of the virus. Educational systems were
widely impacted as they made the difficult decision to close schools affecting over 1.5 billion students globally.
Aims: This study aims to characterize the relationship between COVID-19 lockdown stringency and changes
in several secondary student civic education outcomes.
Sample: We use data on more than 100,000 grade 8 students from 15 educational systems that participated
across multiple cycles of the International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS).
Methods: We utilize multiple regression models with country- and cycle-fixed effects to estimate the relationship between the duration and stringency of COVID-19 containment measures and changes in grade 8 student
civic education outcomes.
Results: We find that longer school closures and greater lockdown stringency were associated with greater
declines in average civic knowledge and trust in civic institutions. They were also associated with increases (or
smaller decreases) in intentions to protest or expectations to participate in politics. Less consistent evidence
was found regarding the relationship with expected participation in elections.
Conclusions: The findings provide valuable insights into the associations between lockdown measures and
early secondary student civic education outcomes, highly relevant for understanding how the pandemic and
related policies may have influenced the future citizens of the world.

1. Introduction
On January 30, 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC)
in response to the rapidly and internationally spreading coronavirus
(WHO, 2020). Many countries responded to the crisis by implementing
emergency lockdown measures to slow the spread of the virus. Examples of such measures included business closures, public event cancellations, restrictions on gatherings or travel, and even stay-at-home
orders (Hale et al., 2021, 2023). While decisions to implement such
measures highlighted the benefits of such policies in battling against a
global pandemic, it was clear that they were weighed against the potential costs to daily life and well-being of citizens. Most sectors were impacted by lockdown measures, especially education where educational
systems were forced to make the difficult decision to close schools,
disrupting learning for over 1.5 billion students globally (OECD, 2021).

Recent research has focused on the impact of COVID-19 on student
academic performance, including meta-analyses incorporating results
from national studies (e.g., Betthäuser et al., 2023; Di Pietro, 2023)
as well as studies using results from international large-scale assessments (e.g., Jakubowski et al., 2023, 2024; Kennedy & Strietholt,
2023). A consistent finding of these studies is that there has been
a significant decline in student academic performance following the
onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. These studies have mainly focused
on cognitive outcomes and, within these, on the core domains of
mathematics and reading. However, there are other important learning
outcomes that might have been affected by the pandemic.
Considering concerns of a shift in the democratic attitudes of the
population amidst the COVID-19 pandemic and the implemented measures (Bol et al., 2020), as well as the potential exacerbation of issues
like increasing inequalities, political polarization, and violent extremism, we consider it crucial to examine whether and how the civic

2.2. COVID-19 lockdown stringency, democratic attitudes, and civic engagement
While COVID-19 containment policies were highlighted as battling
against a global pandemic, they also had other unintended consequences. One notable side-effect was a worldwide increase in demonstration activities and protests (Kishi, 2021). Generally, these protests
focused on governments’ handling of the pandemic, although the specific reasons varied. Some protesters called for safer working conditions
for health workers, for greater economic support to cope with the
restrictions, or for stronger government responses to the pandemic.
In contrast, others rallied against pandemic measures, demanding the
reopening of businesses and schools or opposing vaccine mandates.
Research suggests that the likelihood of such protests varied based
on local socioeconomic and political factors. For example, in the United
States, anti-restriction protests were more likely to occur in counties
with higher levels of socioeconomic inequality and more stringent measures in place (Iacoella et al., 2021). A similar situation was observed
in Germany, where protests against containment policies were more
frequent in areas where coronavirus incidence rates were low and when
containment measures were relatively stringent (Plümper et al., 2021).
In addition to protest activity, the pandemic also influenced democratic attitudes, with some studies reporting increased demands for
more authoritarian government responses (Amat et al., 2020) and
rising distrust in institutions (Amat et al., 2020; Lello et al., 2022;
Raffini & Penalva-Verdú, 2022). For example, Raffini and PenalvaVerdú (2022) describe how protests against vaccine mandates in Italy
brought together diverse groups, yet a common factor among them was
institutional distrust. This distrust extended beyond political institutions to include health institutions, the pharmaceutical industry, and
both mainstream science and media (Lello et al., 2022).
Nevertheless, not all studies indicate a decline in institutional
trust. Bol et al. (2020) found that trust in government actually increased, although this was measured in the early stages of the pandemic. Kritzinger et al. (2021) also observed a temporary rise in
government trust in Austria, and further explain this is consistent with
the ‘‘rally-round-the-flag’’ effect, which often occurs during international crises. However, they found no such effect in France, highlighting
potential cross-national differences in how institutional trust evolves
during crises.
Given this evidence, it remains unclear whether the pandemic’s
effects on democratic attitudes, protest activity, and institutional trust
will have long-term consequences, whether similar trends have emerged
in other countries, and whether adolescents have been similarly affected.

2. Background
In response to the COVID-19 pandemic, governments around the
world implemented various measures to slow the spread of the virus.
These measures included school and business closures, travel bans,
public event cancellations, restrictions on gatherings, mask mandates,
vaccine and/or testing requirements, contact tracing, and stay-at-home
orders (Hale et al., 2021, 2023). Although these actions were intended
to protect public health, they also presented significant challenges to
education and democratic systems. The enforcement of strict social
confinement measures often required the use of exceptional powers,
which often came at the expense of civil liberties (Amat et al., 2020;
Besand, 2020; Bol et al., 2020). Together, these factors raise concerns
about the potential impact of COVID-19 on democratic attitudes (Bol
et al., 2020).
In this section, we examine the relevant literature across three
main topics: (1) the impact of school closures on student learning,
(2) civic engagement and democratic attitudes during the pandemic,
and (3) civic education and civic outcomes. We then explore potential
mechanisms connecting COVID-19 lockdown policies to civic education
outcomes before presenting the aims of our study.
2.1. School closures and student learning
In this study, we focus on school closures resulting from policymakers’ decisions to suspend or shut down in-person classes and activities
in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, often replacing them with some
form of remote learning. The primary aim of these closures was to
slow the transmission of the virus and safeguard the health of students,
teachers, staff, and the broader community. However, while school
closures were seen as a necessary public health measure, they also
raised concerns about their impact on student learning and well-being.
A considerable amount of public and academic discussion has focused on the potential repercussions of school closures on the education
and lives of children and their families (e.g., see DiPietro et al., 2020;
Meinck et al., 2022, for discussion). As empirical evidence continues
to emerge, a growing body of research aims to quantify the impact
of the COVID-19 pandemic on academic learning. This research often
refers to stalled student learning due to school closures as ‘‘learning
loss’’ or ‘‘learning deficit’’. It compares either the performance level or
gains of students, or student cohorts, before and after the onset of the
pandemic to assess this deficit. Recently published meta-analyses and
systematic reviews have synthesized the findings of this research. The
recent meta-analyses by Betthäuser et al. (2023) and Di Pietro (2023),
which draw on over 45 individual studies from 18 countries, reveal
a significant decline in academic achievement following the outbreak
of the pandemic. Both analyses report similar mean effect sizes, with
Cohen’s d values of −0.14 and −0.17, respectively. Notably, both metaanalyses primarily focus on mathematics and reading, with limited
attention given to subjects like social sciences, history, and politics.
Only three studies in Di Pietro (2023) cover these areas, while the
studies in Betthäuser et al. (2023) exclusively examine mathematics
and reading outcomes.

2.3. Civic education and civic outcomes
Beyond developing cognitive skills, education contributes to a wide
range of social outcomes that are essential not only for individual
growth but also for the economy and society as a whole (Dijkstra,
2014). One key area in this regard is civic education, which is defined
as the process of preparing young people to become active, responsible, and informed citizens in a democracy (Besand, 2020; European
Commission/EACEA/Eurydice, 2017; Schulz et al., 2018a).
The implementation of civic education varies across countries. Some
education systems teach it as a standalone subject, while others integrate it within related subjects (e.g., social studies, history) or as

a cross-curricular learning area (Schulz et al., 2018a, 2023a). These
differences may reflect varying degrees of emphasis on civic education
across countries.1 Despite these differences, civic education remains a
key policy focus, particularly in light of contemporary challenges such
as growing socioeconomic inequalities, rising political polarization,
the rise of violent extremism, declining trust in democratic processes
and institutions, and the spread of misinformation (European Commission/EACEA/Eurydice, 2017; Savage et al., 2023; Winthrop, 2020).
Although these issues existed before the pandemic, they may have been
exacerbated by the containment measures.
Beyond its formal instruction, civic education extends into various
aspects of students’ daily lives, as they constantly encounter civic
learning opportunities through interactions in the classroom, at school
(e.g., through extracurricular activities), at home, and with friends
or family. From a human development perspective, Bronfenbrenner’s
(2004) ecological systems theory provides a valuable framework for
understanding civic education, emphasizing how an individual’s development is shaped by interactions across multiple environments.
Bronfenbrenner’s model conceptualizes these environments as a series
of nested systems, each influencing development in different ways. At
the core is the individual — in this case, the student — surrounded
first by the microsystem, which consists of the student’s immediate
environments, such as family, school, and peer groups. These immediate environments interact within the mesosystem (e.g., communication
between a student’s parents and teachers). The exosystem includes
the relationships between social settings where at least one does not
directly include the student (e.g., the relationship between a parent’s workplace and the home environment). At the outer layers, the
macrosystem contains broader societal factors (e.g., cultural norms,
government policies, inequality levels), while the chronosystem accounts for the dimension of time, capturing both life-course changes
and historical events, such as the COVID-19 pandemic. While these
layers are fundamental for understanding development and learning
across all academic subjects, they are particularly relevant for civic
education and its role outside traditional academic settings.
The specific competencies or outcomes emphasized in civic education vary depending on the framework used. In this paper, we
focus on three core elements that are commonly emphasized in various
frameworks (see Alscher et al., 2023; Carretero et al., 2015; Hamilton &
Parsi, 2022; Savage et al., 2023; Schulz et al., 2023b; Winthrop, 2020):
• Civic knowledge, which refers to students’ knowledge and understanding of civic and political systems, structures, principles, and
processes. This includes factual information, conceptual understanding, and the ability to apply this knowledge in real-world
contexts.
• Civic attitudes/dispositions, which refer to students’ judgments
about objects, ideas, events, or situations (e.g., civic institutions,
equal rights, diversity), with a certain degree of affection or
rejection.
• Civic engagement, which captures students’ interest and participation (either past, actual, or expected) in civic activities.
Each of these elements encompasses multiple individual outcomes.
In this study, we aimed to capture the three core elements while
focusing on the outcomes most likely affected by school closures and
COVID-19 containment measures. Specifically, we examine civic knowledge, trust in civic institutions, and expected civic participation. For

the latter, we consider formal political participation — both at the
individual level (e.g., voting in an election or referendum) and the
collective level (e.g., joining a political party) — as well as protest
behavior, which may take legal (e.g., collect signatures for a petition)
or illegal forms (e.g., occupy buildings, damage property) (Ekman &
Amnå, 2012; Schulz et al., 2023b). The selected variables have also
been shown to predict civic participation. Research indicates that both
civic knowledge and students’ trust in institutions are associated with
students’ willingness to participate in politics (Schulz et al., 2018a,
2023a). Furthermore, the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 2001)
highlights strong links between intentions, attitudes, and actual behavior, including political participation (Ajzen et al., 1982; Eckstein et al.,
2013).
2.4. Mechanisms of COVID-19’s relationship with civic outcomes
While there is evidence of a learning deficit among students related
to COVID-19, as well as evidence of changes in civic attitudes and
engagement among the adult population, it is still uncertain whether
adolescents’ civic knowledge, attitudes, and engagement were also
affected. We believe there are several mechanisms that could explain
impacts on these areas. First, school closures and the shift to remote learning resulted in reduced learning time across all subjects,
potentially affecting more severely subjects outside of the ‘‘core’’ of
mathematics and reading (e.g., arts, sports, civic education). Even with
remote learning in place, its effectiveness was likely lower than faceto-face instruction due to disparities in access to quality technology,
students’ self-efficacy for technology use, and student engagement in
remote learning (see Kennedy et al. (2022), Muñoz Najar et al. (2021)).
Second, there is evidence that student well-being was negatively impacted during the pandemic, with students reporting feelings of anxiety
about changes in schooling, concerns about how it impacted their current learning and their future education, worries about getting infected
with COVID-19, and feeling overwhelmed with the local and global
events caused by the pandemic (Rožman et al., 2023). Such stressors
likely affected their civic learning outcomes, as student well-being has
been shown to be associated with student achievement (Gutman &
Vorhaus, 2012) as well as to their sociability and conflict resolution
capabilities (Lyubomirsky et al., 2005). Based on the learning deficit
seen for overall academic achievement (Betthäuser et al., 2023; Di
Pietro, 2023), as well as for achievement in the subjects of mathematics (Jakubowski et al., 2024) and reading (Jakubowski et al.,
2023; Kennedy & Strietholt, 2023), we expect similar learning declines
in civic knowledge. Another possible mechanism influencing student
civic outcomes, particularly their attitudes and engagement, involves
students’ feelings of unfairness regarding the strict and prolonged containment measures, as during the pandemic students were confined to
the microsystem of their home and somewhat isolated from peers and
friends. Related to this, we hypothesize that students might exhibit
similar changes to those observed in adults, such as decreased trust
in institutions and increased propensity for protest activities, as it is
likely that students’ perceptions of COVID-19 measures were mainly
shaped by their parents or other significant adults in their lives, as
well as what they saw in the media. Lastly, an increased awareness
of societal issues during the pandemic, whether discussed at home or
through social media, might have increased students’ expectations and
motivation for future civic participation.

3. Data

Since the onset of the pandemic until the end of 2022, OxCGRT has
collected daily information on the presence of policy measures aimed
at tackling the COVID-19 pandemic (Hale et al., 2021). Relevant to
this study, several indicators collected by OxCGRT capture the extent
to which containment and closure policies have been implemented in
a country. Specifically, OxCGRT collected information about school or
business closures, public event cancellations, restrictions on gatherings
or travel, and stay at home orders. The full dataset is available publicly
online.2 For this paper, we make use of two measures from OxCGRT.
First, their measure of school closures captures daily information
about the extent of school closures in a country. Specifically, each day
is coded into one of four categories: (1) no measures in place, (2)
recommended closing or schools are open with alterations resulting in
significant differences (e.g., hybrid modes), (3) required closing only
for some levels (e.g., only elementary schools, only public schools, etc.),
(4) required closing for all levels. In addition, for each of the mentioned
categories an additional flag is given to note the geographic scope of
the policy. That is, the flag indicates whether the policy is targeted to
certain regions or aimed at the general public.
To construct our measure of the duration of school closures, we first
remove all weekends and academic holidays3 from the daily database.
We then weight each of the school closure codes differently depending
on the geographical and sectoral extent of the policies with the more
expansive policies getting higher weights than more targeted policies.
We count only days in which schools were required to be closed. We
then construct a weighted sum of the days in which schools were
closed, focusing on the time period between the start of 2020 and the
beginning of data collection, the beginning of February 2022 for all
countries in the sample (Schulz et al., 2023a).4 We test the sensitivity
of our results to the use of different weighting schemes or data sources
for the school closures measure and discuss the findings in Section 6.2.
Second, we use an index constructed by OxCGRT that captures
the stringency of COVID-19 lockdown measures on a daily basis. The
index combines information from several indicators for the presence
of several containment and closure policies: closures of schools or
businesses, cancellations of public events or public transport services,
restrictions on gatherings or travel, and ‘‘stay-at-home’’ orders. In

3.4. Sample
As our study aims to examine changes in civic outcomes across time,
we focus our analysis on countries that participated across multiple
cycles of ICCS. We use data for 14 countries and 1 benchmarking
participant, or subnational entity, (Germany’s North Rhine-Westphalia)
that participated in both ICCS 2016 and ICCS 2022. This includes
information on over 100,000 grade 8 students.7 A list of the countries
included in the sample can be found in Table 2. Note that, for the
one benchmarking participant (DNW), COVID-19 lockdown policies are
measured at the national level, rather than regional.

5. Results

ICCS utilized a stratified two-stage cluster sample scheme, first
selecting approximately 150 schools with probability proportional to
size of the school. Classrooms were then sampled within the schools
to get a sample of roughly 4000 students. This sampling design was
employed to ensure that the samples could approximate the national
target population of eighth-grade students in each country. As a result,
all analyses presented here apply sampling weights. In pooled analyses,
senate weights are used so that each country contributes the same, regardless of size. Standard errors have been calculated using a jackknife
repeated replication technique that accounts for sampling variance
arising from the complex sampling design (Schulz et al., 2018b).

6. Sensitivity analyses
To build confidence in our results, we conduct several extended
analyses to check their sensitivity to different considerations and analytical choices. We place the results of these tests in the Supplementary
Material and describe them below. All results presented in sensitivity analyses have not been standardized and should be compared
against the unstandardized coefficient estimates (see Table A3 in the
Supplementary Material).
6.1. Sample considerations and outliers
The analysis focuses on policy variation across a relatively small
sample of countries (15). As a result, there may be concerns over the
influence of outliers in our analysis. To test the presence of outliers,
we rerun our models removing one country at a time to see how
estimates change as a result of removing one country’s data. In addition
to removing one country at a time, we also remove two countries at
once: Denmark and Germany, North Rhine-Westphalia. The reason we
do this is to test whether data quality concerns due to not meeting
sampling requirements in the 2016 and/or 2022 cycles influence the
findings (Schulz et al., 2018a, 2023a). It should be noted that prior
work examining the impact of national COVID-19 lockdown policies
have also done tests excluding federal decentralized countries where
national policies may not be applicable to all states or regions within
a country (Kennedy & Strietholt, 2023). There is only one federal
decentralized country within our sample, Germany. So, this robustness
check would already be examined in the results excluding Germany,
North Rhine-Westphalia (DNW).

6.2. Measures of COVID-19 lockdown stringency and duration
In our main results, we focus on two measures of COVID-19 lockdown stringency and duration: (1) average stringency index and (2)
duration of required school closures as measured by OxCGRT and
weighted by geographical scope. Both were measured from the start
of the pandemic to the beginning of data collection. However, the
definition of such measures was an analytical choice. Here, we explore
how model results change by replacing our main predictor variables
with alternative measures. Results are presented in Table A10 in the
Supplementary Material.
For the measure of lockdown stringency, we examine two alternative measures. First, we calculate the standard deviation of the
stringency index to capture how the stringency index varies within a
country. If a country continually changes the stringency of its lockdown
measures, we may expect students (and adults) to react differently
8

Learning and Instruction 101 (2026) 102243

A.I. Kennedy et al.

than in countries with relatively stable lockdown measures. We also
calculate the number of days in which the stringency index was above
50 (i.e., at least half of the policies tracked were in place for the full
population).
While the comparison of the coefficients cannot be explicitly made
as they are measuring different aspects of lockdown stringency, we can
see mostly consistent results across the different models. For instance,
all measures of stringency were related with greater declines in civic
knowledge and related with larger increases (or smaller declines) in
expectations to participate in protest activities (legal and illegal types).
In the results that were non-significant, the sign of the relationship
remained the same as was observed in the main results. For changes
in expected electoral participation, which was not found to be significantly associated with average stringency, it does appear to be related
with the standard deviation of stringency. We are unable to speak
specifically about the mechanisms based only on this analysis, but
lockdown measures that were more variable in stringency likely seemed
less restrictive and more open to input from the public than those that
remained stringent throughout the pandemic meaning that there may
have been smaller declines in expected electoral participation.
As mentioned in Section 3, our measure of the duration of school
closures is a weighted sum of the days in which schools were required
to be closed. However, we can examine alternative measures. For
instance, we could construct a more narrow measure of school closures
in which we count only those days in which there was required school
closures for all levels with a general (rather than targeted) geographical
scope. That is, we would be certain that the schools under study
would be affected by school closure measures. We call this days in
which schools were ‘‘Fully closed.’’ In contrast, we could also create a
looser measure of school closures and account for recommended school
closures in addition to the required ones. These are ignored in our
original measure. We add these days with lower weight than required
school closures.9
In examining these different ways of measuring school closures,
we generally find consistent results with slightly different magnitudes.
In one case, the more restrictive measure of school closures (‘‘Fully
closed’’) is found to be insignificant (in relation to changes in legal
protest activities) or marginally significant (only at the 0.05 level,
expected political participation). One explanation for this difference
is that the ‘‘Fully closed’’ measure potentially ignores important information about the required school closures for certain levels or
geographical areas which may be more common in decentralized systems. The measure that accounts for recommended school closures (‘‘All
weighted’’), leads to very similar findings as those presented in the
main results with the exception of expected electoral participation.
An alternative data source for the duration of school closures is from
the UIS, which has been used to measure school closure duration in
other studies (Jakubowski et al., 2023, 2024; Kennedy & Strietholt,
2023). There is a high correlation between the UIS measures and
OxCGRT measures (𝑟 = 0.89), yet the UIS measures offers more limited
information on the extent of school closures and whether they were
mandatory or just recommended. The UIS categorizes each day into one
of four categories: closed due to COVID-19, partially open, fully open,
and on academic break. The partially open category accounts for several situations such as schools being open in certain regions, for some
grade levels, or age groups, or utilizing hybrid models. In addition,
the geographical scope of the UIS database is different than the one

offered in OxCGRT. For instance, no information is collected separately
for Taiwan. However, we may still be interested in understanding the
relationship using a different data source collecting a measure of school
closure policies. Therefore, we examine three measures constructed
from the UIS database: days in which schools were fully closed, fully
or partially closed (weighting partially closed days by 0.5), and fully
or partially closed (weighting full and partial school closures equally).
The UIS data is publicly available.10
In comparing coefficients, note that differences are due to the use

to choose the OxCGRT measure because of the greater detail as well
as the ability to include one additional country in the model, yet it
appears that results would slightly change had we used an alternative
data source for school closures.
In reading all these results it is important to note that the coefficient
estimates remain mostly consistent in their sign (with one exception
in relation to the changes in the legal protest activities scale), if not
in their significance. This leads us to have some confidence that our
choice of independent variable measures does not have a large impact
on the direction of our findings.

7. Discussion
We note two areas that have been understudied in the literature
on the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic. First, while an expanding
body of literature has estimated significant learning deficits connected
with COVID-19 pandemic, most have focused on the impacts in core
subjects like mathematics and reading (e.g., Betthäuser et al., 2023;
Di Pietro, 2023; Jakubowski et al., 2023, 2024; Kennedy & Strietholt,
2023; Ludewig et al., 2022). Very few studies of the academic impacts
of COVID-19 have focused on domains outside of these subjects. With
the everlasting importance of and rising attention to civics education,
it is imperative to understand how learning in this realm changed after
the onset of the pandemic and how this relates to the stringency and
duration of lockdown measures. With this understanding, we can have
a more holistic view of the pandemic’s impacts on education.
A second body of research has observed shifts in democratic attitudes of the general population amidst the measures implemented to
combat the spreading coronavirus (e.g., Amat et al., 2020; Bol et al.,
2020; Iacoella et al., 2021; Kishi, 2021; Lello et al., 2022; Plümper
et al., 2021; Raffini & Penalva-Verdú, 2022). Yet, these studies have
focused on the views of adult populations. Therefore, we believe it to be
important to examine how civic trust and expected participation have
shifted for the secondary student population and how this relates to
the stringency and duration of lockdown measures. Not only is this
important to fill a gap in the literature, but it will also provide a
view into the shifting attitudes of the next generation of world citizens
entering the important formative years of their civic outlook.
This study provides evidence for these understudied areas by examining the relationship between COVID-19 containment policies and
changes in (1) civics learning outcomes and (2) student civic trust and
expected participation.

7.2. Limitations
Some limitations of the present study should be acknowledged.
First, there may be a temptation to interpret our findings as causal.
We acknowledge that the assumptions needed to make causal interpretations of the results are not explicitly testable and therefore have
avoided the use of causal language in writing about the results. However, we still feel that the evidence we present is strong. In order to
interpret the 𝛽 coefficient as causal, we would need factors related to
both the stringency or duration of lockdown policies to be unrelated
to changes in civic outcomes, or else explicitly controlled for. We have
attempted to control for several factors that may fit this description,
notably, measures of pandemic severity (COVID-19 case rates, vaccination rates) which are clearly related with the COVID-19 containment
policies as well as likely linked with student civic outcomes.
Second, the estimates presented here are based on a small sample of countries. With a small sample, there may be concern about
the influence of outliers. We attempt to address this in Section 6
by checking the robustness of the estimates to the removal of each
country from the analysis. While we do find that some results change
(significant to insignificant) with the removal of a country providing
some concern about the influence of outliers, we do note that most
results remain statistically similar to the full sample results presented
in the main analysis. The results for changes in legal protest activities
(with average stringency) and changes in trust in civic institutions (with
school closure duration) show the greatest robustness to these tests
providing greater confidence in those findings. Another concern with a
small sample is the generalizability of the results. Interpretation of the
findings should consider the set of countries included in the analysis
which includes mainly European countries.
Third, limitations in the measurement of our main independent
and dependent variables may lead to the estimation of relationships
that are not aligned with our research questions. For the independent
variables, we use data from the OxCGRT which collects very detailed
information on national pandemic containment policies. However, data
