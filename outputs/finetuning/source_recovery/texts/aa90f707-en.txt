Abstract 
 
This paper presents the development and implementation of accessibility guidelines for 
PISA items, undertaken as part of the PISA Research, Development and Innovation (RDI) 
project on improving accessibility. We reviewed the literature and identified key 
accessibility frameworks and principles to develop a set of practical guidelines aimed at 
enhancing the accessibility of PISA items. The guidelines were applied to a set of PISA 
Science items for a new alternative PISA Science form. The form was piloted in 2024 in a 
small-scale observational study across seven countries and targeted students with moderate 
difficulties seeing, specific difficulties reading, and difficulties focusing and controlling 
behaviour. Feedback from participating students and national centres indicated that the 
revised items were significantly more accessible and highlighted areas for further 
refinement. This work lays the groundwork for broader efforts to enhance the accessibility 
in PISA across domains.

1.  Introduction 
Due to logistical and comparability concerns, accessibility has often been overlooked in 
international large-scale assessments (ILSAs). However, as more and more students are being 
diagnosed as having a disability or need that requires testing accommodations in participating 
countries, and as concerns on fairness and inclusion are growing, assessment organisations have 
started to place a greater emphasis on making ILSAs accessible. 
In the context of assessments, accessibility means that every student should get an opportunity to 
demonstrate their knowledge of the target construct (Beddow, Elliott and Kettler, 2013[1]; Kettler, 
Elliott and Beddow, 2009[2]), eliminating barriers and enabling equal access to the test through 
appropriate design or targeted affordances. It is a prerequisite for inclusion and fairness, i.e. that 
scores should reflect the same construct and have the same meaning for all test takers (Shyyan 
et al., 2017[3]). 
Test accessibility can be enhanced through different levels: task presentation (e.g. increasing the 
font size, contrast levels, reading text aloud, providing text descriptions of images or videos), 
response format (e.g. allowing students to use a special pen or keyboard, dictating answers instead 
of typing/writing, using spell-checking software), and administration procedure (e.g. extended 
time, allowing multiple breaks, small group/one-to-one setting). These elements can either be 
provided only to students with specific disabilities or needs, as accommodations, or can be provided 
to every student taking the test, following the Universal Design approach that seeks to “maximise 
accessibility to all intended examinees” (American Educational Research Association, American 
Psychological Association and National Council on Measurement in Education, 2014[4]), 
minimising the need for accommodations. 
Like other ILSAs, the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) has maintained 
strict guidelines on the participation of students with special education needs (hereafter, SEN). 
PISA, carried by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), assesses 
the learning outcomes of 15-year-old students around the world in order to provide comparative 
information on education systems. It is conducted every four years. Up to now, PISA 
administration guidelines have permitted the exclusion of students with “moderate to severe 
permanent physical disability” or with “cognitive, behavioural or emotional disability” such that 
they cannot participate in the PISA testing situation (PISA 2022 School Coordinator Manual). In 
order to reduce exclusions, the OECD has developed an adapted version of the PISA test, called 
the Une Heure (UH) form. The UH consists of a shorter computer-based test (two 30-minutes 
sections) and questionnaire (15 minutes). It is offered in a separate session for students who have 
an official SEN classification and/or would be excluded from taking the regular assessment. In 
addition to an adapted form, students also have access to other accommodations. However, national 
teams have expressed the concern that neither the UH nor the restricted range of allowed 
accommodations adequately cater to the needs of many students with SEN (Gamazo et al., 2019[5]). 
LeRoy and colleagues evaluated PISA 2012 data and found that very few SEN students (7%) use 
the UH form. They also noted that the form did not undergo “rigorous development” ad may have 
been developed primarily to reduce the inclusion of test participants from separate schools and 
those deemed to have limited skills” (LeRoy et al., 2018[6]). 
To address these concerns and improve accessibility in PISA, a new research, development and 
innovation project was launched in 2023. The goals of the project are twofold. The first goal of the

This paper focuses on the latter – the design of accessible items for PISA. In the context of the

providing concrete examples of process and design choices that were made to enhance PISA items’ 
accessibility (section 4). Finally, we conclude and discuss challenges and future perspectives for 
the development of accessible items in PISA (section 5). 
2.  Conceptual background and existing frameworks for designing accessible items 
Any effort to design accessible PISA items shall be grounded in established research and informed 
by existing guidelines. In this section, we present four key frameworks that provide valuable 
insights for developing inclusive computer-based assessments: Universal Design, Cognitive Load 
Theory, Accessibility Theory, and the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines. Each framework 
offers a distinct perspective on reducing barriers and promoting accessibility, ensuring that PISA 
items are testing the constructs they are intended to test, and that they are equitable and cognitively 
manageable for all students, including those with SEN. 
2.1. Universal Design 
According to the US Assistive Technology Act, Universal Design, hereinafter UD, is the concept 
of designing and delivering products that are usable by people with the widest range of functional 
capacities, ensuring an equal access for all individuals. In the domain of education, Universal 
Design for Learning (UDL) emerged as a teaching approach to accommodate the needs and abilities 
of all the learners and eliminate the unnecessary hurdles in the learning process (Rose et al., 
2006[7]). Concretely, UDL emphasises that teachers should use multiple ways to present 
information to students, such as texts, graphs, charts, pictures, videos, demonstrations, objects’ 
manipulations; they should also provide students with multiple ways of expressing their 
knowledge, demonstrating their skills (i.e. through writing, speaking, demonstrating), and 
engaging with the course materials. 
Universal Design for Assessment (UDA) has been developed on the same principles as UDL but 
specifically focuses on assessments to improve validity, reliability and fairness for all the students 
(Lazarus et al., 2022[8]). It is “an approach to assessment development that attempts to maximise 
accessibility of a test for all its intended test takers” (American Educational Research Association, 
American Psychological Association and National Council on Measurement in Education, 2014[4]), 
with the end goal to minimise the need for accommodations. Tests which are universally designed 
should provide clear and explicit information and a testing environment that is equitable, flexible, 
and intuitive to increase the participation of students with disabilities (Ketterlin-Geller, 2005[9]). 
Building on universal design guidelines, Abedi et al. (2011[10]) identified seven elements of 
universally designed assessments:

1. 
Inclusive assessment population: when designing assessments, developers should 
remember that the target population includes all the students, no matter what their cognitive 
abilities or various backgrounds are. 
2. 
Precisely defined constructs: fair assessments should measure the cognitive 
construct they are intended to test. For instance, a test which intends to measure a construct 
in mathematics should not be failed by a student with specific difficulties in reading 
(i.e. dyslexia) because of a heavy reading load. 
3. 
Accessible, non-biased items: items should be understandable by all the cultures 
and genders. To design accessible assessments, it is recommended to avoid names 
and expressions as much as possible. 
4. 
Amenable to accommodations: there exists some difficulties for which the need 
for accommodations will never be removed, even when the test is considered as universally 
designed. For instance, students with blindness will always need a Braille screen reader to 
access text and visual information, even if the assessment is highly accessible according to 
the UD guidelines. Thus, the assessment should be designed in such a way that it is 
compatible with required accommodations. 
5. 
Simple, clear, and intuitive instructions and procedures: test developers should 
try as much as possible to provide simple and clear instructions and procedures, 
understandable by all the test takers by avoiding complex language and terms that may not 
be graspable by the target population. 
6. 
Maximum readability and comprehensibility: readability of a text is assessed 
through the length of the sentence and the number of difficult words: the longer 
and the more difficult, the less readable (Thompson et al., 2005[11]). When designing 
assessments, an effort should be placed in ensuring the use of clear and common words 
and the absence of complex sentences. 
7. 
Maximum legibility: test designers should ensure that test takers can decode text, 
graphs, tables, illustrations, and response format with ease. 
These general elements are applied in checklists and guidelines to assess the extent to which a test 
is accessible and to modify its items accordingly. 
2.2. Cognitive Load Theory 
The human cognitive architecture, on which the cognitive load theory is based, refers to the 
relationship between the working and the long-term memory to process information from the 
external environment. For instance, when navigating in a complex environment, the human 
cognitive architecture requires a large information store provided by the long-term memory to 
recall procedural knowledge – exercised in the performance of a task – as well as episodic 
knowledge – the mental representation of past events specific to a person’s life. In the meantime, 
the working memory buffer, essential to hold an idea in mind, is much more limited in terms of 
storage with ‘7 ± 2’ chunks of information held at the same time (Miller, 1956[12]). These limited 
resources are greatly determined by the type of cognitive demand a given task requires. There are 
three types of cognitive loads: 
• 
The intrinsic load, which comprises the mental processing required for a task; 
• 
The germane load, which reflects the cognitive demands that are not necessary to solve the 
task but improve the cognitive processing; 
• 
The extraneous load, which includes the non-essential cognitive elements to perform a 
primary task, but that might become important for higher cognitive tasks.

The Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) provides a model to understand how the limited working 
memory storage and cognitive demands interact and how to adjust accordingly, thanks to 
instructional procedures (Chandler and Sweller, 1991[13]). Following the CLT principles, we can 
design better tests by eliminating as much as possible the extraneous load that is not necessary to 
demonstrate proficiency in the target construct while maximising the intrinsic load and managing 
the germane load to enhance knowledge acquisition (Kettler, Elliott and Beddow, 2009[2]). This 
process reduces the cognitive information the working memory has to maintain while performing 
a task to allocate the available resources on the primary objectives of the task, increasing the test-
taker’s ability to demonstrate their proficiency in the target construct. SEN students have needs 
that prevent them from managing the different types of loads effectively and are consequently 
and specifically more prone to be impacted by extraneous load. This management issue impacts 
procedural and episodic knowledge, which are essential to show the construct intended to be tested. 
Applying the CLT and its principles could help designing more accessible tests to improve the 
inclusion of SEN students in assessments (Elliott et al., 2009[14]). 
The CLT has been the starting point for the development of principles and guidelines to design 
more accessible test and facilitate learning by maximising the intrinsic load. Twenty-nine (29) 
principles have been proposed to design accessible tests and facilitate efficient learning (Clark 
et al., 2006[15]; Elliott et al., 2009[14]). Among many different guidelines, it has been shown, for 
instance, that presenting essential information directly into diagrams reduces extraneous cognitive 
load (Chandler and Sweller, 1991[13]). Moreover, presenting information in multiple sensory 
modalities (i.e. visual, auditory, tactile, etc.), synchronising information, aligning text and visuals 
to promote transfer between written words and graphics are also effective principles to follow when 
trying to reduce the extraneous load (Mayer and Moreno, 2003[16]). 
2.3. Accessibility Theory and the TAMI/ARM 
Accessibility theory can be defined as the process through which test-takers’ skills are separated 
into five different categories: physical, perceptive, receptive, emotive, and cognitive, and paired 
with some test features (Beddow, Elliott and Kettler, 2013[1]). For instance, the physical category 
involves the physical requirements to engage in the test (enter the room, sit, use a pen, etc.), which 
can be linked with test setting features. On the other end of the continuum, the cognitive skills, 
which involve long-term and working memories, can be paired with the cognitive demand of the 
test reflected for instance by the visual and verbal complexity of the contextual support. Figure 1 
presents a graphical representation of accessibility theory, showing the direct relationship between 
the test-taker characteristics and the test and item features (e.g. how responses to items are 
provided, how information is delivered, contextual support provided), as well as where and how 
accessibility comes into play. Making a test more accessible means adjusting and modifying these 
test features when students show difficulties or disabilities in the corresponding characteristics. For 
instance, students with difficulties in seeing, i.e. impaired visual perception, might benefit from 
accessible ways of delivering the information and the test, such as magnification procedures or 
text- to- speech accommodation. For students with a limited working memory, which falls into the 
cognitive capacity section, the focus must be put on decreasing the cognitive demand by playing 
on the visual and verbal complexity, for instance through the use of schematics and graphical 
representations instead of complex and lengthy paragraph.

Two evaluation and improvement tools have been developed in accordance with the accessibility 
theory to design more accessible test: the Test Accessibility and Modification Inventory (TAMI) 
(Beddow, Kettler and Elliott, 2008[17]) and the Accessibility Rating Matrix (ARM) (Beddow, Elliott 
and Kettler, 2013[1]). These tools intend to systematically evaluate, in a first phase, test 
accessibility, and in a second phase improve it by following specific guidelines. There are five 
goals for modifying items (Beddow, Kettler and Elliott, 2008[17]): increasing the access for all test 
takers, removing extraneous material, maintaining the same depth of knowledge, improving 
efficiency, and increasing validity of inferences from test results. The TAMI/ARM evaluate the 
five constituting elements of a test item: the stimulus, the visuals, the stem, the answer choices, 
and the layout. A set of rating rubrics guide the analysis of each item’s element with a 4-point scale 
(from maximally accessible for nearly all test takers, i.e. 95 to 99% of the population, to 
inaccessible for many test takers, i.e. less than 85%). A final score is produced to estimate the 
accessibility level of the entire item (see Figure 2).

After rating the level of accessibility for each element, scorers can follow modification rubrics and 
guidelines to improve the items and achieve accessibility standards. These guidelines and 
recommendations are supported by experimental findings from the literature on the accessibility of 
text, visuals, layout and answer choices. 
Guidelines regarding the text passages include, for instance, a simple, clear, and short stimulus 
with format specifications, such as important words in bold. The stem should be a clear, short 
question with the use of active voice and the absence of negative formulation, as well as key words 
in bold. Increased salience by bolding some important words in a paragraph can indeed help 
students with attention deficits to better focus their attention (McKnight, 2010[18]). Similarly, using 
plain text with common words as well as the active voice instead of the passive voice were found 
to increase accessibility (Abedi et al., 2011[10]). Moreover, it is recommended to avoid any 
complexity in the language used such as names or cultural words that could be misunderstood and 
could present unnecessary complexity (Abedi et al., 2011[10]). 
Guidelines for visuals include using them wisely, reflecting on the added value of displaying one. 
Visuals should be located at the right position and only contain necessary and useful information 
and not any complexity or distraction. Research indeed suggests that while visuals can help 
illustrate written information, improve clarity and meaning, they could also increase the number of 
distractors and therefore the unnecessary extraneous load (Solano-Flores et al., 2014[19]). For 
instance, visuals can increase the cognitive load for students with attention deficit hyperactivity 
disorder (ADHD) and consequently negatively impact their performance. On the contrary, they can 
convey essential meanings for students with reading difficulties, such as dyslexia, and therefore 
significantly improve their understanding of the test content. 
Guidelines on the answer choices suggest that raters verify whether the answer choices are simple, 
clear, balanced, with no distractors and with no more than one correct response. In addition, having

no more than three options is recommended, as supported by the literature (see meta-analysis by 
Rodriguez, 2005[20]): decreasing the answer options from five to three enables to reduce item 
difficulty without impacting the psychometric properties of the test. 
Finally, the item layout should provide enough space around the different elements and display 
them in a sufficiently large size while keeping the spread of information reasonable across a given 
page. Larger text and character spacing have indeed been found to enable faster reading for students 
with dyslexia (Rello and Baeza-Yates, 2015[20]), as well as lager intervals between lines (Zorzi 
et al., 2012[21]). 
In 2011, the TAMI has been evaluated in an experimental study by Kettler et al. (2011[22]) to assess 
the accessibility of original versions of standardised assessments in maths, reading, and science 
and perform subsequent modifications. The most common modifications they performed on test 
items included removing a multiple-choice option, simplifying the language of text parts, adding a 
visual, reorganising the layout by breaking one paragraph into several, bolding some important 
words and adding more space between text parts. After performing these changes, they recruited 
three groups of participants: students with disabilities eligible to be accommodated national 
assessments, students with disabilities non-eligible to be accommodated national assessments, and 
students without disabilities. Students from the three groups took the original tests and the modified 
ones without any other accommodations provided. The researchers found that mean difficulty was 
significantly lower in all groups in the modified versions of the tests. Reducing the number of 
options in multiple-choice answers was found to be particularly effective in reducing complexity. 
However, providing an explanatory graph was not significantly efficient, confirming the mixed 
findings regarding this type of item modification. Overall, the modifications performed to the 
original tests with the use of TAMI successfully improved the access to the test for eligible 
students, providing them with a differential boost while maintaining reliability between the 
modified and original versions of the tests. 
2.4. Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 
With the growth of online-based assessments, accessibility has become a critical concern in this 
type of testing environment. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), developed by 
the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), are highly valuable for designing computer-based 
assessments such as PISA. The goal behind these guidelines is to provide a single shared standard 
for web content accessibility that meets the needs of individuals, organisations, and governments 
internationally. The WCAG explain how to make web content more accessible to people with 
disabilities, including information such as text, images, and sounds, as well as structure and 
presentation of web sites and web pages. 
Guidelines are separated in four different principles: perceivable, operable, understandable, and 
robust, each including several standards. 
In the lowest level principle, perceivable, guidelines recommend to provide a text alternative for 
non-text content, captions and alternatives to multimedia content, and present content in multiple 
ways, the overall goal being for the users to perceive and access context in the easiest way for 
them. 
Under the operable principle, guidelines are linked to the actual use of the web content. For 
instance, it is recommended that all the functionalities should be available from a keyboard, users 
should have enough time to read and use content, help should be provided to navigate and find 
content, and the use of inputs different than a keyboard should be made available and easy to use. 
At a higher level, the understandable principle ensures that text is readable and understandable, 
that content appears and operates in predictable ways and that users can avoid and correct mistakes. 

       13

Finally, the final step, the robust principle, intends to maximise the compatibility of the web 
content with current and future user tools. 
Each guideline encompasses three levels of conformance reflecting specific success criteria. Level 
A of conformance, the minimum level, includes the basic requirements to meet the WCAG. Level 
AA of conformance is associated with the minimum level of conformance for making web pages 
accessible to most people, whose success criteria involve more advanced requirements. The highest 
level of accessibility is characterised by the level AAA of conformance: when web pages reach 
this level, they are considered very, if not fully, accessible because they follow the most rigorous 
success criteria. For instance, under the ‘Perceivable’ principle, the ‘Distinguishable’ guideline 
states that web developers should make it easier for users to see and hear content including 
separating foreground from background. If we take colours, for instance, level A of conformance 
intends “to ensure that all sighted users can access information that is conveyed by colour 
differences” (WCAG SC 1.4.6). It is reached if colour is not used as the only visual mean of 
conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual 
element. Level AA of conformance, called ‘Contrast (Minimum)’, intends “to provide enough 
contrast between text and its background so that it can be read by people with moderately low 
vision” (WCAG SC 1.4.3). To meet this level of conformance, the visual presentation of text and 
images should have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1, meeting the needs of users with 20/40 visual 
acuity. Finally, level AAA of conformance, ‘Contrast (Enhanced)’ is validated when the visual 
presentation of text and images reaches a contrast ratio of at least 7:1, which is the level required 
for users with 20/80 visual acuity. WCAG guidelines can be followed to review web sites 
accessibility and improve it by following the suggested guidelines. Since the development of web 
tools and environments is growing fast, some new versions of WCAG are developed through time 
to meet new criteria and ensure that evolving web environment remain accessible to all the users. 
3.  Guidelines for developing accessible PISA items 
Based on these findings and recommendations from the literature, we derived the following set of 
criteria to improve item design in PISA with respect to accessibility. These guidelines concern both 
the process that should be followed to revise existing PISA items or to design new ones, and the 
characteristics of the items and test layout. This set of guidelines was developed in the context 
creating of the accessible PISA Science form; they should be revised as appropriate depending on 
the domain at hand (e.g., some points regarding text simplification may not apply directly to a 
reading assessment, were the construct itself is language-based). 
3.1. Guidelines regarding the process for developing accessible items 
1. The item development team should include experts in accessibility. 
Note: At least one accessibility and disability expert should contribute to the item 
development process and review. They collaborate with the domain experts, software 
developers and UI/UX developers in order to identify barriers in the assessment and 
ensure that the item design and platform are accessible to students with SEN. 
2. When developing accessible items or revising existing items, item developers 
should use an item accessibility checklist to ensure that items are accessible and 
identify areas of improvement. 
Note: The item accessibility checklist should cover all aspects of section 3.2 below – 
Guidelines regarding the content of the test. This ensures that all aspects are 
systematically checked for all items. Using this checklist, items should be reviewed by 
at least two different raters. 

14        
IMPROVING ACCESSIBILITY IN PISA: GUIDELINES FOR REVISING AND DESIGNING PISA ITEMS © OECD 2025 
      
3. New and revised items should be tested in qualitative pilot studies, including 
cognitive laboratories, and quantitative pilot studies. These pilots should be 
administered in multiple countries and languages and involve participants with 
diverse special education needs. 
Note: Cognitive laboratories focus on the processes that a test-taker engages in when 
presented with an assessment task. For instance, the laboratories can reveal instructions 
that are difficult to understand, items that are unclear or are subject to different 
interpretations, or vocabulary that is unnecessarily complex. Quantitative pilot studies 
aim to verify at an early stage issues such as the accuracy of data capture, item/task 
difficulty and duration, or dimensionality of the constructs. As much as possible, 
cognitive laboratories and quantitative pilot data collections should be conducted in 
several participating countries and involve students with different needs or disabilities 
in order to ensure the assessment is accessible for all students. Material such as 
administration manuals should be produced to ensure a standardised study protocol 
across countries. The validation of items should include analysis of response process 
data, such as reports of thinking aloud processes in cognitive labs and log-files. 
3.2. Guidelines regarding the content of the test 
3.2.1. Text 
1. The text should only contain essential information. 
Note: To minimise unnecessary reading load, all excess text that is not needed to 
understand the task at hand should be removed, as well an any trivial content. 
2. The text should be plain and easily understandable. 
Note: The text should use common words, avoiding idioms or jargon unless it is defined 
or related to the item content and skills assessed. Sentence structures should be kept 
simple, using as much as possible positive wording and active voice, and preferring the 
use of proper nouns over pronouns. 
3. Multiple-choice questions should have a maximum of three possible answers with 
only one correct. 
Note: Decreasing the answer options to three is well-supported by the literature, 
enabling to reduce item difficulty without impacting the psychometric properties of the 
test (see the meta-analysis by Rodriguez, 2005). When decreasing the number of 
distractors for an item, the least selected option should be eliminated. 
4. Text should be formatted to facilitate reading. 
Note: This includes: 
o Bolding essential words or terms 
o Breaking the text in manageable pieces through the use of paragraphs and white 
spaces 
o Keeping text alignment not justified so that spaces between words and letters 
is identical 
o Using a sans-serif font with large font-size and wide character and word spacing 
o Using increased inter-line interval 

       15 
IMPROVING ACCESSIBILITY IN PISA: GUIDELINES FOR REVISING AND DESIGNING PISA ITEMS © OECD 2025 
      
3.2.2. Visuals 
1. Visuals should only contain useful information. 
Note: Visuals should not be distracting and only contain information that is necessary 
to understand the task. 
2. Visuals should clearly represent intended images for all intended test-takers. 
Note: Information should be conveyed using more than distinctions between colours, 
e.g. through the use of labels, cross-hatching/lines/textures. Graphical objects should 
have a sufficient contrast ratio (3:1 for images/shapes/buttons to meet the WCAG level 
AAA). 
3. Important elements in visuals should be indicated with circles, arrows or 
highlights. 
4. Text in the visuals /graphs should be readable by a screen reader. 
Note: As much as possible, the inclusion of text within images should be avoided. 
5. Visuals should be accompanied by alternative text. 
3.2.3. Layout 
1. As much as possible, the entire item and all necessary information to answer 
should be presented on one screen, minimising the need for scrolling. 
2. Items should be read vertically and from left to right for left-to-right languages, 
and from right to left for right-to-left languages. 
3. The stimulus, item stem and question should be clearly distinguishable from each 
other. 
Note: For instance, this can be done through the use of demarcation lines, white spaces, 
or frames. The layout should be kept as simple and minimal as possible. 
4. Visuals should be integrated into the stimulus or stem, along with the relevant text 
passages. 
Note: Visuals should not be presented separately from the text in the stimulus or stem, 
but rather form an integral part of it. 
5. The mouse pointer should be visible. 
Note: It should be large and sufficiently contrasting with the background (ratio of 3:1). 
6. All layout and navigation elements should be clearly visible for all intended test-
takers. 
Note: This implies ensuring sufficient contrast ratios between any text and their 
background (ratio of 7:1), and between different navigation/layout elements such as 
buttons, pop-up windows, or selected answers (ratio of 3:1). 
4.  Towards accessible items in PISA: example from the PISA Accessibility Pilot Study 
In this section, we exemplify how the above guidelines can be effectively applied in PISA, 
demonstrating how items can be made more accessible through the work carried with an 
observational pilot study that focused on the development of an alternative PISA Science form. We 
describe how we conducted item modification with a view to enhanced accessibility, and present

4.1. The PISA Accessibility Pilot Study 
Following the launch of the PISA Research, Development and Innovation project on improving 
accessibility in 2023, the OECD initiated the development of an alternative PISA test form 
incorporating targeted accommodations and revised PISA items with the goal of enhancing 
accessibility. Due to logistical constraints, this work focused on addressing first and foremost a set 
of priority needs in PISA. These needs were identified through a survey conducted among PISA 
participating countries (Guez, Ketan and Piacentini, 2024[23]), which enabled us to single out three 
priority SEN groups for the pilot, who are most likely to be excluded from the test and who already 
benefit from accommodations in their own national assessments: students with specific difficulties 
reading, students with moderate difficulties seeing, and students with difficulties focusing and 
controlling behaviour. We developed an alternative PISA Science test form to address these three 
needs through two main levers: (i) the inclusion of accommodations, including digital accessibility 
tools embedded in the platform (read-aloud, contrast colours, cursor colours and sizes, font types, 
font sizes, line height options, text spacing options, zoom levels), as well as the possibility to take 
breaks, and (ii) the modification of PISA items to enhance their accessibility. Here, we focus on 
this second lever to illustrate how accessible item design can be implemented in PISA and how 
these accessible items were perceived by students participating in the observational pilot (see Guez, 
Voisin and Piacentini, 2025[25]) for details and results regarding the implementation of 
accommodations). 
The accessible test form was piloted between April and September 2024 in seven countries: Brazil, 
Canada, Colombia, Denmark, France, Portugal and Sweden. A total of 260 students were tested, 
as presented in Table 1.

Following this review, several changes were performed on the items. These modifications involved 
the following elements: 
• 
Reshaping of the item layout: In the original PISA items, the stimulus is on the right, and 
the item stem and questions are on the left. For left-to-right languages, this layout is not 
intuitive, as web pages are usually navigated from left to right. Therefore, we placed all 
the information necessary to answer the question on the left, and the question was placed 
on the right of the screen. A demarcation line was added in the centre of the screen to 
clearly separate the two parts. 
• 
Changes performed on text parts: The original items often included long texts with 
information that was not necessary to answer the question, or with unnecessarily complex 
syntax or vocabulary. To improve the text’s accessibility, key words were bolded for 
saliency, sentences were reformulated when needed (e.g. use of active instead of passive 
voice), unnecessary complexity (sentences, words) was removed, and non-essential text 
was cut. One answer option was removed from an item as it contained two very similar 
possible correct response options. 
• 
Changes performed on graphs and visuals: Original images often were too complex or 
did not provide sufficient contrast for students with difficulties seeing. Changes involved 
adding text labels to identify each element on the visual, as well as ensuring that stimulus 
text described the visual. Colours were changed to increase contrast to a ratio of 7:1 for 
text included in the visuals, and of 3:1 for graphical elements. In addition, features were 
added to enhance visual distinction, such as contour lines, or cross-hatching. Some photos 
were replaced by simpler diagrams. Finally, new visuals were created when needed to 
provide another way of understanding complex written stimuli.

4.3. Results from the pilot study 
In order to understand how effective these modifications to the item design were in making the test 
accessible to students with priority needs for PISA (moderate difficulties seeing, difficulties 
reading, and difficulties controlling behaviour and focusing), we conducted an observational pilot 
study. Among the 260 students tested, 81 had specific difficulties reading, 79 were presenting 
difficulties in focusing and controlling behaviour, 55 had moderate difficulties seeing, and 33 were 
combining difficulties in focusing with specific difficulties reading. 14 students had needs that 
were not included in the three priority needs, which included severe learning difficulties, autism 
disorder or congenital malformation. The recruited students were aged fourteen to sixteen and the 
recruitment was done by the national centres. Here we present results from the pilot that regard 
modifications to item design. Readers interested in results that regard the effectiveness 
of accommodations can refer to (Guez, Piacentini and Voisin, 2025[24]), forthcoming. 
To assess the relevance of the modifications performed on the test items – including changes to 
images and graphs, layout, and text – we collected students’ opinions through 4-point Likert scales 
from a short-questionnaire that was administered at the end of the test, and through post-assessment 
interviews conducted in focus groups. It is worth mentioning that not all the students completed 
the student questionnaire: some of them stopped at the end of the 33 items and did not declare their 
opinions on the accommodations and modifications to item design. Consequently, the following 
results should be interpreted with caution since they do not reflect the full sample’s opinion. 
Figure 4 presents results regarding students’ perception of visuals and text clarity (respectively 
Figure 4A and 4B). The vast majority of students (83%) found the images and graphs clear or very 
clear, and the same was true for the texts (77%). This echoes findings from the focus groups, where 
students reported that the images were of good quality, clear, legible, with enough contrasts, and 
easy to understand. Regarding text, most of the students reported that the text bits were long but 
clear, with an easy language and understandable. However, some of the students highlighted that 
the text was sometimes confusing due to its length, which impacted some students’ ability to focus. 
Additionally, it was reported that questions were sometimes poorly formulated, even confusing, 
which required students to read the item many times to understand the text and content. The focus 
group also emphasised two major areas of improvement regarding visuals. First, images and graphs 
were not readable by the read-aloud tool, preventing the students to use this tool on the text labels 
integrated in the visuals; and there was no alternative text embedded with images, graphs and tables 
to describe the structure of a table or the content of a graphical representation or visual. This was 
indeed an important element of accessibility that we had planned on integrating initially, but which 
was unfortunately not implementable in this first iteration due to logistical issues. Students from 
all the testing countries did notice it and emphasised the need to develop such an improvement. 
Second, students reported the need to be able to zoom specifically on an element of the visuals. 
Indeed, in the current version of the test form, the zoom tool enabled users to increase the size of

Overall, results from the pilot were highly positive, indicating that the modified items were more 
accessible for our target population. National centres concurred and reported during the item 
review process that the revised items were significantly more intuitive and accessible than the 
original ones. The pilot also provided valuable insights for enhancing the test’s accessibility in 
terms of item design, including making the text elements in visuals readable by the read aloud tool, 
adding alternative text to visuals and tables, giving the possibility to zoom into graphical elements, 
and enabling the use of a speech-to-text tool. 
The accessible form developed for this project marked an important first step in enhancing 
accessibility in PISA. It maintained a familiar assessment format for students while incorporating 
effective accommodations and adaptations to improve their testing experience. Nevertheless, we 
acknowledge that this first phase was only a user experience study, and we could not directly 
compare the modified items with original PISA items. Besides, due to the small convenience 
sample, no generalisable conclusion can be drawn. A causal evaluation of the provided

5.  Conclusion 
The PISA Research, Development, and Innovation (RDI) project on improving accessibility marks 
a significant step towards making PISA more inclusive, notably through the design of accessible 
items. This paper outlined the foundational frameworks guiding this work – Universal Design, 
Cognitive Load Theory, Accessibility Theory, and the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 
(WCAG) – and translated them into practical guidelines for developing accessible PISA items. 
These guidelines complement the PISA Quality Standards and provide a roadmap for revising 
existing items and for creating new ones, addressing both the design process and accessibility 
criteria for item content and layout. As a limitation, it is important to note that the guidelines were 
developed specifically in the context of the project on developing an accessible PISA Science form. 
As such, they may not transfer directly to other PISA domains, such as Reading, and should be 
revised accordingly. We applied these guidelines to the revision of a set of PISA items for the 
accessible PISA Science form, demonstrating how accessibility considerations can be integrated 
into PISA item design. This new form was piloted in an observational study conducted in seven 
countries. Feedback from national centres and students was overwhelmingly positive, suggesting 
that the revised items were significantly more accessible for students with moderate difficulties 
seeing, difficulties reading, and difficulties controlling behaviour and focusing, which were the 
target populations for this pilot. However, the need for a larger quantitative study remains to 
rigorously assess how these modified items compare to the originals in terms of difficulty. The 
pilot also provided valuable insights into areas for further improvement which will inform the next 
phase of development. Future efforts will also focus on expanding accessibility to include more 
diverse needs, such as those of students with dyspraxia and blindness. 
To achieve accessibility, inclusion and fairness in PISA, it is critical that the entire PISA item pool 
eventually complies with accessibility guidelines such as those outlined in this paper. This would 
imply not only developing new items, but also revising all existing items according to these 
standards. Revising the entire item pool in one cycle seems neither feasible nor desirable, as it 
would be logistically heavy and costly, and importantly, as it would compromise PISA’s ability to 
track trends over time. A gradual, phased approach, where a portion of the item pool is revised 
each cycle, would allow us to preserve these trends. However, this approach also poses challenges, 
as it would still require significant resources, particularly since new items must be developed 
simultaneously for the major domain of each cycle. One promising path forward may involve 
leveraging key moments in PISA’s development, such as a change in the testing platform, to 
introduce accessibility improvements. These improvements could include modifying the layout or 
adjusting visual elements (e.g. enhancing contrast ratios in images), without altering the item 
content. This approach would provide a practical way to enhance accessibility incrementally while 
minimising cost and disruption to trend continuity. 
In conclusion, the accessibility guidelines developed, and the insights gained from the 
observational pilot provide a solid foundation for future efforts to enhance accessibility in PISA. 
While challenges remain, we are on the path toward creating an accessible PISA assessment that 
will better meet the needs of all test-takers.
