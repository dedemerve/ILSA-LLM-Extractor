In recent years, international assessments have significantly impacted the discourse pivoting educational quality,
often simplifying it to a single achievement continuum. With literature emphasizing quality’s multidimensional
nature, this unidimensional view challenges the concept’s complexity. Although research with international
datasets has identified multiple dimensions of educational quality, they are often considered in isolation. This
paper seeks to expand current state of research by advocating a multidimensional approach using cluster
analysis. Employing TIMSS data, six distinct clusters are portrayed, representing diverse quality configurations
across global educational systems. This nuanced evaluation enhances our understanding of quality patterns and
informs recommendations for future research.

1. Re-imagining educational quality
As governments continue to pursue educational excellence and
recognize their existence within an international landscape, reliance on
international large-scale assessments (ILSAs) as one of the necessary
sources of information on educational quality is increasing (Addey et al.,
2017; Dong & Cravens, 2012; Drent et al., 2013; Kyriakides & Creemers,
2018; Rutkowski et al., 2010). The resulting achievement rankings,
intended to ostensibly compare countries’ educational quality, keep on
making newspaper headlines (Hopmann, 2008; Rutkowski et al., 2013).
In these rankings, educational systems are ordered by a single value,
often achievement, on a high-to-low continuum, which then is seen as
the externalization of their educational quality. However, as international literature is dominated by the idea that educational quality is
multifaceted, it is fair to argue that such a single value continuum represents a certain oversimplification of the complex notion (e.g., Wittek &
Kvernbekk, 2011).
ILSAs do collect data on multiple dimensions of educational quality.
A recent systematic review charted international diversity in the inquiry
of the concept through secondary analyses of ILSA data (Appels et al.,
2022). This charting led to the identification of four distinguishable

research approaches into as many quality dimensions. Although the
review concluded that each approach contributed meaningfully to the
understanding of the rich concept, it also encountered the inevitable
discomfort of the concept’s complexity or, rather, how the complexity of
the concept was evaded (Biesta, 2009; Braathe & Otterstad, 2014;
Hostetler, 2005; Scheerens, 2011). This evasion of complexity is
grounded in the finding that the four research approaches were developed rather in isolation from one another (Appels et al., 2022).
Arguing that the multidimensional and interwoven character of
educational quality is in need to be recognized, we advocate an alternative empirical evaluation. In translating the concept as constituted by
an interplay of related but distinct domains to a country level, a cluster
analysis approach manifests itself as the designated method to employ
(Michaelides et al., 2019b). Additionally, as the concept remains theoretically challenged, detecting clusters or patterns in the data is said to
be a strong step in its multifaceted inquiry (Bartholomew et al., 2008).
This procedure will allow us to explore the diversity in quality features
among educational systems in a fine-grained, complex, and comprehensive manner.

2.1. Defining educational quality

Although the review study identified four approaches, this paper
only considers three, omitting quality of achievement. Its exclusive focus
on output is problematic, as educational output is determined by the
intersection of the other three factors (Bower, 2013; Irvine, 2010; Wood
et al., 2011). This paper does, however, reflect upon how the configuration of these factors impact educational outcomes. Unable to determine a single prevailing definition for each of the quality domains,
agreement on some of their dimensions was found. Table 1 provides a
summary.

The pursuit of quality in education is a self-evident goal in education
(Van Damme & Bellens, 2017). However, its subjective, evolutionary,
and manipulative nature allows for multiple interpretations (Hostetler,
2005; Van Petegem, 2005). The subjectivity of quality is underscored by
the variety of interpretations held by stakeholders from different backgrounds. Moreover, quality in education is not static; it evolves over
time due to societal changes, reflecting shifting expectations. Furthermore, the interpretation and application of quality are often influenced
by those in positions of power, introducing a manipulative element.
From a philosophical standpoint, Pirsig (1974) contends that quality
involves a process of doing things with care. Although he struggles to
define the concept, he is convinced that “we know it when we see it” (as
cited in Levin, 2003, p. 3). Consequently, this discussion raises awareness for the necessary reduction of the concepts when attempting to
measure it. In contrast, the sociological perspective interprets educational quality in terms of meeting societal functions, encompassing
qualification, socialization, and person formation (Biesta, 2009; Brint,
2017; Dewey, 1938; Durkheim, 1956). This focus takes the conversation
on quality away from the educational processes, steering the conversation from a technical question to ethical considerations (Biesta, 2005,
2009).
Shifting to an educational effectiveness perspective, Scheerens
(2011) again introduces the methodological problem (Hostetler, 2005).
Building on Pirsig’s reasoning, he advocates for defining quality
amenable to scientific measurement (Duru-Bellat & Mingat, 2011). This
perspective frequently concentrates on goal attainment, or cognitive
outcomes, through the process of schooling (Creemers et al., 2010;
Kyriakides et al., 2019; Reynolds et al., 2014; Scheerens, 2015). However, concern has been directed at the operational nature of this
approach, which lacks explicit theoretical foundations (Braathe &
Otterstad, 2014, Nordenbo et al., 2009; Scheerens, 2015).
In conclusion, educational quality can be described as a “vague
concept, which runs us into boundary problems and forces us to operate
in grey zones” (Wittek & Kvernbekk, 2011, p. 671), resisting a precise
definition (Popper, 1945; Scheffler, 1974). Interestingly, this lack of
essence allows quality to be interpreted quantitatively, acknowledging a
spectrum where “less passes seamlessly into more” (Wittek & Kvernbekk, 2011, p. 675). Among the various understandings of quality, the
idea of quality as fitness for purpose currently seems to dominate the
field of education (Harvey & Green, 1993; Stensaker, 2007). This idea
emphasizes the alignment of educational processes with the goal of
producing specific outcomes. This understanding of quality as fitness for
purpose gains relevance in the examination of quality through data from
ILSAs, given that ILSA data allows for an examination of contextual,

2.1.4. Quality of school life
As teacher quality creates the foundation for instructional quality,
school climate creates a foundation for both teaching and learning;
hence, its influence on student learning can be direct or indirect (Nilsen
& Gustafsson, 2016). School climate “reflects students’, school personnel’s, and parents’ experiences of school life socially, emotionally,
civically, and ethically as well as academically” (Thapa et al., 2013, p.
13). According to the seminal reviews by Thapa et al. (2013) and Wang
et al. (2015), contemporary research focuses on four dimensions: (1)
academic climate; (2) community; (3) safety; (4) institutional environment. It is important to recognize that these school climate dimensions
and their conditions are deeply interconnected (Thapa et al., 2013).
Academic climate reflects every aspect of the atmosphere related to a
school’s quest for academic excellence (Hoy et al., 2006; Thapa et al.,
2013; Wang et al., 2015). The dimension of community stresses the
importance of interpersonal interactions within the school, which
preferably result in feelings of connectedness and belonging. Safety, as
the third dimension, can be broadly categorized into the provision of
physical and emotional security, as well as the implementation of fair
disciplinary practices. Lastly, the institutional environment dimension is
based on patterns in the organizational or structural features of the
school environment.
2.2. Educational outcomes
To facilitate learning, it is important to work in the student’s zone of
proximal development (Vygotsky, 1978). A task that is either too easy or
too difficult is likely to undermine students’ motivation and self-esteem
(Moreno, 2010). Moreover, research suggests that motivation supports
cognitive learning in the long term (Deci & Ryan, 2000). This unites
multiple perspectives on educational outcomes. Motivation leads to
students being dedicated to cognitively active time on task, which
supports cognitive learning at their own level, which is accompanied by
a growth in self-esteem (Bellens et al., 2019; Nilsen & Gustafsson, 2016).
To address the multidimensionality of educational objectives, this paper
includes both cognitive and affect-motivational educational outcomes in
the analysis.

2.1.3. Instructional quality
Teacher quality must not be confused with instructional (i.e.,
teaching) quality, which reflects what teachers actually do (Goe, 2007;
Senden et al., 2021). Instructional quality includes those teaching
practices leading to students being dedicated to cognitively active time
on task (Bellens et al., 2019; Nilsen et al., 2016). Hence, this domain can
be seen as an outcome of teacher quality. In addition, instructional
quality is a multidimensional construct (e.g., Baumert et al., 2010), with
consensus existing around four key dimensions, creating a
well-established framework: (1) classroom management; (2) cognitive
activation; (3) supportive climate; (4) clarity of instruction (Bellens
et al., 2019; Fauth et al., 2014; Klieme et al., 2009; Kunter & Baumert,
2006; Nilsen et al., 2016).
The first dimensions, classroom management, is considered a prerequisite for learning (Klieme et al., 2009; Kunter & Baumert, 2006). By
establishing classroom rules and overcoming disruptions, teachers
create time and space for learning to occur (Fauth et al., 2014). Cognitive activation, as the second dimension, refers to the teacher’s ability to
foster students’ engagement in higher-level thinking (Fauth et al., 2014;
Klieme et al., 2009; Praetorius et al., 2014). The third dimension, supportive climate, is characterized by an approach that encourages positive, caring and encouraging teacher–student relationships (Klieme

The TIMSS sample design consists of at least 150 schools, with a student
sample of approximately 4000 students in each country. Ultimately,
data from approximately 4000 students in 57 educational systems were
analyzed in this study. The complex sampling design of TIMSS requires
several considerations in analyzing the data (Rutkowski et al., 2010).
First, the TIMSS students are not selected using a simple random sampling procedure. Instead, a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design
is employed. This involves a random selection of schools, based on
well-defined strata, followed by a random selection of entire classes, or
groups of students, within these schools. Of these selected students, both
the students, their parents, teachers, and school principals were questioned within the TIMSS framework. Note that only the teachers of the
selected classrooms were invited to participate in the questionnaire,
ensuring a direct connection between the student and teacher data
collected, as per the TIMSS sampling framework. A second complexity
lies in the fact that not all students answer all items (Martin et al., 2020).

In the process of aligning our theoretical framework with TIMSS
data, we translated theoretical constructs into potential indicators. This

3.2.2.2. Cognitive activation. For the operationalization of cognitive
activation ground can be found in the work of Burroughs et al. (2019),
Blömeke et al. (2016) and Bellens et al. (2019). A new set of items in the
TIMSS 2019 teacher questionnaire (i.e., Teaching the TIMSS Class)
seems to have connections with measures for cognitive activation in all
of these previous works. Bellens et al. (2019), again, suggested adding
items to the TIMSS framework in order to measure this domain of
instructional quality. Burroughs et al. (2019) and Blömeke et al. (2016),
on the other hand, used a variable from the TIMSS 2011 assessment. This
variable, however, is no longer available in the TIMSS 2019 assessment.
Nevertheless, items seem to be repurposed in this new Teaching the
TIMSS Class scale (Yin & Fishbein, 2020).

3.2.1. Teacher quality
In the exploration of teacher quality, we delve into research findings
on teacher qualifications before exploring teacher characteristics.
3.2.1.1. Teacher qualifications. Teacher qualifications, known for their
ease in data collection, emerge as relative effective indicators associated
with enhanced students learning (Goe & Stickler, 2008). Goe’s (2007)
review identifies two key teacher qualification variables consistently
linked to positive effects: subject-matter competency (e.g., Carr, 2006)
and teaching experience (e.g., Hanushek et al., 2005; Rockoff, 2004;
Rowan et al., 1997). Clotfelter et al. (2007) posit that a strong
subject-matter foundation (i.e., major in mathematics) is only effective
when combined with strong educational credentials, being didactical
knowledge (i.e., major in education). Moreover, recent studies question
the positive impact of advanced degrees on student achievement (e.g.,
Clotfelter et al., 2006; Rowan et al., 2002).

3.2.2.3. Instructional clarity. In addition to the new student variable for
classroom management, another new student scale in TIMSS 2019 is
labeled Instructional Clarity (Yin & Fishbein, 2020). We can connect
this, without further ado, to the dimension of clarity of instruction.
3.2.2.4. Supportive climate. Unfortunately, items used to measure a
supportive climate in previous TIMSS cycles do not seem to overlap with
items in the current TIMSS 2019 framework.
This leads to the following set of instructional quality indicators:
Students’ View on Classroom Management (ASBGDML), teacher’s view
on how they cognitively activate their class (ATBG12), and Students’
View on Instructional Clarity (ASBGICM).

3.2.1.2. Teacher characteristics. Teacher characteristics span immutable
traits like gender and potentially changeable aspects such as attitudes
and beliefs (Goe, 2007). This paper will exclude the immutable characteristics as proxies for teacher quality. Furthermore, Goe’s (2007)
review notes a lack of consensus on the impact of the second set of
teacher characteristics on student learning. Consequently, our focus
narrows to the possibilities of measuring this construct within the TIMSS
framework.
Belief variables within the TIMSS teacher questionnaire tap into Job
Satisfaction and Teachers’ Perceived Limitations in Teaching due to
class composition. Items in this latter construct correspond to the
Teachable Pupil Survey (Kornblau, 1982), measuring teachers’ expectations regarding the teachability of their pupils. Contemporary research
suggests an indirect impact of teachers’ teachability expectations on
pupils’ achievement through feelings of academic futility (Agirdag et al.,
2013).
This leads to the following set of teacher quality indicators: Teacher’s
Specialization in mathematics and/or education (ATDMMEM), years of
job experience (ATBG01), Teacher’s Job Satisfaction (ATBGTJES), and
teacher’s teachability expectations (ATBG13).

3.2.3. Quality of school life
The tightly interwoven dimensions of quality of school life do exhibit
a straightforward connection with clearly separatable TIMSS scales (Hoy
et al., 2006; Thapa et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2015). Additionally, it
should be noted that the absence of a measure for supportive climate, a
dimension of instructional quality, might find resonance in the TIMSS
proxies for community.
3.2.3.1. Academic climate. A proxy for academic climate is the TIMSS
scale Emphasis on Academic Success, present in the school questionnaire, teacher questionnaire, and parent questionnaire. This paper
delves into all three of these scales, with a decision between the teacher
and school questionnaire depending on variances in their results at the
country level. Additionally, recognizing the value of including the parents’ voice in the measurement of the quality of school life, the Parent’s
Perceptions of Their Child’s School is also incorporated.

majored in another subject; (5) had no formal postsecondary education.
For the other variables country aggregated means were calculated.
These percentages and means were drawn using the IEA’s International
Database (IDB) Analyzer, to include the appropriate weights and plausible values (Fishbein et al., 2021). After this aggregation, however,
countries were given an equal share in the clustering, for which no
additional weights were added in the actual cluster analyses.
With the full set of possible quality indicators as a starting point, we
attempted to extract the relevant variables for each domain. As the
initial move, in our endeavor to create a rich measure of quality using
TIMSS data, we restricted ourselves to the scales made available by IEA.
This left out Teacher Expectations, Teaching the TIMSS Class, and Instruction Affected by General Resource Shortage. Attempting to include
an indicator for cognitive activation, we explored whether we could
create a new latent variable out of the set of items for ATBG12 (Teaching
the TIMSS Class). In preparing for an exploratory factor analysis, we
found that the Pearson correlation coefficients were unfortunately not
high enough to continue this effort.
Next, various preliminary cluster analyses were conducted. Before
fixing the final number of clusters, we allowed the software to automatically select the number of clusters, though this provided little information. The subsequent preliminary analyses used the TIMSS
interpretation of the country means for each variable. We then removed
variables that had little to no variance at the country level, as they would
not contribute to the clusters’ heterogeneity, including School Emphasis
on Academic Success (teacher report), Student Bullying and Instruction
Affected by Math Resource Shortage (as the indicator of infrastructure).
Both Disorderly Behavior during Mathematics Lessons and Instructional
Clarity in Math Lessons were retained, despite their low variance, in an
attempt to retain a measurement of instructional quality.
The remaining variables were analyzed jointly in a cluster analysis
using their numeric country means, as opposed to the TIMSS interpretations used in the previous set of analyses. This involved
removing variables that, according to the ANOVAs, did not contribute
significantly (alpha level < 0.001) to the clustering, including Teacher
Experience, Disorderly Behavior during Mathematics Lessons (as the
indicator for classroom management), and School Discipline. Finally, we
removed variables based on parent questionnaires (i.e., Parents’ Perceptions of Their Child’s School), since they carried many missing values
and would have excluded several countries. A summary of this process

3.3. Cluster analysis
In pursuit of our research aim, we adopt an explorative approach
aimed at uncovering data patterns, revealing hidden associations, and
creating a preliminary categorization of educational systems based on
their distinctive quality configurations. For these purposes, a cluster
analysis emerges as the more appropriate analytical approach
(Bartholomew et al., 2008). This analytical strategy involves the integration of multiple variables into a unified framework.
Traditionally, research involving ILSA data has predominantly
favored multilevel analysis methods, wherein the variance of each variable is examined while only to a certain extent considering their interdependencies. However, this study sought to transcend this
conventional approach by applying cluster analysis. Although multilevel
modeling techniques are typically suited for handling ILSA data due to
their hierarchical nature (Rutkowski et al., 2010), our research question
diverges from the conventional focus on testing specific hypotheses or
examining variance distribution across hierarchical levels. Instead, this
study took a more exploratory path, aiming to reveal data patterns and
categorize educational systems into distinct groups based on their
characteristic configurations. For these purposes, cluster analysis
emerged as the most appropriate analytical approach (Bartholomew
et al., 2008). This strategy integrated multiple variables into a unified
framework, offering a holistic understanding of the concept and
providing a nuanced perspective beyond a singular high-to-low
continuum.
In a cluster analysis, homogenous observations in the dataset are
identified and grouped together (Bartholomew et al., 2008). Among the
multitude of cluster techniques, we employed a two-step cluster analysis. The Two-Step cluster analysis first uses a distance measure to
identify and create preliminary groups. It then applies a hierarchical
method to merge these preliminary groups based on their similarities,
resulting in the final optimal and meaningful clusters (Benassi, et al.,
2020; IBM Corporation, 2021). By choosing this approach, we reject the
assumption that populations are homogenous regarding their quality
characteristics and how they are interrelated (von Eye & Bogat, 2006).
Therefore, in identifying clusters of countries, this approach looks for
patterns of associations that are similar within clusters but different
between them. Inherent to the two-step cluster analysis is its ability to
handle both continuous and categorical variables in large datasets, like
TIMSS (Michaelides et al., 2019a), making it more suitable for our
purposes in comparison to more traditional clustering techniques.

In selecting the final cluster solution, we used statistical measures as
guidelines to inform our decision. First, the silhouette measure of
cohesion and separation needed to be at least fair (IBM Corporation,
2021; Kaufman & Rousseeuw, 1990). This measure reflects the evidence
available for the presence of a reasonable to strong cluster structure.
Second, the smallest cluster needed to contain at least 7 % of the sample
(Michaelides et al., 2019a). Once these measures were met, we carried
out ANOVAs to assess the significance of the remaining variables’
contribution in the finalized clusters (alpha level < 0.001), and the
variance each explained (eta squared > 50 %; Milligan & Cooper, 1985).
In parallel, we considered the interpretation of the clusters we
derived (Michaelides et al., 2019a). The final number of clusters was
decided based on the first researcher’s assessment. Every decision,
however, was discussed with the two fellow researchers until agreement
was reached. This process of variable selection and cluster formation
was not linear; several loops were made. The final clustering was
interpreted through the theoretical framework (von Eye & Bogat, 2006).
Additionally, we conducted several Tukey Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc tests to examine how much the cluster mean of each
variable included differed from the means of the others. The detailed
statistical measures and the SPSS syntax for this two-step clustering can
be found in Appendices G and H.

different random orders (IBM Corporation, 2021). Besides, analyses
were conducted with either the raw scale means per country or the their
standardized counterparts. The same solution was obtained each time,
verifying its stability. To conclude the process of cluster analysis, the

4.1. Cluster 1 – aware valued content educators (21.1 %)
From the entire sample, Cluster 1 is the second largest group of
countries. This cluster is characterized by teachers who majored in education and mathematics (z-score = .33; mean TIMSS percentage,
39.5 %) or solely in education (z-score = .10; mean TIMSS percentage,
45.7 %), making them mainly educators. Although this cluster did not
stand out in terms of teacher education, it did significantly outperform
the other clusters in most other quality dimensions. More specifically, it
had significantly higher average levels of job satisfaction (z-score = .88,
mean TIMSS score, 10.69), instructional clarity (z-score = 1.24; mean
TIMSS score, 10.79), community (z-score = 1.23; mean TIMSS score,
10.99), and safety (z-score = 1.05; mean TIMSS score, 11.35). For this,
the cluster was given the strong adjectives content, to represent the
quality perspective of the teachers, and valued, to represent the quality
perspective of the students. Noteworthy is that this cluster is considered
to deliver the most outstanding clarity of instruction, sense of community, and safety, and was ranked second-highest for job satisfaction. For
academic climate (z-score = − .16; mean TIMSS score, 9.80), another
story is to be told, since it performed below average and significantly
lower than the top-performing cluster. Therefore, Cluster 1 was given
the label aware rather than the stronger focused for its emphasis on
academic success.
The educational outcomes in this cluster are relatively high. Even
more so, students in this cluster reported the highest levels of intrinsic
motivation (z-score = 1.01; mean TIMSS score, 10.48) and self-esteem
(z-score = .90; mean TIMSS score, 10.75). Their achievement scores in
turn did not differ significantly from the highest-performing, nor from
the lowest-performing clusters (z-score = − .28; mean TIMSS score,
483.53). The countries in this cluster were Albania, Azerbaijan, BosniaHerzegovina, Georgia, the Republic of Iran, Kazakhstan, Kosovo,
Montenegro, Northern Macedonia, Portugal, Serbia, and Spain. Just
over half of these countries reached the intermediate benchmark; the
others were above the low benchmark. These TIMSS benchmarks provide an interpretation of achievement. The low benchmark indicates
basic mathematics knowledge, whereas the advanced benchmark involves communicating conceptual understanding in various complex
situations. Concerning intrinsic motivation, in all countries except Spain
and Serbia, at least half of students liked learning mathematics very
much. In general, patterns for self-esteem were less clear.

3.4. Bigger picture
To complete the story, we performed a cluster comparison to obtain
insight into the possible predictors of affect-motivational and cognitive
outcomes (Michaelides et al., 2019a). The clusters were added as an
additional variable in the country-level dataset. Additionally, country
means for the outcome variables of intrinsic motivation, self-esteem, and
achievement were calculated through the IDB Analyzer and included in
the dataset (Fishbein et al., 2021). Hence clusters were compared in
terms of average educational outcomes in the subject of mathematics.

mean mathematic achievement scores.

However, the influence of the discourse on educational quality extends beyond academic researchers’ discussions in scientific journals
(Addey et al., 2017; Dong & Cravens, 2012; Drent et al., 2013; Kyriakides & Creemers, 2018; Rutkowski et al., 2010). Its societal repercussions permeate governmental bodies, which employ research
findings to inspire policy decisions aimed at improving a country’s education system. Notably, conventional ILSA research typically delves
into specific aspects of educational quality, requiring policymakers to
comprehend this fragmentation (Appels et al., 2022). In contrast, our
research advocates a multidimensional approach, providing a richer
understanding of the complex concept that is quality.
Remaining within the realm of societal impact, incorporating expert
surveys and policy document analysis could add greater contextual
significance to the findings. Engaging educational experts from diverse
countries and examining educational policies may reveal how these elements influence quality configurations. Although our initial exploration based on Hofstede’s cultural dimensions (2011) was inconclusive, a

could offer valuable insights. This approach could, for instance, illuminate which profile aligns more closely with the ideals of a given

(e.g., Alexander, 2001; Leung, 2006; Nagengast & Marsh, 2014). As the
results indicate, no quality configuration was universally optimal.
Consequently, decisions must be made regarding policy focus on
enhancing specific quality dimensions. Additionally, It should be
acknowledged that ILSA data do not exist in isolation as a source of
educational policy formulation (e.g., Oates, 2021). National examinations, for example, offer a unique lens into specific national contexts,
providing valuable insights into historical contexts, closer time lags, and
specific domestic performance analyses.
In terms of our methodological decisions, the results of the cluster
analyses, when compared to the conventional (e.g., multilevel) analyses,
revealed a more holistic understanding of the concept under consideration. The clustering uncovered a deeper and more nuanced perspective
on quality configurations that goes beyond the constraints of a singular
high-to-low continuum. This multifaceted assessment of educational
systems stands as evidence of the complex nature of educational quality
(Harvey & Green, 1993; Wittek & Kvernbekk, 2011). Furthermore, it
enhances our understanding by offering insights into the interconnections among its domains within the educational sphere.
Despite, this paper represents only a first exploration into the
multidimensional nature of quality using TIMSS data. Alternative statistical methods could offer a more comprehensive understanding of
quality’s underlying dynamics. In such further research, the element of
time might also be a relevant addition. The assumption that quality
possesses a linear definition does not hold true (Van Petegem, 2005).

5. Discussion
In this article, we delved into the prospects of asking for a perspective
on educational quality based on the possibilities and constraints of the
TIMSS dataset. Our goal was not to provide definite answers regarding
how educational quality can be defined and measured within this
framework but rather to suggest how previously gained insights can be
combined to contribute to an understanding of educational quality and
how it can be advanced in an international context. The two-step cluster
approach provided a comprehensive division of countries into six clusters based on measures of teacher quality, instructional quality, and
quality of school life. These clusters demonstrate how a multidimensional evaluation of educational systems highlights the complex nature
of the concept of educational quality and provides a more nuanced
outlook in comparison to a single value on a high-to-low continuum.
Before going into these patterns, some clarifications must be made.
First of all, our analysis derived from an existing dataset, for which the
theoretical framework representing all that quality should be, was
restricted to what the dataset allowed it to be (Biesta, 2009). The
acknowledgment of this limitation, however, stems not only from constraints in measurement but also from theoretical ambiguities inherent
in defining the concept of educational quality (Van Petegem, 2005;
Wittek & Kvernbekk, 2011). Second, it needs to be added that the
country averages for the different variables did not always differ greatly,
especially not in terms of TIMSS vocabulary. As a result, the cluster
names were somewhat exaggerated. Moreover, it is crucial to recognize
that cluster analysis carries inherent subjectivity (Michaelides et al.,
2019a). Researchers are required to make critical decisions, such as
determining the number of clusters and interpreting their meaning.
These decisions inject subjectivity into the analysis, relying on the researcher’s judgment and expertise. Finally, using the world as an
educational laboratory (IEA, 2022) can contribute to an international
understanding of educational quality, but also requires a reflection on
the subtle but long-term relationship between culture and education
(Alexander, 2001; Leung, 2006; Nagengast & Marsh, 2014). With this
backdrop, it is interesting that a study like TIMSS permits examination of
whether educational quality transcends space. However, cultural sensitivities and measurement invariance were outside the scope of this
paper and warrant further exploration beyond the scope of this paper.
The clusters demonstrate some patterns of educational quality. Based
on the literature, one could bluntly state that Cluster 1 (i.e., aware
valued content educators) entails the greatest educational quality and
Cluster 6 (i.e., focused detached doubtful specialists) the least. A similar
conclusion could be drawn from considering the quality domains separately. But, more is to be said. The most inconsistent clusters were
Clusters 2 and 3, be it in different directions. For Cluster 2, the fact that
many teachers lacked a formal education could lead to low expectations
of the other quality dimensions. This fortunately is an assumption it
easily surpasses. The opposite is true for Cluster 3, where most teachers
majored in both education and mathematics, it did not redeem the high
expectations of the other dimensions. As such, our results do not confirm
the previously described association between teacher quality and either
subject matter knowledge or didactical knowledge (Clotfelter et al.,
2007).
Notably, the academic climate is left out of the interwovenness of
educational quality. It seems to flow in a different and perhaps opposite
way. In this regard, our findings diverge from theoretical expectations
regarding the perceived significance and strong interconnection of academic climate in the quality of school life (Hoy et al., 2006; Thapa
et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2015). Furthermore, emphasis on academic
success cannot be directly linked to educational outcomes either.

6. Conclusion
To meet the challenge of measuring educational quality in a precise,
valid, multidimensional way on an international scale, we proposed a
cluster analysis using TIMSS data. This approach proved itself to be a
powerful route to identify quality clusters of countries participating in
TIMSS 2019. Even more, the combined evaluation provided a complex
profile of a country’s situated quality rather than a single value on a
high-to-low continuum. This type of quality analysis can lead the way to
a better understanding of countries’ quality patterns and their interaction with educational outcomes.
