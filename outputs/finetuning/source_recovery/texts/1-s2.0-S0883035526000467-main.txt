Education systems and social background inequality of

Yongxiang Xie

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Family background
Academic achievement
Education systems

Prior research has shown that education systems shape inequality of educational opportunity
(IEO). However, two critical aspects of education systems have received limited scholarly
attention: the stratification of general upper secondary education (SGUSE) and the high-stakes
testing (HST) for transition from lower to upper secondary education. Drawing on data from
the 2015 and 2018 cycles of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), along
with existing literature and online sources, this study examines how SGUSE and HST moderate
the association between family background and children’s academic achievement. The study
finds that in education systems characterized by high SGUSE and HST for transitioning from
lower to upper secondary education, the association between family background and children’s
academic achievement is significantly stronger. These results suggest that the structure of upper
secondary education affects IEO at the lower secondary stage.

1. Introduction
Inequality of educational opportunity refers to the unequal distribution of educational resources, environments, and outcomes
across status characteristics beyond individuals’ control (Skopek et al., 2019). It primarily reflects the extent to which ascriptive factors
influence one’s academic achievement or educational attainment (van de Werfhorst, 2015). A higher level of IEO indicates a stronger
impact of these ascriptive factors on individual educational outcomes.
Family, gender, race, and place of birth are important ascribed factors influencing individual educational outcomes. Among these,
the family constitutes the primary context for adolescent socialization (Wu et al., 2019; Zhang & Wu, 2020), profoundly shaping an
individual’s life chances. Family background, as a core measure of family characteristics, refers to the socioeconomic status of a family,
encompassing parental education, occupation, and income. The relationship between family background and children’s academic
achievement and educational attainment has garnered widespread attention and generated a substantial body of theoretical and
empirical research (Breen & Jonsson, 2005; Hannum et al., 2019; Wu, 2020). Existing study has shown that family background is one
of the most influential ascribed factors affecting educational outcomes (Horn, 2009; Lee & Borgonovi, 2022; Teachman, 1987), and its
impact has continued to widen over time (Chmielewski, 2019). Accordingly, this study defines IEO as the strength of the association
between family background and children’s academic achievement or educational attainment.1

have shown that the strength of the association between family background and children’s academic achievement or educational

2012; Palmisano et al., 2022).
Prior research has mainly attributed cross-national variation in IEO to differences in education systems (Gross, 2021; Terrin &
Triventi, 2023; van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 2010; Zapfe & Gross, 2021). The education system refers to the formal rules and organizational structures of a country’s educational institutions (Esser, 2016), representing a stable institutional design that shapes social
interaction and behavior (Gross, 2021). One of its primary functions is to act as a sorting mechanism, categorizing students into
different educational hierarchies (Domina et al., 2017; Kerckhoff, 1995, 2001). By regulating the distribution of educational opportunities, the education systems directly shape the range of choices available to individuals and links family background to children’s
educational outcomes, playing a critical role in the intergenerational transmission of education (Pfeffer, 2008).
Existing studies have primarily examined how three dimensions of education systems—stratification, standardization, and selectivity—affect the relationship between family background and children’s academic achievement or educational attainment. Research
has shown that stratification, such as tracking and ability grouping, tend to exacerbate IEO (Boliver & Capsada-Munsech, 2021;
Feniger et al., 2021; van de Werfhorst, 2019; van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 2010). Findings on the impact of standardization—particularly
through school autonomy and centralized examinations—on IEO remain contested (Bodovski et al., 2020; Horn, 2009; Wößmann,
2005). The effects of selectivity on IEO also remain inconclusive (Neugebauer, 2010; Roth & Siegert, 2016; Traini, 2022).
While the existing literature has systematically examined the relationship between education systems and IEO, several important
limitations persist. Prior studies have primarily focused on tracking between general and vocational upper secondary education and
ability grouping to examine the effects of stratification on IEO. However, stratification within education systems extends beyond these
forms and is also reflected in the general upper secondary education itself. Significant disparities exist among general upper secondary
schools in terms of human, material, and financial resources. This internal stratification prompts advantaged families to secure access
to high-quality education for their children early in their educational trajectories, intensifying academic competition from the outset
(Hou, 2020; Tang, 2015; Wu, 2020), thereby exacerbating IEO even during compulsory education. Therefore, SGUSE must be
considered to more comprehensively reveal how education systems shape IEO.
In addition, previous studies often merge lower and upper secondary school students, focusing mainly on how centralized examinations at the end of upper secondary education affect the relationship between family background and academic achievement.
However, the implementation and function of centralized examinations at the end of lower secondary education vary considerably
across countries. These examinations may exert a more direct influence on the association between family background and academic
achievement at the lower secondary education. IEO at higher levels of education may stem from cumulative disparities that originate in
earlier educational stages (Chen et al., 2023; Li, 2014). Therefore, it is crucial to pay greater attention to how centralized examinations
at the end of lower secondary education shape IEO.
Centralized examinations at the end of lower secondary education serve two main purposes. First, they function as the primary
basis for certification and educational transition, exerting a significant influence on students’ educational trajectories. Second, they are
used to monitor and evaluate schools by enabling inter-school comparisons of academic performance and enhancing overall efficiency.
The first purpose reflects the selectivity dimension of education systems. Only centralized examinations with selective functions can
substantially affect IEO. Therefore, this study constructs an indicator of HST at the end of lower secondary education by integrating
both standardization and selectivity dimensions.
Building on the above discussion, this study focuses on how the education system of general upper secondary education influences
IEO at the lower secondary education. Specifically, it examines how SGUSE and HST in the transition from lower to upper secondary
education moderate the relationship between family background and children’s academic achievement.
2. Literature review and research hypotheses
Existing research has primarily examined the effects of three dimensions of education systems on IEO: stratification, standardization, and selectivity. Stratification was initially defined as the degree of differentiation between schools, particularly in terms of how
school curricula are hierarchically ranked as “higher” or “lower.” Over time, the concept has broadened to encompass both horizontal
and vertical forms of stratification. Horizontal stratification refers to the separation between academic and vocational tracks, whereas
vertical stratification involves ability grouping and grade retention (Triventi et al., 2016).
Standardization denotes the extent to which educational quality meets nationwide uniform standards (Allmendinger, 1989). It can
be divided into standardization of input and standardization of output. Standardization of input refers to school autonomy, specifically
the extent to which national education authorities centrally regulate teacher qualifications, curricula, and funding. Standardization of
output refers to the existence of nationally administered centralized examinations that assess student performance (Checchi et al.,
2014).
Selectivity indicates the degree to which students can freely choose their educational tracks (Kerckhoff, 2001; Sørensen, 1970).
Two extreme forms exist: highly selective systems, which allocate students to academic or vocational tracks based on exam

2.1. Stratification and inequality of educational opportunity
Existing literature has primarily examined the impact of stratification on IEO from two perspectives: academic-vocational tracking
and ability grouping. Tracking refers to assigning students to either an academic track aimed at further education or a vocational track
focused on employment, based on their abilities and interests. Ability grouping involves placing students into classes with varying
curriculum difficulty levels according to their academic ability.
Theoretical frameworks such as rational choice theory, life-course hypothesis, and school effects suggest that in countries with
more pronounced educational tracking, family background exerts a stronger influence on children’s academic achievement and
educational attainment. The underlying logic is that advantaged families tend to steer their children toward the academic track to
maintain social status. In contrast, disadvantaged families, constrained by limited resources and perceiving greater risks in the academic track, are more likely to enroll their children in vocational education (van de Werfhorst, 2018). Moreover, earlier tracking
intensifies IEO because parents and teachers have less accurate information about children’s abilities at younger ages. As a result, track
placement becomes more influenced by family background than by actual academic performance (Ballarino et al., 2016). Additionally,
compared to vocational tracks, academic tracks tend to have teachers with higher qualifications, more rigorous curricula, better
instructional quality, and more supportive peer environments, which further raise students’ academic achievement and educational
expectations (Heisig & Solga, 2015; Maaz et al., 2008; Terrin & Triventi, 2023; van de Werfhorst, 2018).
Empirical evidence supports these theoretical claims, showing that family background has a stronger effect on students’ educational outcomes in more highly tracked education systems (Burger, 2016; Horn, 2009; Hou, 2020; Lavrijsen & Nicaise, 2015; Marks,
2005; Pfeffer, 2008; Reichelt et al., 2019; van de Werfhorst, 2018, 2019).
Regarding ability grouping, theory posits that increasing the homogeneity of academic ability within classes allows teachers to
teach more efficiently. As a result, all students, regardless of social background, may achieve better learning outcomes, potentially
reducing IEO (Dollmann, 2019; Hallinan, 1994). However, the theory does not take into account that children from advantaged
families generally exhibit higher academic ability than those from disadvantaged families. As a result, children from disadvantaged
families may be partially excluded from higher-ability classes. Consequently, the empirical findings deviate from the theoretical expectations. Ability grouping often reinforces IEO, as students from more advantaged backgrounds are disproportionately placed in
higher-ability classes (Chmielewski, 2014; Feniger et al., 2021). These classes typically have more qualified teachers, higher
instructional quality (Hallinan, 1994), and offer greater emotional, organizational, and academic support (Mayer et al., 2018).
A key limitation of existing research is its narrow focus on stratification as general-vocational tracking and ability grouping,
neglecting stratification within general upper secondary education Significant disparities exist among general upper secondary schools
in terms of human, material, and financial resources. For example, in China, key high schools outperform non-key schools in teacher
quality, student intake, facilities, and funding (Wu, 2013; Ye, 2015).
High levels of SGUSE exacerbate IEO during compulsory education, especially at the lower secondary level. Consistent with the
logic of tracking and ability grouping, advantaged families seek to preserve their social status by securing early access to high-quality
education for their children. This early advantage boosts students’ motivation and ability, resulting in cumulative benefits over time
(Hou, 2020; Tang, 2015; Wu, 2020). Therefore, advantaged parents aim to convert their family capital into access to higher-quality
schools early in their children’s educational trajectories. Through strategies such as securing residence in desirable school districts,
they obtain access to high-quality lower secondary schools, laying the foundation for their children’s eventual admission to
high-quality upper secondary schools. Consequently, children from advantaged families often emerge as winners in school choice
competition, while those from disadvantaged families frequently become “losers,” effectively “falling behind at the starting line” of
life’s race (Li, 2021). Furthermore, attending a high-quality lower secondary school significantly improves academic achievement (Xie
& Zhang, 2018), further widening achievement gaps among students from different family backgrounds within highly stratified
general upper secondary schools.
Based on this, the study proposes the following hypothesis:
Hypothesis 1: Compared to education systems with low levels of SGUSE, those with high levels of SGUSE exhibit a stronger association between family background and children’s academic achievement.
2.2. Standardization, selectivity, and inequality of educational opportunity
Theoretically, standardization is expected to reduce IEO. First, lower levels of school autonomy limit the capacity of advantaged
families to influence school curricula, budgets, admissions, and personnel decisions (Horn, 2009; Park, 2008). Second, centralized
examinations provide objective and comparable signals of academic achievement, establishing an institutional basis for fair educational selection and reducing the influence of family background and teacher recommendations (Bishop, 1997, 1999; Wößmann,
2003). Third, transparency of student performance under centralized examinations diminishes information advantages held by high
socioeconomic status (SES) families, promoting educational equity (Bodovski et al., 2017; Strietholt et al., 2019).
Empirical studies support these theoretical claims. For example, in highly standardized education systems, parent-child communication—especially discussions about school performance—has a stronger positive effect on academic achievement among low-SES
families, whereas in less standardized education systems, this benefit is more pronounced among high-SES families (Park, 2008).
Furthermore, in education systems with higher levels of school autonomy, SES has a stronger impact on students’ mathematics and

reading literacy (Horn, 2009), while centralized examinations at the end of upper secondary education significantly reduce
achievement gaps related to family background (Wößmann, 2005).
However, some empirical findings contradict these theoretical expectations. Studies found no significant effect of school autonomy
on IEO (Bodovski et al., 2020). Centralized examinations at both lower and upper secondary education do not always reduce IEO
(Horn, 2009). The discrepancies in empirical findings may be attributable to differences in the datasets used, the countries included in
the analyses, and the measurement of standardization. For example, studies supporting the view that school autonomy exacerbates IEO
rely on PISA 2003 data and the corresponding measurement of school autonomy (Horn, 2009). By comparison, studies that do not
support this view use TIMSS 2011 and PISA 2009 data along with their respective measurements (Bodovski et al., 2020).
Regarding selectivity, theory suggests that in highly selective education systems, students’ academic performance mainly determines their track placement. This reduces the influence of family background on track choice (secondary effect). However, primary
effect remains relatively stable. Consequently, overall IEO decreases (Dollmann, 2019; Jackson & Jonsson, 2013; Traini, 2022).
Empirical evidence partly supports this view, showing that higher selectivity reduces IEO (Dollmann, 2011; 2016; Hu et al., 2020).
However, other studies find no significant reduction (Horn, 2009; Roth & Siegert, 2015; 2016). Some even report increased inequality
under high selectivity (Jackson & Jonsson, 2013). The lack of robustness in empirical findings may also stem from differences in
research contexts. Studies supporting the view that selection reduces IEO are primarily based on changes in teacher recommendation
policies in a specific region of Germany (Dollmann, 2011; 2016). In comparison, studies that do not support this view mainly draw on
policy changes across different regions of Germany and employ more rigorous causal inference strategies (Roth & Siegert, 2015; 2016).
A key limitation of prior research is that it often combines lower and upper secondary school students, focusing mainly on how
centralized examinations at the end of upper secondary education impact the relationship between family background and children’s
academic achievement. However, the design and role of centralized examinations at the end of lower secondary education vary
significantly across countries. For example, with respect to implementation, there is no centralized examination at the end of lower
secondary education in Hong Kong (China) or the United States, whereas such examinations do exist in Taiwan (China) and the United
Kingdom. In terms of functional orientation, the centralized examination at the end of lower secondary education in France is a
criterion-referenced examination, primarily designed to assess graduates’ knowledge and skill levels. By contrast, the corresponding
examination in the United Kingdom is a competitive examination, which is used to identify and select high-achieving lower secondary
graduates (Lamb, 2011; Liu et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2014). These centralized examinations may exert a more immediate and direct
influence on the association between family background and children’s academic achievement during the lower secondary stage.
Centralized examinations at the end of lower secondary education serve two main purposes. First, they act as a credential and basis
for further education, strongly influencing students’ educational trajectories. Second, they monitor and evaluate schools or the overall
education system. Only when the first purpose is fulfilled do these examinations substantially alter incentives and behaviors among
parents, students, principals, and teachers. Specifically, advantaged parents strive to secure early advantages for their children by
enrolling them in high-quality lower secondary schools. This enhances exam performance and helps accumulate cumulative benefits.
Students, as direct exam participants, are incentivized to compete intensely (Baker et al., 2001; Hannum et al., 2019) Moreover,
centralized examinations at the end of lower secondary education serve as the primary institutional tool for monitoring and evaluating
school teaching quality. School reputation and quality largely depend on overall academic achievement measured by these exams,
creating strong incentives for principals and teachers to enhance student performance (Bishop, 1997, 1999). Principals, aiming to
enhance school competitiveness, employ various strategies to attract high-achieving students. They also enhance teaching quality by
hiring excellent teachers and improving educational facilities. Teachers also prioritize academic outcomes due to performance evaluations and incentives. Clearly, parents from advantaged families have greater capacity to invest in their children’s education. They
can mobilize various resources to secure admission to high-quality lower secondary schools, which significantly enhance student
academic achievement. This, in turn, ensures their children’s success in selective centralized examinations, thereby exacerbating IEO.
The first purpose of centralized examinations reflects the selectivity dimension of education systems. Therefore, this study integrates standardization and selectivity to construct an indicator of HST at the end of lower secondary education. HST refers to
standardized, centralized examinations administered by national, provincial, municipal authorities, or upper secondary schools, which
play a decisive role in admission to upper secondary education. Such exams must meet two conditions: (1) standardization: the existence of a centralized examination at the end of lower secondary education organized by governmental or school authorities; (2)
selectivity: the exam results affect students’ placement into upper secondary schools.
Based on the above analysis, the study proposes the following hypothesis:
Hypothesis 2: Compared to education systems without HST for transition from lower to upper secondary education, systems with
such testing exhibit a stronger association between family background and children’s academic achievement.
3. Research design
3.1. Data
The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) constitutes one of the most extensively utilized data sources in crossnational studies on IEO. Implemented by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), PISA aims to evaluate

the ability of 15-year-old students to apply their knowledge and skills in mathematics, reading, and science to real-life challenges.
Since its inception in 2000, PISA has been conducted every three years, with eight cycles completed to date.3 The survey employs a
two-stage sampling design. In the first stage, schools within each country are selected with probability proportional to size, meaning
larger schools have a higher chance of being sampled. In the second stage, students are randomly sampled within sampled schools,
with all eligible students having an equal probability of selection (Adams & Wu, 2002; Wößmann, 2005).
This study draws on PISA data from the 2015 and 2018 cycles to examine the impact of education systems on IEO. Three main
reasons underlie this choice. First, combining two cycles increases the sample size of countries, schools, and students, thereby
enhancing the reliability of the empirical findings. Second, the relatively short interval between the 2015 and 2018 surveys implies
minimal changes in education systems across countries during this period. Third, although the PISA 2022 data have been released, this
study excludes them for two reasons: (1) the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in low response rates in 13 participating countries, which
may compromise data quality (OECD, 2023); and (2) pandemic-related disruptions to secondary education across much of the world
may affect the comparability and reliability of student performance data.
3.2. Variable
3.2.1. Dependent variable
The dependent variable in this study is academic achievement, measured by proficiency in mathematics, reading, and science. To
ensure the accuracy and scientific rigor of the assessments, PISA 2015 and 2018 provide ten plausible values for each domain.
Following previous research (Chen, 2020; Xu, 2023), this study calculates weighted averages of the plausible values to obtain scores for
each domain. An overall academic achievement score is then derived by computing a weighted average of the mathematics, reading,
and science scores.
3.2.2. Independent variable
The independent variable in this study is family background, measured by the index of economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS)
provided by the PISA. This index is constructed based on three dimensions: parental level of education, parental occupational status,
and home possessions.
3.2.3. Moderator variable
The moderator variable is the education system, measured by two indicators: SGUSE and HST at the transition from lower to upper
secondary education.
The SGUSE in each country is calculated using PISA 2015 and 2018 data as follows: First, the standard deviation of academic
achievement among all general upper secondary schools within each country is computed and denoted as SDc; second, a weighted
average of these standard deviations across all countries is calculated, denoted as M; third, countries are classified into high or low
stratification groups based on M. High stratification is defined as SDc > M and coded as 1; low stratification is defined as SDc ≤ M and
coded as 0.
The presence of HST at the transition from lower to upper secondary education refers to centralized examinations conducted by
national, state (provincial), municipal authorities that assess the academic achievement of lower secondary graduates, or entrance
exams administered by upper secondary schools, which substantially affect admission decisions. HST must meet two conditions: (1)
standardization, meaning a centralized examination is in place; (2) selectivity, meaning the examination functions as a selection tool
that influences upper secondary school admission. If a country meets both conditions, it is considered to have HST and is coded as 1;
otherwise, it is coded as 0.
The HST variable is constructed based on publicly available sources from three main channels: (1) monographs; (2) academic
literature—searches conducted in the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) database using keywords such as “[Country] +
high school,” “[Country] + secondary school,” and “[Country] + education,” followed by searches in Google Scholar with the same
keywords; (3) online resources—initially using Google with “[Country] + Ministry of Education” to locate official education ministry
websites, then searching databases including UNESCO International Bureau of Education, European Commission, Nuffic, and Wikiwand for relevant information, and finally using Google to search keywords “[Country] + high school,” “[Country] + secondary
school,” and “[Country] + education” to supplement and verify data.
3.2.4. Control variable
Following prior studies (Hou, 2020; Hu et al., 2022; Hu & Yin, 2021; Sciffer et al., 2022), this study includes gender, age, grade, and
immigration status as control variables. Immigration status has three categories: non-immigrant, second-generation immigrant, and
first-generation immigrant. Additionally, the main language spoken at home, which affects academic achievement (Lopez-Agudo et al.,
2021), is included with two categories: home-test language consistency (HTLC) and other languages. The survey year is also controlled
for in the models.

3.3. Research strategy
In cross-national comparative research, two main approaches have been used to test cross-level interactions: multilevel model and
country fixed effects model. Multilevel model is widely applied for nested data because they decompose between- and within-group
variance and address the non-independence of error terms. However, when the number of countries is small, limiting the control of
country-level confounders; when certain country-level confounders are unobservable; or when error terms violate the normality
assumption, existing studies argue that country fixed effects model is preferable. This model incorporates a series of country dummy
variables to control for all heterogeneity across countries, thus avoiding omitted variable bias at the country level, satisfying the
normality assumption of error terms, and producing estimates less sensitive to the number of countries. Consequently, fixed effects
model yields more accurate coefficient estimates for cross-level interactions (Bol et al., 2014; Bryan & Jenkins, 2016; Höppner, 2021;
Mijs, 2016).
Therefore, this study employs country fixed effects model to examine the moderating effects of SGUSE and HST on the relationship
between family background and children’s academic achievement. The model specification is as follows:

4. Results
4.1. Descriptive analysis
Table 1 reports the relationship between family background and students’ academic achievement across 49 countries. Among
these, 46 countries show a positive correlation between family background and academic achievement, with Montenegro exhibiting
the strongest correlation and Tunisia the weakest. In 3 countries, the relationship between family background and academic
achievement is not statistically significant.
Fig. 1 compares the strength of the association between family background and students’ academic achievement across education
systems with high and low levels of SGUSE. The figure shows that, compared to lowly stratified systems, the correlation between family
background and academic achievement is stronger in highly stratified education systems.
Fig. 2 compares the correlation coefficients of family background and students’ academic achievement between education systems
with and without HST at the transition from lower to upper secondary education. It shows that the relationship between family
background and academic achievement is stronger in education systems with HST compared to those without.
In addition, we conducted T-test to examine the relationships between SGUSE, HST, and the coefficient of family background. The
results indicate that SGUSE is not significantly associated with the coefficient of family background, whereas the association between
HST and the coefficient of family background is statistically significant only at the 10 % level. To more rigorously assess the relationships between SGUSE, HST, and IEO, further analyses will further employ country fixed-effects model.
4.2. Education systems and inequality of educational opportunity
This section employs country fixed effects model to further examine the moderating effect of SGUSE and HST on IEO. Model 1 in
Table 2 includes the interaction term between family background and SGUSE. The results indicate that in education systems with high
levels of SGUSE, the relationship between family background and students’ academic achievement is greater. This finding suggests that
stratification enhance IEO, which is consistent with prior studies (Chmielewski, 2014; Feniger et al., 2021; Lavrijsen & Nicaise, 2015;
van de Werfhorst, 2018).
Model 2 builds on Model 1 by adding the interaction term between family background and HST. Results show that in education
systems with HST, the relationship between family background and academic achievement is greater. In Model 2, the coefficient for
the interaction term between family background and SGUSE decreases but remains significant. Hypotheses 1 and 2 are supported.

5. Conclusion and discussion
This study investigates how the general upper secondary education system shapes IEO during the lower secondary stage. Specifically, it examines the moderating effects of SGUSE and HST at the transition from lower to upper secondary education on the relationship between family background and students’ academic achievement. The findings reveal that in education systems with highly
stratified general upper secondary schools and the presence of HST, the association between family background and academic
achievement is significantly stronger.
This study contributes to the literature in two key ways. First, existing research has primarily examined the stratification dimension
of educational systems in relation to IEO through the lenses of tracking and ability grouping, while the standardization dimension has
typically been assessed by focusing on centralized examinations administered at the end of upper secondary education. However,
limited attention has been paid to how SGUSE and the presence of HST at the transition from lower to upper secondary education affect
IEO. This study addresses this gap by measuring the stratification dimension of education systems using SGUSE and capturing both
standardization and selectivity dimensions through the presence of HST during this critical transition. In doing so, it sheds light on
underexplored institutional characteristics and expands the empirical evidence on the role of education systems in shaping IEO.
Second, prior studies have collected data on centralized examinations for the transition from upper secondary school to university,
but data on centralized examinations at the transition from lower to upper secondary education remain scarce. Drawing on monographs, academic literature, and online sources, this study compiles data on whether centralized examinations exist at the lower-toupper secondary transition across countries, and whether admission to upper secondary schools is based on these exam results. This
constitutes a critical data contribution for investigating IEO during the compulsory education stage.
While these findings advance understanding of the relationship between education systems and IEO, several limitations remain.
The analysis covers 49 countries, a sample larger than most existing studies, yet the sample size could be further expanded. Future
research should aim to collect more comprehensive education system data to broadly examine this relationship. Additionally,
representativeness issues may exist within countries. For example, PISA 2015 and 2018 surveyed only schools in Beijing, Shanghai,
Jiangsu, Guangdong, and Zhejiang—economically developed provinces/cities in mainland China—which may not represent the entire
mainland Chinese context. Finally, given that the measurement of SGUSE is sensitive to variations in student-, school-, and countrylevel sample sizes, its moderating effect on the relationship between family background and students’ academic achievement may not
be robust.
