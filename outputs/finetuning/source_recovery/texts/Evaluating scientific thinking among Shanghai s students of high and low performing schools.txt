ABSTRACT
Shanghai is dubbed as a role model for science and mathematics education as its fifteen-year-olds
have been outperforming all in the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA) since
2009. Shanghai’s achievements are attributed to its interest in adopting innovative international
trends in education and equally effectively implementing these at its high and low-performing
schools. One such trend in science education is based on the univariable and Control of Variable
(CoV) strategies. This model is also used in constructing higher-order thinking items in science
and mathematics assessments in PISA. Our first objective was to understand if students of
Shanghai mastered the CoV strategies. Beyond CoV models, the emerging trend in science educa-
tion promotes multivariable thinking among young adolescents. Our second objective was to
understand if Shanghai has adopted this emerging trend and prepared its students on multivari-
able thinking. Using specially designed and previously validated assessments, we measured and
compared the CoV and multivariable thinking skills of fifteen-year-olds representing one high and
one low-performing school. Our results highlighted the equally exceptional performance of both
schools in the CoV tasks and comparatively poor performance in the multivariable causal reason-
ing and prediction tasks. These findings may offer one aspect to understand Shanghai’s perform-
ance
in
the
PISA,
at
the
same
time
highlight
the
weaknesses
in
its
contemporary
science education.

Introduction
The 2009 results of the Program for International Student
Assessment (PISA) caught the attention of the world when
Shanghai outperformed all the major countries in science
and mathematics. Considering that PISA assesses higher-
order thinking skills, including critical thinking and prob-
lem-solving abilities of fifteen-year-old school students in
science and mathematics (OECD, 2015), it was surprising
for both national and international critics to comprehend
Shanghai’s performance (for a detailed review, see Zhang &
Akbik, 2012). Nevertheless, Shanghai has maintained its top
position in science and mathematics in the PISA of 2011,
2015, and 2018. This continuous educational development of
Shanghai attracted different countries, which looked toward
Shanghai for teaching styles of science and mathematics
(Coughlan, 2014; Gibb, 2015; OECD 2010, 2011).
Since China has historically cherished the traditional
standardized examinations that promote drills and rote
memorization (Tan & Ng, 2018), how students of Shanghai
excelled in the higher-order thinking skills became a topic
of debate in academic circles. Some criticized the education
system of Shanghai as ‘elitist,’ one that rewards the high per-
formers and abandoned the low performing students, thus
producing ‘test-taker experts’ who perform exceptionally
well in the examinations of any sort, including PISA, with-
out
having
mastery
over
higher-order
thinking
skills
(Hongbing, 2010; Ringmar, 2013). Others criticized the high
scores of students which are achieved at the cost of a heavy
workload, low social skills, and insufficient development of
creativity (Xie, 2010; Zhang & Akbik, 2012; Zhao, 2011).
Most highlighted criticism, which is also relevant to this
study, was related to the representativeness of the sample of
PISA, questioning whether Shanghai chose the high-per-
forming schools or students for the test and that the low-
performing schools of suburban areas would struggle in
such international tests (Dillon, 2010).
Liang et al. (2016) have presented a comprehensive
response to these criticisms and highlighted different reform
initiatives behind Shanghai’s success. They stressed on the
leading position Shanghai has taken in China’s socioeco-
nomic and educational reforms since 1978, which made it
sensitive to global trends. Shanghai has adopted an open-
door policy and opened itself up for innovative international
trends in education, most of which are mastered with trial
and error (Zhang & Akbik, 2012). One such contemporary
model of science education is based on the univariable

interesting question that has never been raised in evaluating
Shanghai’s performance in PISA.
Although CoV is an important strategy, in the real world
of science and scientific thinking, multiple variables con-
tinue to be a major presence. This limitation of CoV models
raised the importance of multivariable thinking— the coord-
ination of the effects of multiple variables on an outcome
(Kuhn et al., 2008). This emerging trend in science educa-
tion is getting its place in the West. Has Shanghai already
adopted this emerging trend? Most importantly, are these
scientific thinking skills, i.e., CoV and multivariable think-
ing, equally promoted among the students of high and low-
achieving schools around the greater Shanghai region? All
these unanswered questions reflect a gap in the growing
body of literature around Shanghai’s educational reforms
and its performance in science. This study attempts to
bridge this gap by measuring fifteen-year-old students’ CoV
and multivariable thinking skills using specially designed
and previously validated assessments. Focusing on a high
and a low-performing school located in the greater Shanghai
region, this study offers a new perspective on students’ sci-
entific thinking through their CoV and multivariable think-
ing skills.
Literature review
Much of scientific thinking and scientific theory building
pertains to the development of causal models between varia-
bles of interest. The most common approach used in science
education for the causal models is referred to as Control of
Variable (CoV), which, as mentioned earlier, is embedded in
the national curricula of science education of many devel-
oped countries.
CoV
The concept of a variable and its primary role in doing sci-
ence is usually introduced in early grades. This concept is
usually introduced through a simple experiment: ‘An out-
come O occurs when a positive level (e.g. a flame) of an
antecedent variable A, such as heat, is introduced, but O
fails to occur when this positive level of A is absent (e.g. no
flame or other heating agent is present)’ (Kuhn, 2016, p.
393). This experiment is based on single variable logic,
where all other variables are removed or kept constant
except the focal one to assess its influence on an outcome
(Zimmerman, 2000, 2007). This strategy is usually referred
to as CoV and was first introduced by Inhelder and
Piaget (1958).
These univariable designed experiments help students in
constructing data that influence their conceptual representa-
tions of a single cause and effect (Koerber et al., 2005; Rind
et al., 2019; Rind & Kadiwal, 2016).
However, this model has its limitations. For example, it
has been argued that construal of mechanism and processing
of evidence very often function together to support causal
inference (Keil, 2006; Klahr, 2002; Koslowski, 1996). With
this assumption, student’s data-influenced conceptual repre-
sentations of causal effects should be facilitated by generat-
ing plausible mechanisms that might underlie such effects
(Kuhn, 2016). However, if a student’s explanation and avail-
able evidence conflict, it is most probable that the student’s
explanation will overpower the evidence (Williams et al.,
2013). Therefore, students must not only learn to construct
data-based explanations but also learn to evaluate whether
their explanations are correct. These limitations of the uni-
variable model have generated concerns and criticisms
among educators and researchers who encourage science as
inquiry and that scientific practice should be enriched by
contextualization through the use of a more authentic multi-
variable model (Duschl, 2008; Kuhn, 2016, 2018; Williams
et al., 2013).
Multivariable model
The advocates of the multivariable model argue that the
purpose of science education is not only to prepare students
to deal with the problems presented in the controlled envir-
onment of laboratories but to prepare them for real-life
problems. In real-world situations, however, single variables
rarely contribute to an outcome. A combination of multiple
variables jointly influences, additively or interactivity, to an
outcome, a concept contrary to the CoV approach. The abil-
ity to realize the power of multivariable on an outcome
enhances students’ deep analytical and predictive skills
(Kuhn, 2016; Kuhn et al., 2015). Moreover, students need to
comprehend that ‘covariation between two variables need
not be perfect in order for a relationship to exist between
them because effects of other factors, as well as measure-
ment error, are likely to play a role’ (Kuhn et al., 2017, p.
234). This understanding of multivariable causality is a
move toward authentic scientific thinking (Bo et al., 2020;
Rind, in press).

Participants
Participants were 26 fifteen-year-old 9th-grade students (with
an equal representation of males and females) drawn from
one HPS located in downtown Shanghai and 29 students of
the same age and grade (female 58%) from one LPS located
in the outskirts of the Jinshan district of Shanghai. Schools
in Shanghai are usually tagged as high or low-performing by
the school district administrators using a broader evaluation
and accountability practices (see Jensen & Farmer, 2013 for
a detailed review). Since we were interested in students’

Assessments
Three assessments used in this study were prepared by
Kuhn and colleagues for the students of the same age group.
The first task assessed students’ CoV skills. The next two
tasks assessed students’ multivariable thinking, particularly
their multivariable causal reasoning skills, and multivariable
analysis and prediction skills.
Assessment of CoV skills
This assessment was adopted from Kuhn et al. (2017), which
reads as follows:
The city of New York wants to get new trains for the subway.
They can make trains with short or long Car Size and they can
make trains with four or six Number of Wheels
This description was followed by three pairs of multiple-
choice items. Each pair presents a case for students to evalu-
ate regarding different car designs and offers different justi-
fications to students for their choice. The first item of each
pair had four options, each option having two images of car
designs with different sizes and several wheels. The second
item of the pair had four options, each option presenting
different justifications related to the first item (see Figure 1
for an example). Students were required to evaluate and
identify the correct design and justification for each pair.
The aim was to assess how many students from each group
consistently constructed a control comparison across three
cases as well as provided appropriate justifications.
Assessment of multiple causal reasoning skills
The second test was adopted from Kuhn et al. (2015) to
assess students’ multiple causal reasoning skills. It was based
on

Validation and pilot testing
All three assessments we translated into Mandarin and pilot
tested on a separate group of students. To check the validity
of the translated assessments, we followed different proce-
dures Kuhn and colleagues used in their studies. First, we
selected 10 students from the LPS. One of the researchers,
in the presence of facilitating teacher, instructed students on
the first task and allowed them sufficient time to complete
it. In the end, the facilitating teacher asked students to point
out any word or phase in the task or in the oral instruction
of the task that they couldn’t understand. Meanwhile, the
researcher evaluated the scores of randomly selected three
students. The researcher, then, conducted an oral examin-
ation of each of the three students separately. During the
oral examination, students were asked 3 to 4 questions con-
structed on the CoV principle. Their responses were scored,
which were then compared with their scores on the CoV
task. The same process was repeated for task 2 and task 3.
The comparative analysis of scores of tasks and oral exami-
nations suggested that all three tasks assessed what they
were intended to assess.
Based on students’ feedback on the tasks, some words
and
phrases
were
modified
for
better
comprehension.
Students viewed the researcher as an outsider, thus experi-
enced fear and anxiety. Therefore, it was decided that facili-
tating teachers from each school would conduct the real
tests for the ease and comfort of the students.
Analysis and results
Findings on COV skills
This task attempted to answer the first research question,
i.e., how students of HPS and LPS performed on the CoV
assessment and if there is any significant difference in the
performance of both schools. It is done by analyzing stu-
dents’ skills to consistently construct a control comparison
and provide appropriate justification across all the three
cases presented in the task. The results show that 24 of 26
students (92%) of HPS, and 26 of 29 students (90%) of LPS
consistently constructed a controlled comparison across all
three cases, with no significant difference in the perform-
ance of both groups, X2(1) ¼ .525, p ¼ .469.
Similarly, 22 students (84%) of HPS and 25 students
(86%) of LPS consistently constructed a controlled compari-
son and also provided appropriate justifications across all
three cases, with no significant difference in the perform-
ance of both groups, X2(1) ¼ .035, p ¼ .853.
These findings suggest that students of both schools have
a good command of the problems based on CoV principles
and that there is no significant difference in their perform-
ance on the CoV task.

Discussion and conclusion
The present study offers a new perspective on scientific
thinking skills among the Shanghai students of high and
low-performing schools. The findings show that the students
of both schools performed well in the CoV task and that
there is no significant difference in the performance of both
schools. As mentioned earlier that the CoV task was adopted
from Kuhn and colleagues who used it to measure the CoV
skills among the USA students of the same age. They found
exceptional results on this task by those students who were
specially trained on these CoV skills for weeks (Jewett &
Kuhn, 2016; Kuhn et al., 2017). In this study, however,
Shanghai students of both high and low-performing schools
scored exceptionally well on the same task without any
training. This suggests that Shanghai schools, either high or
low-performing, have been effectively teaching CoV strat-
egies through the regular science curriculum. These findings
may also provide a new perspective to explain Shanghai’s
good performance in the PISA, considering that most of the
higher-order items in the science and mathematics section
of PISA are based on the CoV principle (OECD 2015).
The good performance of the students on the CoV task
may offer a glimpse of the impact of recent reforms in sci-
ence and mathematics education in Shanghai that have spe-
cifically focused on the development of scientific thinking
through inquiry curriculum and teaching (Liang et al.,
2016). The review of the science and mathematics curricu-
lum suggests that many activities are mainly based on the
CoV principle (Ma, 2016). Teachers have also trained them-
selves accordingly. The multi-layered training offered to the
teachers at different stages of their careers reinforces the use
of CoV strategies in their teaching (Yao & Guo, 2018).
Besides, each school is allowed to develop contextually rele-
vant, inquiry-based courses and introduce at least one of
such courses from grades 1 to 5, and two courses from
grades 6 to 9. Such freedom in course design and implemen-
tation may allow teachers to be more creative in their teach-
ing (Liang et al., 2016).
At the same time, the equally good performance of LPS
on the CoV task in this study may offer a glimpse of educa-
tional reforms Shanghai introduced to deal with the regional
disparity in teaching quality. Using different financing, man-
agement, and twinning strategies, Shanghai has improved
the low-performing schools in many ways. For example, the
municipal government transfers half of education surplus
taxes to economically weak districts, and mandates that top
secondary schools reserve some spaces for middle school
graduates from poor districts (Liang et al., 2016). The most
important
initiative
has
been
the
introduction
of
an
‘entrusted school’ management system (Jensen & Farmer,
2013), which allows additional financing, management, and
professional support from HPSs to LPSs. This marriage
between HPSs and LPSs generates mutual professional
development training, teacher and student exchanges, and
joint goals (Liu, 2016).
Despite the remarkable performance on the CoV task,
students of both schools performed poorly on the multivari-
able causal reasoning and prediction tasks. These findings
highlight that although the students reflected developed sci-
entific thinking skills based on the CoV principle, there are
opportunities to achieve what Kuhn et al. (2017, p. 247) call
‘authentic scientific practice’ that incorporates multivariable
thinking among students. Kuhn et al. (2017) argue that the
execution of CoV-based experiments presents a ‘narrow slice
of authentic scientific inquiry and arguably needs to be
enriched by contextualization in a more authentic multivari-
able model’ (p. 233). Authentic scientific practice prepares
students for real-life situations. Everyone makes inferences
about causality as a part of thinking (Bo et al., 2020; Rind,
2014, 2015, 2016; Rind et al., 2016, 2019). However, single
cause thinking only prepares students for the controlled
environment
of
laboratories.
Although
contemporary
research on developing scientific thinking skills involves the
management of multiple variables, the focus is on neutraliz-
ing the effects of additional variables to ascertain the effect
of a single variable on an outcome. This trend in science
education has attracted Shanghai, which adopted this model
and mastered it, as reflected in the PISA scores as well as
from the findings of the current study.
The findings of this study have wider implications
beyond Shanghai, as this study attempted to highlight one
of the core principles, i.e., CoV, of scientific thinking used
in the national curricula of science all around the world,
and showed its limitations. The emerging research in scien-
tific thinking argues that mastery over the use of CoV

reflective in the performance of the students of both schools

and prediction task, students made the faulty predictions

across

(in

context
in

Kuhn et al. (2008) explained these faulty errors of the
individuals by conceptualizing the relationship between CoV
and multivariable analysis. They argued that both CoV and
multivariable analysis and prediction can be regarded in an
analysis of variance (ANOVA) framework. The first assump-
tion of ANOVA is that causes have consistent effects under
the same considerations, and second that multiple effects
may operate jointly on an outcome, in either additive or
interactive manner. However, the faulty errors made by
Kuhn and Dean’s (2004) participants as well the students in
this study, show a pattern, which violated the analysis of
variance assumptions of consistency and additivity of effects.
Kuhn and Dean (2004) characterized these patterns as
reflecting an immature mental model of multivariable caus-
ality. This study as well as earlier research (Dean Jr & Kuhn,
2007; Kuhn & Dean Jr, 2004; Rind, in press; Zohar, 1995)
makes it clear that children and even many adults can not
readily conceptualize additive main effects, much less the
interaction
effects.
This
calls
to
upgrade
the
current
approach that conceptualizes scientific thinking using uni-
variable models. There is a need to move to multivariable
models to achieve a more authentic scientific thinking.
We conclude with an acknowledgment that the conclu-
sions drawn from this study are not representative of all of
Shanghai. We used a very small sample of students from
only two schools to make our case. However, what we have
tried to show here is that Shanghai has great potential to
bring highly effective change to its education system. It
adopted the univariable and CoV models in its science edu-
cation and has surprised the world with its extraordinary
performance in PISA since 2009. However, the contempor-
ary discourse in science education is moving slightly away
from univariable models toward multivariable models. This
may be something that Shanghai and the rest of the world
need to consider for the future educational reforms.
