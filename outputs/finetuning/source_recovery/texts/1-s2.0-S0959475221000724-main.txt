In the present study, we consider teachers’ self-efficacy as indicator for teacher motivation on the teacher level
and at the school-level. We examine the interplay of teachers’ individual self-efficacy (subscales classroom
management and instruction) with school-level efficacy and more-external school characteristics (collaboration,
instructional leadership, and participation) when predicting teachers’ self-reported cognitive activation and
classroom management. Austrian TALIS 2018 data from 4255 teachers at 246 schools were analyzed. Multilevel
analyses revealed relationships between teachers’ self-efficacy and self-reported cognitive activation and
classroom management both, at the teacher and school levels. For self-reported cognitive activation, no significant context effect or cross-level interaction was found. Classroom management differed depending on whether
a teacher was situated in a low- or a high-efficacy school (context effect). Also, teacher collaboration moderated
the association of teachers’ self-efficacy and their self-reported classroom management (cross-level interaction).
The findings indicate the importance of addressing the school context when investigating teacher self-efficacy.

1. Introduction
Extant research on teacher motivation has been consistently
increasing and has identified teacher motivation as a relevant predictor
of positive educational outcomes, such as student engagement,
instructional quality, and teacher job satisfaction (e.g., Klassen & Chiu,
2010). A multitude of different constructs are investigated under the
term motivation (e.g., autonomous motivation, goal orientation, interest). In the present study, we focus on teacher self-efficacy as a
well-established motivational construct. Studies on teacher self-efficacy
investigate how teachers’ individual self-efficacy is related to educational outcomes (e.g., Zee & Koomen, 2016). As such, studies are primarily dominated by an individual teacher perspective. As most teachers
teach their classes individually, this individual perspective is legitimate.
Since teachers interact with colleagues and collaborate with their
principals, they do not operate in a fully autonomous manner but are
also bound in certain ways to the conditions in the schools at which they
teach. Although, more than a decade ago, Woolfolk Hoy (2008) called
for addressing contextual factors when investigating teacher motivation,
research on teacher self-efficacy in particular, and on teacher motivation

in general, still hardly addresses school characteristics.
Interactions between individual teachers and the school context are
relevant in three perspectives: First, with regard to the relationship
between self-efficacy and teaching behavior at the school level, selfefficacy beliefs are supposed to be related to behavior (Bandura,
1997). Until now, it is not yet clear whether the relationships between
teacher self-efficacy and outcome behavior on the individual teacher
level can be generalized to the organizational level of a school. Second,
beyond research on teacher collaboration, the interrelations between
individual teachers with their colleagues has hardly been addressed. It
may make a difference whether an individual teacher is together with
high or low efficacious colleagues. Thus, the school-level efficacy
(school composition of teacher self-efficacy) may be a relevant context
affecting individual teachers’ outcomes (Chen & Kanfer, 2006). Third,
schools may provide resources for teachers’ instruction. Gitomer and
Bell (2013) as well as Youngs et al. (2015) claim that research on
teachers’ instruction needs to address school context characteristics. To
investigate school characteristics, they should not only be measured at
the teacher level, but rather be modeled at the school level. Finally, the
relevance of individual self-efficacy beliefs may depend on school

In the present study, we extend research on teacher self-efficacy and
its relationship to teachers’ self-reported instructional quality by
examining the complex interplay between individual teachers with
school-level processes and colleagues characteristics. To find out more
about possible interactions with the school context, it is crucial to apply
a multilevel perspective and to assess not only self-efficacy beliefs and
outcome behavior but also school characteristics from multiple teachers
within a school. We first examined whether the relationship between
teacher self-efficacy and self-reported instructional quality at the individual teacher level can be established at the school level as well. We
therefore modeled teacher self-efficacy as not only an individual variable but also as a school variable. We additionally examined whether the
school context (school-level efficacy, collaboration, leadership, and
participation) affects individual teachers’ self-reported instructional
quality beyond teacher’s individual self-efficacy (context effect). Third,
we examine cross-level interactions and assessed whether the relationship between teacher self-efficacy and self-reported instructional quality
depends on school characteristics. Thus, we aimed to gain more differential knowledge on how the relationship between individual teacher
self-efficacy and self-reported instructional quality may be affected by
school variables.

both on the individual and the group level.
Teachers are embedded within the organizational context of a
school, led by a principal, and filled with interactions with colleagues.
Teachers form a “collective system by which team members coordinate
direction, intensity, persistence of efforts” (Chen & Kanfer, 2006, p.
233). Therefore, when focusing exclusively on the teacher level, interactions with the school, the colleagues, and the principals are
neglected. To address efficacy beliefs at the group level, Bandura (1997)
introduced the concept of collective efficacy as “a group’s shared belief
in its conjoint capabilities” (p. 477) for attaining a specific goal. According to Bandura (1997), this belief in conjoint capabilities can be
measured in two ways: (1) individual appraisals of personal capabilities
aggregated to the group level and (2) individual appraisals of the group
capability aggregated at the group level. Goddard et al. (2000) delineate
that collective efficacy form group norms that may instigate positive
interactions predicting instructional quality. In line with this, teacher
collective efficacy – with focus on group capability – has been shown to
be related to several outcome variables, such as teacher’s job satisfaction
(Klassen, 2010), teacher collaboration (Meyer et al., 2020), or student
achievement (Goddard et al., 2000). However, little is yet known about
the association of teacher school-level efficacy with instructional quality/teaching behavior as well as about the interplay between
school-level efficacy and individual efficacy beliefs.
In order to find out more about the role of the school as an organizational unit and the interactions among teachers, it is crucial to separate the teacher level from school-level associations with relevant
outcome variables. Therefore, in order to understand whether teacher
self-efficacy has comparable associations with relevant outcome variables on the teacher as well as the school level, we apply a multilevel
perspective and address teachers’ self-efficacy both as a variable related
to individual teachers’ self-reported instructional quality and as a
school-level variable (individual appraisals of personal capabilities
aggregated to the group level; Bandura, 1997) related to school performance (pure Level 2 associations).

1.1. Teacher efficacy and instructional quality: multilevel considerations
Self-efficacy can be defined as an individual’s belief in their capacity
to execute specific behaviors (see, e.g., Bandura, 1997). With this definition, self-efficacy can be regarded as a precursor of motivation and is,
in turn, theoretically assumed to determine behavior. With regard to
teaching behavior, multiple aspects of instructional quality can be
distinguished (for an overview, see Klieme et al., 2009; Pianta & Hamre,
2009). In the present study, we focus on cognitive activation and
classroom management. Cognitive activation aims at students’ understanding and has been shown to predict student achievement (Baumert
et al., 2010). Classroom management describes efficient time use and an
orderly classroom environment, monitored by the teacher (Emmer &
Stough, 2001), and has been shown to be related to cognitive as well as
emotional student outcomes (Kunter et al., 2013).
Domain-/Task-specific aspects of teacher self-efficacy have been
established comparable to those of instructional quality. Tschannen-Moran and Woolfolk Hoy, (2001) differentiated between teaching efficacy for student engagement, instructional strategies, and classroom
management in order to touch the domain- and task-specific nature of
efficacy beliefs. In line with the present focus on cognitive activation and
classroom management as indicators for instructional quality, we chose
teacher efficacy in instruction and in classroom management as relevant
predictor variables.
A multitude of empirical findings in the last few decades have
revealed relationships between teacher efficacy and outcome behaviors
(see Klassen and Tze, 2014 and Zee & Koomen, 2016 for review articles).
Recently, Lazarides et al. (2020) as well as Buric and Kim (2020) found
positive relationships between teacher self-efficacy and their
self-reported instructional quality and engagement. The vast literature
on teacher motivation and more specifically on teacher efficacy, however, examines teacher self-efficacy almost exclusively on the teacher/class level and rarely addresses the school level. At a first glance, it
might be straightforward to generalize results from the individual level
to the school level. However, there might be important discrepancies in
associations between variables across levels that may result from
divergent processes at play at each level potentially resulting in even
opposing associations among the same variables at different levels (e.g.,
Hamaker, 2012). In a school with a more self-efficacious staff, for
example, there might be less training offers, thus hampering the
achievement of the full potential at school level. There are several

1.2. School context effects on cognitive activation and classroom
management
When we address teacher self-efficacy and instructional quality from
a multilevel perspective, the question is not only whether relationships
at the teacher can be generalized to the school level. We also have the
opportunity to investigate whether the context in which teaching takes
place is related to the individual’s performance beyond the teacher’s
individual self-efficacy (unique context effect). Hence, it would be
assumed that cognitive activation and classroom management are not
only related to individual teacher’s self-efficacy, but also that school
variables have an effect over and above that. In other words, teachers
with the same level of self-efficacy may differ in their self-reported
cognitive activation and classroom management depending on which
school they are in.
In addition to the school-level degree of efficacy beliefs, several
school characteristics have been assumed to predict teaching (Hill et al.,
2015; Holzberger & Schiepe-Tiska, 2021). As conceptualized in models
within school effectiveness research (e.g., Scheerens, 2015), it is
reasonable that school variables, such as principal leadership or
collaboration among colleagues, affect teachers’ teaching. In our study,
we focus on teacher collaboration, principal leadership, and participation as school characteristics and will outline their importance. With
regard to principal leadership behavior, principals may collaborate with
teachers in order to solve classroom problems, may introduce new
teaching ideas, or provide feedback on classroom observations. These

motivated teachers reporting more or less instructional quality in a more

school with more or less instructional leadership. These multilevel

research designs when investigating instructional quality.

cognitive activation and classroom management both within

2. Are school environment characteristics (i.e., school-level efficacy,
collaboration, leadership, participation) associated with differences

3. Can school environmental characteristics moderate the relationship

1.3. Cross-level interactions of the school environment in the relationship
between self-efficacy and instructional quality
While cognitive activation and classroom management may be predicted by school characteristics over and above individual teacher selfefficacy (context effect), the question also arises whether the relationship between teacher self-efficacy and self-reported cognitive activation
and classroom management is independent from the context in which
teachers are in. Holzberger et al. (2014) found that high self-efficacy
elicited a positive teacher-student relationship (as an indicator of
instructional quality) only when teachers perceived their school environment to be supportive with respect to basic psychological needs. As
such, school environment variables may affect the efficiency of the individual teacher’s self-efficacy (a cross-level interaction effect).
Theories in the fields of work and organizational psychology (e.g.,
Cable & DeRue, 2002; Latham & Pinder, 2005) point to the relevance of
the interaction between personal and environmental characteristics that
can be decisive for outcome behavior. The relationship between personal characteristics and outcome behavior may vary depending on
environmental characteristics. Transferring this idea to the school
context, teachers’ self-efficacy may only, or more efficiently, translate
into high instructional quality when the school environment exhibits
certain characteristics. For example, low-efficacy teachers may need a
supportive environment (e.g., highly collaborative or highly efficacious
colleagues, high instructional leadership) in order to provide good
classroom management or high cognitive activation. Teachers with high
self-efficacy beliefs would be not as dependent on the level of collaboration or the self-efficacy of their colleagues. Reflecting on strong and
weak situations (e.g., Meyer & Dalal, 2009), a teacher’s self-efficacy
might translate more efficiently into instructional quality at schools
that offer more opportunities to participate (a weak situation, allowing
for interindividual differences) whereas teachers’ self-efficacy might be
less relevant in schools that offer less opportunities to participate (strong
situation). Thus, in the present study we will analyze whether school
characteristics moderate the relationship between teacher self-efficacy
and self-reported cognitive activation and classroom management.

3. Methods
3.1. Sample
For this study, we re-analyzed data from the OECD Teaching and
Learning International Survey 2018 (TALIS 2018; OECD, 2019). Fortyeight countries participated in TALIS 2018 across all ISCED levels. The
present analyses are based on country-level data from Austria. Austria
participated at the ISCED level 2. In the Austrian sample, 4255 teachers
from 246 participating schools answered a questionnaire. Most of the
schools were located in large cities (n = 58), smaller cities (n = 41) or
villages (n = 41), and fewer in small towns (n = 14) and towns (n = 14; n
= 78 location not stated). On average each school had a total staff of
22.5 teachers (SD = 13.2), of which 17 teachers (SD = 4.3) per school
participated. Teachers had, on average, 17.6 years of teacher experience
(SD = 12.7), with 69.5% of them being female.

self-reported instructional quality: one indicator for cognitive activation
and one indicator for classroom management. As self-efficacy is

3.2.1. Self-efficacy at the individual and the school level
Two self-efficacy subscales of the short form of the Teacher SelfEfficacy Scale from Tschannen-Moran and Woolfolk Hoy (2001) were
included, self-efficacy in classroom management (e.g., “In your teaching, to what extent can you do the following? Control disruptive
behavior in class”) and self-efficacy in instruction (e.g., “[can] provide
an alternative explanation, for example when students are confused”),
measured with four items each. Teachers indicated their responses on a
four-point scale (1 = “Not at all” to 4 = “A lot”). In order to assess a
school-level measure of self-efficacy, we computed school-mean aggregates of teacher ratings per school.
3.2.2. Cognitive activation and classroom management as indicators for
instructional quality
Teachers rated instructional quality on two subscales, with four
items each and a four-point response format. The subscale cognitive
activation was assessed with the question “how often do you do the
following?” (1 = “Never or almost never” to 4 = “Always”; e.g., “I
present tasks for which there is no obvious solution”) and one subscale
describes disciplinary climate as an indicator for classroom management
(e.g., “I tell students to follow classroom rules.”).

3.2.3. School characteristics
With respect to school characteristics, teachers rated the frequency
of collaboration activities on a six-point response format (1 = “Never” to
6 = “Once a week or more”) with four items (e.g., “Exchange or develop
teaching materials with colleagues”). Principals rated the level of
participation at school (three items; e.g., “This school provides staff with
opportunities to actively participate in school decisions”) and their
instructional leadership behavior (three items; e.g., “I collaborated with
teachers to solve classroom discipline problems”) on a four-point
response scale (1 = “Never or rarely” to 4 = “Very often”).

4.4. Is perceived school environment associated with differences in the
relationship between teacher self-efficacy and teachers’ self-reported
cognitive activation and classroom management (cross-level interactions)?
To investigate whether the relationships between teacher selfefficacy and self-reported cognitive activation/classroom management
(slopes) differed across schools, we first estimated random slope models,

5. Discussion
The present study extended previous research by investigating selfefficacy and cognitive activation/classroom management from a
multilevel perspective, with a specific emphasis on the importance of
school characteristics. We included self-reported cognitive activation
and classroom management as indicators for instructional quality and
the equivalent self-efficacy beliefs (self-efficacy in instruction and in
classroom management) as indicators for teacher motivation. As school
characteristics, we focused on the school-level of teachers’ efficacy,
teacher collaboration, school participation, and principal leadership.
With our attention on the school level and the interaction between selfefficacy and school characteristics, we sought to find out more about the
role of the school environment in which teachers are situated and
thereby contribute to the discussion on contextual features in teacher
motivation (Woolfolk Hoy, 2008) and instructional quality (Gitomer &
Bell, 2013).
5.1. Positive relationships between school-level efficacy and self-reported
cognitive activation/classroom management at the school level
With our first research question, we extended previous research by
investigating whether the positive relationship between self-efficacy and
self-reported cognitive activation/classroom management can be also
established at the organizational level (Chen & Kanfer, 2006). At the
teacher level, the positive relationships between self-efficacy and
self-reported cognitive activation and classroom management
confirmed previous research. At the school level, our analyses revealed
even stronger relationships than at the teacher level. Even though the
results seem promising, they should be treated with caution given the
small nesting effects for teacher self-efficacy and self-reported cognitive
activation and classroom management.
Although previous research on teacher self-efficacy has also applied
multilevel relationships, the analyses were mainly restricted to the
student and the teacher level (e.g., Lazarides et al., 2020). The lack of

5.3. Interaction between teacher self-efficacy and school characteristics
Finally, we modeled the interaction between teachers’ self-efficacy
and school characteristics. We investigated whether the school environment moderated the associations between teachers’ individual selfefficacy and self-reported cognitive activation/classroom management
(cross-level interactions). In our study, we assumed that low-efficacy
teachers may need a supportive environment in order to provide good
instructional quality. Hence, the gap in self-reported instructional
quality between more or less self-efficacious teachers should be lower in
highly supportive environments, whereas in less supportive environments instructional quality should depend more on the individual
teacher’s self-efficacy. With respect to self-reported classroom management, this assumption was partially supported in that our analyses
revealed stronger positive relationships with self-efficacy in classroom
management when the staff had low efficacy. In contrast, teachers’ selfefficacy in classroom management showed a positive association with
self-reported classroom management only when there was a high degree
of teacher collaboration. Thus, whereas self-reported classroom management was positively associated with the congruent self-efficacy in
classroom management irrespective of the school environment, the potential of the incongruent self-efficacy in instructional quality seems to
be activated by high levels of teacher collaboration. These findings add
to previous research that also found differential relationships between
self-efficacy and instruction (Holzberger et al., 2014). However, no
interaction was found for participation contradicting our hypothesis that
participation would be kind of weak situation allowing for interindividual differences (cf. Meyer & Dalal, 2009). Beyond this, our analyses
revealed that the relationship between self-efficacy and self-reported
cognitive activation did not vary across the investigated school characteristics. The results should be interpreted with caution given the
small magnitude of between-school variance in associations between
teacher-level self-efficacy and self-reported cognitive activation/classroom management (no significant random slopes; see Mathieu et al.,
2012 for the power to detect cross-level interaction effects).

5.2. Context effects: cognitive activation independent from the school
context, classroom management stimulated by school-level efficacy
With our second research question we aimed to investigate predictors of self-reported cognitive activation/classroom management at
the school level over and above individual teacher self-efficacy. So far,
research has only rarely addressed institutional sources of instructional
quality. Hill et al. (2015) selected “test preparation activities,” “testing
has changed instruction,” “school resources,” and “district” as potential
institutional resources for instructional quality (measured with multiple
indicators).
Our findings here partly support our assumption that school characteristics predict self-reported instructional quality. Neither school
characteristics predicted cognitive activation beyond individual teachers self-efficacy. Thus, cognitive activation seems to be rather independent from the school context. However, being a teacher at a highly
efficacious school (with regard to classroom management) was associated with higher self-reported classroom management at the teacher
level compared with being an equally efficacious teacher at a less efficacious school. Future research should illuminate possible underlying
mechanisms. It is possible that school-level efficacy is confounded by
socioeconomic composition at school (e.g., Rjosk et al., 2014) or school
climate (e.g., Berkowitz et al., 2017) that may also affect a teacher’s
teaching.
These findings are also informative for school evaluation, where
aggregated instructional quality ratings are used in order to assess
school quality and can be conceptualized as a relevant performance
outcome (Gitomer & Bell, 2013; Taut & Rakoczy, 2016). When using
ratings of teacher quality as criterion for school success, it is essential to
consider systematic differences between schools and its dependence on
school characteristics. In addition to the context effects, there was only a

5.4. Limitations and future research directions

to understand how self-efficacy may change over time, for example,
depending on colleagues’ motivation or other school characteristics.

Our research took advantage of the rich TALIS 2018 data set, in
which multiple teachers per school participated, data that would be very
expensive to collect otherwise. The TALIS design has the exceptional
advantage to model variance at the teacher and school levels. The
random sampling strategy in TALIS argues for the representativeness of
our findings and their generalizability at least for the educational system
in Austria, the country in which we performed our analyses. Nevertheless, with this secondary data analysis we were bound to the operationalizations and the design in TALIS. In particular, we discuss the use
of teacher ratings of instructional quality, the measure of school-level
efficacy, and the cross-sectional data for the interpretation of the
findings.
First, teachers rated self-efficacy, instructional quality, and the
collaboration level at school. Assessment of instructional quality has
been intensely debated, with low intercorrelations found across teacher,
students, and external observers (see Kunter & Baumert, 2006; Wagner
et al., 2015). Both student as well as teacher ratings of instructional
quality have been criticized as being biased or undifferentiated (den
Brok et al., 2006). Kunter and Baumert (2006) found, with regard to
classroom management, substantial agreement between teachers and
students. On the other hand, interrater differences were found for
cognitive activation. The key aim of the present study was to decompose
variance on the teacher and school levels instead of evaluating the absolute level of cognitive activation and classroom management. Wenger
et al. (2018) analyzed student ratings of instructional quality based on
PISA data and found only slightly higher intraclass coefficients for the
two subscales of instructional quality than the ones we found for the
teacher ratings. Thus, it is reasonable to assume that student and teacher
ratings of instruction operate similarly on the school level. Due to
perspective differences as well as to avoid a common methods bias,
future research should not focus exclusively on teacher self-reports.
Second, in the present study, we used teacher self-efficacy as a predictor variable for self-reported cognitive activation and classroom
management and its interaction with the school context. We used the
school-level aggregated scores of teachers’ self-efficacy as school characteristics. While Bandura suggested that collective efficacy can also be
measured with aggregates of individuals’ beliefs in personal capabilities,
the common conceptualization of teachers’ collective efficacy is rather
the appraisals of group capabilities (e.g., “Teachers in this school are
able to get through to difficult students”, Goddard et al., 2000, p. 492).
In both cases, beliefs are rated by the individual teachers, and multilevel
approaches take into account that teachers from the same school may
have differing perceptions. However, using the same ratings for individual and school-level efficacy prohibits analysis of mutual dependencies (e.g., school-level efficacy may affect individual self-efficacy
and vice versa, Chen & Kanfer, 2006).
Finally, the cross-sectional design of the study only allowed us to
analyze a snapshot of how teachers perceive both their self-efficacy,
school environments, and cognitive activation and classroom management. Of course, these variables interact with each other, and ratings are
not independent. Leaning on social group-dynamics theory (e.g., social
learning theory Bandura, 1977), it can be assumed that teachers
collaborating at the same school might adapt their efficacy beliefs toward a common school level. The cross-sectional data kept us from
exploring the processes of how teachers might have adapted to their
school environment over time or whether we captured a representative
slice among potentially time-varying assessments (e.g., Geiser et al.,
2015). Also, with regard to the sources of self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997),
it is likely that cognitive activation or classroom management serve as a
source of teachers’ self-efficacy (Holzberger et al., 2013). Thus, we
cannot deduce the direction of influence with these data. The focus of
the present study was to model interactions with school characteristics
and the interplay between an individual’s and the school level, regardless of causality assumptions. Future longitudinal studies will be crucial

5.5. Significance of findings and conclusion
Our study extends prior research by addressing the role of the school
context in the relationship between self-efficacy and self-reported
cognitive activation and classroom management. First, it highlights
the importance of modeling teacher self-efficacy not only as a teacher
variable, but also on the school level. Our findings showed that in
schools with higher efficacy, teachers also reported higher cognitive
activation and classroom management. As a cautionary note, we found
that schools only slightly differ in teachers’ self-efficacy. Second, our
analyses emphasize differences in cognitive activation and classroom
management with regard to their dependency on the school context. In
the present study, self-reported cognitive activation seems to be rather
independent from school context characteristics, but was predicted only
by teacher self-efficacy. On the other hand, with regard to self-reported
classroom management, it makes a difference whether teachers teach in
high vs. low efficacious schools. Third, our results show that the relationship between teacher self-efficacy and self-reported classroom
management is not independent of school characteristics. Rather,
teacher self-efficacy seems to be more relevant to classroom management if school-level efficacy was low and teacher collaboration was
high. Although the effects are rather small and not consistent across all
indicators of teacher self-efficacy and self-reported instructional quality,
the study constitutes a first attempt at illuminating teacher-school processes in the field of teacher motivation and instructional quality.
