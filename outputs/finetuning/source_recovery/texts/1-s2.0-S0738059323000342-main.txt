The efficiency of schools can be defined and measured as the ratio between the outputs produced (for example,
achievement and competences) and the inputs utilized (human and technical resources). Technology holds the
promise of increasing the efficiency of schools’ operations, although empirical evidence on this is still scarce.
This paper explores whether the ICT availability and use in 2757 schools from 10 Latin American countries are
associated with higher efficiency, relying on PISA 2018. A method based on a double-bootstrap Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) is employed. The results highlight that the availability of ICT is positively correlated with
higher school efficiency. This evidence is relevant for policy implications in setting the post-COVID priorities for
educational systems in the region.

1. Introduction
The global emergency brought about by the COVID-19 pandemic led
education policymakers to be more focused on the role that technology
can have on education. Most schools closed in-class activities for many
weeks or months in many countries, with the educational processes
shifting to an online delivery (see Schleicher, 2020). The question
arising from this policy shift (i.e., the widespread use of distance
learning) is whether the effectiveness of using computers instead of
bricks-and-mortars education is comparable in determining students’
academic results. This issue is particularly relevant in the current situation shaped by COVID-19, characterized by alternate periods of in-class
and remote learning for many students. At the same time, the discussion
is much broader and deals with the ability of Information and
Communication Technologies (ICT) to complement (not substitute)
traditional ways of conducting schools’ activities. In other words, the
recent pandemic reinforces the necessity to understand whether and
how ICT can be beneficial to educational experiences and results in
normal times.
This debate is not new. A rich stream of the educational literature
devoted its efforts in empirical assessing the effectiveness of online
learning when compared to in-presence educational activities. For
example, Means et al. (2013) conducted a meta-analysis of 45 studies

encompassing K-12 and higher education levels, finding that students
involved in blended learning (a mix of online and in-presence) overperformed their counterparts exposed to on-campus alternatives –
though the same does not hold for purely online experiences. In addition, a meta-review of more than 120 studies by Chauhan (2017) on the
role of technology in mediating learning for elementary students, confirms that students using ICT in their educational activities obtain benefits in terms of academic achievement. Both these reviews point at
demonstrating that ICT can have an effect on students’ instructional
results. Our paper provides overall evidence on the potential effects
(positive or negative) of the availability and use of ICT in educational
activities.
The use of technology in education must not be judged on the
effectiveness side alone, however. One of the key objectives of technology is, indeed, to promote high-quality education in a cost-efficient
manner, by providing adequate instructional materials and educational experience improving the educational experience at a lower cost
(Bakia et al., 2012). In economic terms, a more intensive and better use
of technology holds the promise of improving the efficiency of schools,
namely, their ability to maximize educational results for a given level of
resources invested in their activities (Schorr, McGriff 2011).
The angle of evaluating the efficiency of technology adoption in
education is of paramount importance from a political economy

specific role in affecting performance, and filling this empirical research
gap is one of the main contributions of this article. The hypothesis we
adopt is that ICT can play a significant role in improving educational
results for the same level of resources invested, and the paper investigates whether there is evidence of these effects in school efficiency.

To answer the research questions, we employ Data Envelopment

school efficiency. Importantly, most of the existing studies in the field
investigate the effects of ICT on school performance (student achievement), whilst this paper focuses on the effects on school efficiency (the

This perspective is somehow different and considers the production
process of the schooling activity. In other words, schools which obtain
high performance levels can do so using different levels of resources, and
their efficiency can be dependent upon the way in which the productive
process (teaching activities, class composition, etc.) is organized. By
looking at the impact of ICT on school efficiency, we do not explore
(only) whether more technology can raise students’ results, but specifically whether it impacts the way in which schools operate in a more
general way.
Unpacking these three topics could be vital in the light of policies to
be undertaken for the post-COVID situation. Technology has been
claimed as a potential means for providing student support at scale,
especially for those students who largely suffered the impacts of school
closures. Moreover, if a side effect of the current pandemic were a more
constrained potential economic growth, ICT ability of scaling up student
support with reduced costs would also be a crucial element to be
evaluated.
The paper innovates the current state-of-the-art of the existing
literature in three main ways. First, it is the first empirical investigation
of the role of ICT in affecting school efficiency in the LATAM context,
with most of the previous studies limited to the effectiveness side. Second, the empirical evidence refers to the most recent wave of internationally comparative data on students’ achievement, PISA 2018. Third,
the empirical analysis includes schools and students from ten LATAM
countries, while most of the previous literature concentrates on a single
country or on a much smaller sample. Thus, the motivation of this study
is to provide the most complete possible overview of the role of ICT on
educational efficiency in the region as well as on dissimilar patterns
driven by country heterogeneity.

There are quite a few studies looking at the impact of ICT on students’ achievement relying on PISA data, but the focus tends to be on
education systems from developed economies. When assessing the
empirical evidence for the LATAM region, it is important to highlight the

ICT’s situation in the region. The LATAM region still faces significant
challenges such as access gaps, low learning and structural inequalities,
having less than half of the students at secondary schools achieving basic
competences according to PISA 2018 (BID/UNESCO-OREALC, 2020).
These deep inequalities are compounded by poor ICT access. In 2019,
only 7 out 10 of the people of the region have an Internet connection,
and 46% of girls and boys between the ages of 5 and 12 live in households that do not have an Internet connection (CEPAL, 2020). There is,
too, significant wealth gaps on ICT access – students in the top wealth
quartile are at least four times as likely (in comparison to the students
from the bottom quartile) to have a laptop at home (BID/UNESCO-OREALC, 2020).
Regarding the relationship between access and use of ICT and school
performance in the LATAM region, the evidence is inconclusive, at least
at a country level. Nevertheless, studies such as Román and Murillo
(2014), which includes 16 countries from the region find for primary
schools (grade six, SERCE dataset), finds a positive effect of both ICT
access and use on performance. Though this finding relies on the assumptions of a high ICT access (both at home and school) and digital
skills from teachers.1 Equally at a regional level, relying on a sub-sample
of LATAM countries using PISA 2012, Botello and Rincón (2014) find
that the availability and use of technologies positively affect students’
performance, highlighting the importance of teacher training in the use
of technologies.2
Country evidence is quite limited. In the case of Uruguay, Formichella and Alderete (2018), using PISA 2012, obtain that the use of
and access to ICT at school has a positive effect on educational performance in mathematics, reading and science, which is reinforced by ICT
at home. Thus, they conclude that ICT dissemination programs in
schools should include transferring computers to students’ households
and improving connectivity. Formichella et al. (2020a, 2020b) analyze
the role of ICT in relation to the educational performance of adolescents
who are in a situation of social vulnerability. To do this, they use a
dataset from the city of Bahía Blanca (Argentina) finding that access to
ICT is not statistically associated with performance, while ICT use has a
positive impact on educational achievement.
Country studies for the region, based on specific interventions
boosting access of ICT, suggest positive effects on learning. Alderete and
Formichella (2016) evaluate the public program “Conectar Igualdad”
launched in 2010 in Argentina. The program provided one computer to
each secondary student in order to guarantee ICT access, and also
teachers’ training on its use. The authors find positive effects linked to
participating in the program. In a follow-up study, Formichella et al.
(2020a, 2020b) find that the same program leads to increases of 2.5%
and 3.5% in educational performance among the group of students with
a home computer connected to the Internet, as well as a reduction of
8%-18% in school failure. Earlier evidence on access programs in other
countries, such as Cristia et al. (2017) for the case of the “Laptop per
Child” program in Peru, and Barrera-Osorio and Linden (2009) for the
“Computers for Education” program in Colombia, obtain parallel findings, though requiring proper ICT use and training for teachers on digital
skills as a complement.
On the other hand, and contrary to ICT interventions evidence shown
above, Melo et al. (2017) analyze the effect of One Laptop per Child
(OLPC) program in Uruguay (a national scale plan: CEIBAL) on students’
reading and math scores using a quasi-experimental design method and
the intensity of the treatment (number of days a student is exposed to the

treatment). They do not find an impact on learning scores for the first
two running years of the program, and they argue that this is due to the
lack of widespread use of laptops in public schools and the optional
component for teacher training.
As mentioned before, the role of technology on school efficiency has
not been the object of research in LATAM countries. Existing studies for
the LATAM region suggest a significant heterogeneity on school efficiency within countries (i.e., Antequera, 2019; Delprato and Antequera,
2021a) or by key features of the education system, such as the
private-public divide (de Jorge-Moreno et al., 2018; Delprato and
Antequera, 2021b; Mizala et al., 2002; Vera 2011).
3. Theoretical background – ICT use/availability and school
efficiency
The existing literature about the impacts of ICT in education is
clearly oriented towards understanding the effects on students’
achievement as a focal point (see Section 2). Our paper is different as it
focuses on the impact of ICT on the efficiency of schools. This different
focus has two key implications. First, this study considers schools (and
not students) as units of analysis, so it does not explore the heterogeneous effects of ICT on students’ performance within schools. Second,
the effect of ICT on school efficiency is not equivalent to the effect on
student achievement. Efficiency is defined as the ability to maximize
student achievement given the available resources, measuring the
transformation process of resources into students’ results. Within this
transformation process, we specifically look into the role of ICT on the
school efficiency. In this framework, it is important to discuss whether
the use of technology by students is likely to play a role in affecting
school efficiency.
We adopt a theoretical framework based on the possibility of
modeling the educational process through an educational production
function (EPF) at the school level, following Hanushek (1979, 2020).
Thus, the educational result obtained by any student is the product of
the interaction of variables specific to the student, his family, the context
where the student lives and the school he/she attends. However, this
process is not exempt from the possibility of inefficient behavior that can
distance the student from the result that he/she should have obtained
with the factors used.
In this way, we could analyze the determinants of inefficiency/efficiency as:

technology on efficiency must preliminarily take the actual availability
ICT. This justifies decomposing the factor Ci into two components,
exploring the association of ICT availability with a school’s efficiency
separately from its use. Likewise, the leverage impacts of ICT use on
school efficiency will depend on the actual level of ICT.

Checchi et al. (2019) highlight how the specific impact might be
strongly determined by the design of the intervention. The design details
deal with key elements like teacher training, actual practices implemented, frequency of use, homework assigned, etc. In this vein, what
emerges as relevant for students’ achievement (and consequently school
efficiency) would be the actual use of technology much more than its
mere availability.
To take this into account, the EPF includes a separate variable
indicating how the ICT is practically used in the context of the school’s
teaching activity. The frequency of ICT use at school is particularly
interesting. For instance, Karlsson (2020) uses TIMSS 2015 data to show
that students in schools where there is a daily use of computers perform
worse than their counterparts in schools with a more moderate use (all
else equal). This finding suggests that an excessive use of computers in
teaching activities could be detrimental for school efficiency, confirming
earlier research on the validity of the hypothesis of a U-shaped relationship between ICT use and achievement (see, for instance, Rodrigues
and Biagi, 2017).
A comprehensive framework on which channels make the use of ICT
productive is proposed by Falck et al. (2018). Specifically, the authors
highlight how any use of school time in one activity is subject to a
constraint (opportunity cost) related to renouncing to the use of the
same for a different activity. Besides, the authors indicate that there are
specific ICT uses that positively contribute to learning (e.g., processing
and analyzing data) while others ICT uses don’t have a positive impact
on learning (e.g., looking up for ideas and information), which, in turn,
suggests substitution effects on the use of ICT for diverse activities.
Moreover, teachers are responsible for planning the specific use of
computers in schools, so they can act as designers of the ICT use in
learning and can supervise it. At least in theory, teachers decide to use
the ICT during school time to the extent to which they believe that time
invested into ICT-assisted activities are marginally more productive than
alternative activities (as lectures or teamwork).3 If this assumption is
credible, then the more intense use of time for productive activities
should result into higher school efficiency, once the resources invested
in ICT are held constant, although the leverage impacts of ICT use will be
related to the level of ICT stock.
Based on the discussion presented here, this paper assesses the
following theoretically-driven hypotheses:

• H1: the availability of ICT by students could have a positive effect on
the efficiency of the school they attend, to the extent that it is a proxy
for the enabling factors to improve the educational process. However, the magnitude of this positive effect is smaller than a direct
measure of the practical use of ICT.
• H2: the intensity of use of ICT in the school, subject to its specific use
(i.e., in activities guided by teachers and directed towards learning)
positively contributes to the school efficiency, given its potential
productivity higher than alternative use of time.
• H3: availability and intensity of use of ICT and their relationship
with school efficiency can be mediated by different structures and
key dimensions of education systems.
A conceptual note is important here. In addition to the focus on ICT
availability and use, the inclusion of an appropriate set of school-level
characteristics (defining how education systems are configured and
operate) in the EPF is necessary to understand whether apparent differences in efficiency due to ICT might be driven by school-specific
features (as in De Witte and Rogge, 2014). For example, private
schools could be more efficient and at the same time have more computers available (or use them more intensively). If the analysis overlooked the school status (private vs public), this would result into a
problem of omitted variables which can lead to an upward or downward
estimate of the ICT impact on school efficiency. Conceptually, school
ownership and linked segregation in LATAM education systems can
bring out education sub-systems with their own equilibriums and patterns of school efficiency (see, for instance, Delprato and Antequera,
2021b). Hence, accounting for school type is essential for minimizing
the bias of ICT estimates of school efficiency.

4.1. Methodology and empirical strategy
The approach used to obtain the school efficiency scores for each of
the 2757 schools in LATAM sample is Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA),
a non-parametric technique (Farrell, 1957; Charnes et al., 1978). The
underlying assumption of the method is that each decision-making unit
(in this paper: each school) transforms inputs into outputs through a
production process (Johnes, 2004; Worthington, 2001) and a measure of
technical efficiency is computed relative to the estimated frontier which
denotes the maximum output that can be achieved given the level of
available resources. The production process through which inputs are
transformed into outputs implicitly assume a given technology which,
here, we assume is the same across all schools. This assumption has been

4.3. Variables included in the empirical analysis
For the efficiency analysis, we consider a set of school inputs which
are transformed into outputs mirroring an education production function (Hanushek, 1979, 2020). Also, in line with the research questions,
we rely on a set of environmental variables which are selected to explain
the variation of school efficiency scores (Worthington, 2001). First, our
selection of inputs and outputs follows earlier studies on school efficiency (see, for example, Agasisti and Zoido, 2018; Cordero et al., 2017;
De Witte and López-Torres, 2017). Outputs are school average values for
math, reading and science scores and, following the standard practice
found in the literature, we use the first (out of ten) plausible values.9
Second, we employ four educational inputs measuring distinct dimensions of school resources: school (physical) infrastructure, the
student-teacher ratio (STR) – a measure of teachers’ availability and
capacity – and the school average of household wealth of students.

5.1. Research question 1 – efficiency scores and country differences
The estimated school efficiency scores obtained through the DEA
analysis are shown in Table 2. Panel A displays the estimates for the
entire sample (i.e., the LATAM region) of technical efficiency (TE) and
its bias-corrected bootstrap version with 95% CI (technical efficiency,
bootstrap corrected: TEBC). According to TEBC estimates, the outputs
obtained from the 10 educational systems in the region could increase
by almost 25% keeping the level of inputs constant, as the estimated
average technical efficiency coefficient is 0.75. Besides, the difference
between the most efficient school and the less efficient school is 0.57,
showing great dispersion in efficiency among schools within the region.
Thus, the LATAM region efficiency scenario posits two major challenges:
(i) how to increase the average level of school efficiency, and (ii) how to
deal with a significant degree of heterogeneity of school efficiency
within countries.
The first column of Panel B in Table 2 contains the average TEBC by
country. The estimates show a great gap between the most and the least
efficient country (i.e., Chile against Dominican Republic), of around
0.17 in terms of technical efficiency. Chile is then closely followed by
Mexico in efficiency performance. In a second bracket of efficiency one
can find Colombia, Brazil and Costa Rica, and a third group is formed by
Uruguay, Peru and Argentina; and, finally, Panama and the Dominican
Republic as the less efficient countries.11
Moreover, the interquartile range (IQR) for efficiency scores indicates a greater within-country dispersion in Uruguay, Argentina, Peru
and Brazil (above 10%), i.e., those countries located in the second group
of performance. Chile, on the other hand, by having a relative lower IQR
and top efficiency performance, indicates that its top average efficiency
level is not achieved at the expense of higher heterogeneity on school
efficiency within the country.
Country efficiency gaps can be related to a country’s income level –
for instance, Chile has a GDP per capita 35% higher in comparison to
Dominican Republic. Yet, a country’s income levels cannot solely
explain the poor performance in terms of efficiency – at least for Panama, which has the highest GDP per capita (at PPP - Purchasing Power
Parity) in the sample, though it falls into the group of less efficient
countries.12
Moreover, when profiling countries according to education spending
at the secondary level as a percentage of GDP (per capita), countries
with the largest share are Costa Rica and then Argentina, although these
two countries fall into the second and third groups when ranking efficiency. Further, among the countries leading the efficiency estimates,
Chile is in fourth place and Mexico is in the eighth place in the ranking
with respect to that expenditure indicator.13

efficiency distribution based on conditional quantiles of the distribution,
rather than a maximum efficiency benchmark (for details, see: Aragon
et al., 2005).
In this section, we carry out a robustness analysis of our main results
to assess whether they are valid once the possibility of outliers in the
estimation is considered following the implementation work in partial
frontier efficiency analysis by Tauchmann (2012). We proceed in two
steps. First, we use the order-α based for outliers detection which relies
on the idea that the share of super-efficient DMUs (schools) should be
decreasing (Daraio and Simar, 2007) and so, this technique, looks into a
discontinuity above which super-efficient DMUs are more likely to
occur.18 After finding the α where the discontinuity happens, we exclude
those schools and re-estimate the DEA model and compare it to the
earlier estimates.
Fig. 2 shows that the discontinuity happens for α ≈ 0.93, with a share
of outliers of 33.7% (or non-outliers schools = 1826). When we re-run
the analysis based on the non-outliers sample of schools, we obtain
similar results as those presented in Table 2 (column 1). Table 5 shows
that excluding school’s outliers estimates tends to over-estimate the
country level of efficiency as there are higher in eight out of the ten
countries, but only with differences of 3%-4% in three countries (i.e.,
Dominican Rep., Mexico, Panama), whereas there are lower in just two
countries (Chile and Uruguay; by 1.6%-3.4%). Again, the inclusion of
outliers or super-efficient schools do not modify our main conclusions,
and we do not find significant changes on the pattern of efficiency scores
presented in Table 2.

6. Conclusions
This study analyzed whether and how the availability of ICT and its
use in schools is related to their performance (efficiency) in the context
of Latin American countries that participated in OECD PISA 2018. Data
from more than 2500 schools were employed. The paper’s main findings
can be summarized as follows.
Firstly, we found that for the LATAM region the level of inefficiency
is around 25%, with a high degree of school efficiency dispersion. This
average level of inefficiency for the region is not evenly distributed
among the analyzed countries, although there is a greater level of
disparity within each country than between countries.
Secondly, when analyzing the relationship between the availability
of ICT by students with school efficiency, a significant and positive
relationship was found: the degree of access of ICT matters for efficiency. Therefore, higher levels of technical efficiency were found in
schools whose students showed a level of access to ICT higher than the
country’s average.
Thirdly, we also found a positive relationship between technical efficiency and the use of ICT by students, although this relationship was
not as strong as for ICT availability. Plainly, increases in levels of access
to technology were shown to be much more influential on levels of efficiency than increases in its use. When considering the determinants of
efficiency based on the second stage formulation, estimates suggested
that what matters is the level of access to technology as a significant
determinant of school efficiency, especially for those schools with a
stock of ICT falling into the highest quartile of ICT access. Yet, this
overreaching relationship was not found when gauging the impact of
level of ICT use on efficiency because results were more nuanced,
varying across countries, and in a few of them this relationship lacked
statistical significance.

Finally, as for the key characteristics of education system configurations, we found that schools using the applicant’s academic trajectory
as admission criteria tend to be significantly more efficient than those
which don’t employ it; also, urban schools were found to be more efficient when compared to the ones located in rural areas. Regarding the
type of educational establishments, our results were mixed, finding
positive relations between efficiency and private schools for some
countries, but the opposite for other countries.
It is important to highlight that the present study refers to pre-COVID
conditions, as data refer to students and schools in 2018. Therefore, the
results presented here are informative of various aspects that can support policy-making in the post-COVID era, where a more systematic and
widespread use of technology can actually be more common than in the
past. While the findings are not appropriate for studying specific effects
of COVID (as, for example, learning loss) these can be of paramount
importance to explore the role of ICT in affecting school efficiency in
more ordinary times – with much stronger implications for policymakers in the next years.
The first policy implication from this study is that, through OECD
data, national policy-makers could and should have access to information about ICT in different educational contexts (countries) and schools,
by means of the indices that describe ICT availability and use and their
components. This way, decision-makers could identify and share best
practices from different contexts and apply them in the specific educational system and with specific schools.
Second, the results provided in this paper can quantify the benefits of
adopting ICT in schools (in terms of their efficiency), and these estimates
can be complemented with cost estimates to provide cost-benefit and
cost-effectiveness analyses of possible ICT interventions. These evaluation tools are very important to assess the possible policies to be
implemented in the future.
Third, the study also holds managerial implications. School principals can use the information about the efficiency of other schools as a
benchmark for developing improvement plans at the level of their

institution. Efficiency estimates are based on microdata at school level,
with indicators about inputs, outputs, and institutional features
(including the ICT availability and use). This is the type of data that can
help principals in better identifying the peer institutions for a comparison of characteristics and results – with the aim of grasping suggestions
and good practices. Such benchmarking exercises can be favored by
recently developed ad-hoc projects, as for example OECD’s PISA for
School.
The findings presented in this paper pave the way for future research.
On one side, replicating this study with OECD PISA waves after the
COVID emergency will help understand whether the availability and use
of ICT in schools changed dramatically after the pandemic, and
whether/how it would influence measured students’ results. On the
other side, the empirical approach used in this paper can be applied to
expand the empirical analysis to different areas of the world (for
example Europe, North America, Asia) to highlight whether the patterns
revealed here are specific for LATAM or they hold in different educational contexts.
