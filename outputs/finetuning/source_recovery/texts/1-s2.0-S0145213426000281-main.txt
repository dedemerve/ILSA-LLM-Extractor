Background: Bullying victimization among adolescents is a major public health concern, and its
prevalence varies considerably across countries. While country-level economic factors are often
cited to explain this variation, the roles of gender inequality and national legal frameworks
remain underexplored.
Objective: To examine the association between country-level gender inequality and bullying
victimization among adolescents across 69 middle- and high-income countries and whether national laws prohibiting corporal punishment modify this association.
Participants and setting: Data were obtained from the Programme for International Student
Assessment 2018, a nationally representative cross-sectional survey of 15-year-old students.
Methods: The primary outcome was the victimization score, derived from six self-reported experiences of bullying victimization. Multilevel regression analysis was used to explore the association between the Gender Inequality Index (GII) and the outcome. We also analyzed effect
modification by national laws prohibiting corporal punishment (no/partial; fully banned for ≤10
years; fully banned for >10 years).
Results: The study included 433,836 students (50.6% female). Higher GII was associated with
increased victimization (b = 0.52; 95% CI, 0.32–0.73 per one-standard deviation increase),
explaining 35.6% of the country-level variance. This association was stronger for boys and was
modified by corporal punishment laws. Compared to countries with no or partial prohibition,
countries with full prohibition enacted for ≤10 years exhibited an attenuated association. This
attenuating effect was not observed in countries with a full prohibition for >10 years.
Conclusions: Reducing societal gender inequality and promoting the full prohibition of corporal
punishment may be relevant strategies for mitigating adolescent bullying victimization, particularly for boys.

1. Introduction
Bullying victimization (hereafter, victimization) is defined as the repeated and targeted experience of aggressive behavior in the
context of an imbalance of power (Olweus, 1999). It is a major public health concern among adolescents, exerting adverse effects on
their health and well-being (Arseneault, 2018) that can persist into adulthood (Takizawa et al., 2014). Approximately 30% of adolescents worldwide experience victimization more than once a month, though notable variations are observed across countries,
ranging from 9% in South Korea to 65% in the Philippines (Hosozawa et al., 2021). Given this substantial variation across countries,
identifying country-level factors that influence victimization is critical for developing effective prevention strategies at the population
level.
Bullying is a complex social phenomenon that is best understood through a social ecology framework, where individual characteristics such as aggression are shaped by the social environment to which adolescents are exposed (Hong & Espelage, 2012).
Examining the role of broader society-level factors is therefore particularly important (Thomas et al., 2018). To date, research
considering society-level factors related to adolescent victimization has focused extensively on country-level economic indicators, such
as national or individual income inequalities (Due et al., 2009; Elgar et al., 2009, 2015, 2019). Consequently, it remains unknown
whether other structural factors, such as country-level gender inequality, a strong structural determinant of health (Heise et al., 2019),
can help explain cross-country differences in adolescent victimization.
Gender equality refers to equal rights, responsibilities, and opportunities for all genders (Heise et al., 2019). Societal gender
inequality, which varies considerably across countries, has been linked to multiple forms of violence within families, including intimate partner violence and child abuse (Klevens & Ports, 2017; Willie & Kershaw, 2019). This association may be explained by the
reinforcement of restrictive societal gender norms that perpetuate and privilege inequitable power relations based on gender (Murphy
et al., 2021).
Restrictive gender norms typically privilege traits which are considered masculine over those considered feminine (Heise et al.,
2019). This influence may be particularly strong during adolescence, as individuals strive to establish their group membership by
adhering to the dominant social norms (Kågesten et al., 2016). Given that masculinity norms often foster and reward social dominance
and aggressive behaviors, and because bullying is, in itself, an attempt to demonstrate social dominance and maintain social hierarchies that exist among adolescents' relationships, the prevalence of victimization may be higher in countries with greater gender
inequality where these restricted social norms are more pervasive.
Beyond fostering aggression, restricted gender norms may also increase victimization risk by shaping victim and peer responses.
First, adherence to masculinity norms is associated with reduced help-seeking, particularly among boys (Ando et al., 2018). This may
deter victimized adolescents from seeking help, which can embolden perpetrators and increase the victim's risk. This creates a distinct
vulnerability, particularly for boys, who may be socialized to norms that simultaneously reward aggression and stigmatize the
vulnerability associated with help-seeking. Second, bullying is a group process in which peer reactions are vital in maintaining or
resolving it (Salmivalli et al., 1998). Bullying is likely to continue when peer-witnesses implicitly or explicitly support it (O'Connell
et al., 1999). In societies with greater gender inequality, peers may be more likely to accept bullying as a legitimate way to maintain
social hierarchies. This peer acceptance can create an environment where aggression is normalized and intervention is discouraged,
thereby allowing bullying to emerge and persist.
At the individual level, a higher level of social masculinity traits has been associated with bullying aggression among adolescents of
both sexes (Morales et al., 2015). However, the influence of societal-level gender inequality on adolescent victimization remains
largely unexplored. To date, only one study, conducted across 46 European and North American countries, has examined this association (Cosma et al., 2022). While that study focused on gendered differences in adolescent victimization (Cosma et al., 2022), a
significant gap remains in understanding how societal gender inequality relates to the overall frequency of adolescent victimization
and whether this association differs by sex. Addressing this gap requires a comprehensive analysis across countries with varying
gender-equality levels.
Examining the role of legal measures in modifying the gender inequality–adolescent victimization relationship could provide
valuable insights for prevention. For example, prohibiting corporal punishment is reported to reduce both its use and its public
acceptance (Lokot et al., 2020; Zolotor & Puzia, 2010). Corporal punishment is a violent form of adult discipline that can occur in any
setting, including home and school (United Nations Children's Fund, 2014). Experiences of corporal punishment can promote social
learning of aggression and reinforce violent communication patterns in children (Gómez-Ortiz et al., 2016), which in turn can trigger
increased peer aggression, including bullying (de Vries et al., 2018; Gómez-Ortiz et al., 2016). Therefore, the legal prohibition of
corporal punishment may reduce aggressive behaviors among adolescents, including bullying, by minimizing violent discipline and
shifting social norms around violence and aggressive communication patterns (Lansford et al., 2014, 2017; Zolotor & Puzia, 2010).
However, whether the national prohibition of corporal punishment modifies the gender inequality–adolescent victimization association remains unexplored.
This study examined the association between country-level gender inequality and adolescent victimization across 69 countries and
explored whether national laws prohibiting corporal punishment modify this relationship, while controlling for economic factors (both
country and individual income levels) and students' knowledge of gender equality. We hypothesized that greater gender inequality at
the country level would be associated with a higher frequency of adolescent victimization. Based on the mechanisms outlined above,
we further hypothesized that this effect would be stronger for boys and that the national legal prohibition of corporal punishment
would modify this association. The current study is among the first to explore the intersection of societal gender inequality, legal
frameworks, and adolescent peer victimization on a global scale, offering valuable implications for policies and intervention.

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Data source
Data were obtained from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2018—a nationally representative crosssectional study of 15-year-old students conducted in 2018 by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development
(OECD) (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2019). The PISA employs a two-stage probabilistic, stratified, and
clustered sample design, with schools stratified and then randomly selected with a probability proportional to size. All countries met a
response rate of 85% for schools and 80% for students included in the study (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2019). We chose to base the present study on the PISA 2018 because it is the only survey that includes questions on students'
perceived knowledge of gender inequality (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2017). We excluded subsamples (“economies”), countries, and territories due to missing country-level gender inequality indices, as well as countries and
students with missing victimization data.
2.2. Measurements
2.2.1. Victimization score
Our primary outcome measure was a composite score indicating the frequency of verbal, physical, and relational (spreading rumors, social exclusion) victimization over the past 12 months, based on students' self-reports on six questions (“total victimization
score”; range: 6–24; higher scores indicated more frequent victimization, details are presented in Method S1). This scoring method has
been employed in several previous studies using PISA data (Hosozawa et al., 2021; Odigie et al., 2025). Cronbach's alpha coefficient in
the present study ranged from 0.74 to 0.92 across countries, indicating acceptable internal consistency. To avoid bias due to listwise
deletion, participants were included in the study if they responded to at least five of the six victimization questions. The missing
bullying item (2.3%) was imputed using person-mean imputation, as in previous studies (Hosozawa et al., 2021; Odigie et al., 2025).
The imputed results were broadly similar to those obtained using the observed cases; therefore, the imputed results are presented
herein.
2.2.2. Country-level gender inequality
Country-level gender inequality was measured using the 2018 Gender Inequality Index (GII) provided by the United Nations
Development Programme (https://hdr.undp.org/data-center/documentation-and-downloads). The GII is a composite of measures
across three dimensions: 1) reproductive health (i.e., maternal mortality ratio and adolescent birth rates), 2) empowerment (i.e., the
proportion of parliamentary seats occupied by women and the proportion of adult females and males aged 25 and older with at least
some secondary education), and 3) economic status (i.e., the labor force participation rate of female and male populations aged 15 and
older). The GII ranges from 0 to 1, with higher scores indicating greater inequality between women and men.
2.2.3. Sex
Sex (male/female) was coded based on students' self-reports.
2.2.4. National prohibition of corporal punishment
The national prohibition of corporal punishment was identified based on data obtained from End Corporal Punishment (End
Corporal Punishment, n.d.), which provides detailed reports on the legality of corporal punishment in each setting (i.e., home,
alternative care settings, day care, schools, penal institutions, and sentences for crime) for all states and territories globally. Based on
this report, we first classified the countries by their 2018 legal status: (1) full prohibition in all the above settings; (2) partial prohibition (prohibited in either home or school); or (3) no legal prohibition (prohibited in neither home nor school). Countries with
prohibition in some regions, but not nationally, were classified as not having a prohibition for that setting. Based on the rationale that
prohibiting corporal punishment in all settings is essential for protecting children from violence (Lokot et al., 2020), countries with
“no” and “partial” prohibitions were combined into a single group. This decision was supported by preliminary analyses, such that
treating “no” and “partial” legal prohibitions as separate categories showed no significant main or interaction effects for partial bans
relative to no bans (Table S1). Furthermore, because legal bans may require time to influence social norms and behavior (Cramm et al.,
2023), we subdivided countries with full prohibition into two groups based on the time since enactment: (1) fully prohibited for over
10 years and (2) fully prohibited for up to 10 years. This cutoff value was informed by a previous study which observed the greatest
impact of corporal punishment law on reducing adolescent suicide approximately 12 to 13 years after enactment (Cramm et al., 2023).
2.2.5. Covariates
The covariates listed below at both the individual and country levels were included. Individual-level covariates were: individuallevel socioeconomic status measured using the Economic, Social, and Cultural Status score, a composite score for each student's socioeconomic status provided by the OECD, and students' self-reported knowledge of gender equality (categorized as “never heard of,”
“have heard of but cannot explain,” and “have heard of and can explain”). The latter variable was not measured in 10 of the included
countries (see details in Method S1).
Country-level covariates included the following indicators of economic status: gross national product per capita for 2018, and the
Gini index, both obtained from the World Bank. For the Gini index, we used the latest available values between 2008 and 2018 (data

3. Results
Of the 612,004 students from 82 countries, economies, and territories surveyed, we excluded five subsamples (“economies”) and
four countries or territories due to missing country-level gender inequality indices, and four countries due to missing victimization
data. This yielded 545,368 students from 69 eligible countries (Fig. S1). After excluding students with missing victimization data,
433,836 students (50.6% female) were included in the final sample.
Descriptive statistics for the study participants, categorized by country and overall, are presented in Table S2. On average, the total
victimization score was higher for boys (9.3; standard deviation [SD], 4.2) than for girls (8.5; SD, 3.6); however, the scores exhibited
high heterogeneity across countries for both sexes, ranging from 6.6 (Korea) to 13.3 (Philippines) for boys and 6.5 (Korea) to 12.3
(Philippines) for girls. The GII also varied across countries, ranging from 0.02 (Denmark and Norway) to 0.45 (Morocco).
Multilevel linear regression models revealed that 5.9% of the variance in the total victimization score was attributable to betweencountry differences (Model 0; see Table 1 for fixed effects and Table S3 for the full results). Victimization was more frequent in
countries with higher gender inequality (b = 0.52; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.32 to 0.73; Model 1, Fig. 1A). The GII explained

4. Discussion
This study observed that greater country-level gender inequality is associated with higher victimization among 15-year-old students, with a stronger association for boys than for girls. Furthermore, we found that national laws prohibiting corporal punishment
modified this association. Specifically, compared to countries with no or partial bans, those with a full ban enacted within the past 10
years exhibited a significantly attenuated association between country-level gender inequality and victimization. However, this
attenuation was not observed in countries where the full ban had been enacted for more than 10 years.
Previous studies have linked societal gender inequality to increased family violence, such as child maltreatment and intimate
partner violence (Klevens & Ports, 2017; Willie & Kershaw, 2019). Our findings extend this literature to adolescent peer interactions,
indicating that societal gender inequality is linked to adolescent victimization, independent of individual- and country-level economic
statuses, or student knowledge of gender equality. This association was significant for both sexes, albeit more pronounced for boys.
There are several possible explanations for this finding. First, countries with greater gender inequality may tend to endorse
restrictive gender norms, such as masculinity (Heise et al., 2019), which are linked to increased aggressive behavior among adolescents
(Gini & Pozzoli, 2006; Morales et al., 2015). Under such restrictive gender norms, boys may be particularly less likely to seek help
(Ando et al., 2018), potentially contributing to their risk of victimization. Second, a society that favors greater gender inequality may
be more likely to accept a power imbalance in relationships (Heise et al., 2019), which may foster a climate conducive to victimization.
In contrast, gender-equal societies have been reported to have more supportive and less competitive school climates (Looze et al.,
2018), which may potentially reduce victimization. Third, society-level gender inequality is associated with increased violence within
the family (Klevens & Ports, 2017; Willie & Kershaw, 2019), which may foster children's aggressive behavior and increase their
involvement in bullying at school (Gómez-Ortiz et al., 2016). Our study was not designed to elucidate the specific pathways underlying
this relationship; however, the results suggest that approaches to address societal gender inequality may be relevant for attenuating
adolescent victimization. Although the variance explained by country-level gender inequality was small (approximately 2% of the
variance in the total victimization score), it may not be negligible when considered at the population level.
This study also revealed that the association between country-level gender inequality and adolescent victimization was attenuated
in countries that had enacted national laws prohibiting corporal punishment within the past 10 years, particularly among boys.

Previous research suggests that bans on corporal punishment are associated with reduced public support for the use of corporal
punishment and violent practices toward children (Lansford et al., 2017; Zolotor & Puzia, 2010). Our findings indicate that the implications of these legal bans may extend beyond adult–child relationships to peer relationships among adolescents. However, this
attenuating pattern was not observed in countries where the ban was enacted more than 10 years ago. Several mechanisms may explain
this finding. First, early adopter effects may be relevant (Higham, 2024), as countries that pioneered corporal punishment bans decades ago often exhibit lower societal gender inequality than countries that adopted them later. In this context, the adoption of bans
may have formalized existing norms rather than introducing a novel intervention. Second, policy diffusion theory suggests that
motivations for enactment differ between early adopters, who typically respond to internal cultural shifts, and more recent adopters,
who may act in response to global norms (Shipan & Volden, 2008). The stronger attenuation effects observed in countries with recent
bans may therefore reflect the active and transitional nature of ongoing anti-violence efforts in those settings. Finally, sociocultural
responses to legal reform may evolve over time; although legal prohibition may initially align with shifts in social norms, its salience
may diminish as awareness plateaus This aligns with a recent study on the association between national corporal punishment bans and
adolescent suicide, which found that suicide rates exhibited a decreasing trend, with this trend peaking around 12–13 years after
enactment (Cramm et al., 2023).
The strengths of this study include the use of a nationally representative sample of 15-year-old students from 69 middle- and highincome countries across diverse cultures and regions. This large and diverse sample enabled us to examine country-level factors with
sufficient statistical power, thereby enhancing the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, victimization experiences in the PISA
2018 were measured through concrete description of actions rather than using the term “bullying,” which likely minimized the effect
of cultural differences on the concept of victimization (Smith et al., 2002). Finally, multilevel analysis enabled us to appropriately
account for the clustered nature of the data within schools and countries.
This study has certain limitations. First, the study's cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, although it is unlikely that
adolescent victimization precedes changes in country-level gender inequality. Ecological design is subject to ecological fallacy, where
the associations observed at the country level may not hold at the individual level. Second, the PISA 2018 survey did not include lowincome or African countries, thereby limiting the scope of our findings. Furthermore, adolescents who were not enrolled in school were
excluded from the survey, which could have been a source of sampling bias. Third, although PISA 2018 used behaviorally specific
items to assess victimization, self-reporting may vary across cultural contexts. Stigma and social desirability bias may discourage
disclosure, particularly among boys, in countries with higher gender inequality. Such underreporting may therefore result in a conservative estimate. Fourth, the GII is sometimes criticized for reflecting a country's economic development. However, our sensitivity
analysis, adjusted for country-level income and income inequality, yielded similar results. Fifth, our operationalization of the corporal
punishment ban into three categories may not fully capture the complexity of legal enforcement and associated cultural change.
Relatedly, we cannot rule out the possibility of endogeneity. Specifically, if an unobserved variable confounds the co-variation between the GII and the corporal punishment ban, it could bias the estimate of the interaction terms. For example, both societal gender
inequality and the presence of corporal punishment bans may be endogenous to broader national welfare policies and cultural factors.
In our data, we observed some regional clustering in the adoption of legal prohibitions (e.g., in Eastern Europe and the Eastern
Mediterranean), suggesting that shared geopolitical or cultural environments may shape both societal gender inequality and legal
frameworks. Consequently, the observed interaction may reflect these broader sociopolitical contexts rather than legislative effects
alone. Future research using quasi-experimental designs is needed to corroborate the associations identified in this study.
The findings highlight that approaches that address societal gender inequality may be relevant for reducing adolescent victimization and its negative consequences. Given that societal gender inequality is reinforced through restrictive gender norms, approaches
that aim to transform these norms, targeting both adolescents and the whole community, may be most beneficial (Levy et al., 2020;
Mmari et al., 2023). Based on our finding that greater self-reported knowledge of gender equality was associated with lower
victimization, programs addressing bullying could benefit from including a component on gender equality (Corboz et al., 2019).
Promoting the legal prohibition of corporal punishment at the national level in all settings may also be associated with reduced
adolescent victimization, although this association appeared weaker in contexts where the ban was long-standing. This observed
pattern could indicate the importance of supplementary measures to maintain the relevance of legal prohibitions over time. Together,
these approaches may correspond to reduced adolescent victimization, better health and well-being among adolescents, and potential
mitigation of the intergenerational transmission of aggressive behaviors.
