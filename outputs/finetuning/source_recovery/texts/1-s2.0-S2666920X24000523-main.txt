International large-scale assessments (ILSAs) have used graphical response-based items to measure student
ability for decades, but they have yet to implement automated scoring of these responses and instead rely on
human scoring alone. To investigate how scores provided by machine algorithms compare to those provided by
human raters, we applied convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to classify image-based responses from eight
Timss 2019 items. Our results show that the most accurate CNN models classified over 99% of the image responses into the appropriate scoring category for dichotomous items and almost 98% for one trichotomous item.
Additionally, during the modeling process, the CNNs correctly classified numerous image responses that human
raters had scored incorrectly. For most items, the number of incorrectly human-scored responses exceeded the
average number of responses misclassified by the most accurate models. These results suggest that automated
scoring using CNNs is comparable to, and in many cases more accurate, than human raters, even across a wide
variety of graphing tasks. This paper argues that the machine learning procedure explored could be implemented
in ILSAs as a verification method to improve the accuracy and consistency of graphical response item scores. In
lieu of additional human raters, ILSAs could implement CNN-based automated scoring to provide a second set of
scores, thus reducing the workload and costs associated with human scoring.

1. Introduction
Constructed response (CR) items have long been used in international large-scale assessments (ILSAs) to measure students’ understanding of content topics and critical thinking in an open-ended format
(Bennet, 1991). While most CR items in ILSAs ask students to write responses to show their answers, some ask students to draw or complete an
image to show their understanding. These items, known as graphical
response items, encourage creativity and require non-routine problem-solving (Martinez, 1991). They are also useful for measuring certain
skills. For example, the Trends in International Mathematics and Science
Study (TIMSS) has historically assessed Geometry-related skills from the
Mathematics framework by asking students to complete tasks on a grid,
like plotting points or completing figures according to given specifications (Mullis & Martin, 2017). More recently, in TIMSS 2023
free-drawing items that ask students to complete a model have been
used in both the fourth and eighth grade versions of the assessment to
measure knowledge of some Earth Science content (Centurino & Kelly,
2021).

CR items, particularly graphical response items, benefit from the
digital environment because of the unique tools technology affords
them, making them more critical for measurement now than ever. For
TIMSS specifically, the 2019 cycle (eTIMSS) took advantage of the
computer-based administration by developing items that merged measurement and digital tools to make items more equitable and engaging.
New graphing tool items utilized straight-line plotting, snap-to-grid, and
erase and reset tools, which promoted student engagement while
measuring higher-order mathematics reasoning. In eTIMSS, students
could draw and complete images to show their understanding more
easily than in previous paper-based TIMSS cycles. At the same time,
measurement precision was improved by reducing individual error
associated with hand drawing (Cotter et al., 2020).
Traditionally, CR items, particularly graphical response items, have
been scored by human raters, which are considered the gold standard in
scoring of open-ended responses. However, hiring human raters is both
cost and resource-intensive, and moreover, they are subject to rater effects such as fatigue, leniency, and severity that can impact the reliability of scores (Bejar, 2012; Wind, 2019). Scoring can be improved by

rater effects—severity and leniency—can also impact the accuracy and
consistency of rater scores. While scoring guides may cover many unique
responses, they cannot include all possible item responses. Thus, some
unique responses may require interpretation from the scoring guide,
leading to different judgments from different raters (Zhang, 2013).
Unique “borderline” cases, which may be on the border between correct
and incorrect, may receive credit from a rater giving the student the
benefit of the doubt (leniency) but no credit from another rater
(severity). Therefore, an individual student’s response score may
depend on who is rating the answer rather than the response’s content
(Bejar, 2012). Wind (2019) found that rater effects, particularly
severity, threaten accurate student achievement estimates, potentially
leading to systematic differences in how the items function.
Wind (2019) and Bejar (2012) both recommend assessing response
classifications for potential rater effects, ideally with double scoring of
some kind, to evaluate and attend to discrepancies in scoring across
raters (and, by extension, in ILSAs, across countries). TIMSS uses
double-scoring for all human-scored items, but is limited in the number
of responses that can be scored twice. To assess the raters’ scoring
consistency, a within-country reliability scoring procedure is carried out
where two raters score a subset of responses, and their scores are
compared. The number of double-scored responses is only 200 per item
per country because hiring a second full-time rater team to review all
student responses would double the cost and training resources, which
would be impossible for many participating countries to execute (Martin
et al., 2020). While these reliability statistics can identify if a particular
country struggled with scoring a CR item, they do not provide information about scoring consistency across countries.
Because double-scoring all responses to CR items using additional
human raters is cost-prohibitive in large-scale assessments, automatically double-scoring CR responses with ML has been used to improve
scoring reliability and consistency. Automated scoring of CR items has
already been implemented operationally in some large-scale assessments. For example. educational Testing Service (ETS) has an e-rater
engine that has been operational since 1999, providing a second set of
scores for the Graduate Record Examinations (GRE) essay portions. The
e-rater engine uses natural language processing (NLP) to evaluate students’ essays based on linguistic features such as grammar, sentence
variety, and organization. The GRE uses a human-in-the-loop approach,
where a second human rater evaluates responses only when the e-rater
score is vastly different from the original human rater score. According
to ETS, the e-rater system has improved over the past two decades, with
advancements in AI incorporated into its engine (ETS, 2020). Likewise,
Pearson implemented the Intelligent Essay Assessor (IEA) operationally
in 2010, which can score essays and short answers comparable to
humans using ML and NLP. Like the e-rater, the IEA evaluates responses
based on different features and produces a second score with a corresponding confidence that the score is accurate. Responses challenging
for the IEA to score (e.g., those with low confidence) are routed to
additional human scorers for manual scoring (Foltz et al., 2013).
Advancements in NLP and AI in recent years have led to an influx of
research on classifying open-ended responses using deep learning with
neural networks, but almost all of the work has focused on text-based
input. For example, pre-trained large language models like BERT and
GPT (including ChatGPT) that are built on neural networks have risen in
popularity for their ease of use and accuracy, allowing researchers to
classify short text responses by fine-tuning parameters (Fernandez et al.,
2022; Latif & Zhai, 2023; Lottridge et al., 2023; Mizumoto & Eguchi,
2023; Ormerod et al., 2023). Researchers have also built and trained
models on available human or student responses using open-source
packages to reduce cost (Chen & Zhou, 2019; Uto et al., 2020; von
Davier et al., 2022). Relevant to this study’s focus on TIMSS data, Jung
et al. (2022) recently had success building and training ANNs on short
text-based responses from TIMSS 2019. They achieved up to 92%
agreement between human rater and ANN classifications across a selection of items and countries. More recently, Jung et al. (2024)

introducing a second set of scores, either through an additional human
rater or through machine scoring. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have bolstered the credibility of machine learning (ML) based
scoring classifications for many item types, suggesting that it can be a
successful validation technique in assessments (Chen & Zhou, 2019;
Fernandez et al., 2022; Hao & Ho, 2019; Jung et al., 2022; Lottridge
et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Zhai et al., 2020).
While much of the automated scoring research has focused on textbased responses (ETS, 2020; Foltz et al., 2013; Funayama et al., 2022;
Jung et al., 2022; Ormerod et al., 2023; Yamamoto et al., 2017), a recent
study by von Davier et al. (2022) found that graphical response items in
international large-scale assessments can be scored on a similar level to
human raters (achieving up to 97.5% agreement) using artificial neural
networks (ANNs). von Davier et al. (2022) also discovered ANNs can
help identify borderline responses that receive incorrect credit or are
scored inconsistently across human raters. AI-based automated scoring
of image responses shows promise as a quick and inexpensive alternative
to hiring additional full-time raters and should be explored in ILSAs
further.
Therefore, this study aims to expand von Davier et al. (2022)’s
research by extending the same ANN-based validation technique to
more TIMSS items. The study thus examines how well convolutional
neural networks (CNNs), a type of ANN, perform compared to human
raters when automatically scoring eight TIMSS 2019 items across a selection of different tasks and complexity. The study also proposes how
this ML-based validation technique can be implemented in ILSAs. In
particular, this study addresses the following research questions (RQs).
1. Can CNN-based automated scoring classify student graphical responses with similar performance to human raters using TIMSS 2019
data?
2. Moreover, does CNN-based automated scoring provide comparable
performance to human rater scoring across different tasks and
varying numbers of unique responses?
3. How can CNN-based automated scoring be used operationally as a
validation technique in ILSAs?
2. Literature review
CR items are vital to measurement in ILSAs but have historically been
underutilized because they require scoring by human raters. CR items
evaluate students understanding of content topics in ILSAs while
engaging more complex cognitive functioning. Rather than relying on
students to select from given options, CR items require students to
formulate their own responses (Bennett, 1991). Often, human raters
score open-ended CR items in ILSAs, which is a time-consuming and
costly process for both the assessment developers and the participating
countries. TIMSS, for example, has human raters undergo extensive
training before data collection so that written and graphical response
items are scored as accurately as possible. After in-person scoring
training led by experts from the TIMSS & PIRLS International Study
Center (ISC), country representatives translate the English scoring materials into their native language and train their human rater teams,
which takes weeks (Martin et al., 2020). Naturally, the more CR items in
an assessment cycle, the more time must be spent translating the
training materials and scoring guides.
Even with extensive training and comprehensive scoring guides,
human raters are subject to rater effects that ultimately impact scores’
accuracy, reliability, and consistency (Wind, 2019). One common rater
effect is fatigue, which can cause raters to make mistakes and potentially
assign the wrong credit to a student’s response. Fatigue is bound to occur
at some point, as human raters review thousands of responses across
days to weeks, depending on the size of the scoring team and the volume
of student responses. Additionally, over time, raters may drift in their
scoring behavior and assign scores for an item differently for responses
seen at the end versus at the beginning (Bejar, 2012). Another pair of

When it comes to graphical-based input, successful use of ANNs has
been researched and implemented operationally in the medical field for
some years. ANNs have been able to successfully classify images on the
same level as experts across various tasks like predicting cancer from
pictures of cells (Couture et al., 2018; Klauschen et al., 2018) and
identifying diseases in images of tissue samples (Stidham et al., 2019; Yu
et al., 2018). These results indicate the great potential of image classification with AI in other fields, particularly psychometrics and ILSAs.
Unfortunately, only a few recent studies have explicitly examined how
student graphical responses can be automatically scored using ML and
AI techniques in psychometrics (Baral et al., 2023; Lee & Zhai, 2023; von
Davier et al., 2022).
The research on graphical response classification in ILSAs was
identified as relatively unexplored by von Davier et al. (2022). Thus,
they conducted a study to examine whether ANNs could be successful
classifiers of student image responses from a TIMSS 2019 Problem
Solving and Inquiry Task (PSI) item. The study used a released trichotomous item from the “Building” PSI task, which asked students to draw
the back wall and sides of a shed on a grid according to given specifications. Students received credit if they drew both the back and sides
correctly, and partial credit if only the back wall was drawn correctly.
The study compared the performance of feed-forward neural networks
(FFNs) to convolutional neural networks (CNNs) across various training
epochs, optimizers, and model structures to see how the different types
of ANNs would perform. FFNs are the simplest type of ANN and the
fastest to train and run. CNNs are more powerful because they have
additional layers, known as convolutional layers, which allow them to
process information non-linearly, allowing for more flexibility and
improved pattern recognition and feature detection (O’Shea & Nash,
2015). The study found that CNNs outperformed the FFNs in terms of
loss, accuracy, and F1-Scores. The best-performing CNN model classified
an average of 97.53% of the image responses into the appropriate score
category across five cross-validation samples. Additionally, the CNN
models correctly classified 193 responses that were incorrectly classified
by human raters, indicating that ML can be successful in identifying
mistakes and inconsistencies in human scoring as well.
The promising results of von Davier et al. (2022) prompted interest
in further research to expand the literature on AI-based scoring of student graphical responses in assessments. Their findings demonstrate the
potential of AI in improving the accuracy and efficiency of scoring student responses by serving as a quality control measure for human
scoring. This study was devised to examine the efficacy of ANN validation across multiple items from TIMSS 2019 with varying complexity.
Moreover, findings from the study would provide practical insights into
how ANN-based automated scoring of student graphical responses could
be implemented operationally in TIMSS and, by extension, other ILSAs.

4.1. Item selection
Several factors were considered when selecting the TIMSS 2019
items for the study. First, all graphical response items in TIMSS 2019
were identified: 18 items required students to draw or complete a picture on a grid to demonstrate their understanding in eTIMSS, 11 of
which were Grade 4 items and six of which were Grade 8 items. The
Grade 4 items were from the Measurement and Geometry, Data, and
Number content domains, while all of the Grade 8 items were from the
Geometry content domain. One of these Grade 8 items was the “Building” PSI Task item, which was already modeled by von Davier et al.
(2022), making it ineligible for this study. Due to time constraints, a
subset of items was selected. Two Grade 8 items, ME52048 and
ME62300, were selected because they could be explicitly discussed and
used as examples under the restricted use guidelines. They also showcase two extremes of the spectrum of item complexity (which will be
discussed further in the next section). The remaining items selected were
those that would be used in TIMSS 2023 and scored using screenshots, as
the potential operational use of CNN-based scoring in TIMSS was one of
the primary RQs of the study (RQ3). Six of the Grade 4 items and four
Grade 8 items were eligible for selection after these filtering steps.
The remaining ten items were evaluated according to the skills they
assessed and their complexity (i.e., how many possible responses could
receive credit). To answer RQ2, selecting items that assessed a wide
range of content was imperative to test how the CNNs would handle
graphical input of various shapes and forms. Variety in possible responses was also a point of consideration: it was expected that items
with few correct response options would be easier to classify for the
CNNs than items with many correct response options (see Figs. 1–5).
Eight of the ten items were selected for the study: five Grade 4 items and
three Grade 8 items, with one of the Grade 8 items being trichotomous
(having an additional score category of partial credit). One item at each
grade was not selected because they were on the lowest end of the
complexity spectrum, which was already covered by secured item
ME72119 (which had three possible correct responses) and restricted
use item ME52048.

4.3. Image response pre-processing
Prior to modeling with CNNs, the image responses were preprocessed to retain as much information as possible when placed in
the modeling arrays. Graphical responses must be shrunk down to a
small dimension to ensure reasonable processing time. For example, in
the MNIST fashion dataset, a popular choice for beginners experimenting with ANNs, the images are only 28 by 28 pixels (Xiao et al., 2017). In
our study, the average size for student responses was 355 by 382 pixels.
When placed in the arrays, the target size varied by item but was either
64 by 64 pixels (the size used in von Davier et al., 2022) or 75 by 75
pixels, depending on the complexity of student responses. These final
response sizes were chosen to balance processing speed and classification accuracy.

Before modeling and following pre-processing and array creation,
the sample of responses for each item was split for simple hold-out
validation, where a subset of responses was assigned to a training
sample. The remaining responses were used as a validation sample so
that the performance of the CNNs could be judged on responses that the
models had not seen before (Chollet, 2018). The assignment was conducted using stratified random sampling on a by-country and by-score
category basis to ensure the training sample was representative of the
overall sample, with no country or score category included with
significantly higher or lower frequency.
For ANN modeling, larger training samples are desirable so that the
models are exposed to as many different responses as possible for feature
detection and pattern recognition. However, it is also important to
maintain a large validation sample to ensure that the trained models are
generalizable (Chollet, 2018). When beginning modeling with the first
item, ME52048, a sample split of 70% training to 30% validation was
selected based on modeling conducted by von Davier et al. (2022).
However, after some initial CNN models were run with varying training
sample sizes, it was quickly found that the models had comparable
performance when 20–70% of the data were used for training. Thus, for
the majority of modeling conducted in the study, the CNN models were
trained on 20–30% of the responses (depending on the item).

cleaning, two stages of modeling occurred—the initial stage and an Item
Response Theory (IRT)-based modeling stage, both used to identify errors and inconsistencies in the human rater scores and one final data
modeling stage to produce the final response classifications for comparison against the human rater scores to answer the research questions.
Each modeling stage used a different percentage of responses assigned to
the training and validation samples. Additionally, the final stage of data
modeling used cross-validation, where the sample assignment process
was conducted five times to produce five different sets of training and

the responses were pixelated by shrinking them down to their target size

After image modification, the responses were shrunk down to their
target size and placed in arrays using the EBImage package (Ole, 2022).
The corresponding human rater classifications were also saved in a
separate array using integer label format (0 for incorrect, 1 for correct or
partial credit, 2 for full credit).

4.6. Data cleaning stage 1: initial modeling
During the initial modeling stage, a sample was created using 30% of
the responses, which were assigned to the training set. CNNs were
constructed with varying layer structures, as detailed in the “Convolutional Neural Network Model Training” section. After training, the CNNs
were applied to the validation sample. Any discrepancies in human rater
and CNN classifications of the validation sample were manually examined by the research team. For each reviewed image response, both
classifications were considered while judging the response against the
item’s scoring guide. Any responses determined to have received
improper credit from the human rater were identified and set aside (i.e.,
removed from the data sample keras arrays and placed into their own
array), as were any responses found to have inconsistent scoring across
raters. Using keras, CNN models can also provide final classifications for
the training sample after training. While the number of disagreements
between the trained CNN classifications and the human rater scores
were small, these responses from the training sample were also manually
reviewed and any incorrectly or inconsistently human-scored responses
identified and set aside.
Following the identification of improperly or inconsistently scored
responses, the classification accuracies of the CNNs were computed by
comparing the final CNN classifications on the validation sample to the
human rater classifications. Based upon the performance of the CNNs (i.
e., the classification accuracy and the loss metrics), the sample assignment ratio for final modeling was determined (either using 30% of the
data for training or reducing to 20% for training if the models were
highly accurate).

4.5. Data cleaning: overview
One of the goals of the data cleaning stage of the study was to ensure
that the responses used in data modeling had correct classifications
according to the items’ scoring guides so the CNNs would be trained on
responses where the image features and the classifications matched.
Otherwise, errors or rater effects may be introduced in the training that
could impact the models’ learning and classifications (Chollet, 2018;
Wind et al., 2017). von Davier et al. (2022) found that during individual
review of responses where the classifications between the ANNs and
human raters did not match, roughly 1.3% of the data were incorrectly
scored by the human raters (given the improper credit according to the
scoring guide). Additionally, they found that some responses received
different scores depending on the rater (identified as “inconsistent
human scoring”), reflecting severity and leniency effects that arise when
stray marks or incomplete lines are present. von Davier et al. (2022)
suggested in their conclusion that CNN-based validation can help
identify inaccuracies and inconsistencies in human rater scoring. Thus, it
was decided that a combination of CNN-based scoring and manual review would be used to clean the dataset.
Therefore, data cleaning consisted of constructing CNNs and then
manually reviewing misclassifications made by the models. Of the
misclassified responses, any responses determined to be incorrectly
scored by the human raters were set aside from the data sample. Likewise, any responses identified as inconsistently scored by the human
raters—“borderline” responses that could be scored as correct or
incorrect depending on the raters’ judgments about stray marks and
incomplete lines—were also set aside. Two separate stages of neural
network modeling were conducted. First, an initial round of modeling
was done to give the research team an idea of how the CNNs would
perform in the final data modeling and, more importantly, to identify
the majority of incorrectly and inconsistently scored responses. A second
round of modeling was conducted using the IRT-based CNN modeling
technique explored in von Davier et al. (2022), which examined whether
samples constructed based upon agreement between expected scores
(ES) and human rater scores could improve model classification and
detection of responses given improper credit by the human raters. In this
round of modeling, ES were produced for each response based upon a
students’ ability estimates and the items’ IRT parameters. Only responses where ES and the corresponding human rater scores matched
were used in the training samples. This second step of data cleaning
aimed to test the IRT-based ES modeling approach across multiple items
and catch any remaining incorrectly or inconsistently human-scored
responses to set aside from the main data sample. For the number of
student responses assigned to the training and validation samples in
each stage, see Table 2.

4.8. Data modeling: final modeling
Following the data cleaning stage, the final data modeling was
conducted to produce CNN classifications for comparison against human
rater scores. 20% of the responses were assigned to the training sample
for ME71177 and ME72119, while the remaining items had training
samples composed of 30% of the responses. This approach allowed us to
maximize the number of responses receiving classifications from the
models, while still maintaining high agreement with the human rater
scores (see Table 4). The most accurate models were also tested on the
incorrect and inconsistently scored image responses identified during
the data cleaning. This additional testing aimed to examine how many
incorrectly scored image responses the final models could classify into
the correct score category. These results would indicate the efficacy of
the final CNNs’ detection of errors and inconsistencies in human rater
scoring, and their potential application in ISLAs (RQ3).
For the trichotomous item ME62300, augmentation was used to increase the size and representation of partial credit responses in the
training sample for the final modeling. Augmenting datasets using
existing responses is a technique used in ML to improve model learning
when data is limited in size either across the entire sample or for
particular classifications since large amounts of data are required for
neural networks to be trained reliably (Chollet, 2018; Perez & Wang,
2017; Shorten & Khoshgoftaar, 2019). When pursuing augmentation to
reduce class imbalance, responses are included more than once in the
training data, either as pure copies or with some changes added (such as
noise, rotation, or mirroring) to maintain the same classification but
appear as a different response (Leevy et al., 2018). For ME62300, 30% of
the responses within each country and score category were assigned to
the training sample. Then, the sample of responses that received no

4.9. Convolutional neural network model training
CNN models were trained using the keras package in R (Allaire &
Chollet, 2021) and compiled using the sparse_categorical_crossentropy
loss option. The models’ structures were informed by the results of
parameter experimentation conducted by von Davier et al. (2022). All
models had two or three fully connected layers, with each additional
fully connected layer including 64 more units than the previous layer.
Additionally, the models had the final fully connected layer with two or
three units, representing incorrect, partially correct (when applicable),
and fully correct. For every model, the convolutional layers used a
kernel size of 3x3 and were always followed by a 2-dimensional max
pooling layer with a pool size of 2x2.
Modeling was conducted for 50 epochs of training using the Nadam
(Dozat, 2016) optimizer. These settings were used based on von Davier
et al. (2022), who found that models trained on 50 epochs tended to
have slightly higher accuracies and lower losses than those trained on 25
epochs and that those compiled using the “Nadam” optimizer performed
better than those compiled using the “Adam” or “AdaMax” (Kingma &
Ba, 2014) optimizers. von Davier et al. (2022) also found that including
dropout layers in CNN models reduced model loss; thus, all models in
this study included dropout layers following each max pooling layer and
fully connected layer, except for the final fully connected layer in the
model. Each dropout layer used a dropout rate of 0.25, indicating that
25% of inputs would be dropped randomly from the layer during each
iteration of training. Finally, CNNs with zero and no padding were
generated.
Four CNNs were compiled for each training/validation sample
modeled in the initial and IRT modeling stages of data cleaning. These
CNNs had either one convolutional layer with 32 filters or two convolutional layers with 32 and 64 filters, respectively. They also had two
dense layers with 128 and 192 units, respectively. In the final data
modeling stage, sixteen CNNs were produced in total. In addition to the
one and two convolutional layer models created in the data cleaning
stages, the final data modeling stage also had CNNs with three and four
convolutional layers, with each additional layer including 32 more filters than the previous layer. They also had two or three dense layers,
with each additional layer having 64 more units than the previous layer.

5. Results – data cleaning
5.1. Stage 1: initial modeling
The models produced in the first data cleaning stage had close model
fit, with losses ranging from 0.024 to 0.22. The models were highly
accurate, with classification agreement between the CNNs and human
raters ranging from 96% to 99%. During the review of responses where
there was disagreement between the human rater classification and the
CNN classification, 14 to 317 responses across the items were identified
as incorrectly scored by the human raters, and 16 to 143 responses
across the items were identified as inconsistently scored across the
raters. After removing these responses from the validation sample, the
model accuracies increased. The best-performing CNNs (those with the
highest accuracies and lowest losses) agreed with the human raters for
98.79%–99.84% of the dichotomous item responses and 96.64% of the
trichotomous item responses (see Table 5).
5.2. Stage 2: IRT-based expected score modeling
The second stage of data cleaning with IRT-based ES modeling
involved creating eight models for each item, four of which were trained
on 50% of the data where human rater and IRT-based ES matched
(Tmatch), and four of which were trained on the other half of the data
where human rater and IRT-based ES matched (Vmatch). For all eight
models, proficiency was also evaluated using a validation sample consisting of responses where the human rater and IRT-based ES did not
match (Vno_match).
The first set of four models, trained on responses where the human
rater and IRT-based ES matched (Tmatch), tended to perform better when
classifying responses where there was an agreement between the human
rater sore and the IRT-based ES (Vmatch) than they did when classifying
responses where there was no agreement (Vno_match). For the matching
human rater-ES validation sample (Vmatch), the best-performing models
had low losses, ranging from 0.01 to 0.04 for the dichotomous items and
0.12 for the trichotomous item, which shows that the models closely fit
the data. Upon review of the responses misclassified by the CNNs, most
items had no additional responses identified as incorrectly scored by the

6.3. RQ3: Understanding how automated scoring can Be used as a
validation technique in ILSAs
To better answer RQ3, it was imperative to investigate the misclassifications made by the human raters and the CNNs, as well as the
inconsistencies in human rater scoring. This investigation, coupled with
an examination of performance metrics across the items, is explored in
the following three sections. The insights from these examinations are
further elaborated in the Discussion section.
6.3.1. Findings related to incorrect human scoring
During data cleaning, it was found that for some responses, the
human rater score did not match the image response during the individual review by the research team. Responses that were misclassified
by the human raters were typically quite close to the classifications
given but had one or a few features that placed them in a different score
category. Across the dichotomous items, most of them had many more
responses that were given credit by the human raters but should have
been incorrect. These misclassifications likely come from the raters
giving the students the benefit of the doubt or missing essential elements
of the response that make it incorrect. For example, in ME52048, human
raters gave credit to responses where four lines of symmetry were
drawn, but one line was just off from being correct, while some responses that only had two of the four correct lines of symmetry also
received credit (see Fig. 8).
Unsurprisingly, more incorrectly human-scored responses were
identified for items with more variation in correct responses, as can be
seen in items ME61081A and ME61081B, where the number of incorrectly human-scored responses is high (especially compared to the
neural network misclassifications). While little can be said about these
secured items, the disparity is likely because both items use the same
image response, meaning that each response must be scored twice,
requiring the human rater to distinguish information drawn answering
Part A from that answering Part B. For these items in particular, the
CNNs were adept at learning the features associated with a correct score
for each item part while ignoring all other information, which can be
challenging for human raters. Additionally, the scoring guides provide a
tolerance for students to draw within, making the items even more
challenging to score since the tolerance could not be included in the
screenshots while scoring.
The trichotomous item also saw many incorrectly human-scored
responses, which was unsurprising given that an additional score category was present and that students could draw one of six rectangles to
receive partial credit. For the most part, the human raters misclassified
the responses into an adjacent score category. The highest number of
misclassifications was for responses that should have received partial
credit but the human raters scored them as incorrect. Of these responses,
many included rectangles that were implied but not explicitly stated in
the scoring guide. Some scorers likely did not realize or forgot that these
measurements were also worth partial credit. The next highest category
of misclassifications was for responses that should have been incorrect
but received partial credit. Of these responses, many had more than one
rectangle present, with at least one having the correct or partial credit
dimensions. The raters who gave these responses partial credit likely
gave students the benefit of the doubt. However, during TIMSS scoring
training, it is taught that if the student provides contradictory information (e.g., two rectangles), they have shown confusion and cannot

6.3.2. Findings related to inconsistent human scoring
During the review of the misclassified responses, some were found to
have features present or absent, resulting in them being scored differently depending on the human rater or even the country. These
“borderline” responses tended to have extraneous lines (information),
stray marks, or incomplete lines, which made them more difficult to
score (see Fig. 9). Some scorers gave students the benefit of the doubt,
while others were more severe, giving credit only if responses were
identical to those included in the scoring guides. For inconsistently
scored “borderline” responses, it is difficult to say whether the students
knew or did not know the correct answer, so the independent rater did
not provide their own scores for comparison.
For the trichotomous item, most of the inconsistently scored responses were given credit of some kind. For all but one of the dichotomous items, the human raters did not give the majority credit,
expressing more severity in their scoring. ME61081A had 186 responses
identified as inconsistently scored, with most of them having stray
marks or additional lines on one part of the image that likely muddied
the scoring for this item part but not ME61081B. ME61224 had the most
inconsistently scored “borderline” responses identified, and the human
raters gave most of them credit. For this item, one country in particular
had 98 borderline responses where the correct angle was drawn but, an
additional line was also present. While specifics cannot be given about
this secure item, it can be said that this more common unexpected
answering pattern was likely caused by the translation of the item stem
in the country’s context.

7.3. Research question 3
The primary benefit of using CNNs to provide a second set of scores
for graphical response items is that it is a time- and cost-efficient procedure that can replace the need for additional human raters and ultimately improve the measure’s reliability. Hiring more human raters to
classify each response twice would require hours of additional training
and scoring, thus increasing costs for the participating countries. CNNs,
on the other hand, take just hours to train and can classify most student
responses (e.g., those in the validation sample) in seconds. Only one
researcher is required to write the scripts and run the models, with one
or two content experts needed to review discrepancies and establish
final item scores. Moreover, using multiple open-source packages in R
improves the accessibility of the technique, where the true challenge lies
in crafting the programs and learning how neural networks function
rather than cost. Because CNNs have been shown to classify student
graphical responses on the same level, and often better than, human
raters, there is the potential that with enough refinement, this method of
automated scoring could reduce human scoring further by only having
human scorers score a subset of graphical responses for the training
samples and have the CNNs classify all remaining responses.
Another benefit of automatically producing a second set of scores is
the potential to identify student responses that human raters have scored
incorrectly and inconsistently. This study indicates that CNNs can help
identify these responses through individual review of model misclassifications. Operationally, any responses identified as incorrectly
scored by the human raters could be re-scored by a content expert
following data collection. Content experts could also manually score
responses that fall into the “borderline” category, where it is unclear
which credit the response should receive. The two benefits of using this
approach are that it can pool all “borderline” responses across participating countries and score them with a single rater to increase scoring
consistency. In the case of TIMSS, this approach would be a marked
improvement from past cycles, where human raters sometimes received
individual feedback for specific “borderline” responses from scoring
queries emailed to the ISC, resulting in potential scoring differences for
countries that did not reach out with questions.
Furthermore, there is the potential that CNN-based automated
scoring could be used to bolster scoring materials made for human
raters, provide country feedback, and inform decisions made during
data review. Responses commonly scored incorrectly by human raters
and frequent “borderline” responses could be included in scoring guides
updated for data collection or future cycles. Also, human scoring
training sessions could use some common incorrectly scored and
“borderline” responses as examples to prepare scorers and evaluate the
consistency of their scoring (for non-rule-based scoring items). Feedback
could also be provided to countries before human scoring for data
collection if specific patterns of incorrect scoring or “borderline” responses arise during the validation of the field test results. Another
benefit could occur during item development, where “borderline” responses for similar older items could be used to inform the wording of
the item stem and the selection of tools available to students for
responding. Finally, during the data review of each item by country,
CNN-based validation results could be used to inform decisions. For
example, a table of agreements between the CNNs and human raters
could be produced for the countries, and any participant with an unusually low agreement could be flagged for further examination and
discussion.

7.2. Research question 2
For the seven dichotomous items, the CNNs all achieved low losses
and extremely high F1-Scores and classification accuracies, indicating
that the neural networks had successful classification ability regardless
of the measured skill and variation in the number of possible responses.
That being said, there was some variation in performance between 99%
and 100% classification accuracy. As anticipated, the models tended to
perform better for less complex items. The CNNs had the highest classification accuracies and lowest number of misclassified responses on
average for easier items like ME72119 (0.9992 F1, 11 misclassifications), ME52048 (0.9991 F1, 13 misclassifications) and
ME7177 (0.9986 F1, 27 misclassifications), which had between 42%
and 48% of students getting them right, on average. Additionally, these
three items had low variability for correct responses because there were
only a few ways for students to receive credit. Therefore, most of the
correct responses looked the same, further bolstering models’ training,
particularly when learning to distinguish between correct and incorrect
features. It was surprising to find that the models had extremely high
performance for ME61081A (0.9992 F1, 16 misclassifications), which
was expected to be more challenging because the images also included
responses to ME61081B (0.9981 F1, 42 misclassifications). While it
cannot be said precisely why this is the case, it may be because many of
the correct responses had lines drawn similarly (going across the same
grids in the image), as opposed to the lines drawn for ME61081B, which
had more variation in responses that could receive credit.
Also, as expected, the CNNs had lower performance for more
complicated items with a lot of variability in correct responses and/or an
additional score credit category, specifically ME71181 and ME62300.
Both items had the most flexibility in possible responses since students
could draw their shape (answer) anywhere on the grid. For ME71181,
the model’s performance was the lowest among all the dichotomous
items (0.9995 F1, 99 misclassifications), with more misclassifications
than the human raters (67 misclassifications). For ME62300, the lower
F1-Score of 0.9766 and classification accuracy of 97.99% were unsurprising, as the models had difficulty distinguishing between some
partially correct and incorrect responses. However, it is worth noting
that the best CNN model still performed exceptionally well, on average,
when considering it was trained on only 20–30% of the responses.
Moreover, the success of achieving almost 98% classification accuracy
for the trichotomous item speaks to the benefit of using augmented
training data for more complicated items and those with additional
score categories.

7.4. Limitations
There are two main limitations to using CNNs for automated scoring
of student responses. First, machines must be powerful enough to run
the models efficiently, with sufficient gigabytes of RAM to train models
on thousands of responses. We recommend a workstation with at least
64 GB or access to a cluster of processors. Second, cleaning the data by
14

manually reviewing some responses is necessary; for the best training
samples, the data must not include incorrectly scored responses (Chollet,
2018). Additionally, decisions about handling borderline responses that
may be inconsistently scored by human raters and the CNNs must be
made before sample cleaning, preferably by content experts. Cleaning
the data requires additional modeling at the beginning of the process
and some manual review, which can be inefficient. However, the associated time and labor increase is still less than hiring a second set of
full-time human raters. This study, in particular, spent much effort on
data cleaning both to determine human rater accuracy on graphical
response items in TIMSS 2019—a task that had never been assessed
across all student responses before— and to explore the efficacy of using
IRT-based ES to identify misclass cations by human raters. For assessments that want to use CNN automated scoring for graphical responses,
data cleaning could be limited to one round, using only CNN models, a
mixture of CNNs and IRT-based ES, or potentially unsupervised scoring
with generative AI tools like GPT-4.

even better—than the human raters. For six of the eight items examined,
the number of responses misclassified by the human raters was higher
than the number of responses misclassified, on average, by the CNNs.
These results suggest that CNNs are a powerful classification tool
regardless of item and response complexity, achieving F1-Scores of
0.995 and above for the dichotomous items and an F1-Score of 0.977 for
the trichotomous item. In addition to using CNNs to provide a second set
of scores to the items, this study also examines how CNNs could be used
to clean the data before response classification, as improper scores are
expected to occur whenever only a single human scorer is used. The
novelty of using ES based on IRT modeling is emphasized and found to
purify the training data successfully. During data cleaning, the CNNs
helped to identify up to 2% of student responses that were either
incorrectly or inconsistently scored by the human raters. Finally, the
results indicate that CNN-based scoring of student graphical responses
can be conducted efficiently across multiple items that assess different
skills and tens of thousands of responses. Operationally, CNN-based
validation can be used to improve the reliability and consistency of
scoring by flagging responses for re-scoring by content experts. Results
from modeling could also inform decision-making during the review of
item functioning and aid in scoring material development. In sum, our
study shows that automated scoring with CNNs in large scale assessments is possible with a high degree of success and many potential
operational applications. By implementing an AI validation procedure,
ILSAs can include more graphical response items with better precision to
assess students’ critical-thinking skills and understanding of
domain-specific tasks. Improving measurement by ILSAs will improve
how countries make changes to facilitate student learning based on
comparative assessment results.

7.5. Future work
Because CNN-based automated scoring with graphical response
items performs so well, it is recommended that this validation technique
be explored further by other research teams and be implemented operationally in ILSAs. Within the ISC, current work is being done for the
TIMSS 2023 cycle to validate human rater scores for free-draw items and
mathematical scoring of stored coordinate strings for graphing items. In
future TIMSS cycles, there is the possibility that student graphical responses from previous assessment cycles could be used as the training
data to validate responses from the current assessment cycle. Other
research teams may also want to pursue pre-trained models, such as the
GPT family, which can now process graphical input. A recent study by
Lee and Zhai (2023) used GPT-4V to automatically score images of
science models drawn by students. They found that the models yielded
low to medium classification accuracy compared to human raters
depending on students’ proficiency levels. While they note some limitations, such as GPT struggling to classify more complex drawings and
its conversational function potentially contaminating some scores, their
results are promising and indicate the potential that large language AI
models have.
Another area of improvement is the identification and review of
misclassifications made by the CNNs. In this study, content experts
conducted individual review for all responses where the human rater
classifications differed from the CNN classifications. However, not all
assessments that use AI validation will have the same resources or time
available for individual review, as in the case of high-volume scoring.
Therefore, additional classifications should be provided to identify responses that are more likely to be true validation misclassifications. For
open-source options, different CNNs could provide multiple classifications for comparison. Any responses where the CNNs show vast differences in scoring, suggesting that they are more challenging to score,
could be selected for individual review to ensure the proper classification is assigned. Another option is to utilize generative AI models like
GPT-4 to assign an additional set of scores based primarily on the items’
scoring guide and some example responses. Any responses where the
generative AI score does not match the CNN score could be flagged as a
possible misclassification by the machine, requiring further review by
human experts.

split. For example, we may select 30% of responses to be in the training sample and the remaining 70% of responses to be in the validation sample. An
example function could be make_assignment_file(), which would do the following.
1. Read in data that have student IDs, the name of the images that correspond to their responses, and their human rater scores
2. Loop through each country, within each country and score category assign 30% of responses to the training sample and 70% to the validation
sample
3. Save CSV files of the country assignments (if desired)
4. Save RDS file for all of the country data together
For cross-validation, the example function can be run additional times with separate assignment files produced.

Image Pre-Processing
Next, we make the data arrays for modeling, which involves reading in the image responses, modifying them to enhance feature detection, and
placing them in a keras array. We have found that it saves time to create one array that includes all data, and then reshape that array into separate
training and validation arrays. This is especially useful when testing different training sample sizes and going through multiple iterations of modeling.
Three example functions could be used. First, the modify_image_responses() function, which would do the following.
