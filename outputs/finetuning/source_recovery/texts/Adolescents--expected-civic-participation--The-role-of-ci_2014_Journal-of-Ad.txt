In the present study we examined the role of civic knowledge and efﬁcacy beliefs as factors
that can promote adolescents’ expectations to participate in civic activities, while also
taking into consideration the inﬂuences of socio-economic background and gender differences. Structural equation modelling techniques were used to examine data from the
International Civic and Citizenship Study, collected from 3352 eighth grade Italian students. Gender was found to signiﬁcantly moderate some relationships between the variables, while efﬁcacy beliefs, rather than civic knowledge, positively inﬂuenced expected
civic participation. Socio-economic background inﬂuenced all the variables included in the
study, but it had a very small direct inﬂuence on adolescents’ expected civic participation.
It therefore appears that adolescents’ expected civic participation can be encouraged by
making them more conﬁdent about their civic and political abilities. These results extend
our understanding of civic engagement in adolescents, and can inform policies aiming to
promote it.

Adolescents’ civic engagement has been regarded as an expression of a successful and healthy development (Sherrod,
Torney-Purta, & Flanagan, 2010) and as the foundation of civic and political involvement in adulthood (Flanagan &
Sherrod, 1998). It is considered critical for the health of communities, governments, and societies (World Bank, 2007).
Civic engagement is a complex phenomenon that is best described by using multiple components (Bobek, Zaff, Li, & Lerner,
2009; Luengo Kanacri, Rosa, & Di Giunta, 2012; Zaff, Boyd, Li, Lerner, & Lerner, 2010). Political participation has been widely
regarded as a core element of civic engagement (Obradovic & Masten, 2007; Walker, 2002), but several authors (Haste &
Hogan, 2006; Jugert, Eckstein, Noack, Kuhn, & Benbow, 2013; Youniss et al., 2002) have argued that also other forms of
participation should be taken into account, especially when focussing on adolescents. These forms include civic actions
pertaining to civil society (Flanagan & Faison, 2001) with people attempting to further their interests and uphold their
opinions (e.g., signing petitions, taking part in a demonstration), as well as newer forms of participation (e.g., internet and
consumer activism; Jugert et al., 2013).
Since adolescents have limited opportunities to participate as active citizens, at least until they come of age, research has
often focused on their expectations to participate in political and civic activities, considering them as informative and

predictive of future engagement (Schmid, 2012; Schulz, Ainley, Fraillon, Kerr, & Losito, 2010; Torney-Purta, Lehmann, Oswald,
& Schulz, 2001). This approach is based on the large body of literature that has regarded behavioural expectations as immediate antecedents of behaviour and its best predictors (Fishbein & Ajzen, 2010), as well as on the results of studies that have
shown a close correspondence between expectations to vote and voting behaviour (e.g., Granberg & Holmberg, 1990). The
connection between adolescents’ current expectations to participate and their future civic engagement is also supported by
longitudinal studies, which have shown a high stability and persistence in political attitudes and participation from youth to
adulthood (Braungart & Braungart, 1990; Jennings & Stoker, 2004). They also provide evidence that the civic context during
adolescence predicts civic engagement in later life (Zaff, Malanchuk, & Eccles, 2008), and that attitudes toward political
engagement and willingness to participate in politics become stable throughout the years of adolescence (Eckstein, Noack, &
Gniewosz, 2012).
Building upon the literature summarized above, in the current study we assessed adolescents’ expected civic participation
by means of a construct that includes their expectations to participate in political activities (e.g., voting, helping a political
candidate) as well as in civic activities, both traditional and more modern (e.g., taking part in a peaceful march, contributing to
an online discussion). The deﬁnition of “civic” was chosen for this construct because nowadays this term has a broader
meaning than the term “political” (Flanagan & Faison, 2001) and is thus more appropriate for the different forms of
participation included in the present study.
As Amnå (2012) recently pointed out, there is a need for studies which aim to identify the factors that can promote civic
participation in youth, and which can provide indications for implementing effective initiatives. Civic knowledge and efﬁcacy
beliefs referring to civic and political activities have been considered as core elements for the development of civic
engagement and participation (Galston, 2001; Verba Scholzman, & Brady, 1995) also within some recently proposed theoretical frameworks (Schulz, Fraillon, Ainley, Losito, & Kerr; 2008; Torney-Purta, Amadeo, & Andolina, 2010). The inﬂuence of
the socio-economic background on civic participation, attitudes and competencies has also been pointed out (Mahatmya &
Lohman, 2012; Sherrod, Flanagan, & Youniss, 2002). However, the majority of studies carried out hitherto have investigated the relationships between civic engagement and civic knowledge or efﬁcacy beliefs independently, usually focussing on
speciﬁc forms of participation separately, without evaluating the inﬂuence of socio-economic background on all these
variables.
The aim of the present study was therefore to simultaneously examine the inﬂuence of civic knowledge and efﬁcacy
beliefs regarding civic and political activities on adolescents’ overall expected civic participation, also taking into consideration the inﬂuence of their socio-economic background. Moreover, since gender differences have been detected in the
ﬁeld of civic engagement (Cicognani, Zani, Fournier, Gavray, & Born, 2012; Metzger & Ferris, 2013; Schulz et al., 2010) we
also investigated whether the above-mentioned factors had the same effect on expected civic participation in both boys and
girls.
The present study is based on the Italian data from the International Civic and Citizenship education Study (ICCS 2009;
Schulz et al., 2010) of the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA). Widespread feelings
of disaffection and mistrust of politics, with low levels of participation and interest have recently been detected among Italian
adolescents (ISTAT, 2010; Marta & Cristini, 2012), together with a slight shift toward alternative forms of civic action
(Vecchione & Mebane, 2006). Several reforms have been recently introduced in Italian schools, which intend to encourage
innovative teaching strategies so as to promote students’ civic engagement (e.g., Terrinoni & Stringher, 2013). How these
reforms should be implemented is still a matter of considerable debate, and there is a need for further indications concerning
effective teaching strategies. Therefore, the study of the factors that promote civic engagement is particularly relevant to Italy,
especially as very few studies have been conducted on Italian adolescents.
In the remainder of this paper, we ﬁrst review the research literature on the relationships between efﬁcacy beliefs, civic
knowledge, socio-economic background, and adolescents’ civic participation. Then, we describe the methods and the results
of our empirical study. Finally, we discuss our main results, also drawing out some implications for programmes aiming to
promote civic engagement in youth and for future research.
Efﬁcacy beliefs and civic participation
Research on the relationship between efﬁcacy beliefs and civic and political engagement has mainly focused on internal
political efﬁcacy, which refers to individuals’ conﬁdence in their ability to understand politics and to act politically (Vecchione
& Caprara, 2009; Zimmerman, 1989). In order to include civic activities that are more familiar to adolescents in their everyday
lives, the concept of citizenship self-efﬁcacy has recently been developed (Schulz et al., 2008; Solhaug, 2006). This refers to
adolescents’ beliefs about their capability to perform activities regarding a general concept of citizenship participation at or
outside school (e.g., discuss a newspaper article about a conﬂict between countries).
Efﬁcacy beliefs inﬂuence the likelihood of adolescents’ engagement because if they feel they are not able to deal effectively
with the political system and civic issues, they will tend to avoid opportunities for involvement (Beaumont, 2010; Pasek,
Feldman, Romer, & Jamieson, 2008). Internal political efﬁcacy and citizenship self-efﬁcacy were found to be positive predictors of youths’ expected political participation and participation in legal protests (Ainley & Schulz, 2011; Schulz, 2005;
Solhaug, 2006). Moreover, internal political efﬁcacy assessed in secondary school students was found to be a predictor of
their political activity during early adulthood and to positively inﬂuence their voting behaviour (Krampen, 2000; Pasek et al.,
2008).

In accordance with the research literature, in the present study we hypothesized a direct inﬂuence of both internal political efﬁcacy and citizenship self-efﬁcacy on adolescents’ expected civic participation. Furthermore, due to the similarities
between the two efﬁcacy constructs, a correlation between them is also expected.
Civic knowledge and civic participation
Civic knowledge has been deﬁned as an inclusive and structured construct that encompasses both the possession of information in various content domains (e.g., civic societies and principles) and the skills for using these contents (Schulz et al.,
2008; Torney-Purta et al., 2001). A basic level of civic knowledge is required for making judgements regarding civic life and for
electoral participation (Dahl, 1992; Galston, 2001). Civic knowledge has been shown to be a consistent positive predictor of
adolescents’ expected electoral participation across countries (Schulz et al., 2010; Torney-Purta et al., 2001), but its inﬂuence
on expectations of active political participation has proved to be absent or even negative (Schulz et al., 2010), as well as being
negligible as regards expected participation in protest activities (Ainley & Schulz, 2011). Finally, those few studies that have
evaluated the effects of civic knowledge on participation in speciﬁc political activities, while also taking efﬁcacy beliefs into
consideration, have shown that the effect of civic knowledge is similar to or even weaker than that of efﬁcacy beliefs
(Krampen, 2000; Solhaug, 2006).
In the present study we therefore hypothesized a direct inﬂuence of civic knowledge on expected civic participation, but,
due to the inclusion of different forms of participation, we anticipated that this effect could be substantially less than that
found in previous studies on electoral participation. Moreover, consistently with previous ﬁndings (e.g., Solhaug, 2006), we
hypothesized a reciprocal relationship between civic knowledge and efﬁcacy beliefs.
Socio-economic background and civic participation
Adolescents’ socio-economic background usually refers to the occupational status and the educational level of their
parents and the family’s literacy resources (e.g., OECD., 2012; Schulz et al. 2008). Socio-economic background inﬂuences
adolescents’ civic experiences and their opportunities for acquiring civic knowledge and practicing civic activities (Flanagan,
Levine, & Settersten, 2009; Zaff, Youniss, & Gibson, 2009). In contexts with low socio-economic resources, families have
limited means for promoting civic interest or skills, schools are unable to include citizenship in the essential educational
goals, and there is a lack of local organizations for encouraging civic values (Flanagan et al., 2009; Zaff et al., 2009). Some
studies have shown that a higher level of parent education was linked to a higher likelihood of political and civic participation
(Mahatmya & Lohman, 2012; Marzana, Marta, & Pozzi, 2012). However, in other studies socio-economic background had only
small positive effects on youths’ expected electoral participation (Manganelli, Alivernini, Lucidi, & Di Leo, 2012; Vecchione &
Mebane, 2006) and it had negative or not signiﬁcant effects on active political participation (Schulz et al., 2010). On the other
hand, adolescents’ socio-economic background was found to have a great inﬂuence on their civic knowledge (Alivernini &
Manganelli, 2011; Schulz et al., 2010; Torney-Purta 2001). Finally, while some studies indicated a positive impact of socioeconomic background on adolescents’ efﬁcacy beliefs (Perliger, Canetti-Nisim, & Pedahzur, 2006), others provided evidence of insigniﬁcant or rather weak effects (Schulz, 2005; Solhaug, 2006).
In this study we examined the inﬂuence of adolescents’ socio-economic background on each of the other constructs taken
into consideration. In accordance with the research literature, we anticipated that this inﬂuence would be stronger on civic
knowledge rather than on efﬁcacy beliefs or expected civic participation.
Gender differences
Studies highlighted signiﬁcant gender differences in the ﬁeld of civic engagement during adolescence (e.g., Hooghe &
Stolle, 2004; Metzger & Ferris, 2013; Schulz et al., 2010). In Italy some ﬁndings showed a gender gap, with higher intentions of future political involvement (e.g., Jahromi, Crocetti, & Buchanan, 2012) and interest in politics (Cicognani et al.,
2012) in boys, while other results showed similar levels of civic involvement in both boys and girls (Cicognani et al., 2012;
ISTAT, 2010; Marta, Guglielmetti, & Pozzi, 2006). Gender differences in civic engagement have been seen as the result of
an interaction between various elements (e.g., Burns, Schlozman, & Verba, 2001), such as a gender-biased socialization that
affects gender roles in the ﬁeld of civic engagement (Hooghe & Stolle, 2004) and provides different experiences for boys and
girls (Flanagan et al., 2009). However, there is still little consensus about how these differences can be explained.
In the present study we investigated whether the factors that inﬂuence expected civic participation are the same or
different for boys and for girls, by examining possible moderating effects of gender on the inﬂuences that socio-economic
background, civic knowledge, and efﬁcacy beliefs have on expected civic participation.
The current study
The aim of the present study was to evaluate the role of civic knowledge and efﬁcacy beliefs as factors that can explain
adolescents’ expected civic participation, also taking into consideration the inﬂuences of socio-economic background and
gender differences. To achieve this goal, on the basis of theoretical frameworks (Schulz et al., 2008; Torney-Purta et al., 2010)
and the research literature summarized above, a model of civic engagement was developed and tested (see Fig. 1). In the

model, expected civic participation was deﬁned as a construct including adolescents’ expectations to participate in electoral,
political, informal political, and legal protest activities. Civic knowledge, internal political efﬁcacy and citizenship self-efﬁcacy
are inter-correlated factors that have a direct inﬂuence on adolescents’ expected civic participation. Adolescents’ socioeconomic background is an exogenous factor that affects each of the other constructs included in the model. The model
was analysed also by means of a multi-group approach, in order to explore the possibility that gender might moderate the
pattern of relationships between the variables. Since the study of factors associated with adolescents’ civic engagement
currently has a special relevance in Italy, we performed all our analyses on the Italian sample from the IEA ICCS.
Method
Participants and procedure
The data analysed in the present study came from 3352 Italian eighth grade students who took part in the IEA ICCS 2009
(Schulz et al., 2010). The purpose of this study was to investigate the extent to which young people are prepared to undertake
their roles as citizens. The average age of the students’ was 13.81 years (SD ¼ .50) and 51.9% of the students were male.
Participating students were sampled using the stratiﬁed two-stage probability design deﬁned by the IEA1 (Schulz, Ainley, &
Fraillon, 2011) and they are a nationally representative sample of eighth grade students. The distribution of socio-economic
background in this sample is approximately normal (Skewness ¼ .46; Kurtosis ¼ .26).
Data was collected in the classes during an ordinary school day by means of the ICCS cognitive test and student questionnaire, following the IEA assessment protocol (Schulz et al., 2011). Each participating school gave its informed consent and
students were given a standardized introduction, which informed them of the purpose of the study and gave instructions on
how to complete the test and the questionnaire.
Measures
The ICCS cognitive test was used to assess civic knowledge while the ICCS student questionnaire was used to assess all the
other variables (Schulz, 2009; Schulz et al., 2011).
Expected civic participation
Four questionnaire scales were used to measure adolescents’ behavioural expectations regarding civic participation either
in the near future or when they would become adults (Schulz et al., 2011). In each of the scales, students were asked to rate
the probability with which they expected to take part in different activities, using a 4-point response scale (ranging from “I
will certainly do this” to “I will certainly not do this”). A three-item scale measured students’ expected electoral participation
(i.e., “vote in national elections”; “vote in local elections”; “get information about candidates
before voting in an election”). A four-item scale assessed expected participation in political activities (e.g., “help a candidate or
party during election campaign”; “join a political party”; “join a trade union”). A four-item scale assessed expected informal

students’ expected participation in four different forms of activities were used in the civic engagement model as indicators of
the latent variable of expected civic participation.
National Index of Students’ Socioeconomic Background (NISB)
The NISB was derived from three different indices (Schulz et al., 2011). The highest occupational status of parents index was
computed by assigning codes (International Labour Organization, 1990) to the students’ answers to open-ended questions
about their parents’ jobs, and then mapping these codes to the International Socioeconomic Index of Occupational Status
(Ganzeboom, de Graaf, & Treiman, 1992). The highest educational level of parents index was computed using students’ reports
on the level of education of their parents based on the six categories of the International Standard Classiﬁcation of Education
(UNESCO, 2006). The home literacy resources index was computed using students’ reports on the number of books they had in
their homes using a scale ranging from “0 to 10 books” to “more than 500 books”. A principal component analysis was
performed on the three indices and the resulting factor scores were adopted as the ﬁnal NISB scores. The NISB showed a
reliability of .68 (Schulz et al., 2011).
Internal political efﬁcacy and citizenship self-efﬁcacy
Internal political efﬁcacy was measured using a six-item scale in which students rated (on a 4-point scale ranging from
“strongly agree” to “strongly disagree”) statements reﬂecting their beliefs about their own capacity to understand politics and
engage in politics (e.g., “I have a good understanding of the political issues facing this country”, “I know more about politics
than most people of my age”; “I have political opinions worth listening to”). Citizenship self-efﬁcacy was measured using a
six-item scale, in which students rated (on a 4-point scale ranging from “very capable” to “not at all capable”) how capable
they thought they would be in performing activities related to citizenship participation at or outside school (e.g., “argue your
point of view about a controversial political or social issue”; “organise a group of students in order to achieve changes at
school”; “discuss a newspaper article about a conﬂict between countries”). Whereas internal political efﬁcacy considered
global statements regarding students’ general capacity to act politically, citizenship self-efﬁcacy considered students’ selfconﬁdence to undertake speciﬁc tasks in the context of civic participation (Schulz et al., 2008). The reliability of the scales
was .84 and .80 respectively (Schulz et al., 2011). Consistently with the measurement model developed for the ICCS (Schulz
et al., 2011), each item was used as an indicator of the efﬁcacy construct that it was designed to measure, and the two latent
factors were speciﬁed as correlated.
Civic knowledge
Civic knowledge was measured using a test consisting of 79 items (6 constructed-response and 73 multiple-choice), which
covered four content domains (civic society and systems, civic principles, civic participation and civic identities) and two
cognitive domains (knowing, reasoning and analysing). The test items were grouped into seven clusters, and each student
completed one achievement booklet consisting of three of these clusters (Schulz et al., 2011). The Rasch model (Rasch, 1960)
was employed to derive the cognitive scale from the 79 items and plausible value methodology was used to generate ﬁve
estimates of civic knowledge scores for each student (Schulz et al., 2011). These estimates were used in the model as indicators of the latent variable of civic knowledge.
Analysis plan
The small amount of missing data (approximately .2%e2.6%) was handled by means of the expectation-maximization
procedure of IBM SPPS 19.0. The process of analysis was carried out in three stages using the MPlus 6.0 software (Muthén
& Muthén, 2006), with the Robust Maximum Likelihood (MLR) estimator, which proved to be suitable for data with sampling weights (Asparouhov, 2005). A conﬁrmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed ﬁrst, in order to examine the latent
construct of expected civic participation. A structural equation modelling analysis (SEM) was then performed in order to
empirically test the civic engagement model. The model ﬁt was assessed using the MLR chi-square test statistic and multiple
ﬁt indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR; Hu & Bentler, 1999; Schreiber, Nora, Stage, Barlow, & King, 2006). Finally, a multi-group
SEM analysis was conducted in order to test the invariance of the model between boys and girls, thus exploring possible
gender differences. This was done by comparing the full invariance model, in which cross-group equality constraints were

As expected, citizenship self-efﬁcacy and internal political efﬁcacy both had a signiﬁcant direct inﬂuence on expected civic
participation but the inﬂuence of the former was twice that of the latter. These variables also proved to be signiﬁcantly
correlated. Students’ civic knowledge instead did not have a signiﬁcant inﬂuence on expected civic participation (p ¼ .115) but
proved to have positive reciprocal relationships with both of the efﬁcacy beliefs constructs. Students’ socio-economic
background had a signiﬁcant direct inﬂuence on all the other variables in the model: its inﬂuence on civic knowledge was
twice than that on citizenship self-efﬁcacy or internal political efﬁcacy, and its inﬂuence on expected civic participation was
very small. The total indirect effect of socio-economic background on expected civic participation was also signiﬁcant (b ¼ .16;
p < .001) and it was stronger than the direct effect.

across gender for the civic engagement model and the results of the difference tests between the baseline model and each of
the two invariance models.
The hypothesis of partial invariance of the model across boys and girls was retained because the chi-square difference test
was not statistically signiﬁcant (p ¼ .347), DCFI was 0, and the partially invariant model showed good ﬁt indices that were
essentially the same as those of the baseline model (see Table 2). Although the majority of the relationships between the
variables of the civic engagement model did not vary as a function of gender, differences between boys and girls were found in
four of these relationships. The parameter estimates for boys and girls for these four path coefﬁcients are shown in Table 3.
The inﬂuence of students’ socio-economic background on civic knowledge and on internal political efﬁcacy proved to be
stronger for boys than for girls, while in girls civic knowledge had a stronger relationship with citizenship self-efﬁcacy than in
boys. Finally, the direct inﬂuence of civic knowledge on expected civic participation was not statistically signiﬁcant for girls
but had a very small, but statistically signiﬁcant, negative effect in boys.
Discussion
The results of the present study showed that civic knowledge, efﬁcacy beliefs, and socio-economic background accounted
for 60% of the variance of adolescents’ expectations to participate in civic activities. These ﬁndings extend previous knowledge because they have quantiﬁed the total inﬂuence on the overall expected civic participation of factors that previously had
mostly been studied independently, with various forms of political and civic participation considered separately.
Some important differences emerged as regards the speciﬁc effect of each factor on expected civic participation. Citizenship self-efﬁcacy and internal political efﬁcacy proved to be the only factors that have a positive and strong direct effect on
expected civic participation, in both boys and girls. A greater willingness to participate civically is thus associated with the fact
that adolescents believe themselves capable of performing activities related to citizenship participation or engaging in

they also extend them by demonstrating the positive inﬂuence of efﬁcacy beliefs not only on speciﬁc areas of participation
(e.g., voting) but also on overall expected civic participation, as well as by underling the primary role of citizenship selfefﬁcacy, the inﬂuence of which was twice that of internal political efﬁcacy.
On the other hand, civic knowledge did not have, on the whole, any signiﬁcant effect on adolescents’ expected civic
participation and actually showed a slight negative inﬂuence in boys. The fact that adolescents are informed and understand
civic matters may thus have different consequences in boys and girls and, in any case, it does not seem to increase the
likelihood of their participation in civic activities. This result was partially expected because previous research found that civic
knowledge promoted only electoral participation, while it had no or very slight impact on other kinds of participation (Ainley
& Schulz, 2011; Schulz, 2005; Torney-Purta et al., 2001). Our ﬁndings showed that civic knowledge could be not relevant for
adolescents to get involved when participation includes voting as well as more active and demanding civic activities (e.g.,
boycotting products, helping a political candidate). Instead, efﬁcacy beliefs seem to positively affect their willingness to
participate in all kinds of activities. Although they are signiﬁcantly correlated, civic knowledge and efﬁcacy beliefs thus seem
to play different roles in adolescents’ expected civic participation. Further studies will be required to improve our understanding of the relationship between civic knowledge and efﬁcacy beliefs. For example, moderation models could be hypothesized, since a basic level of civic knowledge is required for making political judgements and participating in public life
(e.g., Dahl, 1992).
In accordance with previous research (Perliger et al., 2006; Schulz et al., 2010; Torney-Purta et al., 2001), our ﬁndings
conﬁrmed that a higher socio-economic background is associated with higher levels of civic knowledge and efﬁcacy beliefs.
They also revealed that socio-economic background had a limited effect on expected civic participation, which is mostly
indirect, due to the inﬂuence on civic knowledge and efﬁcacy beliefs. These ﬁndings can be explained by considering that a
higher socio-economic background is associated with more opportunities for civic experiences and more resources for
learning about politics and exercising civic skills (e.g., Flanagan et al., 2009; Zaff et al., 2009). A similar stimulating environment can enhance adolescents’ civic knowledge and efﬁcacy beliefs, which in turn inﬂuence their willingness to
participate.
The multi-group analysis showed that, although the same pattern of factors can explain a substantial part of expected civic
participation in both boys and girls, there are similarities as well as differences in the magnitude of speciﬁc relationships. The
similarities principally concern efﬁcacy beliefs, which had the same positive effects on expected civic participation in both
boys and girls. The differences mostly regard civic knowledge and socio-economic background. Our ﬁndings seem to conﬁrm
(Hooghe & Stolle, 2004) that gender differences in civic engagement during adolescence are less prominent than those seen in
adults. This could be explained by the fact that during adolescence the differences in resources (e.g., income, professional
roles) that are considered as one of the causes of the gender gap in civic engagement (Verba et al., 1995) have yet to come into
play (Hooghe & Stolle, 2004). The similarity between boys and girls could be a speciﬁc characteristic of Italy, since previous
studies also found no gender differences in the levels of civic involvement of Italian adolescents (e.g., Marta et al., 2006).
Further studies are nevertheless needed to corroborate our ﬁndings and to better understand if civic knowledge could have a
different role in the development of civic engagement in boys and in girls.
In conclusion, adolescents’ civic engagement positively stimulates their personal development (Lerner, Phelps, Forman, &
Bowers, 2009) and their future civic and political participation (Eckstein et al., 2012; Flanagan & Sherrod, 1998). The recent
increase in the tendency of young people in Italy to become detached from politics and to shift toward alternative forms of
civic action (e.g., Marta & Cristini, 2012) make it crucial to better understand which factors can promote civic participation in
youth. The current study provides valuable knowledge by clarifying which factors can really inﬂuence adolescents’ expectations to participate in various different forms of civic activities considered as a whole. Adolescents’ expected civic participation appears not to be predetermined by their socio-economic background but it can be promoted, independently of
whether they are male or female, by making them conﬁdent about their ability to participate in political and civic issues. Civic
knowledge alone does not seem sufﬁcient to promote adolescents’ willingness to participate in civic life. To become civically
involved adolescents need to be conﬁdent about their effectiveness, especially in performing civic activities that are familiar
in their everyday lives. Finally, a higher socio-economic background is associated with higher levels of civic knowledge and
efﬁcacy beliefs, and it has a mostly indirect effect on adolescents’ expected civic participation, due to its inﬂuence on these
two other factors.
From these results it is possible to derive some indications for the implementation of programmes in schools aimed at
encouraging adolescents to engage in civic activities. These indications are especially useful for Italian schools which, as a
result of recent reforms (e.g., Terrinoni & Stringher, 2013), are expected to implement some innovative instructional strategies
for promoting students’ civic engagement. Since the existing indications are rather unclear and each school can autonomously choose what strategies to adopt, there is an ongoing debate on the question. This debate has hitherto been conducted
only on a theoretical level, but our study has now provided the ﬁrst indications based on concrete empirical data. Our ﬁndings
suggest that schools should work to promote adolescents’ citizenship and political efﬁcacy beliefs. In order to achieve this
aim, schools could adopt, for example, speciﬁc methods (e.g., experience of mastery, modelling) that are proven to successfully promote self-efﬁcacy beliefs (Bandura, 1997).
Finally, some limitations of our research should be pointed out. Firstly, our data was based on a cross-sectional design.
Although the system of relationships between the variables analysed in this study is supported by previous studies, it has an
exclusively theoretical basis and thus cannot prove the existence of causal relationships. Longitudinal studies are needed in

order to corroborate our ﬁndings and provide evidence for causal relationships. Secondly, we made no hypothesis about the
direction of the inﬂuences between efﬁcacy beliefs and civic knowledge because the cross-sectional data did not allow us to
make predictions about causal relationships when there were no strong theoretical reasons for them. Longitudinal studies
would be useful to clarify this issue. Thirdly, the ﬁndings are limited by the absence of data regarding young people’s actual
behaviours. Research on civic engagement in youth has extensively studied expectations of participation, and behavioural
expectations are considered to be the best predictors of actual behaviours (Fishbein & Ajzen, 2010). In addition, several
longitudinal studies have pointed out the stability of civic attitudes and behaviours from youth to adulthood (Braungart &
Braungart, 1990; Jennings & Stoker, 2004). However, the few studies that have evaluated whether expected participation
in youth can effectively predict actual participation during adulthood have had mixed results (Hooghe & Wilkendfeld, 2008).
Further studies are thus needed in order to conﬁrm that the promotion of expectations of participation can have a positive
impact on actual participation. Finally, although we used a nationally representative sample of eighth grade students, only
Italian data was considered: future research should therefore be conducted in order to generalize our ﬁndings across various
other nations and cultural contexts.
