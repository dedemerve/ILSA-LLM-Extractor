A B S T R A C T
The question of why people abstain from voting has been extensively analysed and discussed in previous 
research. One line of research particularly emphasizes education when explaining non-voting and highlights 
education as a resource needed in democracies. Yet despite the continued expansion of formal education, voter 
turnout has declined over time. This puzzle needs to be addressed: Our paper asks whether democracy can be 
learned. Focusing on voter turnout, we conducted focus group discussions to learn who can be brought back to 
the polls and how, using the concept of passive citizenship. The results show, firstly, that political knowledge is 
generally underdeveloped. Secondly, we were able to show that activation is also a matter of fit. Different types 
of non-voters need different political education programmes as they lack different types of political knowledge. 
In conclusion, it is possible to learn democracy. However, it is a lifelong task that should begin in childhood and 
continue into old age. Since voter turnout is closely linked to political knowledge, programmes should be tailored 
to the needs of different social groups in order to engage non-voters in politics.
1. Introduction
Reasons for not voting belong ‘to one of the most fundamental puz­
zles in empirical electoral research’ (Eder, 2013, pp. 18–19, own 
translation). While Smets and van Ham (2013) conclude that education1
constitutes an important predictor of turnout that many studies have 
reinforced (for example Cabarello, 2014; Dassonneville & Hooghe, 
2017; Roβteutscher & Sch¨afer, 2016), some empirical cases paint a 
different picture: “It appears that there is no correlation between the 
educational level of citizens and political participation: Education is 
increasing, but participation is declining,” (Pickel, 2017, p. 128, own 
translation). That is why we ask whether democracy can be learned and 
how. More specifically, we focus on civic education and state the 
research question: Which non-voters can be brought (back) to the ballot 
box?
Developments in Germany illustrate Pickel's (2017) observation. In 
Germany, the expansion of education started in the 1950s (Geiβler, 
2014). After gaining momentum in the 1960s followed by stagnation in 
the 1970s and 1990s, debates on Germany's educational performance in 
international comparison and shortages in skilled labour accelerated 
further expansion in the 2000s (Geiβler, 2014).
Contrary to the findings, turnout in Germany decreased at the same 
time. While a turnout of 80% or more used to be the norm for national 
elections before the 1980s, a steady decrease set in thereafter. Similarly, 
70% to 80% cast their vote for state elections. These figures dropped to 
60% to 70% after the 1980s—with elections in Saxony (2014: 49.1%), 
Saxony-Anhalt (2006: 44.4%), Brandenburg (2014: 47.9%), North 
Rhine Westphalia (2022: 55.5%) marking the lowest points of partici­
pation (Die Bundeswahlleiterin, 2023, p. 202). Fig. 1 illustrates both 
developments: while turnout decreased, the rate of people gaining de­
grees to enter university increased.
These developments are not unique to the German case. Smets and 
van Ham state more generally that ‘notwithstanding the rise of educa­
tional levels in advanced western democracies, turnout levels have not 
risen’ (2013, p. 348). The fact that aggregate turnout did not increase­
—in fact, it even decreased—despite the expansion of educational op­
portunities remains a conundrum for researchers (Burden, 2009, p. 
540). Thus, this research seeks to shed light on the paradox of education 
and turnout. The example of Germany seems to be an interesting case:

Resolving this paradox is important because high voter turnout 
strengthens democratic legitimacy. Therefore, it is crucial to understand 
how voter turnout can be increased and why better formal education 
does not necessarily contribute to this positively. Political knowledge 
and orientations are an essential factor in this context. This article 
consists of five parts. In the next section, we introduce current research 
on the resource model and education. We then describe two conceptual 
approaches from quantitative research and modify and operationalise 
these for qualitative analysis. After this, we present our research design, 
including the methodology, data, and material. In the results section, we 
present the most important findings of the focus group research. We 
provide further insights with the help of direct quotes. The article ends 
with a discussion of the findings, followed by the conclusion, limitations 
and outlook on future research.
2. The resource model of participation and the role of 
education2
Scholars have elaborated three main principles why people do or do 
not go to the polls (Brady et al., 1995). The principle ‘they don't want to’ 
(a) describes psychological reasons. This implies a personal benefit 
calculation as posited in rational choice theories (Downs, 1957). 
Moreover, it refers to the feeling of self-efficacy, dissatisfaction, party 
loyalty, or the (lack of) interest in recent political events and issues 
(Althoff, 2014; Braun & Sch¨afer, 2022; Falter & Schumann, 1994; 
Schoen & Falter, 2003). Attributions of competence also play a role here: 
voters perceive candidates with higher levels of education to be more 
competent and are more likely to vote for them (Arceneaux & Vander 
Wielen, 2023). Apart from these factors, the internalisation of the 
electoral norm of eligible voters positively affects participation 
(Rattinger & Kr¨amer, 1995). The principle ‘nobody asked’ (b) refers to 
an individual's socialisation, such as experiences of political conversa­
tion (with family members), peer group talks, direct approaches in 
election campaigns, or contrary social isolation (Eilfort, 1995; Marquart 
et al., 2020).
The principle ‘they can't’ (c) is described by the resource model. 
According to this model, it is the individual's endowment with 
participation-relevant resources that is decisive for voting. These 
include income and certain demographic variables, such as age. In 
particular, the resource model highlights the role of education as a 
resource needed to vote. Overall, while these principles reveal hetero­
geneous motives for not going to the polling station on election day,3 we 
focus on the variables of the resource model in general and the factor of 
education in particular.
The factor income positively affects turnout. Citizens belonging to 
higher social classes go to the polling stations at higher rates (Smets & 
van Ham, 2013), whereas those who are economically disadvantaged 
turn out less frequently. This effect especially applies to the relatively 
poor and lower middle class citizens; middle and upper classes experi­
ence diminishing returns of income when it comes to the effect on voting 
(Schafer et al., 2022). Abstention is therefore unevenly distributed 
across socioeconomic groups.
Age also positively affects turnout as young citizens abstain at higher 
rates than older citizens (Smets & van Ham, 2013). Researchers provide 
two explanations for this. On the one hand, generational effects point to 
the impact of participation at a young age on future participation 
(Franklin et al., 2004; Leininger et al., 2024). According to the expla­
nation of generational effects, the political context of reaching voting 
age matters (Clarke et al., 2004). On the other hand, life cycle effects 
result in the importance of politics varying during different life phases 
(Blais & Rubenson, 2013), with the importance of politics increasing 
with age. For example, while young citizens prioritise their education, 
parents feel motivated to vote for their children's future (Blais, 2000; 
Goerres, 2009). However, eventual social isolation and health problems 
making polling stations and postal voting less accessible can prevent 
high-age groups from voting (Purdam & Taylor, 2023). The effect of age

Studies on the connection between education and turnout can be 
separated into different literatures (Althoff, 2020). One body of litera­
ture has identified education as a causal factor for turnout, whereby a 
higher level of (absolute or relative) education increases the likelihood 
of voting (Kim, 2023; Wolfinger & Rosenstone, 1980). Other research 
refers to the indirect effects of education on turnout and treats education 
as a proxy (Nie et al., 1996). Studies within this approach have identified 
characteristics that correlate with education level. Characteristics such 
as social status, political interest, language skills, and understanding the 
political process correlate with education and therefore might be more 
important to understanding abstention on the individual level (for 
example Condon, 2015; Hillygus, 2005; Kaeding et al., 2016; Le & 
Nguyen, 2021; Tenn, 2005, 2007). Some studies add that this effect is 
not uniform across groups: attending higher education and developing 
participation-relevant resources boosts voting for citizens from disad­
vantaged backgrounds rather than privileged peers (Ahearn et al., 
2023).
Additionally, some research has explored the variation of education 
and turnout across different contexts. Gallego (2009, 2015) for example 
observes that compulsory voting mitigates the effect of education on 
turnout. The presence of strong left-wing parties and trade unions as a 
contextual factor does not foster equal participation anymore (Gallego, 
2010).

(2010) emphasizes that “turnout is more equal where voting is easy” (p.

Similarly, highly educated voters may abstain out of dissatisfaction for 
example with policymaking (Althoff, 2014). The influence of education 
also varies across national contexts, though research supports a strong 
effect of education on turnout in Germany and the United States 
(Althoff, 2020; Gallego, 2010).
Despite this research attention, the paradox of rising levels of 
(formal) education and decreasing turnout persists. This may be because 
most research focuses on formal education and views “education as a 
quantity” (Willeck & Mendelberg, 2022, p. 90). Some recent research on 
twins has revealed that formal education is not necessarily associated 
with political knowledge (Weinschenk & Dawes, 2022; Weinschenk 
et al., 2023). Studies that consider political knowledge often use items of 
factual knowledge or the number of civic courses taken that can be 
measured quickly and easily and show little to no effect on voting 
(Holbein & Hillygus, 2020; Willeck & Mendelberg, 2022).
Yet interdisciplinary research (e.g. from educational sciences and 
didactics) reveals that general education does not necessarily increase 
interest in or understanding of politics and that learning methods make a 
difference (Willeck & Mendelberg, 2022). No one is a democrat by birth, 
nor do most people consider democracy a way of life (Bartels 2010); 
rather, most people understand democracy as a form of government. 
However, without democratic values being incorporated into everyone's 
socialisation, people will not experience democracy as both an indi­
vidual and collective way of life that needs to be supported, defended, 
and protected against enemies from inside and outside. Democracy must 
be learned (Fritzsche, 2005; Krammer, 2012; Krondorfer, 2016). Key 
concepts here are political education, political knowledge, and indi­
vidual political interest. People who do not understand the various 
processes of policymaking are unlikely to participate. Deimel and 
Hahn-Laudenberg (2024, p. 218) find that students' willingness to 
participate increases if they feel competent and informed. Less infor­
mation therefore means less participation (e.g., not voting and being a 
passive citizen). When considering passive citizens, it is particularly 
interesting to question which groups could be activated and how.
3. Conceptual framework and operationalisation
To understand the relevance of political knowledge for (not) voting 
on the individual level, we built a comprehensive conceptual approach. 
First, we drew from the International Civic and Citizenship Education 
Study (ICCS; Schulz et al., 2023), which focuses on political learning and 
socialisation in school contexts. Second, we sought inspiration from 
Amna and Ekman (2014), who connect interest and knowledge to 
different types of non-voters and their likelihood to return to the ballot 
box. 
(1) The International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (Schulz 
et al., 2023) is the only international comparative study on civic 
education that records not only attitudes and participation in­
tentions but also political knowledge, examining political 
learning and socialisation processes in schools.4 Explanatory 
variables include student backgrounds, information sources, ex­
periences with participation, perceptions about the political 
system and its institutions, and dispositions towards engagement 
(Schulz et al., 2023, p. 246). Empirical results for the German 
case demonstrate that willingness to participate in politics is 
lower than in other countries. Moreover, the level of political 
knowledge is more strongly linked with aspects of family back­
ground, such as socio-economic status and history of immigra­
tion, 
compared 
to 
most 
other 
countries. 
In 
North 
Rhine-Westphalia in particular, pupils are less likely to want to 
vote in elections for the national parliament than the European 
average. The willingness to participate in local elections is 
significantly below average in both German states analysed in the 
study (North Rhine-Westphalia and Schleswig-Holstein). Overall, 
the authors underscore a great need to teach traditional forms of 
participation in the democratic system to young people (Schulz 
et al., 2023, p. 257).
(2) According to the concept of active and passive citizens, non- 
voters can be differentiated by their level of political participa­
tion and interest. Amna and Ekman (2014) inductively developed 
a typology based on young respondents to understand passive 
citizens in more detail.5 They determined that the large group of 
passive citizens is not homogeneous and contains three sub­
groups: disillusioned (a), unengaged (b), and standby (c) citizens. 
Disillusioned citizens differ from the unengaged and show the 
least interest in politics. They seem to be ‘lost’ from the ballot box 
forever. Unengaged citizens instead have a low level of interest 
and hardly participate at all but are still retrievable. Standby 
citizens appear to be most interesting for further analyses. 
Although the so-called ‘everyday makers’ hardly participate 
politically, they show a high level of political interest. Indeed, 
there is no significant difference in political interest between 
them and active voters who frequently participate in elections. 
Therefore, among the three groups, standby citizens are consid­
ered the most likely to return to the ballot box (Amnå & Ekman, 
2014).

While Amna and Ekmann include 11 items for different types of 
participation and the ICCS asks for seven items each on school partici­
pation and participation in organizations, our study concentrates on 
vote abstention and seeks to explore the broader context of political 
knowledge. The reason for this relates to the fact that voting provides 
one of the most low-key forms of participation that does not require 
many resources (Brady et al., 1995).
There are two main aspects to look at (1 and 2) and a supplementary 
list of aspects (3). 
• The first aspect is participation in elections and personal reasons for 
not voting. Neither quantitative study explicitly includes arguments 
for not participating. Related to Amna and Ekman's questions about 
the likelihood of future engagement in political organizations and to 
the ICCS’ items on willingness to participate, an appropriate item 
would be the people's needs to participate in future elections. By 
adding this, we supplement Amna and Ekman's typology with the 
measures needed to mobilise different groups. As a practical impli­
cation, this enables tailored solutions to mobilise different types of 
passive citizens.
• The second aspect of political knowledge contains central places and 
actors of politics in one's private sphere and social environment. 
Examples include the recognition of a city hall, a state parliament, or 
even naming political actors. As this study targets all age groups, 
explicit items on political education in a school context are omitted. 
In contrast, items on political institutions and processes are included. 
While quantitative surveys like Amna and Ekman's study or the data 
set of Allbus do a great job of testing factual knowledge, they are (yet) 
to include process knowledge. We therefore include items regarding 
how participants allocate responsibilities as well as competencies on 
different political levels and during decision-making processes.
• A supplementary cluster encompasses context items: politics in 
conversations, people's interest in politics, and, per Amna and 
Ekman, activities in other contexts. A recent experimental study has 
indicated that lowering the voting age can lead to more discussions 
about politics at school or at home (Leininger et al., 2024). The items 
in this supplementary cluster seek to investigate with whom and on 
which occasion political issues are discussed, broadening Amna and 
Ekman's, the ICCS’, and Leininger et al.’s items regarding parents, 
friends, school, and the internet.
Furthermore, items relating to political trust, satisfaction with poli­
tics, and democracy were adapted for the qualitative nature of the 
research and to capture the context. The appendix provides a detailed 
list of all items, including the operationalisation.
4. Research design
Moving beyond the aggregate level, we explored non-voting quali­
tatively through focus group discussions. To understand the mechanisms 
of education on the individual level, we concentrated on non-voters as a 
heterogeneous group of passive citizens. Statements from focus groups 
may help to estimate whether citizens are disillusioned, unengaged, or 
on standby.
4.1. Focus group research methodology
Broadly defined, a focus group is ‘any group discussion (…) as long as 
the researcher is actively encouraging of, and attentive to, the group 
interaction’ (Kitzinger & Barbour, 1999, p. 20). In practice, focus groups 
are often carried out in order to obtain individual statements ‘quickly 
and cheaply and easily’ (Bürki, 2000, p. 102) rather than to compre­
hensively interpret participants' interactions (B¨ar et al., 2020, p. 210). A 
narrower definition refers to the analysis of what is said (Flick, 2021) 
and only takes into account the moderated discussion of a specific topic, 
which is discussed in the group in a targeted manner (B¨ar et al., 2020, p. 
212). The focus of this research is on the content of the discussions and 
not on group dynamics. During the talks, statements were collected and 
summarised rather than extensively interpreted. An important detail of 
this approach is that the participants did not know each other. More­
over, focus groups need to be homogeneous in terms of participants' 
educational 
backgrounds 
to 
create 
a 
comfortable 
discussion 
atmosphere.
Focus groups are most often used in exploratory research designs. 
Integrated into a mixed-methods design (Krueger & Casey, 2015; Mor­
gan, 1996), the exploratory phase can support survey data interpretation 
(Bloor et al., 2001; Hennink, 2007; Schulz, 2012) and facilitate the 
inductive formation of hypotheses; the latter is a research goal of this 
study. Accordingly, we worked with focus groups to obtain individual 
statements and surpass what is known from positivism6 on non-voting so 
far.

Since 1949, turnout in NRW has remained below average (Die 
Bundeswahlleiterin, 2023, p. 202). Electoral districts in Duisburg 
frequently record the lowest turnout for federal and state elections (Der 
Landeswahlleiter, 2021, 2022). Furthermore, other studies have re­

the next national elections than the European average (Schulz et al., 
2023). Our two cases thus represent extreme cases that allow studying 
abstention in more detail.
The data for this research originates from two studies using focus 
groups, namely on abstention in a) the municipal elections in Duisburg 
in 2020 and b) the state elections in NRW in 2022. The projects were 
conducted in 2021 (Duisburg) and 2023 (North Rhine-Westphalia). Both 
projects involved a similar research design and consisted of three stages: 
definition and preparation, data generation, and interpretation of the 
results. Fig. 2 summarises the research design.
During the first stage, definition and preparation, the research team 
developed a recruitment questionnaire. The populations of interest 
included non-voters during the municipal elections in Duisburg in 2020 
and non-voters in the NRW state elections in 2022. The questionnaire 
aimed to filter for members of these populations. In addition, potential 
participants answered several questions on their socioeconomic and 
demographic backgrounds based on previous research to select partici­
pants. Following a theoretical sampling model, participants were 
selected to “reflect a range of the total study population” (Kitzinger, 
1995, p. 300). To explore a variety of perspectives, non-voters with 
diverse characteristics concerning their socioeconomic and de­
mographic backgrounds were chosen. An institute specialising in market 
and opinion research carried out the recruitment (e. g. through phone 
calls) and obtained informed consent from the participants to record and 
transcribe the discussions. Furthermore, the opinion research institute 
administered a cash incentive that participants received for taking part 
in the focus group discussions.
At the same time, the team developed discussion guidelines. These 
guidelines had to be developed from scratch for the Duisburg project, 
while the NRW study drew from the Duisburg project. The team used 
previous research to develop discussion questions and included multiple 
stages to break the ice, introduce the method, familiarise participants 
with each other, discuss topics of interest, and debrief all participants 
(Hilpert et al., 2012). The topics of interest and general discussion 
questions are provided in the appendix.
Before the discussion started, the moderator informed all partici­
pants about the recording and transcription of the discussion for 
research purposes and the rules of procedure. The recording was only 
started after providing the information. The moderator pointed out that 
participants can leave the discussion at any time if they feel uncom­
fortable discussing a topic. The research team opted for an inverted 
funnel approach, moving from personal to more abstract questions, to 
assist participants who did not have immediate associations with the 
topic (Morgan & Hoffmann, 2018).
Data generation involved conducting the focus group discussions.7 In 
Duisburg, five group discussions with a total of 27 participants took 
place in September 2021. For NRW, 52 participants joined eight group 
discussions that were conducted between March and May 2023. All 
groups consisted of four to seven participants to allow everyone to 
participate (Morgan & Hoffmann, 2018; Prinzen, 2020). The discussions 
lasted for approximately 90 min. Two out of five (Duisburg) and four out 
of eight groups (NRW) included participants with at least 
university-entrance-level qualifications; all other groups consisted of 
participants with a lower level of formal education. Participants varied 
in terms of gender, age, profession, and migration background to spark 
discussion and maintain a comfortable setting (Morgan and Hoffmann, 
2018). Table 2 summarises the characteristics of the participants. We 
opted to conduct the focus group discussions online through Zoom as 
online discussions eliminate travel time. Therefore, they help to recruit 
participants in remote locations. In addition, this allows participants to 
discuss the potentially sensitive topic of abstention in a familiar envi­
ronment at home (Flynn et al., 2018). Experienced moderators of the 
research team led the discussions, facilitated interactions, and aimed to 
involve all participants (Schetula & Gallego Carrera, 2012). All group 
discussions were recorded and transcribed.
The research teams analysed these transcripts, conducting a quali­
tative content analysis using MAXQDA during the final stage of the 
project (interpretation). They developed and refined a code system 
based on the data, aiming to identify the most important topics and to 
interpret attitudes and ways of thinking (Hilpert et al., 2012). Each 
discussion was coded separately by two coders. An intercoder reliability 
of slightly above 80% (Cohen's Kappa)8 was achieved in both projects.
5. Results
This section presents the results of the focus group discussions. 
Statements within the discussions can be clearly allocated to the three 
subgroup types of passive citizens proposed by Amna and Ekmann 
(2014), namely disillusioned, unengaged, and standby citizens. This 
tripartite division proved to be extremely useful for drawing a picture of 
non-voters and their motivations for not participating in elections. 
Confronting the division with our conceptional framework for ‘paths to 
participation’ (see Table 2), lessons can be learned regarding how the 
different people behind the statements could be brought back to the 
ballot box.
In the following sub-sections, the statements from focus group dis­
cussions are illustrated according to the three subtypes. Quotations 
mirror the different categories and their characteristics.9
5.1. Disillusioned citizens
Disillusioned citizens connect politics with negative feelings. Some 
participants revealed that they usually do not talk about politics because 
of their negative feelings. One participant described a need for positivity 
that talking about politics does not bring: ‘I don't talk about politics 
because everyone has their own opinion and these discussions will lead to an 
endless loop. And when I meet people, I talk to them about positive things, and 
I don't want to emphasise negative aspects even more when I talk to my 
people, because nobody needs that’ (NRW-G6-P3-M-56-HE). Similarly, 
other participants described politics as ‘boring and exhausting’ (NRW-G3- 
P1-F-26-LE) and ‘stressful and […] annoying’ (NRW-G3-P3-M-27-LE)— 
terms that have a clear negative connotation. In addition, the level of 
interest in politics was rather low or even non-existent; as one inter­
viewee explained, ‘as harsh as it sounds, I'm not interested in [politics]’

In line with this, participants also exhibited very negative and even

their concerns and put them on the agenda; they view politicians as self- 
centred individuals who show no interest in the problems of average 
citizens and lie to benefit themselves.
Deficits in political knowledge accompany these negative attitudes. 
Some knowledge is even incorrect, as the associations that participants 
have with democracy reveal. One participant described democracy as a 
synonym for another type of state and government: ‘For me, democracy is 
synonymous with monarchy. There's one person who calls the shots, and 
everyone must follow. I don't think our system has much to do with a de­
mocracy anymore’ (NRW-G8-P4-F-53-HE). Other participants believed 
that democracy is non-existent in Germany anymore: ‘Democracy was 
what we had until 2016 […]’ (DU-G4-P1-M-32-LE). Another participant 
added the self-centeredness of politicians has led to a sham democracy: 
‘In my opinion, we are living in a sham democracy now. In other words, we 
are led to believe that we have a say, that we can vote for parties, and that we 
can express our opinion, but in the end, nobody cares what our opinion is; 
instead, decisions are made from the top, as the politicians would like them to 
be’ (NRW-G7-P6-M-58-LE).

5.2. Unengaged citizens
Unengaged citizens exhibit a low level of interest in politics and 
participate very little. During the focus group discussions, participants 
who belong to this category reported that they are barely interested in 
politics. Most of them named lack of knowledge and familiarity as rea­
sons for their disinterest. One participant articulated this difficulty as 
follows: ‘I don't deal that much with politics in general and even less with our 
state politics, and it's simply difficult for me to evaluate state politics here if 
I'm not familiar with it’ (NRW-G7-P4-F-44-LE). Similarly, another 
candidate cited a lack of knowledge as a reason for not having interest: ‘I 
personally don't know much about politics and it's not an issue for me because 
I'm not involved in it’ (NRW-G5-P4-F-26-LE). Across all group discussions, 
just one participant acknowledged that the lack of knowledge may be 
their own fault, stating that ‘it could also be that I don't pay enough 
attention to it myself, and it's certainly a bit of my own fault. Of course, you 
can find out more […]’ (DU-G2-P1-F-50-LE).
As a result, unengaged participants say that they do not like to talk 
about politics in conversations and that discussing politics feels un­
comfortable. Some commented that they rarely talk about politics, even 
if conversation partners start talking about the topic. For example, this 
participant talked about politics only in response to her husband: ‘When I 
do [talk about politics], I talk about it with my husband because he always 
has an opinion about it and deals with it a lot. But I generally don't like talking 
[about politics] to other people because one may usually say the wrong thing 
anyway’ (NRW-G5-P4-F-26-LE). Another participant revealed having a 
friend who starts political conversations that they then engage in: ‘I have 
a friend and he likes to talk about politics; that's not quite my subject area, but 
if I get started, then that works, too’ (NRW-G6-P5-F-27-HE). Another 
occasion for participants in this category to talk about politics includes 
policies or events with an immediate and profound effect on many as­
pects of their daily lives. One participant, for example, mentioned the 
rules during the COVID-19 pandemic as a rare occasion to talk about 
politics: ‘Since COVID, we've been talking about how it works at the 
university and what measures are in place.’ (DU-G5-P1-F-31-HE). One 
participant admitted that ‘I haven't looked into it [politics] at all, so I can't 
say anything about it’ (NRW-G3-P3-M-27-LE) when it comes to politics in 
conversations.
In line with these irregular interactions with political topics, par­
ticipants in this category claimed to have neutral attitudes towards 
politics and politicians. Many admitted that they cannot evaluate poli­
tics because of their lack of involvement; as this participant summarised, 
‘I'm not involved enough in the topic, and I can't judge whether they [politi­
cians] do it well or whether they do it badly’ (NRW-G6-P5-F-27-HE). 
Similarly, another participant questioned who is involved in lower levels 
of government and can evaluate processes and results: ‘One really must 
deal with it, I think. Who deals with the city council?’ (DU-G1-P3-F-32-LE). 
Apart from the lack of involvement, some participants noted the prev­
alence of negative news items in politics. Given that the majority of news 
items are negative and the rarity of seeing anything positive on the news, 
participants felt that it would be hard to develop attitudes and points of 
view. As this participant expressed, ‘I only hear negative things, so I can't 
judge the positive things. [Politicians] should simply be much more present’ 
(NRW-G3-P1-F-26-LE).
These findings insinuate a lack of political knowledge on the part of 
the participants that becomes more prevalent in relation to the political 
process specifically. Many participants belonging to the unengaged 
category were unable to recognise the central locations of political ac­
tion. In reaction to a picture of the state parliament of North Rhine- 
Westphalia, participants did not recognise the building and made 
comments such as ‘I can't say anything about it’ (NRW-G5-P5-F-61-LE). 
Similarly, important actors in state or municipal politics were unknown 
to many participants, as this participant explained: ‘You just can't relate to 
it. You don't even know the people. I wouldn't even know who is responsible 
here in NRW. I have no idea. I haven't seen our mayor yet either’ (NRW-G7- 
P2-F-61-LE). Apart from locations and actors, participants have diffi­
culties allocating tasks, responsibilities, and competencies within the 
multilevel system. For example, one participant admitted having diffi­
culty understanding what the federal state does: ‘I'm not quite sure what 
the state parliament is really there for. I don't know what its tasks are, so it's 
very difficult for me to evaluate whether I'm satisfied with what is and isn't 
decided there’ (NRW-G4-P4-F-24-HE). Similarly, the municipal level is 
hard to understand for participants, as this statement illustrates: ‘I also 
wonder what cities still have in their hands. […] I would say that the room for 
manoeuvrability is relatively limited. Every federal state has something to say, 
then the federal government has something to say, and then there are a lot of 
things coming from Brussels in the meantime. To be honest, I don't know what 
is still being decided by the city’ (DU-G5-P4-M-39-HE). This lack of 
knowledge is accompanied by difficulties articulating an attitude or 
existing knowledge. One participant recognised that compromise within 
a coalition government is difficult at times. They observed that ‘it's not 
their fault, and it takes time for several parties to reach an agreement. And if 
two extremes are elected, then it is also difficult to come to a common de­
nominator. You have to take all that into account’ (NRW-G6-P5-F-27-HE).
The individual motivation to abstain ties into this bigger picture of 
lacking knowledge and avoiding politics as a topic. The first reason 
mainly relates to the lack of knowledge about the functioning of the 
political system. Some participants mentioned that they have difficulties 
understanding the tasks of different layers of the system, and they 
therefore do not understand the stakes of an election. For example, one 
participant shared, ‘I think it's very important to vote, but I didn't vote in the 
last [election] either. I have the problem of state parliament politics; I don't 
really know what they do or what the difference between state parliament and 
the Bundestag is’ (NRW-G4-P5-M-36-HE). Another participant reported a 
similar motivation for skipping the municipal elections: ‘I didn't vote [in 
local elections] either, and I don't know what's it about at all … no idea what 
is being done there. Either the state decides or Germany itself decides' (DU- 
G4-P1-M-32-LE).

efficacy of voting. One participant mentioned his age as a reason for this

5.3. Standby citizens
Standby citizens are interested in politics but do not participate. The 
discussions revealed that there are indeed several participants who are 
interested in politics. Their reported interest is more fine-grained than 
politics in general. Many participants who can be placed into this group 
named specific times and events of interest or policy fields they are 
interested in. One participant, for example, cited elections as a point in 
time when their interest increases: ‘I usually become more interested in 
politics right before the elections’ (DU-G4-P4-F-31-LE). Other mentioned 
specific policy fields, such as ‘environmental policies or social policies for 
young people’ (DU-G1-P1-M-33-LE). In addition, several participants 
identified levels within the multilevel system, like ‘the general political 
issues that arise in Berlin’ (NRW-G4-P2-F-65-HE). Another participant 
specifically mentioned ‘world politics’ as an area of interest compared to 
lower levels like state or local politics (DU-G4-P1-M-32-LE).
Similarly, participants who belong to this group mentioned that they 
do talk about politics in conversations. However, they delimit politics as 
a conversation topic to specific times, events, and people. For example, 
one participant stated that politics is a topic ‘within the family, but I 
refrain from discussing political issues with friends or colleagues’ (NRW-G8- 
P7-F-43-HE). Hence, participants keep political discussions within their 
inner circles and only discuss political issues openly with family mem­
bers and close friends. Acquaintances or coworkers are not part of such 
discussions. Furthermore, timing and events influence these discussions 
and whether politics is an issue or not. For one participant, politics is ‘a 
topic that I talk about with friends, especially now before the elections’ (DU- 
G3-P3-F-32-HE). Another participant revealed that ‘politics hasn't been an 
issue for us. At the moment, it's an issue because of the air filters in schools 
because the legislation on this is just nonsense’ (DU-G5-P2-F-42-HE).11
Statements like these illustrate under which circumstances politics is 
discussed among participants. These conditions involve large-scale 
events, like federal elections, or policies with immediate effects on 
daily life, such as legislation during the pandemic or rising costs of 
living.
Participants who belong to this group revealed no hostile feelings 
towards politics and do not perceive politics and politicians as overly 
negative. When discussing the problem-solving capacities of politics and 
politicians, some participants noted the willingness of politicians to deal 
with problems: ‘I have to say that they try hard, but they can't achieve what 
they would like to do’ (NRW-G6-P3-M-56-HE). Participants named 
various reasons for politicians' inability to realise perfect solutions. Most 
of these reasons are not attributable to politics directly but concern the 
complex nature of the problems, which complicates finding solutions, as 
well as bureaucracy that hinders timely solutions and the inadequate 
financial resources left behind by predecessors. Specific politicians are 
sometimes mentioned, but the unwillingness or inability to solve prob­
lems is not generalised to all politicians.
These perceptions are situated within the context of knowledge 
deficits concerning politics. Although these participants are interested in 
politics, they admitted that they struggle with deficits in political 
knowledge that hinder participation in general and voting in particular. 
For example, one participant complained about speeding cars on a street 
in his neighbourhood and the dangers it causes. However, he confessed 
that he does not know who to turn to with this problem, concluding that 
‘there are so many hurdles that I don't dare to [bring it up]’ (DU-G1-P1-M- 
33-LE); this illustrates that participants have a hard time identifying the 
contact person responsible for their neighbourhoods. Some can name 
the city's major, the state prime minister, or prominent Bundestag 
members from their district, but other state or municipal representatives 
remain unknown. As one participant admitted, ‘I wouldn't even know who 
is responsible here in NRW’ (NRW-G7-P2-F-61-LE). Another deficit relates 
to competencies and the difficulty of allocating them within a multilevel 
system. One participant shared, ‘I'm lacking an overview of […] what be­
longs to the state level and what belongs to the federal level’ (NRW-G7-P4-F- 
44-LE). Similarly, another participant commented, ‘It's complicated when 
there are three levels. Who is responsible for what? It's all a little opaque’ 
(DU-G4-P2-M-29-LE).
These difficulties extend to the participants' understanding of de­
mocracy, which was revealed to be limited. When asking participants 
about their associations with democracy, many referred to elections (e. 
g., ‘It means that there is a public election’; NRW-G6-P2-M-65-HE). Others 
referred to abstract concepts like freedom and freedom of expression, 
like this participant: ‘For democracy to exist, I definitely need freedom of 
expression’ (NRW-G6-P2-M-65-HE). Beyond these concepts, participants 
often remained silent. Nonetheless, they expressed positive attitudes 
towards democracy in general and elections in particular. For example, 
‘I feel very positively about it because it is for the inhabitants of the state’ 
(NRW-G6-P4-M-22-HE). Another participant added that ‘democracy and 
the right to vote are also very important to me’ (DU-G2-P3-F-23-LE). Others 
gave reasons for the importance of elections that were related to civil 
rights and duties: ‘I have to be honest and say that from the moment I was 
actually allowed to vote, I saw it as my right and my duty because it hasn't 
always been common for us to be allowed to vote, especially for women’ 
(NRW-G2-P4-F-48-HE). Another participant highlighted the possibility 
of facilitating change: ‘Elections are definitely important because you can 
have a say or try to change something every four years’ (DU-G5-P3-M-40- 
HE).

6. Discussion
The focus group discussions demonstrate that non-voters are not a 
homogeneous group. The focus group discussions reveal that a deficit in 
political knowledge manifests itself at all levels: details about German 
polities, politics, and policies were unclear or completely unknown to 
the participants. Moreover, participants from all kinds of formal 
educational backgrounds exhibited a lack of political knowledge. Con­
trary to the argument of the resource model, this is not an issue exclu­
sively for disadvantaged groups. Only the degrees of ignorance differ. 
With participants coming from a variety of educational backgrounds, 
this confirms that the quality of education matters instead of the 
quantity such as years of schooling or number of courses taken as Wil­
leck and Mendelberg (2022) summarize. Our finding goes well beyond 
the resource argument and emphasizes the role of political knowledge 
across societal groups.
To make sense of these shades of ignorance, the proposed distinction 
of different types of passive citizens (Amnå & Ekman, 2014) was 
applied. Participants in the focus group discussions were categorised as 
standby, unengaged, or disillusioned. The level of (or the lack of) po­
litical knowledge varied across these groups. In the group of unengaged 
citizens, there was a lack of understanding of the political system and its 
basic features. In comparison, the group of standby citizens ‘only’ fails to 
understand electoral campaigns and party communication in detail. In 
principle, these two groups can be regarded as recoverable, and some 
participants belonging to these categories even made suggestions about 
what would bring them back to the ballot box.
This adds to the finding that turnout is high when voting is easy 
(Graeb & Bernhagen, 2023) and that simple institutional designs miti­
gate the effect of education (Gallego, 2010). Apart from institutional 
designs simplifying the process of voting, explaining the usefulness of an 
election clearly and crafting communication carefully contributes to the 
accessibility of an election as our participants mention. Difficulties of 
understanding political communication and a lack of understanding 
about the usefulness of an election withheld participants from voting. 
Candidates play a role in communicating clearly – in addition to 
educational background (Arcenaux & Vander Wielen, 2023) communi­
cational skills that respect voters’ needs matter to draw citizens to the 
polls.
The disillusioned group exhibited a lack of political knowledge as 
well. Contrary to the other groups, members of this group clearly stated 
that they will most likely not vote in the coming elections. However, in 
terms of numbers, this group is significantly smaller than the other two 
groups. Reasons for this might include that this group is not interested in 
politics anyway or holds negative attitudes towards politics and politi­
cians and is therefore even harder to motivate to participate in research 
on politics than general non-voters. In addition, social desirability might 
factor in: some disillusioned participants might not have been 
completely honest about their interest in politics and their future in­
tentions (not) to vote due to the negative connotation and stigma con­
nected to abstention. Moreover, they might have exaggerated their 
interest in politics or might not have openly admitted being 
disinterested.
Furthermore, it became apparent that there are overlaps between the 
different groups—they could not always be clearly separated as dialogue 
effects12 are likely to stimulate followers or defectors. Nonetheless, 
certain characteristics emerged that provide useful insights for winning 
back voters. It seems most important to understand that standby and 
unengaged citizens have different needs. They should be addressed with 
different political education programmes targeting more general polit­
ical knowledge for the former group and helping to elucidate political 
communication for the latter. Political education cannot be a one-size- 
fits-all programme; it must be designed specifically for each group and 
their specific needs.
7. Conclusion
In this study, we strove to solve the paradox of education and turnout 
by asking whether democracy can be learned and who can be brought 
(back) to the ballot box and how. To answer this question, we first 
explored how the interconnection between voting and knowledge does 
not concern general education but political knowledge in particular. 
With the help of a qualitative study design using focus group discussions, 
it became clear that political knowledge is generally underdeveloped. 
The lack of political knowledge does not exclusively concern disad­
vantaged groups.
Our research illustrates that the degree differs. To make sense of 
these degrees in ignorance, Amnå and Ekman's (2014) distinction of 
disillusioned, unengaged and standby citizens was applied and 
extended. While the disillusioned group exhibits a lack of knowledge or 
even incorrect knowledge and is unlikely to be brought back to the polls, 
the unengaged group lacks knowledge about the political system and its 
functioning. This group can be retrieved from passivity with political 
and civic education targeting these deficits. Of all three groups, standby 
citizens present the most knowledge about the system. Their deficits are 
rather found in understanding electoral campaigns and political parties.

Greater political interest and knowledge contribute to a greater 
willingness to participate. However, simply increasing the proportion of 
people with higher formal education is unlikely to be effective in further 
promoting participation (Pickel, 2017, p. 133). Our research has 
demonstrated that different faces of passivity require different ap­
proaches for civic education to participate in elections.
There are already numerous offers for democracy education. Thus 
far, these have mainly been initiatives by foundations and (federal and 
regional) agencies for political knowledge and education.13 Commem­
orating the fact that public funding is being cut back, there is growing 
concern about how to meet the ever-increasing need for basic demo­
cratic education. "What is needed is high-quality political education in 
schools,” (Pickel, 2017, p. 133, own translation). It is possible and 
necessary to learn democracy. However, political education must start 
as early as possible, ideally in the early childhood (Van Deth, 2017, p. 
114).
This also applies to the recently discussed reforms of the electoral 
law to lower the voting age. Experimental studies that deal with this 
topic have concluded that lowering the voting age makes sense because 
it leads to more communication about politics and elections at home and 
at school (Leininger et al., 2024). This conclusion is undoubtedly plau­
sible, but it is overlooked that ignorance about politics at home does not 
simply (passively) disappear and cannot (actively) be overcome so 
easily. Neither disinterest in politics nor parents' lack of political 
knowledge changes immediately with the newly sparked interest of 
children, who, due to the election reform, are eligible to vote for the first 
time. In fact, parents can act as negative reinforcers at home. This em­
phasizes once again the urgency of providing young people with dem­
ocratic education at school (Schulz et al., 2023) and addressing adults 
with lifelong political education at the same time. Additionally, the 
focus should be on political participation knowledge rather than just on 
theoretical knowledge (van Deth, 2017, p. 114).
Due to the limitation of our study – we covered only a limited age 
range in the focus groups and did not include first-time voters and 
participants older than 70 years – further research is needed regarding 
which parts of society to reach with which type of educational resources 
to direct programmes and resources effectively.
