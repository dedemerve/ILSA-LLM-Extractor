A B S T R A C T
In the context of the Global South, including Bangladesh, education is often advocated for its 
labor market and economic returns. However, education also yields non-labor-market benefits 
that contribute to societal and economic transformation. Understanding these non-economic 
private and social returns is critical to justify continued public investment in education. This 
paper examines the relationship between education and civic engagement in rural Bangladesh. 
Using nationally representative data from the Bangladesh Integrated Household Survey (BIHS) 
and applying logit, Poisson, and Propensity Score Matching (PSM) models, we investigate how 
different levels of formal education are associated with civic behaviors. The findings indicate that 
higher educational attainment significantly increases individuals’ likelihood of public speaking 
and community contributions, highlighting the civic benefits of education. While acknowledging 
the limitations of causal inference, the analysis underscores the importance of education in 
fostering active civic participation in rural settings. These results suggest that public investment 
in education not only promotes economic growth but also strengthens civic engagement, a 
cornerstone for sustainable development and social cohesion.
1. Introduction
The concept of human capital has evolved beyond its traditional focus on economic or labor market outcomes to encompass non- 
labor-market or non-economic outcomes of education, such as civic engagement (Desjardins, 2001). This broader perspective reflects 
growing recognition of education’s societal roles, including its contribution to fostering civic participation, enhancing social capital, 
and addressing societal cohesion. In Global North contexts, concerns about declining levels of social capital have prompted a reas­
sertion of education’s purposes to include fostering civic engagement (Mac Labhrainn & McIlrath, 2016). Accordingly, scholars and 
policymakers have called for enhanced investment in education, emphasizing its potential to foster active citizenship and collective 
responsibility (Behrman, 1997; Haveman & Wolfe, 1984; McMahon, 1998, 2007; McMahon & Oketch, 2013, 2010; Wolfe & Zuvekas, 
1997).
While civic engagement is often celebrated for its societal benefits, it remains a contested concept shaped by diverse ideological and 
theoretical underpinnings (Skocpol & Fiorina, 1999). Conservative perspectives emphasize traditional forms of participation, such as 
community service and adherence to established societal norms (Etzioni, 1995; Putnam, 2000). In contrast, progressive interpretations 
advocate for civic engagement as a tool for systemic change, often involving dissent and protest against injustices (Almond & Verba,

1963; Hoskins & Mascherini, 2009). These debates extend to education’s role in shaping civic engagement, raising questions about 
whether educational systems reinforce social order or empower individuals as agents of transformative change (Freiri, 1970).
The seminal work of Almond and Verba’s Civic Culture (1963) offers foundational insights into these debates, describing civic 
engagement as a spectrum of behaviors encompassing participation in political processes and contributions to community life. Their 
emphasis on participatory and subject-oriented cultures highlights the diverse ways in which civic engagement manifests across so­
cieties. More recently, the International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS) has expanded these understandings by exam­
ining the role of education in cultivating civic knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors (Schulz et al., 2016). ICCS underscores the 
complexity of civic engagement, noting how education interacts with cultural, social, and political contexts to shape individual and 
collective actions.
Civic engagement is a significant non-economic outcome of education with indirect effects on societal development. The argument 
is that education provides a civic pay-off alongside economic returns, contributing to social capital (Brewer, 2003; Rahn, 1997), 
improving the performance of social institutions, and enhancing the quality of public life (Putnam, 1993, 2000). Civic engagement is 
essential for societal well-being, as active participation by citizens promotes improved living conditions, social cohesion, and collective 
problem-solving (Hauser, 2000; OECD et al., 2010). From an economic perspective, the positive externalities generated by civic 
engagement reinforce its role in the effective functioning of society (Dee, 2020).
Civic engagement has been conceptualized and measured in various ways (Boles, 2009; Shah, 1998). Scholars have examined it in 
terms of volunteering, political interest, trust/tolerance, voter turnout, group membership, leadership roles, or contributions to 
community development projects (Dee, 2004; Milligan et al., 2004; OECD, 2010). These activities range from traditional forms of 
community service to more progressive actions, such as advocating for societal needs or protesting against institutional misbehavior. 
The contested nature of these behaviors highlights the nuanced interpretations of civic engagement as both a tool for social stability 
and a mechanism for transformative change (Ekman & Amnå, 2012).
In this paper, civic engagement is operationalized through two primary indicators: (1) community leadership, measured by in­
dividuals’ comfort in public speaking and active membership/leadership in civic or charitable groups, and (2) contributions to 
community betterment, assessed through time or monetary investments in development projects, including infrastructure mainte­
nance, public works, and religious establishments.
Community leadership is a critical dimension of civic engagement. It reflects individual agency and the capacity for collective 
action, measured by comfort in public speaking (Cicognani et al., 2012; Wang & Graddy, 2008) and group membership (Meinzen-Dick 
et al., 2014). These activities encompass speaking out on infrastructure decisions, ensuring fair wages, and protesting authorities’ 
misconduct—actions that illustrate how education empowers citizens to shape their communities (Adler & Goggin, 2005). Similarly, 
contributions to community betterment through infrastructure and service projects demonstrate the tangible outcomes of civic 
engagement (Diller, 2001).
Despite substantial evidence from Global North settings, the relationship between education and civic engagement remains 
underexplored in Global South contexts. In Bangladesh, this gap often leads to the undervaluation of education’s societal contributions 
in public discourse and policymaking, perpetuating underinvestment and hindering overall development (McMahon, 2009, W.W. 
2018). Recognizing this limitation, this paper seeks to generate rigorous evidence on civic engagement as a non-labor-market private 
outcome of education in rural Bangladesh.
The research aim of this paper is to examine the extent to which different levels of formal education are associated with civic 
engagement. Although some scholars contend that non-labor-market outcomes such as civic engagement primarily emerge from 
tertiary education (McMahon, 2007, 2009; Oketch, 2022; Unterhalter et al., 2018), this paper extends the analysis to include primary, 
secondary, and higher secondary education. In Bangladesh, civic education is introduced at the primary level through school practices 
and social studies curricula, highlighting the need to assess its impact across all levels of formal education.
The focus on rural Bangladesh is particularly relevant for three reasons. First, a significant majority (61%) of the country’s pop­
ulation resides in rural areas (World Bank, 2021). Second, while substantial research exists on education’s labor market outcomes in 
rural Bangladesh (Asadullah, 2006; Asadullah & Rahman, 2009; Wodon, 2000), there is limited evidence on its non-labor-market 
outcomes, such as civic engagement. Third, the availability of a nationally representative rural dataset provides a robust basis for 
analyzing the relationship between education and civic engagement.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the conceptual framework, outlining the theoretical 
rationale for a plausible relationship between education and civic engagement. Section 3 situates the investigation within the context 
of Bangladesh. Section 4 then details the data sources and methodological approach employed in the analysis. Section 5 presents the 
empirical results, and Section 6 offers a discussion of the findings, including their policy implications, and concludes the study.
2. Conceptual framework
The potential for formal education to foster civic engagement lies in its ability to provide individuals with the knowledge, skills, and 
resources necessary to participate actively in society (Kahne & Sporte, 2008; Torney-Purta, 2002). Education increases awareness 
about the importance of civic engagement for societal well-being (Hauser, 2000) and offers lived experiences of civic participation 
through student committees, representative roles, and voluntary work within educational institutions (Camino & Zeldin, 2002; 
Campbell, 2010; Morse, 1989; Peters et al., 2010; Watson et al., 2011). Furthermore, education enhances individuals’ earning po­
tential, thereby empowering them with resources to contribute meaningfully to their communities (Becker, 2009; Mincer, 2018; 
Psacharopoulos & Patrinos, 2018; Schultz, 2022).
This paper employs a conceptual framework that delineates the pathways linking education and civic engagement, drawing on the

2.1. Human, social, and identity capital
Formal education plays a critical role in cultivating human capital by equipping individuals with cognitive skills, knowledge, and 
problem-solving abilities essential for effective participation in civic activities (Schuller et al., 2002). Human capital provides in­
dividuals with the intellectual tools to understand complex societal issues, engage in public discourse, and contribute to community 
development initiatives (Becker, 2009; Coleman, 1988; Schultz, 2020; Son, 2018).
Social capital, on the other hand, refers to the networks, relationships, and norms that facilitate collective action (Putnam, 1993, 
2000). Education fosters social capital by encouraging group participation, trust-building, and collaboration (D’Agostino, 2022), 
enabling individuals to connect with broader community efforts (Asadullah, 2008). These elements are crucial for effective civic 
engagement, particularly in contexts such as rural Bangladesh, where social structures are often tightly woven into the fabric of 
community life (Hossain, 2009; Khan & Maki, 2022).
In rural settings, education can create opportunities for individuals to participate in civic activities by building social capital 
through formal and informal group interactions (Flora & Flora, 2013; Warren et al., 2016). For instance, school environments often 
encourage group projects, participation in extracurricular activities, and leadership roles, which serve as early experiences of collective 
action and decision-making (Eccles & Gootman, 2002; Putnam, 2015; Schafft & Jackson, 2010). These experiences help develop norms 
of reciprocity and mutual trust, which are foundational to civic engagement (Coleman, 1988). Moreover, education strengthens 
community-level benefits by empowering individuals with the skills and networks necessary to advocate for collective needs, such as 
infrastructure development and local governance (Levine, 2016; Putnam, 2000; Woolcock & Narayan, 2000).
Finally, identity capital represents the personal attributes and sense of agency developed through education (Cˆot´e, 2016; Cˆot´e & 
Levine, 2015; Schuller et al., 2004). By nurturing self-efficacy, leadership skills, and a civic identity, formal education empowers 
individuals to take on active roles in community leadership and advocacy (Bynner et al., 2003). These capacities are particularly 
relevant in rural Bangladesh, where the development of individual agency often intersects with collective community goals (Hossain, 
2018; Kabeer, 2011; Mahmud et al., 2012; White, 2017).
Overall, the conceptual framework highlights formal education as a pivotal channel for fostering civic engagement through its

3. Civic engagement in the context of rural Bangladesh
The conceptualization of civic engagement in the Bangladeshi context is closely tied to the nation’s approach to civic and citi­
zenship education. As Kerr (1999) emphasizes, the structure and implementation of civic education are deeply influenced by historical, 
socio-political, and economic factors. In Bangladesh, civic and citizenship education is integrated within social studies curricula, 
reflecting a commitment to fostering civic engagement at multiple levels of education (Bhowmik et al., 2021b).
3.1. Civic education in education policy
Bangladesh’s National Education Policies of 2000 and 2010 underscore the importance of civic education. The 2000 policy 
explicitly introduced social studies education at the primary level (Ministry of Education, 2000), while the 2010 policy expanded this 
integration under the subject Bangladesh and Global Studies, spanning both primary and secondary levels (Ministry of Education, 
2010). The policy outlines 30 general objectives, many of which emphasize civic and citizenship education (Ministry of Education, 
2010, pp. 8–9). These objectives are reflected in the curriculum and textbooks across various educational levels, aiming to inculcate 
civic values and responsibilities in students (Bhowmik et al., 2021a).
Integrating civic education into mainstream subjects is a pragmatic approach that ensures all students, regardless of their field of 
study, are exposed to civic values and practices (Hess & McAvoy, 2015; Levine & Kawashima-Ginsberg, 2017; Parker, 2008). This 
strategy embeds citizenship education into the broader educational framework, promoting civic engagement at scale (Kahne & Sporte, 
2008; Levinson, 2012; Torney-Purta et al., 2015). For instance, the Bangladesh and Global Studies curriculum encourages schools to 
assess students’ civic participation, including discipline, leadership, and patriotism (NCTB, 2012b). However, implementation chal­
lenges persist, as limited teacher training and a lack of systematic assessment mechanisms hinder the realization of these goals 
(Bhowmik et al., 2021a).
3.2. Civic education in the curriculum and textbooks
In Bangladesh, civic education is not delivered as a standalone subject but is instead integrated into the social studies curriculum at 
the primary, secondary, and higher secondary levels. Table 1 below outlines the civic education-related subjects offered at various 
educational stages and their alignment with specific domains of civic engagement.
The integration of civic and citizenship education within the social studies curriculum spans different educational levels, including 
primary, secondary, and higher secondary grades. Details of this integration are summarized in Table 1 below. To evaluate the 
emphasis placed on civic engagement issues within the curriculum and textbooks, Bhowmik et al. (2021a) provide valuable insights, 
employing the framework established by the International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS).
The ICCS framework identifies four overarching domains—civic society and system, civic principles, civic participation, and civic 
identities—which are further divided into twelve sub-domains (Schulz et al., 2016, pp. 15–22). For the purpose of this study, particular 
attention is given to the sub-domains associated with civic engagement and participation, which include: 
1. Community participation—comprising activities such as volunteering, participation in organizations, and acquiring information to 
benefit the community.
2. Influencing—encompassing engagement in public debate, advocacy, policy development, and recognition of corruption.
3. Decision-making—involving active participation in implementing policies and practices.

Furthermore, integrating civic education topics into mainstream subjects positions civic engagement as an essential component of

4. Data and methodology
4.1. The BIHS data and sample specifics
The Bangladesh Integrated Household Survey (BIHS) is a three-round panel survey conducted by the International Food Policy 
Research Institute (IFPRI) in 2011–12, 2015 and 2018–19. This BIHS survey data is nationally representative of rural Bangladesh, i.e., 
representative of the rural areas of each of the eight divisions in Bangladesh. The number of sample households that are nationally 
(rural) representative are 4423 in 2011–12, 4619 in 2015 and 4886 in 2018–19. In this paper, we use all three rounds of data from the 
BIHS survey, thereby leading to the creation of a pooled dataset.1
The BIHS data is a good fit for the investigation in this paper because, as per the focus and research question, the data includes both 
a range of civic engagement outcomes and information on individuals’ education. Considering that the civic engagement information 
is only available for a particular subset of the sample respondents, these questions were posed to only one male adult and one female 
adult member per household, the final sample size in this paper is 24,440.
4.2. Variables of interest
In terms of the variables of interest, we use respondents’ answers to questions in the BIHS survey to construct the civic engagement 
outcomes used as dependent variables. Each of these dependent variables depicts either respondents’ affirmative (yes) or negative (no) 
answer to questions related to particular measures of civic engagement.
In addition to using each variable separately as a dependent variable, we use integrated constructs of the variables where we treat 
the integrated construct as a score in the form of a count2 variable. In each case, we add the ‘yes’s (=1) and the ‘no’s (=0) from each of 
the variables under the construct to form the additive score.

The primary covariate of interest in this study is the level of education, categorized into five groups: no formal education, primary 
education, secondary education, higher secondary education, and tertiary education. By employing this ordinal variable as the key 
explanatory factor, the analysis investigates whether individuals with higher levels of education are more likely to exhibit civic 
engagement.
In addition to the level of education, the empirical model includes several control variables to account for potential confounding 
factors. These covariates are age, experience, gender, household size, marital status, religion, region, monthly per capita household 
expenditure, and an asset index. The asset index encompasses scores derived from the possession of items such as radios, televisions, 
jewelry, bicycles, rickshaws, mobile phones, sewing machines, livestock (e.g., cows), fishing nets, solar panels, and computers. Table 3
provides summary statistics for various civic engagement outcomes across different levels of education, highlighting several notable 
patterns.
First, with respect to the ability to speak confidently on matters such as infrastructure decisions, wage payments, or protests, a clear 
trend emerges. A substantial proportion of respondents with no formal education—for example, approximately 59.5% in the context of 
infrastructure decisions—indicate a lack of participation. In contrast, as education levels increase (e.g., primary, secondary, and higher 
secondary), the proportion of respondents affirming their participation consistently surpasses those responding negatively, with this 
gap widening progressively at each higher educational level. Notably, the largest disparity between affirmative and negative responses 
is observed among individuals with tertiary education.
Second, regarding contributions to various community causes in the form of time or monetary resources, a stark distinction is 
evident between different types of contributions. Specifically, there are fewer affirmative responses for contributions to road or 
irrigation maintenance or public works compared to contributions to religious establishment building, where affirmative responses 
outweigh negative ones.
These descriptive patterns raise the question: Do these trends persist when examined through regression analysis?
4.3. Empirical strategy
There are two types of dependent variables, each of which informs the empirical approach taken in exploring the association 
between the particular dependent variable and the covariates. These two3 types of dependent variables are: (1) Dummy variables with 
two categories, and (2) Count variables as scores in the case of the ‘integrated’ constructs. In the case of the dummy variables as 
dependent variables, we use the logit model as an empirical approach, and in the case of the integrated scores, we adopt a count data 
model which we estimate using Poisson/negative binomial regression models. In each of the logit or Poisson or negative binomial 
models, we control for several factors such as age, experience, gender, household size, marital status, religion, region, monthly per 
capita household expenditure and asset index.
Second, for the integrated scores treated as a count variable, the Poisson model serves as the natural starting point for estimation 
(Hirvonen & Hoddinott, 2017; Kouser & Qaim, 2011). The Poisson model is a standard approach for modeling count data, assuming 
that the mean and variance of the dependent variable are equal, a property referred to as equidispersion (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013; 
Gardner et al., 1995; Hilbe, 2014). However, this assumption is often restrictive, as count data in real-world applications frequently 
exhibit overdispersion, wherein the variance exceeds the mean (Long, 1997; Ver Hoef & Boveng, 2007). Overdispersion can lead to 
biased parameter estimates and underestimated standard errors if not properly accounted for, compromising the reliability of the 
results (Breslow, 1990; Ismail & Jemain, 2007). To address this issue, the negative binomial regression model is employed as it in­
troduces an additional parameter to account for overdispersion (Hilbe, 2011; Lawless, 1987). This parameter allows for greater 
flexibility by explicitly modeling the variance as a function of the mean, thereby accommodating instances where the variance exceeds 
the mean (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013; Greene, 2008). In this analysis, both the Poisson and negative binomial models are considered to 
ensure robustness in estimation.
Specifically, the choice between these models depends on statistical tests for overdispersion in the count data (Dean, 1992; Hilbe, 
2014; Lawless, 1987). When the overdispersion parameter is statistically insignificant, indicating no evidence of overdispersion, the 
Poisson model is preferred due to its simpler structure and efficiency (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013; Winkelmann, 2008). Conversely, 
when the overdispersion coefficient is significant, the negative binomial model is used as it provides more accurate parameter esti­
mates under such conditions (Greene, 2008; Ver Hoef & Boveng, 2007). Although the functional forms of the Poisson and negative 
binomial models are similar, the key distinction lies in the estimation of the overdispersion parameter, which allows the negative 
binomial model to adjust for the additional variability in the data (Cameron & Trivedi, 2015; Hilbe, 2011). This methodological 
approach ensures that the most appropriate model is selected based on the underlying characteristics of the data, thereby enhancing 
the validity of the results (Long & Freese, 2014; Zeileis et al., 2008).
Furthermore, the observed differences in civic engagement across education levels are not merely cross-sectional but may also be 
observed across cohorts over time, that is, across generations. A comparison between such cohorts may reveal evidence of diminished 
civic engagement (Galston, 2001). Specifically, the potential association, in terms of both significance and extent, between education 
and civic engagement may vary across different age groups (see, for example, Flanagan & Levine, 2010). In the context of rural 
Bangladesh, we hypothesize that the relationship between education and civic engagement may be influenced by the historical and 
structural changes that occurred after the country’s independence in 1971.

To explore this, we divide the sample into distinct age cohorts, assuming that individuals from different generations may have had 
varying educational experiences due to the temporal differences in their formal education. Specifically, we categorize individuals 
based on the year in which they completed their secondary school certificate (SSC) exams, which serve as the national benchmark for 
the completion of secondary education in Bangladesh. Given that the average age at which an individual completes their SSC exams is 
sixteen, we distinguish between two primary cohorts: the pre-1971 cohort, comprising individuals who completed their SSC exams (i. 
e., reached at least sixteen years of age) by 1971, and the post-1971 cohort, comprising individuals who completed their SSC exams 
after 1971.
The rationale behind this division is that the historical and structural context surrounding formal education in Bangladesh un­
derwent significant transformations after 1971, with the country gaining independence and undergoing substantial political, eco­
nomic, and educational reforms (Hossain & Zeitlyn, 2010). These structural changes may have had profound implications for the 
quality and accessibility of education, as well as for the opportunities available for civic engagement (Lewis, 2011). The post-1971 
cohort attended formal schooling in a newly independent Bangladesh, a period marked by national reconstruction and the estab­
lishment of a more formalized educational system, which may have influenced their attitudes toward and engagement with their 
communities (Riaz, 2019).
The differences in educational quality between the pre- and post-1971 cohorts are substantial and contribute significantly to 
variations in civic engagement. Prior to independence, education in Bangladesh was predominantly focused on a more limited cur­
riculum, with less emphasis on civic education, critical thinking, and community participation (Imam, 2005). Access to education was 
also more restricted, especially in rural areas, where limited resources and a lack of infrastructure hindered educational attainment 
(Asadullah, 2006). After 1971, however, the new government invested heavily in educational reforms, increasing both the quantity 
and quality of formal education, particularly in the aftermath of the independence movement, which emphasized nation-building and 
the development of a politically aware citizenry (Chowdhury & Kabir, 2014).
The post-1971 cohort benefited from these reforms, including greater access to education, improvements in educational facilities, 
and a more inclusive curriculum that aimed to foster greater awareness of civic duties and responsibilities (Kabir, 2013). Moreover, the 
expansion of educational opportunities for women and marginalized groups further contributed to differences in civic engagement 
between the two cohorts (Hossain & Tisdell, 2005). As a result, individuals from the post-1971 cohort are more likely to have been 
exposed to educational programs that promoted active civic participation and democratic values, potentially leading to a stronger 
sense of community engagement (Mannan, 2015).

4.4. A propensity score matching (PSM) set-up
To test whether there is a significant relationship between consecutive higher levels of education and different civic engagement 
measures, we use propensity score matching as an empirical method. To do this, three dummy binomial variables are used to depict 
individuals with consecutive levels of education: (1) Primary-educated versus secondary-educated individuals (0 if primary and 1 if 
secondary), (2) Secondary-educated versus higher secondary-educated individuals (0 if secondary and 1 if higher secondary), and (3) 
Higher secondary-educated versus tertiary-educated individuals (0 if higher secondary and 1 if tertiary). Essentially, the propensity 
score matching (PSM) set-up constructs a statistical comparison group based on each of the above-mentioned consecutive educational 
level variables- indicating the individuals’ education level- using observed individual characteristics (e.g., age, experience, gender, 
household size, marital status, religion, region, monthly per capita household expenditure and asset index). Thereby, individuals 
educated up to a particular level- the ‘treated’ group- are matched on the basis of a probability, or propensity score, to individuals with 
education up to a different level, and the average treatment effect (ATT) of the treated individuals is then calculated as the mean

For the PSM4 analysis, we use nearest neighbour matching within a specified caliper distance, thus enforcing common support 
(Sianesi et al., 2003). This method is similar to nearest neighbour matching with the further restriction that the absolute difference in 
the propensity scores of matched subjects must be below a prespecified threshold, i.e., the caliper distance (Austin, 2011). As per 
specifications, we match more than a single unit with another, i.e., a 1 to many matching scheme, and so we specify ‘neighbour’ as ‘2′. 
This- as compared to simple 1:1 matching- allows the retainment of more participants in the matched sample, especially since the 
sample sizes of the treated and untreated individuals are substantially different (Thoemmes & Kim, 2011). Then, in an effort to 
improve the PSM set-up as best as possible, we follow the advice of Thoemmes & Kim (2011) and apply a ‘small caliper’ as a more 
stringent conditioning. As per the width of the caliper, Cochran and Rubin (1973) suggest that using a caliper that is a proportion 
(0.25) of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score can substantially reduce or eliminate bias, but there is, in actuality, 
no uniformly agreed upon caliper width that constitutes a maximal acceptable distance (Austin, 2011). However, a ‘tighter’ or narrow 
caliper can lead to reduced bias, closer matches and can thus improve the performance of propensity score matching (Lunt, 2014). As 
such, we use a caliper of ‘0.001′. In terms of balancing tests, we follow three methods suggested by Lee (2013)- (1) ensuring no sig­
nificant differences in the mean values of explanatory variables between the treatment and control groups post-matching, (2) 
observing a reduction in the pseudo-R² value after matching, and (3) verifying that the standardized percentage bias is less than 20 
percent for each explanatory variable and less than 10 percent on average across all explanatory variables. These criteria, based on the 
guidance of Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983), confirm the comparability of the treatment and control groups after matching.
Furthermore, we assess the overall variance using Rubin’s B and Rubin’s R metrics, following Rubin (2001). Rubin’s B, which 
measures the standardized difference in the means of the propensity scores across groups, falls well below the recommended threshold 
of 25 percent. Similarly, Rubin’s R, which compares the ratio of the variances of propensity scores, lies within the acceptable range of 
0.5 to 2. These results indicate that the post-matching balance is sufficient, demonstrating that the covariates are appropriately 
balanced and that the assumptions underpinning the matching procedure are met. These robustness checks ensure the reliability of the 
PSM estimates and provide confidence in the validity of the findings. Having satisfied these balancing requirements, the results 
indicate that the overall balance5 achieved in the PSM analyses conducted in this paper is robust and sufficient to ensure the validity of 
the matching procedure.
At this point, it is important to clarify that we do not claim causality in the case of our PSM estimates because several issues remain 
that restrict doing so. For example, propensity score matching cannot account for the omitted variable bias in a model (Grunwald & 
MacDonald, 2014; Loughran et al., 2015; Sainani, 2012). That is, a factor such as ability and how it affects education and consequently 
civic engagement would remain unknown. This is why even though we use PSM- a causational approach that controls for multiple 
sources of unobserved endogeneity- we refrain from claiming causality.
4.5. Data and method limitations
Our empirical analysis is correlational rather than causal. We are unable to control for variables such as cognitive development, 
prior attitudinal biases, and abilities, which may influence both educational experiences and civic participation. Moreover, the 
Bangladesh Integrated Household Survey (BIHS) is a repeated cross-sectional dataset, making it impossible to assess whether changes 
in formal education lead to corresponding changes in civic engagement. Additionally, the specific mechanisms through which edu­
cation may influence civic engagement remain unidentified in our empirical framework.
Notwithstanding these limitations, this paper addresses the question of the degree to which formal education is associated with 
civic engagement in the context of rural Bangladesh. In light of the absence of prior rigorous quantitative evidence on this topic, this 
study represents an effort to contribute to the understanding of the social benefits of education, particularly in relation to civic 
engagement. The findings may serve to inform policymaking decisions regarding the value of public investment in education.

5.10. PSM results in terms of consecutive levels of education
Several significant relationships between specific measures of civic engagement and consecutive levels of education are evident in 
the propensity score matching (PSM) results. First, when comparing primary-educated individuals to secondary-educated individuals, 
Table 5 reveals that secondary-educated individuals are statistically significantly more likely by 5, 4.5, 8.5, 19, and 2 percentage points

integrated constructs of community leadership and contributions to development or public works. Second, when comparing

civically in specific measures of civic participation.

Similarly, individuals in the post-1971 cohort, relative to those in the pre-1971 cohort, generally exhibit a significantly higher 
likelihood of contributing to their communities in various forms. However, this pattern does not hold in all cases. Notably, there are 
instances where individuals who primarily pursued formal education prior to 1971 demonstrate a higher likelihood of contributing to 
their communities. This is particularly evident in the case of contributions to religious establishment building, where primary- 
educated and secondary-educated individuals who largely pursued formal education before 1971 are significantly more likely to 
contribute to the construction or maintenance of religious establishments in their communities compared to their counterparts who 
primarily pursued formal education after 1971.
6. Discussion and conclusions
The findings of this study provide critical insights into the relationship between formal education and civic engagement in rural 
Bangladesh, addressing a significant gap in the literature on the non-economic outcomes of education. First, higher levels of education 
are strongly associated with increased civic engagement, particularly in the realm of community leadership. Individuals with more 
advanced education levels are significantly more likely to feel comfortable speaking publicly on issues such as infrastructure decisions, 
wage payments, and protests. This capacity to articulate community concerns publicly is a vital component of active citizenship, 
fostering equity, accountability, and collective decision-making (Briggs, 2008; Heater, 2004). These outcomes emphasize the trans­
formative role of education in developing the confidence and agency required for civic participation (Watts & Flanagan, 2007; Youniss 
et al., 2002).
Additionally, individuals with higher education levels are more likely to contribute time or financial resources to community 
causes, highlighting the broader societal benefits of education. This raises pertinent questions about the mechanisms driving this 
relationship. One explanation could be that financial stability, often associated with higher education, provides individuals with the 
means to make such contributions (Becker, 1964; Verba et al., 1995). Alternatively, formal education may cultivate intrinsic civic 
values and a heightened sense of responsibility, motivating individuals to engage in community activities regardless of their economic 
circumstances (Dewey, 1930; Nie et al., 1996). Although socio-economic variables, including household expenditure and asset index, 
are controlled for in the analysis, disentangling these influences remains a valuable area for future research.
The findings also reveal important cohort-specific differences in civic engagement. Individuals who pursued formal education after 
1971 are more likely to feel comfortable speaking in public across various contexts, including infrastructure decisions, wage payments, 
and protests. This increased comfort in public speaking among the post-1971 cohort can be attributed to educational reforms and 
broader socio-political changes following Bangladesh’s independence (Chowdhury & Sarkar, 2018; Rahman, 2019). These reforms 
emphasized nation-building and democratic values, integrating civic awareness and leadership skills into the curriculum to create a 
politically informed and participatory citizenry (Chowdhury & Sarkar, 2018; Westheimer & Kahne, 2004).
In contrast, individuals educated before 1971 were most likely shaped by an education system marked by limited resources, urban 
concentration, and a curriculum less oriented toward civic engagement. The socio-political instability of pre-independence Bangladesh 
perhaps further constrained opportunities for civic participation, particularly in rural areas where educational access was more 
restricted. The pre-1971 cohort’s relatively lower levels of civic leadership may be a reflection of these historical and structural dif­
ferences. However, this cohort demonstrates a stronger propensity for contributing to religious establishment building, reflecting the 
central role of religious institutions in pre-independence community life. This pattern suggests that the pre-1971 cohort’s civic 
engagement was perhaps influenced by traditional and religious priorities, whereas the post-1971 cohort perhaps engaged in a broader

In essence, the findings of this paper align with and contribute to the broader literature on the non-economic outcomes of edu­
cation, a topic that has been largely underexplored in the context of Bangladesh. They resonate with studies from other Global South 
contexts, such as Sabates et al. (2021), which demonstrate that formal education increases individuals’ likelihood of civic engagement. 
Situated within the frameworks proposed by McMahon (2007, 2009) and Oketch (2021), this study underscores the potential of 
education to act as an additional source of learning beyond familial and community influences, fostering the development of more 
civically engaged citizens.
Additionally, the findings substantiate the conceptual framework of this study, illustrating how formal education influences civic 
engagement through the interconnected pathways of human, social, and identity capital. Education contributes to human capital by 
equipping individuals with cognitive skills, problem-solving abilities, and knowledge essential for active participation in civic ac­
tivities (Coleman, 1988; Torney-Purta et al., 2001). The observed increase in public speaking confidence among individuals with 
higher education levels reflects the development of identity capital, fostering self-efficacy and a sense of agency (Cˆot´e, 2002). 
Furthermore, education enhances social capital by building trust, facilitating collaboration, and fostering networks, enabling in­
dividuals to contribute effectively to collective community initiatives (Putnam, 2000). These pathways align closely with insights from 
the International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS), which highlights the role of education in cultivating civic knowledge, 
attitudes, and behaviors (Schulz et al., 2016, W. 2018; Torney-Purta & Amadeo, 2013). By situating these findings within a rural, 
Global South context, this study addresses a critical gap in the literature, offering new insights into the non-economic outcomes of 
education and their implications for fostering active citizenship.
Despite its contributions, this study is not without its limitations. While Propensity Score Matching (PSM) addresses selection bias, 
it cannot establish definitive causality. The cross-sectional nature of the data limits the ability to analyze changes in civic engagement 
over time or establish temporal precedence. Additionally, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as cultural norms or 
informal networks, cannot be entirely ruled out. Future research using longitudinal data and experimental designs could strengthen 
causal inferences and provide deeper insights into these dynamics.
Nonetheless, this paper activates a broader discussion on the non-economic outcomes of education in Bangladesh, providing 
pioneering and contextually relevant evidence on whether and to what extent formal education fosters civic engagement. The findings 
of this paper lay a foundation for rethinking education policies in Bangladesh and similar contexts, advocating for education as a public 
good with far-reaching societal benefits. The implications of these findings for policy and practice are significant. First, they provide 
empirical evidence for the civic payoffs of education, which complement its labor market benefits.
Second, the findings reinforce the transformative potential of education as both a public and societal good (Marginson, 2011; Sen, 
1999). Formal education, particularly at higher levels, is strongly associated with increased civic engagement, contributing to societal 
well-being and development (Borgonovi & Miyamoto, 2010). This evidence underscores the urgent need for greater public investment 
in education, particularly in rural areas, to ensure equitable access to quality education. Policymakers now have a compelling rationale 
to advocate for more substantial investments in formal education at all levels, with a particular focus on tertiary education. Such 
advocacy aligns with the recommendations of W.W. McMahon (2018) and Oketch (2022) and emphasizes the critical role of higher 
education in fostering active citizenship and societal transformation.
The study also opens avenues for future research, which are vital for deepening the understanding of education’s civic payoffs. 
Investigating the interplay between financial capacity and intrinsic civic values in driving community contributions could offer 
valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying civic engagement. Longitudinal studies exploring the long-term impacts of education 
on civic behaviors would further enrich the evidence base, while examining the role of informal education and community-based 
initiatives in complementing formal education could provide a more comprehensive understanding of how diverse educational ex­
periences shape civic engagement.
In conclusion, this paper offers a pioneering and contextually relevant understanding of the non-economic outcomes of education 
and demonstrates that formal education, particularly higher levels of education, is strongly associated with increased civic engagement 
in rural Bangladesh. It demonstrates that education fosters civic engagement through the development of human, social, and identity 
capital, thereby highlighting the transformative potential of education not only as a tool for enhancing labor market outcomes but also 
as a vehicle for cultivating active citizenship. By highlighting these civic payoffs, this paper provides a foundation for rethinking 
education policies in Bangladesh and similar contexts, advocating for education as a public good with far-reaching societal benefits.
