A B S T R A C T
In this review, we map and summarize studies examining the effects of teachers’ participation in professional 
development (PD) on teaching practices and student achievement based on nationally representative datasets, 
reflecting both the teacher population and the PD they typically receive. We also critically assess the statistical 
approaches used in these studies to evaluate the credibility and robustness of their findings. The reviewed studies 
demonstrate that large-scale datasets (e.g., TIMSS and NAEP) offer valuable opportunities to study PD in 
representative settings. However, only a minority of the studies adequately address the methodological chal­
lenges associated with these datasets. As a result, while our meta-analysis indicates that PD participation cor­
relates with teaching practices and student achievement, we caution against interpreting these results as causal 
due to methodological limitations in most of the reviewed studies. Finally, we propose strategies for future 
research to produce more credible results.
1. Introduction
More than 90 % of teachers internationally participate in profes­
sional development (PD) every year (OECD, 2019), and governments 
invest billions in PD for teachers with the intention of enhancing their 
knowledge and practice (e.g., (Kraft et al., 2018; TNTP, 2015). Re­
searchers have also made great investments in studies of teacher PD, and 
the number of randomized control trials has now accumulated to a state 
that enables meta-analyses with rigorous standards for inclusion (Basma 
& Savage, 2018; Basma & Savage, 2023; Garrett et al., 2019; Kraft et al., 
2018; Sims et al., 2023). Considering that all these meta-analyses esti­
mate that the studied PD programs, on average, improve both teaching 
and student achievement, the billion-dollar investments in teacher PD 
may be money well spent. Although the studies included in these 
meta-analyses provide a robust basis for causal conclusions, the condi­
tions under which the trials have been conducted differ substantially 
from those usually faced by teachers; thus, the conclusions are not 
necessarily transferable to more typical PD settings. For example, the 
investigated PD programs are often conducted by experienced facilita­
tors and with motivated participants (Kennedy, 2016), the dosage is 
often high (i.e., 20–100 h), and programs are run on a small scale (i.e., 
with less than 100 teachers; (Garrett et al., 2019). Furthermore, most of 
the studied programs were carried out in the US or the UK (Sims et al., 
2023).
Based on the arguments above, it is important to investigate PD ef­
fects in representative PD settings (Garrett et al., 2019; Kraft et al., 2018; 
Sztajn et al., 2017). As argued by Akiba (2015), large-scale international 
studies such as TALIS, TIMSS, and PIRLS could be important empirical 
sources for this purpose. Such large-scale studies not only provide data 
that are representative on the national level; they also enable explora­
tions of the relationships among PD, policy, and organizational context, 
and different types of outcome variables. Large-scale studies enable the 
investigation of whether findings from small-scale studies are general­
izable to larger populations, including comparisons over time, across 
grades, among subgroups within countries, and among countries. In 
addition to the international studies such as TIMSS and TALIS,

To make sense of the growing body of research on the effects of 
teacher PD based on these large-scale datasets, it is essential to sys­
tematically synthesize the findings and assess their methodological 
quality. This is where systematic research reviews play a crucial role. 
Systematic research reviews are a powerful tool for synthesizing findings 
across studies, helping to avoid arbitrary conclusions based on indi­
vidual results. However, the quality of such reviews depends on rigorous 
inclusion criteria, as not all empirical studies offer equally valid evi­
dence. In the context of teacher PD, for example, narrative reviews and 
thematic analyses (e.g., (Desimone, 2009; Timperley et al., 2007) have

(Darling-Hammond et al., 2017). Yet, these reviews face significant 
methodological limitations, including reliance on studies with 
nonequivalent control groups (Sims & Fletcher-Wood, 2021). Similar 
concerns apply to meta-analyses. For instance, Fullard (Fullard, 2023) 
conducted a replication study of a meta-analysis on the effects of teacher 
PD on student outcomes (Fletcher-Wood & Zuccollo, 2020) and found 
that, after excluding invalid studies, correcting biased selection criteria, 
and including a previously omitted valid study, the estimated effect size 
dropped from 0.09 to −0.008 standard deviations. Given the consider­
able methodological challenges associated with analyzing data from 
large-scale assessments (LSAs), it is essential to critically evaluate the 
methodological choices made in such studies before including them in a 
meta-analysis.
Analyzing the datasets generated by large-scale studies involves 
several difficulties that are not always addressed in secondary analyses, 
which may therefore bias the findings (Jerrim et al., 2017; Nilsen & 
Teig, 2022; Rutkowski et al., 2010; Teig et al., 2022). For example, all 
large-scale studies use a complex sampling design in which teachers and 
students are nested in schools. This design necessitates the use of sta­
tistical techniques considering sampling weights and clustering. More­
over, because students only answer a subset of all items in each 
assessment, plausible values are used to take into account the uncer­
tainty in estimating students’ true achievement levels. In addition, 
although studies such as TIMSS and PIRLS are conducted recurrently, 
with a few years between each cycle, most of them only contain data on 
one occasion for each student and teacher (i.e., they are cross-sectional 
rather than panel studies). This “snapshot” nature of the studies 
considerably complicates attempts to determine the direction of cau­
sality between measured variables. In particular, the so-called endoge­
neity bias has been a problem in observational studies of the effects of 
educational interventions (Webbink, 2005). For example, teachers that 
are already more skilled or motivated may apply to PD programs to a 
greater extent than those who are less skilled or motivated. If this is the 
case, post-participation comparisons may mirror selection into partici­
pation rather than causal effects of PD. Thus, the question remains how 
it can be ensured that the change in, for example, student results is the 
result of the PD and not of some factor(s) the researcher has not 
observed. Fortunately, substantial methodological progress has 
increased the potential to credibly identify causal effects based on 
observational data (cf. (Gustafsson, 2013; Gustafsson & Nilsen, 2022; 
Schwerdt & Woessmann, 2020). In relation to large-scale studies in 
education, this has enabled analyses using instrumental variables, 
regression discontinuity designs, difference-in-differences, and pro­
pensity score matching to isolate causal effects of factors such as class 
size, public versus private schools, early tracking, instruction time, and 
teaching practices (Cordero et al., 2018). Although all methods have 
drawbacks, these approaches significantly improve the potential to 
isolate causal effects.
In summary, analyzing data from large-scale studies offers a prom­
ising avenue for examining the effects of teacher PD in more 
representative contexts. However, leveraging these data requires careful 
attention to methodological rigor. This review aims to synthesize studies 
using nationally or state-representative datasets to assess the effects of 
teacher PD and evaluate the robustness of their statistical approaches. 
We set out to answer the following research questions:
What characterizes the studies examining the effects of teachers’ PD 
participation in terms of their (a) sample; (b) data; and (c) statistical 
approach?
What effects of teachers’ PD participation are estimated by the 
studies?
Considering the methodological challenges mentioned above, we 
conclude by critically discussing the credibility of the results regarding 
PD effects in relation to the statistical approach used in the included 
studies and offer methodological suggestions for further PD studies 
based on data that are representative at national or state level.
2. Method
This systematic review contains element of both those aiming to “pile 
up” findings from homogenous studies in order to gain greater precision 
in their empirical statements (aggregative reviews) and those aiming to 
locate different types of studies next to each other to paint a bigger 
picture (configurate reviews; (Gough & Thomas, 2012; Sandelowski 
et al., 2012). Concerning the first research question, we use a configu­
rative approach and conduct analyses that share many similarities with 
what has been categorized as a framework synthesis (Dixon-Woods, 
2011; Xiao & Watson, 2019). For the second research question, we set 
out to use an aggregative approach and conduct a meta-analysis of the 
effects of teachers’ PD participation estimated in the included studies.
The study was carried out in four phases: literature search, literature 
selection, data extraction, and synthesis.
2.1. Literature search
In search of relevant literature, we consulted a total of three data­
bases (ERIC, Scopus, Web of Science) that together assemble most in­
ternational educational research. We searched for articles in peer- 
reviewed journals, conference papers, books, book chapters, and re­
ports covering our area of interest and written in English. The search was 
conducted in November 2024.
In the search string, we strived to include concepts (including syn­
onyms) that would capture LSAs and teacher PD. Concerning LSAs, we 
included in the search string the five major current and ongoing inter­
national LSA programs in education: PISA, TIMSS, PIRLS, ICCS, and 
ICILS (Hern´andez-Torrano & Courtney, 2021). In addition to these LSAs 
we also included TALIS, which is likely the most widely recognized in­
ternational study explicitly focusing on teachers (including their PD). 
Moreover, to avoid the risk of selection bias in terms of countries to the 
greatest extent possible, we also chose to include the LSA programs the 
Southern and Eastern Africa Consortium for Monitoring Education 
Quality (SACMEQ), the Programme for the Analysis of Education Sys­
tems (PASEC), and the Latin-American Laboratory for Assessment of the 
Quality of Education (LLECE), even though these are not as well known 
or widely used as the other international LSAs. In addition to these 
programs, we also included search terms that may capture studies using 
representative samples at the country level, including studies using 
national exams and the like.
Concerning the concept of teacher PD, the plethora of words used to 
describe it is extensive, and it is not uncommon to simply talk about 
professional learning or sometimes just teacher learning. Thus, we 
strived for as broad a search string as possible in an attempt to capture 
all the synonyms for teacher PD.

2.2. Literature selection
A total of three criteria for inclusion were used to select relevant 
references for the purpose of the review. The first two were applied to 
titles and abstracts, and if the information there was insufficient for 
making a decision the criteria were applied to the full text. Criterion 
three was applied to the full texts directly. The literature selection was 
conducted by the first author; however, all uncertain cases were dis­
cussed with the second author.
The first criterion was the study investigates the effects of Grades 1–12 
teachers’ PD participation. Excluded studies entailed, for example, those 
focusing solely on various aspects of teachers’ participation in PD in 
itself (e.g. their participation rate, their views of the quality of the PD, 
their PD needs) rather than the desired effects (e.g., (Cooc, 2019) and 
those emphasizing teachers’ perception of PD impact (e.g., (Garet et al., 
2001) rather than actual effects. Moreover, we recognize that the term 
professional development has been used to refer to a wide range of 
complex, interrelated, formal, and informal teacher learning opportu­
nities (see for example (Desimone, 2009; Lieberman & Miller, 2014). 
The review is restricted to studies of teacher PD as formal and planned 
activities teachers engage in to improve their practice. Thus, we did not 
include studies of professional learning outside formal and planned ac­
tivities (e.g., informal learning opportunities such as participation in 
informal discussions with colleagues; see further Maci`a & García, 2016). 
Thus, studies examining teacher professional learning but not in the 
context of PD programs (e.g., (Sargent, 2015), as well as those mainly 
operationalizing PD as informal rather than formal PD opportunities (e. 
g., (Choi & Kang, 2019), were excluded.
The second criterion was the study investigates effects of PD partici­
pation in general rather than the effects of participating in a particular PD 
program. This criterion in particular excluded many studies, as several 
studies investigated the effects of particular PD programs (e.g., (Fisher 
et al., 2017; Garet et al., 2016) rather than teachers’ PD participation in 
general.
The third criterion was the data are based on a sample of teachers that is 
representative at a national or state level. Examples of excluded studies 
were those with a sample that can only be said to be representative of, 
for example, a city (e.g., (Jacob & Lefgren, 2004), and those with a 
strategic but not representative sample (e.g., (Schmitt, 2004).
The application of the Inclusion criteria resulted in the selection of 
56 papers. This was supplemented with papers that met all the criteria 
and that were found through a hand-search of leading educational 
journals on teacher PD and book series on LSAs (4 papers), a screening of 
the reference lists in papers found relevant in the database search (0 
papers), and the inclusion of studies based on our own and colleagues’ 
knowledge of the field (8 papers). These steps of the literature selection 
resulted in the selection of a total of 68 papers.
2.3. Data extraction
We read the papers included in our final sample and extracted in­
formation on their characteristics, methodology, and results. Examples 
of characteristics include type of text and year of publication. In total, 54 
journal articles, five book chapters, three dissertations, three reports, 
two conference papers, and one book were included in the review. The 
publication years of the included papers spanned between 2000 and 
2024, with six of them being published between 2000 and 2009, 24 
between 2010 and 2019, and 38 from 2020 onward. The information on 
methodology included sample (e.g., country, grades, subjects), data (e. 
g., outcome measures, LSA), and statistical approach. Concerning a 
study’s results, we summarized the main results concerning PD effects 
and noted effect size estimates. For a complete account of all the 
descriptive categories and codes, see Table 1.
2.4. Synthesis
In the last phase we analyzed the papers in relation to the informa­
tion they provided in relation to our research questions. We set out to 
conduct two analyses, one for each research question.
2.4.1. Synthesis of sample, data, and statistical approaches (RQ1)
Concerning the first research question, we followed recent system­
atic literature reviews on studies that use LSA assessment data and that 
emphasize the importance of mapping the studies’ sample, data, and 
how these data are analyzed (e.g., (Nilsen & Teig, 2022; Teig et al., 
2022). Firstly, and as illustrated in Table 1, we characterized the sample 
used to estimate PD effects. This involved characterizing each study in 
relation to sample characteristics (country, grade, subject, N). Secondly, 
we emphasized the data used to estimate PD effects, which included 
mapping the “what” of the PD effects (i.e., the outcome measure used in 
the papers to determine the PD effects) and the data source (e.g., TIMSS, 
NAEP). Thirdly, we mapped the statistical approaches used in the 
studies to estimate PD effects. To begin with, we briefly described the 
statistical techniques used in the study (e.g., ANOVA, hierarchical linear 
modelling, multilevel modelling). Thereafter, we distinguished between 
correlation studies and quasi-experiments of observational data. The 
latter should be used if the aim is to draw valid causal inferences (as 
when analyzing the effects of PD) rather than to simply investigate 
correlations (Cordero et al., 2018). Moreover, for quasi-experiments, we 
coded which kind of quasi-experimental technique was used. More 
specifically, we proceeded from the four quasi-experimental techniques 
that served as a basis for the review by Cordero et al. (2018) of such 
techniques applied in studies of PISA, TIMSS and PIRLS: instrumental 
variables, regression discontinuity designs, difference-in-differences, 
and 
propensity 
score 
matching.

described as necessary for obtaining nationally representative results,

2.4.2. Synthesis of PD effects (RQ2)
Concerning the second research question, we set out to conduct a 
meta-analysis. In the data extraction phase, two things became 
apparent. Firstly, data for calculating effect sizes were reported on with 
enough frequency to warrant a meta-analysis for two outcome variables: 
student achievement and teachers’ self-reported teaching. Effect sizes 
for other outcome variables were reported too rarely (see also the Results 
section). Therefore, we decided to only investigate the effects of teach­
ers’ PD participation on these two outcome variables. Secondly, most of 
the included studies employed a design that allowed for an examination 
of the relationship between teachers’ participation in PD and other 
variables (e.g., student achievement) rather than the causal effect of PD 
on these variables. Therefore, Pearson’s r, the correlation between 
teachers’ PD participation and the outcome variable, was used as a 
measure of effect size. When correlations were not reported in the article 
they were calculated based on the other available information, such as F 
tests, regression model estimates, and effect sizes in other metrics. The 
equations for calculating Pearson’s r, or converting other effect size 
metrics to Pearson’s r, can be found in Lenhard and Lenhard (2016) and 
Lipsey and Wilson (2001). Note that several of the included studies re­
ported standardized regression coefficients, which were converted into 
R-values using the guidelines proposed by Peterson and Brown (2005). 
We recognize that this approach has received some criticism in terms of 
beta coefficients not reflecting a “pure” relationship between two vari­
ables due to the presence of other variables in the regression (Roth et al., 
2018). However, there was not enough information in the primary 
studies to carry out alternative conversions, and excluding these effects 
would seriously reduce the power of our analyses and may also result in 
a systematic loss of information (Borenstein et al., 2009). If insufficient 
information was provided for calculating an effect size, authors were 
contacted to retrieve the missing data (6 papers). However, some au­
thors did not respond or could not retrieve sufficient information, 
whereupon these studies (3 papers) were excluded from the 
meta-analysis. In line with recommendations by Borenstein et al. 
(2009)), we converted the Pearson’s r correlations coefficients to the 
Fisher’s z scale. The results were then converted back to correlations for 
ease of interpretation.
Many of the reviewed studies include multiple effect sizes. We 
handled this in three different ways depending on the study character­
istics. Firstly, if a study included multiple effect sizes as well as a pooled 
model, we used the results from the pooled model. For example, 
Bl¨omeke et al. (2016) reported on the effects of PD on student 
achievement in 47 different countries, as well as a pooled effect, of 
which we used the pooled effect. Secondly, if a study reported on effect 
sizes that were non-correlated (i.e. for different samples) we performed 
a meta-synthesis based on the individual study to obtain a pooled 
measure, which was then used in the meta-analysis. For example, for Son 
et al. (2016), reporting on the effects of teachers’ PD participation on 
student achievement in the US and South Korea separately, we calcu­
lated a pooled effect size for these results. Thirdly, if a study reported on 
effect sizes that were correlated (i.e. several effect sizes based on the 
same sample) we included all of these effect sizes in the final 
meta-analysis. For example, for Liang et al. (2015), reporting on the 
effects of teachers’ PD participation on student achievement using six 
different PD measures (participation in PD with six different contents), 
we included the effect sizes for each PD measures in the meta-analysis. 
In order to then address the clustered nature of our outcomes, we fol­
lowed the recommendation to estimate all models using robust variance 
estimation methods (Hedges et al., 2010; Tanner-Smith & Tipton, 2014), 
which account both for the different precision of estimates across studies 
as well as for the nonindependence of effect sizes within studies. In total, 
we used 61 effect sizes across 24 studies for student achievement, and 50 
effect sizes across 22 studies for teaching practices. The meta-analysis 
was performed in SPSS using the RobustMeta SPSS macro 
(Tanner-Smith & Tipton, 2014). As real-world data in social sciences are 
likely to have variable population parameters (e.g., (Field, 2005), we 
used a random-effects model. Tests for heterogeneity in effect sizes 
across studies used in traditional meta-analysis are not available with 
the robust variance estimation approach (Tanner-Smith & Tipton, 2014; 
Tanner-Smith et al., 2016). Instead, we report the between-studies 
variance component (τ2) as a measure of between-study heterogeneity

3. Results
3.1. RQ1 – sample, data, and statistical approaches
Table 2 provides a summary of key features coded for each of the 68 
papers regarding a) sample characteristics, b) data, and c) statistical 
approaches.
Regarding sample characteristics, 24 of the studies made use of 
samples from several countries (10–50 countries per study) by using 
international LSA databases (e.g., TIMSS, TALIS). The remaining studies 
used samples from one to five countries, most commonly the US (25 
papers), South Korea (6 papers), Japan (3 Papers), Norway (3 papers), 
Singapore (3 papers), South Africa (3 papers), Sweden (3 papers) and 
Turkey (3 papers). Most of the studies focused on teachers’ PD partici­
pation in one or two specific subjects, commonly Mathematics (29 pa­
pers), Science (19 papers) and/or Language Arts (reading, writing, and/ 
or literacy; 7 papers). Other studies, mostly those using TALIS data, did 
not emphasize the subject in which the teachers were teaching (26 
papers).
Regarding data, the data sources used were most commonly those 
provided by international LSAs including TIMSS (25 papers), TALIS (25 
papers), PISA (4 papers), ICILS (2 papers), PIRLS, SACMEQ and PASEC 
(1 paper each). The studies conducted solely in the US sometimes used 
other outcome measures, such as the National Assessment of Educa­
tional Progress (NAEP; 6 papers), state data (3 papers), instruments 
developed for the specific study (2 papers), or the SASS (1 paper). Ten of 
the studies made use of multiple cohorts of data (e.g., both TIMSS 2003 
and 2007), whereas the remaining studies used data from a single year. 
Also regarding data, the outcome measure most commonly used was 
student achievement (25 papers), conceptualized in all studies as stu­
dent test scores. The second-most common was instructional practices 
(24 papers), measured using teacher and/or student surveys. Most of 
these studies investigated the extent to which PD participation was 
associated with teaching in line with reform-oriented instruction (e.g., 
(Smith et al., 2005) or high instructional quality (e.g., (Bl¨omeke et al., 
2016), which involves, among other things, factors such as student 
cognitive activation, teachers’ clarity of instruction, and a supportive 
climate. However, four studies conceptualized instructional practices as 
teachers’ use of information and communication technology. Additional 
outcome measures that were reported several times include teachers’ 
self-efficacy (12 papers) and job satisfaction (11 papers). Other outcome 
measures reported more rarely (1–2 papers per outcome measure) 
include teachers’ knowledge for teaching, confidence in teaching, 
innovativeness, classroom stress, participation in professional learning 
communities, desire to move to another school, collaboration with col­
leagues, expectations of student achievement, and understanding of 
school curriculum, as well as school climate, and students’ motivation to 
learn.
Regarding statistical approaches, the studies included in the review 
used a wide variety of statistical methods to analyze their data, such as 
different types of regression analyses and ANOVAs. The most commonly 
used, however, were structural equation modeling (15 papers) and hi­
erarchical linear modelling (11 papers). Notably, 59 out of 68 of the 
studies (87 %) were categorized as correlational studies, whereas the 
remaining nine were categorized as quasi-experiments, most of which 
used a difference-in-differences approach. Of these nine quasi- 
experiments, five applied value-added models and made use of previ­
ous tests scores concerning either student achievement (4 papers) or 
teachers’ knowledge for teaching (1 paper). Note that this cannot be 
done with data from the largest international LSAs (TIMSS, PIRLS, 
PISA), but is possible with some state-level data in the US (as in (Akiba & 
Liang, 2016; Harris & Sass, 2011; Wallace, 2009), PASEC data (as in 
(Fehrler et al., 2009), and data collected using instruments particularly 
designed for the study (as in (Hill, 2011). Two quasi-experiments 
employed a longitudinal design at the country level, using multiple 
cohorts of data from TIMSS or PIRLS to investigate the effects of PD on 
student achievement in mathematics (Gustafsson & Nilsen, 2016) or 
reading (Van Damme et al., 2019). One quasi-experimental study 
compared student achievement across different school subjects within 
the same students (a within-student between-subjects design), based on 
variation in their teachers’ PD participation (Kirsten et al., 2023). 
Finally, one quasi-experimental study used propensity score matching to 
create a control group of teachers with characteristics similar to those 
who participated in PD (McJames et al., 2023).
We also mapped whether and how the studies dealt with methods 
that have been described as necessary for obtaining nationally repre­
sentative results that appropriately take into account the statistical un­
certainty in LSA data (Jerrim et al., 2017; Rutkowski et al., 2010). 
Concerning probability weights, less than half of the 45 articles provided 
information on the types of sampling weights used and how they were 
integrated into the analysis. In relation to clustering and/or nesting of 
data, 23 of the papers did not include an adjustment for (or a description 
of the adjustment for) this. Articles that did take into account clustering 
and nesting of data typically did this by means of hierarchical linear 
modelling (16 papers) or multilevel structural equation modelling (12 
papers), by using software tools specifically designed for the LSA data­
base (e.g., IDB analyzer; 4 papers), or by using cluster-robust standard 
errors (Kirsten et al., 2023). Concerning plausible values (PVs), this 
criterion was applicable to the 23 studies that investigated the effects of 
PD on student achievement using data from either TIMSS, PISA, PIRLS, 
or NAEP. Of these studies, nine did not mention how PVs were taken into 
account. Of the remaining studies, about half applied PVs inappropri­
ately, for instance by selecting only one PV, averaging the PVs, or using 
PVs as indicator variables for a latent variable. Finally, concerning 
control variables, 22 of the studies did not use any.5 Among the 
remaining studies, the number of control variables varied widely 
(ranging from 1 to 12). The most commonly used ones were students’

included in the analysis is > 10 (Tanner-Smith & Tipton, 2014). As such, 
for our papers, data for calculating effect sizes were reported on with 
enough frequency to warrant a meta-analysis for two outcome variables: 
student achievement (18 correlational studies studies) and teachers’ 
instructional practices (21 correlational studies). The studies included in 
the meta-analyses are marked in Table 2 with either “a” (student 
achievement) or “b” (instruction). For the outcome variables teacher 
job-satisfaction and teacher self-efficacy, the number of studies was just 
above the minimum threshold required to conduct the analysis. There­
fore, we decided to focus the analyses on the outcome variables where 
we had a notably higher number of studies than the recommended 
value.
Table 3 presents results from the meta-analytic models related to the 
effects of teachers’ PD participation on student instruction and student 
achievement, based on the estimated PD effects in the included corre­
lational studies.
Mean effect sizes indicated that there were significant and positive 
associations between teachers’ PD participation and instructional 
practice (r = .20, p < .001; CI[.11,.29]). This indicates that teachers who 
reported a higher level of PD participation tended to report higher levels 
of instructional practices, either reform-oriented instruction, high- 
instructional quality practices, or use of ICT. However, the association 
between teachers’ PD participation and student achievement failed to 
reach statistical significance (r = .06, p = .11; CI[-.02,.13]), even though 
the results indicate that students whose teachers reported a higher level 
of PD participation received higher test scores.
The analysis also identified high heterogeneity between studies, 
particularly in relation to student achievement. For instructional prac­
tices, the effect sizes of the individual studies spanned between −.11 
and.74. The τ2 is.021, yielding a prediction interval ranging from −.09 
to.49, which indicates the anticipated range of correlations in future 
studies. For student achievement, the effect sizes spanned between −.45 
and.42. The τ2 is.024, yielding a prediction interval ranging from −.31 
to.43. This suggests that correlations in future studies based on repre­
sentative data are likely to range from negative to positive, although 
meta-syntheses are likely to show positive mean correlations.

4. Discussion
This review contributes to the growing body of research on teacher 
PD by systematically examining studies that use national or state- 
representative datasets, an area that has received comparatively little 
attention in prior meta-analyses of PD effects, which have largely 
focused on small-scale, experimental studies conducted in controlled 
settings. By mapping the methodological approaches used in these large- 
scale studies, our review provides insights into the robustness and 
credibility of their findings. In doing so, we provide guidance for future 
studies seeking to better understand whether the positive effects of PD 
observed in experimental trials generalize to more typical, large-scale 
educational contexts.
We found several correlational studies on how the PD that teachers 
typically participate in affects instructional practices and student 
achievement. Our meta-analytic model based on these studies showed a 
positive and significant mean correlation between teachers’ PD partic­
ipation and their instructional practices, and a positive (although not 
statistically significant) correlation between PD participation and stu­
dent achievement. To compare our results from the correlational studies 
with those from recent meta-analyses of experimentally studied PD 
programs, we converted our results to Cohen’s d for both instructional 
practices (.40 standard deviations) and student achievement (.12 
standard deviations). Compared to previous reviews (Basma & Savage, 
2018; Garrett et al., 2019; Kraft et al., 2018; Sims et al., 2023), which 
have found average effects on instruction of.42 to.49 standard de­
viations and average effects on student achievement of.05 to.23 stan­
dard deviations, the effect sizes for instruction and student achievement 
in the correlational studies we reviewed are relatively similar although 
slightly more modest. Considering that the PD programs analyzed in 
previous reviews are likely well designed in relation to research-based 
frameworks of what constitutes high-quality PD (e.g., (Desimone, 
2009; Darling-Hammond et al., 2017) in comparison to the PD in which 
teachers typically participate, it may be seen as surprising to find almost 
as large effects. One likely explanation for this unexpectedly large effect 
is selection bias—specifically, that teachers who participate in PD may 
differ systematically from those who do not. For example, teachers 
working in schools with higher-achieving students or in environments 
characterized by particular instructional approaches may be more likely 
to engage in PD. This interpretation is supported by findings from 
Kirsten et al. (Kirsten et al., 2023), who compared effect sizes using the 
same dataset through both correlational and quasi-experimental 
methods. For grade eight, the correlational analysis indicated a posi­
tive effect of 0.12 standard deviations, whereas the quasi-experimental 
approach yielded a negative effect of −0.02 standard deviations. This 
pattern aligns with our review, which found that the few 
quasi-experimental studies included tended to report mixed results, 
often close to zero (see Section 3.2.2), suggesting that causal effects of 
PD may be smaller than those implied by simple correlations.

interpreting these results as evidence of causal effects. The following 
section builds on our critical review by discussing how methodological 
challenges may influence the robustness of meta-analytic findings. 
Drawing on examples of high-quality studies identified in our review, we 
offer suggestions for future research aimed at strengthening causal 
inference in studies using nationally or state-representative data.
4.1. Limitations and future research
First of all, most of the studies included in the review were conducted 
with data from Western countries, particularly the US, even though 
there are several LSA databases that provide data from a range of 
countries. Admittedly, this may also be due to our search strategy and 
the fact that we only included papers written in English. However, we 
consider this unlikely as a) we took measures to include LSA programs 
specifically conducted in African and Latin-American countries, and b) 
the tendency to emphasize results from Western countries, even though 
international LSA databases are used, has been observed in other studies 
as well (Hopfenbeck et al., 2018; Teig et al., 2022). Moreover, more than 
half of the studies concerned teachers’ PD in mathematics and/or sci­
ence, whereas the effects of PD in Language Arts were only observed a 
couple of times, even though data on this are available in several LSAs 
(e.g., PIRLS, PASEC). We did, however, find some examples of studies 
showing that datasets allowing for the estimation of PD effects in 
non-Western countries as well as additional subjects besides mathe­
matics are indeed available and offer a promising way forward for future 
studies (e.g., (Fehrler et al., 2009; Nguyen et al., 2021). These studies 
may also emphasize a cross-country or cross-subject comparison to un­
derstand how patterns of PD effects differ between countries and 
subjects.
Secondly, this review focuses on the general effects of teacher 
participation in PD, rather than on specific PD programs. Thus, we do 
not draw conclusions about the particular programs teachers partici­
pated in. However, teacher surveys in LSAs do provide some opportu­
nities to explore the characteristics of PD. For instance, the TIMSS 
teacher questionnaire includes items on PD content areas (e.g., subject 
content, technology integration), while the PISA teacher survey focuses 
more on PD types (e.g., teacher networks, mentoring). However, as most 
of the studies included in our meta-analysis measured PD participation 
in binary or quantitative terms (whether teachers participated and to 
what extent) without capturing detailed characteristics of the PD itself. 
Because too few studies included measures of PD characteristics, it was 
not possible to use such characteristics as moderators in a meta-analytic 
model. Future research would benefit from more nuanced and stan­
dardized measures of PD characteristics across LSAs, which would 
enable more robust analyses of what types of PD are most effective. 
Considering findings on the characteristics of effective PD from recent 
meta-analyses (Sims et al., 2023; Lynch et al., 2025), questions may ask 
teachers to what extent the PD they participated in provided insights 
into how specific teaching techniques support student learning, moti­
vated them to use these techniques, demonstrated the application of 
such techniques, and supported them in integrating these techniques 
into their own practice.
Thirdly, the statistical approaches used in the reviewed studies var­
ied greatly and many studies used statistical approaches that may have 
biased their results. However, we also found several examples of high- 
quality studies that may suggest directions for future research. For 
example, 87 percent of the studies included in the review were corre­
lational. As such, there may be a problem with endogeneity bias 
(Webbink, 2005) and the observed effects possibly not being the result of 
teachers’ PD participation but rather factors unobserved by the 
researcher. For example, results from previous studies (e.g., (Richter 
et al., 2021) suggest that skilled teachers participate in PD to a greater 
extent than non-skilled teachers. Fortunately, in recent years, substan­
tial methodological progress has increased the potential to credibly 
identify causal effects based on observational data (cf. (Cordero et al., 
2018; Gustafsson, 2013; Gustafsson & Nilsen, 2022; Schwerdt & 
Woessmann, 2020). Indeed, 13 percent of the studies included in the 
review make use of such methods and show that it is possible to conduct 
quasi-experimental analyses concerning the effects of teachers’ typical 
PD participation using data from either international LSAs that are often 
relatively easy to access, such as TIMSS (Kirsten et al., 2023), PASEC 
(Fehrler et al., 2009) and PIRLS (Van Damme et al., 2019), or data that 
are collected regularly (independent of research) by municipalities, 
states, or countries (e.g., (Harris & Sass, 2011; Wallace, 2009). We 
encourage scholars to make use of these techniques to improve the po­
tential to isolate causal effects of teachers’ PD participation. Further­
more, we recommend that both correlational and quasi-experimental 
studies explicitly and prominently (not only in a limitations section) 
discuss the extent to which their findings represent causal effects versus 
alternative explanations, such as selection effects, to prevent mis­
interpretations of correlations as causal relationships and highlight po­
tential sources of bias.
Fourthly, many papers included in the review did not use, or omitted, 
information on the use of sampling weights, clustering, plausible values, 
and control variables in the analysis. This may, for example, lead to 
biased estimates of standard errors and the strengths of the correlations 
of interest (Rutkowski et al., 2010). Still, some studies handled all these 
factors appropriately and transparently (e.g., (Grodsky & Gamoran, 
2003; Liu & Liao, 2019). We advise researchers to follow these examples 
and use and provide information on the sampling weights, clustering, 
plausible values, and control variables used in their analysis and thereby 
increase their transparency and credibility.
In conclusion, we encourage researchers to follow the advice 
regarding study design statistical approaches based on the findings from 
this review. If this is done, conclusions from future meta-analyses on the 
effects of teachers’ typical PD participation may provide more robust 
findings as they would then be based on studies that involve a range of 
settings (e.g., countries, subjects) and estimate causal effects of teachers’ 
PD participation rather than correlations.
