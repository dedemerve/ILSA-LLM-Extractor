Abstract
Previous studies on student well-being have focused on a limited number of factors.
However, well-being is facilitated or hindered by many different factors. Therefore,
focusing on a limited set of constructs could lead to an incomplete understanding
of the various factors that predict student well-being. The current study drew on the
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) dataset to understand the
importance of background, non-cognitive/metacognitive, and schooling constructs
in understanding well-being. This study focused specifically on understanding different well-being dimensions including positive affect, negative affect, life satisfaction, and eudaimonic well-being. The data were from 12,058 15-year-old Chinese
students from Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang. China presents an interesting case given its high levels of achievement but low levels of well-being. Using
a machine learning approach (i.e., random forest regression), the results indicated
that factors belonging to “non-cognitive/metacognitive” and “schooling” constructs
were found to be the most important predictors of well-being. More specifically, students’ positive affect and life satisfaction were best predicted by school belonging
and resilience. Negative affect was best accounted for by school belonging and fear
of failure. Eudaimonic well-being was best predicted by resilience and work mastery. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed.
Keywords Subjective Well-Being · Eudaimonic Well-Being · Machine Learning ·
Chinese Students · PISA 2018

1 Introduction
Students’ well-being is an issue of critical social and educational significance
(McNaughton & Li, 2021; Suldo et al., 2009a, b). It is not only an end in and of
itself, but it also contributes to the other valued social and academic outcomes (see
Kaya & Erdem, 2021; Lyubomirsky et al., 2005 for reviews). For example, studies have shown that students with better well-being have higher intrinsic motivation
for academic tasks, higher academic achievement, and greater school satisfaction
(Bücker et al., 2018; Deci & Ryan, 2012). Given its importance to students’ social
and academic lives, there is an urgent need to understand the facilitators and barriers
to students’ well-being.
Many studies have been devoted to uncovering the determinants of student wellbeing (e.g., Deci & Ryan, 2008; Huebner et al., 1999; Suldo et al., 2009a, b). Wellbeing is complex and determined by many factors. However, previous studies have
focused on only one or two factors that predict well-being (e.g., resilience in Chow
et al., 2018; bullying and belonging in Xu & Fang, 2021). Therefore, this study aims
to examine a broad range of factors that predict well-being. Specifically, we harness
the power of a large-scale educational survey, i.e., the Programme for International
Student Assessment (PISA). The most recent PISA 2018 had well-being as a critical
focus.
In this study, we focused on Chinese students in particular. Despite their high
achievement level, Chinese students have consistently performed poorly in terms of
well-being. For example, although students from Mainland China scored the highest
scores in reading, mathematics, and science among students from 79 participating
economies on the 2018 PISA, Chinese students’ well-being was one of the lowest
among participating economies (Schleicher, 2019). Chinese students also demonstrated low levels of well-being in the PISA 2015 cycle (Peña-López, 2017).
Although previous studies have explored the well-being of Chinese students,
most of them focused on only a few explanatory factors (e.g., Chen, 2016; Xiang
et al., 2019). Hence, a more comprehensive study that accounts for the various enablers and barriers to well-being is needed. To do so, we adopted a machine learning
approach for the data analysis to overcome some of the limitations of classical statistics. Without a holistic view of these factors, it may be difficult for educators and
policymakers to understand and identify the relative importance of different variables that provide essential clues for developing effective interventions to improve
student well-being. Therefore, based on the PISA framework, we aimed to examine
the role of background, schooling, and non-cognitive/metacognitive constructs in
explaining Chinese students’ well-being.

2 Literature review
2.1 Subjective and Eudaimonic Well‑Being
The importance of well-being has been widely recognized across different disciplines (Clark, 2016). In previous research, well-being is generally defined using
either a hedonic or a eudaimonic approach (Casas & González-Carrasco, 2021; Kesebir & Diener, 2009). These two types of well-being (i.e., hedonic and eudaimonic
well-being) should ideally be measured together to provide a more comprehensive
picture of students’ well-being (Martela & Sheldon, 2019; Ryan & Deci, 2001).
The hedonic perspective focuses on subjective well-being (see Busseri, 2018 for
a review). It refers to individuals’ subjective beliefs that their life is desirable and
pleasant. It includes three elements: positive affect (i.e., positive moods and emotions), negative affect (i.e., negative moods and emotions), and life satisfaction (i.e.,
cognitive judgmental evaluation of one’s life) (Andrews & Withey, 1976; Diener,
2009; Lyubomirsky et al., 2005). Many empirical studies have relied on this tripartite conceptualization to examine predictors of subjective well-being, such as personality (Keyes et al., 2020), as well as economic and sociodemographic factors
(Diener et al., 2015).
The eudaimonic perspective emphasizes important positive functional aspects
beyond subjective well-being (Delle Fave, 2016; Graham & Nikolova, 2015; OECD,
2013). In other words, a person’s well-being can be measured by the extent to
which he or she is feeling good (subjective well-being) and doing well (eudaimonic
well-being, Ryan & Deci, 2001). Eudaimonic well-being (i.e., contentment that is
achieved through self-actualization and having a meaningful purpose in one’s life)
can be used to complement subjective well-being (Bauer et al., 2015).
These two definitions of well-being have been proposed for a long time (Keyes
et al., 2002; Ryan & Deci, 2001). However, most existing studies only focus on one
aspect (e.g., hedonic well-being in Llosada-Gistau et al., 2015; eudaimonic wellbeing in Pritchard et al., 2020), few studies exploring both aspects of well-being.
For example, Bodner et al. (2021) only focused on aspects of meaning in life, while
Andreou et al., (2020) addressed the aspects of subjective well-being. This study
took subjective and eudaimonic well-being into account and explored important factors that could facilitate or hinder the two aspects of well-being.

2.2 Conceptual Framework
This research was based on the PISA 2018 framework. The PISA framework recognizes that multiple factors are responsible for student well-being. Numerous studies
have been conducted to examine the factors that influence student well-being. The
effects of different constructs need to be considered to obtain an integrative understanding of well-being (see Žukauskienė, 2014 for a review). Therefore, the OECD (2019a)
has proposed a comprehensive framework for student well-being. The framework
includes three broad types of variables, namely background (i.e., student, family, and

2.2.1 Background Factors
According to the PISA framework, background factors include student demographic
information such as gender, socioeconomic status (SES), immigrant status, grade repetition, and length of early childhood education and care. Numerous studies have examined the relationship between these background factors and student well-being (e.g.,
Bodner et al., 2021; Goswami, 2014). We discussed a few of the most relevant factors
below. Please refer to Table 1 for a full list of the variables.
Gender Gender differences in well-being have been explored by several researchers
(Tomyn & Cummins, 2011; Zhou & Peng, 2018). Studies have reported that boys
have higher levels of overall subjective well-being (Llosada-Gistau et al., 2015) and
lower negative affect (Burns & Machin, 2010). However, some studies also found
no significant differences in subjective well-being across gender, but boys reported
higher scores on eudaimonic well-being (Tomyn et al., 2015). Since previous
research into gender differences in well-being has yielded inconsistent results, this
study attempted to examine the effect of gender together with other factors.
Socioeconomic Status (SES) Socioeconomic status (SES) has been studied extensively in relation to students’ well-being (Bonhauser et al., 2005; Savahl et al.,
2017). In this study, students’ SES refers to their economic status (i.e., household
possessions), parental educational attainment, and parental occupational status
(OECD, 2019b). Disadvantaged students were more likely to experience mental
health problems (Alivernini et al., 2020), negative affect (Chen, 2004), and poor
health (Bradley & Corwyn, 2002) compared to affluent students. Lower SES has
also been associated with lower sense of purpose in life, which is an essential component of eudaimonic well-being (Ryff & Singer, 2008).
Other Background Factors Other potential influential background factors that have
been studied to well-being include migrant status (Wang and Mesman, 2015), grade
repetition (Rathmann et al., 2020), and early childhood education and care (Sandseter & Seland, 2016). In general, these studies have provided insightful findings that
underscore the importance of background constructs on student well-being.

2.2.2 Non‑Cognitive/Metacognitive Factors
The second group of variables within PISA is non-cognitive or metacognitive factors. These factors refer to students’ personal characteristics, such as resilience, goal
orientation, competitiveness, fear of failure, and other variables. They also include
different types of metacognitive learning strategies. We considered some core variables under this dimension.
Resilience Resilience is generally defined as the ability to recover from adversity
and negative emotional experiences through effective coping and flexible adaptation
to stressful situations (Tugade & Fredrickson, 2004). Resilience processes can be
understood from both individual and social-contextual perspectives (Peña-López,
2019; Ungar, 2011). In this study, we focused on students’ resilience which PISA
defined more as an individual factor. However, we want to emphasize that resilience
is not just an individual difference variable but emerges out of a complex interaction
between the person and the environment (Ungar, 2012). The school context as an
environmental factor can exert an important influence on students’ resilience. Resilience is a protective factor that promotes various aspects of well-being in the face
of adversity (Duan et al., 2020; Hartson et al., 2021). For example, students with
higher levels of resilience tended to have higher overall well-being (Chow et al.,
2018). Previous studies also confirmed the positive relationships between resilience
and both subjective and eudaimonic well-being, such as positive emotions (Andreou
et al., 2020), life satisfaction (Noor & Alwi, 2013), and meaning in life (Di Fabio &
Palazzeschi, 2015).
Fear of Failure Fear of failure is the tendency to feel ashamed or embarrassed after
failure (Caraway et al., 2003; Conroy, 2004). Fear of failure leads to negative behaviors such as procrastination (Haghbin et al., 2012a, b) and helplessness (Elliot &
Thrash, 2004), which can further negatively affect students’ subjective well-being
(Huang, 2021). Students who scored higher on fear of failure had lower levels of
life satisfaction (OECD, 2019a). Similarly, previous studies have found that students
who have a high fear of failure experience more negative emotions (Berger & Freund, 2012).
Mastery Goal Orientation Students with mastery goals focus on their learning and
believe they can improve their capabilities by making more effort (Patrick et al.,
2011). Mastery goals are positively related to subjective well-being (Mouratidis
et al., 2013) and eudaimonic well-being. For example, Tuominen-Soini et al., (2012)
found that mastery-oriented students were more likely to find meaning in school life,
such as in schoolwork.
Other Non‑Cognitive/Metacognitive Factors Other non-cognitive/metacognitive
factors such as self-concept, enjoyment, learning goals, meta-cognitive skills, and
effort in learning are also critical to individual subjective (Jiang et al., 2020; Kiaei &

2.2.3 Schooling Factors
Schooling factors include contextual variables that affect students externally, such
as a sense of belonging to the school, the experience of being bullied, emotional
support from parents, and other factors.
Sense of School Belonging A sense of belonging to the school represents students’
sense of being respected and safe in the school context (Anderman, 2003). Some
longitudinal studies have shown that developing a sense of belonging to the school
can promote subjective well-being. For example, Gillison et al., (2008) found that
a sense of belonging to the school (i.e., connectedness) had reciprocal and positive
effects on subjective life evaluation. Similarly, a significant longitudinal relationship
between feelings of school belongingness and subjective well-being was found in
school-aged children (Tian et al., 2016). A sense of belonging to the school may protect students from negative experiences such as bullying (Wormington et al., 2016;
Xu et al., 2021). Conversely, a low sense of belonging to the school is considered
a predictor of illness and behavior problems (Pittman and Richmond, 2008; Shochet et al., 2006). Regarding eudaimonic well-being, there is sufficient empirical
evidence to show a positive association with some indicators of school belonging
(Johnson, 2009; Zhang et al., 2018).
Experience of Being Bullied Bullying occurs when students are victimized by the
negative actions of other students (Olweus, 1993). The experience of being bullied
has negative effects on students’ mental health, such as a high risk of depression,
avoidance, and fear (Klomek et al., 2007; Vidourek et al., 2016), leading to mental illness (Varela et al., 2021). Consequently, the experience of being bullied will
significantly reduce students’ subjective well-being (Huang, 2021; Olweus & Breivik, 2014). However, there is a lack of empirical research on the direct relationship
between bullying in school and eudaimonic well-being. Previous studies have found
that the meaning of life can explain and buffer the negative influences of bullying on
students, such as suicidal ideation and online gaming addiction (Henry et al., 2014;
Zhao et al., 2020).
Parental Involvement in Education Parental emotional support of students’ academic activities is a form of parental involvement in schooling (Wong et al., 2018).
Compared with parental coercive control, parental emotional support can reduce the
risk of illness (e.g., psychological distress) and enhance self-esteem (BoudreaultBouchard et al., 2013), which in turn improves children’s well-being (Lim et al.,
2015). Furthermore, past studies have demonstrated that high levels of emotional
support from parents are directly and positively associated with children’s mental health and emotional well-being (Caldwell et al., 2004). Similarly, perceived

Other Factors In this study, teachers’ teaching style, school climate of cooperation,

2.3 Machine Learning
As shown in the previous discussion, well-being is multiply determined. However, previous empirical studies have focused only on a limited number of predictors of subjective well-being (e.g., Noor & Alwi, 2013) or eudaimonic well-being
(Ryff & Singer, 2008). For example, Noor and Alwi (2013) examined the relationship between stressors, resilience, and life satisfaction. Ryff et al., (2003) examined
the effects of status inequalities and perceived discrimination on eudaimonic wellbeing. Though these studies provide valuable information, there is a lack of coverage on other factors that might promote or hinder student well-being. In this study,
a machine learning approach was used to examine the predictors of well-being in an
integrative manner to address this gap.
The machine learning approach is a statistical technique that overcomes several
limitations of classical statistical analyses in understanding well-being (Zhang et al.,
2019). First, most classical statistical methods assume a linear relationship between
independent and dependent variables which reduces model accuracy (Breiman,
2001). Hence, predictive power is relatively low. Machine learning can address this
problem as it considers non-linear relations and interaction between predictors without assuming linearity thereby maximizing predictive power (Hong et al., 2020).
Interaction in machine learning refers to complex and non-linear relationships
between predictors that are automatically incorporated into the random regression
model. The irrelevant factors are excluded from the random regression model, which
makes non-linear and interactions easier to accommodate than in linear regression
modelling (Hastle et al., 2009). Hence, machine learning might be more suitable
than classical approaches when the goal is to analyse the effects of many predictors
simultaneously (Hong et al., 2020). Several studies have shown that machine learning approaches can predict a greater amount of variance in the outcome variables
compared to classical statistics such as multiple linear regression or structural equation modeling (e.g., Chen et al., 2021; Fox et al., 2019).
Second, classical statistics may encounter the problem of overfitting, where the
complex model has an accurate estimate but does not fit new data well (Sinclair et al.,
2021). Classical statistics relies on the Null Hypothesis Significance Testing (NHST).
An over-reliance on the p-value and model fit indices can lead to overfitting problems (Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017). Indicators of “significance” in classical analyses are

highly dependent on sample size, i.e., the larger the sample, the greater the possibility
of obtaining “significance” (Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017). Machine learning can effectively help researchers test complex models to avoid overfitting problems. The machine
learning algorithm achieves generalization through a specific testing protocol. It learns
the association between independent and dependent variables through a given training
dataset and the model evaluates the performance of the data through the test dataset
(Biau & Scornet, 2016). This ensures the generalizability of the results.
Machine learning is increasingly used in the analysis of student performance in
large-scale educational surveys (Wang et al., 2022a, b; Gabriel et al., 2018; Çınar et al.,
2020). However, its usefulness in predicting well-being is still underutilized (e.g.,
Zhang et al., 2019). Hence, we utilized the power of this analytic approach in understanding Chinese students’ well-being.
2.4 The Chinese Context
This study was conducted in Mainland China, which has a considerable geographical
area with a population of about 1.4 billion. Mainland China has a unified education
system that is influenced by the Confucian tradition (Guo, 2013). The Ministry of Education formulates education policies, which are implemented by provincial education
authorities (OECD, 2016). In the last two waves of PISA, Chinese students ranked
among the lowest in the world in terms of well-being (OECD, 2019a; Peña-López,
2017). This finding means that students from Mainland China are doing better academically than the rest of the economies, but they are not happy. Previous research has
shown that students from Confucian Asian countries, such as China, have high levels
of academic performance but also suffer from anxiety and self-doubt (Stankov, 2010).
In addition, it is well known that the education system is highly academically oriented
in the Chinese context. Students have to work hard for exams and spend much time on
academic activities, leading to a low level of well-being (Xiang et al., 2019).
Previous work on student well-being has mainly focused on the personality traits
and background factors of Mainland Chinese students (Malone & Wachholtz, 2018;
Xing & Huang, 2014). Few studies have emphasized the effects of contextual factors
such as parents’ parenting style (Yang et al., 2008) and social support (Kong et al.,
2013; Sun et al., 2014). However, a comprehensive investigation of the different facets
has been neglected. Moreover, many studies have been conducted in one or two cities mostly confined to the location of the researchers’ universities (see Chen & Davey,
2008 for a review). In this case, we utilized the affordances of the PISA 2018 Chinese
dataset which draws on students from four core regions in mainland China.

subjective and eudaimonic well-being. Subjective well-being was indexed by students’ life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect. Eudaimonic well-being
was operationalized as meaning in life. We drew on the PISA 2018 conceptual

4 Methods
4.1 Sample
This study drew on the PISA 2018 Chinese data, publicly available on the OECD
website (https://​www.​oecd.​org/​pisa/​data/). The sample consisted of 12,058
15-year-old students (M = 15.77, SD = . 29) from Mainland China. Specifically,
students were from Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang (B-S-J-Z). Of these
students, 47.9% were female, and the majority of the students were in grade 10
(63.0%) and grade 9 (34.0%), with the rest (3.0%) in other grades.

4.2 Variables
4.2.1 Dependent variables
Subjective Well‑Being The measure of subjective well-being includes positive affect,
negative affect, and life satisfaction. Students were asked to rate their feelings in life
on a 4-point Likert scale from 1(never) to 4 (always) using five and four adjectives
for positive and negative affect respectively. These adjectives describe positive feelings (i.e., "joyful", "cheerful", "happy", "lively", and "proud"), and negative feelings (i.e., "afraid", "scared", "sad", and "miserable"). One item (i.e., "Overall, how
satisfied are you with your life as a whole these days?") was used to assess students’
overall life satisfaction rating from a scale of 0 to 10, with 10 being the highest.
Both positive (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) and negative (Cronbach’s α = 0.78) affect scales
had acceptable internal reliability.
Eudaimonic Well‑Being According to OECD (2019b), eudaimonic well-being was
operationalized as individuals’ sense of meaning and purpose (i.e., meaning in life),
in line with previous studies (Ryan & Deci, 2001; Ryff & Keyes, 1995). This scale
was measured using three items (e.g., "My life has clear meaning or purpose.") with
a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1(strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). This
scale showed adequate reliability in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.85).
4.2.2 Independent Variables
The independent variables were selected from the student questionnaire in PISA
2018. These variables included important predictors that have been addressed in previous literature and other potential factors as discussed. Thirty-five variables related
to students’ well-being were identified from the dataset and were categorized into
three types according to the PISA framework: students’ background, schooling, and
non-cognitive/metacognitive constructs (see Table 1). Student SES was represented
the economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS) of the students, which was measured by the availability of home possessions (i.e., household possessions), parental educational attainment, and parental occupational status (OECD, 2019b). PISA
ensures the cross-country comparability of constructs/items when developing scales/
items. Furthermore, all the scales included in this study demonstrated adequate
internal consistency and were comparable across cultures. For the sake of brevity,
we refer interested readers to the PISA 2018 Technical Report (OECD, 2020). All
scales showed high reliability and validity (OECD, 2020).
4.3 Analytic Strategies
Preliminary Analysis Markov Chain Monte Carlo multiple imputations (MCMC)
were used to impute the missing data entirely at random. Given its high rate of

estimation accuracy, it has been widely used in PISA (Chiu & Chow, 2010). Thus,
MCMC allows us to include all the variables we are investigating. The descriptive
statistics and the correlations between independent variables and well-being were
generated. Before primary analysis, all variables were standardized into zero mean
and unit SD because this study concentrates on the interrelationship within variables
instead of the magnitude and dispersion.
Primary Analysis One of the machine learning approaches, i.e., random forest
regression algorithm, was used in this study. The machine learning approach was
applicable in this study because it can handle a large volume of predictors while preventing overfitting and ensuring accuracy (Breiman, 2001; Lu & Ishwaran, 2018).
Specifically, the random forest regression method was used to determine the importance of factors affecting students’ well-being. Random forest regression combines
the randomized decision tree with bootstrapping procedures that iterate the predictive process until the error is minimized (Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017). The indices of
mean squared error (MSE) and explanation of variance (­R2) were reported in this
study to indicate the prediction accuracy and actual prediction effects of the random
forest regression model (Aldrich, 2020). The algorithm starts with a baseline model
where all factors (or IVs independent variables) are included. To measure the importance of a factor, the algorithm removes each factor and tests the predictive role of
the model. Errors will be increased as we have one factor (or IV) less in explaining
the outcome. The bigger the increase, the more important the factor. This statistic is
labelled as “%IncMSE”, which is the percentage increase of mean square error from
the baseline model and is not necessarily limited to 100%. Higher values denote that
it is more important. Subsequently, we selected the most crucial set of predictors by
replicating the tenfold cross-validation procedure five times. The final model with
the optimal accuracy was tested using the most critical set of variables.
Supplementary Analyses A hierarchical linear model was used to double-check the
robustness of the final models. Due to space limitations, the methods and results
were provided in the supplementary material (See supplemental materials).

5.2 Importance of Overall Variables
Using a random forest regression algorithm, we initially generated four prediction models for each well-being outcome with thirty five variables. The variables
explained 21.41% of the variance in positive affect with a mean of squared residual
(MSE) of 0.79, as well as 19.21% of the variance in negative affect with an error
rate of 0.81. The overall factors explained 26.44% and 32.47% variance in life satisfaction (MSE = 0.74) and eudaimonic well-being (MSE = 0.68) respectively. The
decrease in accuracy (%IncMSE) and the ranking of variables were displayed in
Table 2. Results showed various dominant factors impacting four well-being outcomes (%IncMSE > 30). For positive affect and life satisfaction, two prominent factors were found: a sense of school belonging and resilience. Results indicated two
salient factors regarding negative affect, i.e., a sense of school belonging and general
fear of failure. Last, resilience and work mastery were the dominant factors in influencing eudaimonic well-being. We also explored the differences in variable importance across gender groups (see the supplementary table S2). Results showed that
the variable importance of the top predictors (%IncMSE > 30) across genders were
broadly similar, with very minor differences.

5.3 Top Important Predictors of Well‑Being
We replicated five times tenfold cross-validation to streamline the prediction model.
The model’s prediction accuracy was optimal when we included all 35 factors in predicting positive affect ­(R2 = 21.41%, MSE = 0.79). In comparison, the decrease rate

affect. Hence, we selected the top 9 variables as the best predictors of positive affect
(see Fig. 2a). They were resilience, self-conception of competence, work mastery,
mastery goal orientation at non-cognitive/metacognitive influence, and five schooling factors of sense of school belonging, teachers’ stimulation teaching, parents’
emotional support, cooperation at school, and bullying experiences.
We also selected a model with the top 9 factors as the dominant predictive model
for negative affect ­(R2 = 18.13%, MSE = 0.82). They included both background and

reading for enjoyment, and self-conception of competence. Last, four schooling factors were identified: a sense of school belonging, bullying experiences, teachers’

The model with the top 9 factors (see Fig. 2c) was selected to explain the 24.14%

accuracy. Six schooling factors were selected. Among these, sense of belonging and
parents’ emotional support were the most critical variables, contributing more than
45% mean decrease in the model accuracy.
According to the results of the tenfold cross-validation, we selected the nine most
important factors (see Fig. 2d) in the final model predicting eudaimonic well-being
­(R2 = 32.47%, MSE = 0.78). The final model includes five non-cognitive/metacognitive factors. Resilience and work mastery were the salient non-cognitive/metacognitive factors accounting for more than a 62% decrease in accuracy. Schooling factors
included cooperation at school, parents’ emotional support, and a sense of school
belonging.

The present study proposed several random regression models for predicting students’ positive affect, negative affect, life satisfaction, and eudaimonic well-being
with high predictive accuracy ranging from 76 to 78%. Each optimal random regression model predicting four different dimensions of well-being included nine key factors from other domains. With the exception of SES, all of these important factors

(ranked ­2nd). As for life satisfaction, we found that school belonging (ranked ­1st out

6.1 Top Predictors in Different Categories
Background Constructs Among the background predictors, SES was the only key
factor predicting students’ negative affect in this study. Consistent with earlier
research, this study revealed that SES was strongly and negatively associated with
student well-being. Specifically, students in low SES families were more likely to
have negative emotions than those in high SES families (Chen, 2004). However, the
random forest algorithm did not identify SES as a critical factor for other aspects of
well-being (i.e., positive affect, life satisfaction, meaning in life). This underscores
the importance of looking at multiple indicators of well-being as SES seems to be
only pertinent to specific types of well-being.
Non‑Cognitive/Metacognitive Construct The top non-cognitive/metacognitive factors included resilience, self-concept of competence, work mastery, mastery goal
orientation, competitiveness, fear of failure, enjoyment in understanding well-being.
In this study, models that predicted different dimensions of well-being contained a
variety of sets of critical factors with distinct orders. Positive associations among
factors such as resilience, mastery goal orientation, work mastery, enjoyment, and
competitiveness (e.g., Andreou et al., 2020; Lever et al., 2005; Mouratidis et al.,
2013) and well-being were investigated. Resilience was the most crucial non-cognitive/metacognitive factor for subjective and eudaimonic well-being.
In line with the previous studies (e.g., Andreou et al., 2020), a higher level of resilience was related to more subjective well-being for younger students. Furthermore,
echoing past literature (Di Fabio & Palazzeschi, 2015), resilience was also a significant predictor of eudaimonic well-being. The results showed that resilience was a
critical resource related to optimal functioning (i.e., life meaning). Resilient people
regard challenges as opportunities for development (Zeng et al., 2016). Moreover,
they can identify positive meaning in adversity that increases their positive emotions
and buffer negative ones (Mak et al., 2011). Karreman and Vingerhoets (2012) found
that resilient people can improve their well-being by regulating their emotions.

Another factor, work mastery orientation, was a vital predictor of all positive
well-being variables (i.e., positive affect, life satisfaction, and meaning in life).
These findings are consistent with previous studies (Diseth et al., 2012; Mouratidis et al., 2013; Tuominen-Soini et al., 2012), suggesting that work mastery
orientation, intrinsic interest and motivation can further enhance subjective wellbeing. On the other hand, students with a high level of mastery orientation were
more likely to identify meaning in school life, which indicates a higher level of
eudaimonic well-being.
Eudaimonic well-being and negative affect were predicted by fear of failure.
This finding was consistent with previous studies (Baumeister et al., 2013; Berger
& Freund, 2012; Huang, 2021), indicating that fear of failure might significantly
impact students’ meaning in life and affective well-being. When students pursue
the goal, unsatisfactory progress leads to a strong focus on future failure (Carver
& Scheier, 1998). Students who are afraid of failure are prone to anxiety and fearfulness, resulting in negative affect (Berger & Freund, 2012).
In prior research, enjoyment and competitiveness were positively related
to well-being (Kuykendall et al., 2015; Lever et al., 2005). The study also provided evidence to support their significance in forecasting well-being. Other factors were found to affect well-being indirectly. For instance, the importance of
students’ self-concept of their levels of competence was indirectly linked with
students’ positive affect. Also, the self-perception of competence is strongly correlated with resilience (Martins & Neto, 2016; Mota & Matos, 2015), which was
also a key predictor for well-being.
Schooling Constructs Top schooling factors that influence students’ well-being
include a sense of school belonging, parental emotional support, perceived cooperation at school, teachers’ stimulation, the experience of being bullied, teachers’ feedback, and teachers’ interests.
In the school context, students’ sense of belonging, the experience of being
bullied, and cooperation had salient impacts on students’ well-being. Students’
sense of school belonging has been frequently identified as an essential factor
influencing students’ subjective and eudaimonic well-being, given the sense of
school belonging is students’ fundamental need to connect with the school, teachers emotionally, and peers (Gillison et al., 2008; Johnson, 2009; Tian et al., 2016;
Zhang et al., 2018). Similar findings have been demonstrated in previous studies.
For example, a strong sense of belonging is generally positively associated with
need satisfaction, such as school satisfaction (McMahon et al., 2008) and quality
of life (Gillison et al., 2008). The findings are consistent with self-determination
theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000), which considers school belonging as a core aspect
of relatedness.
Perceived cooperation was another key school contextual factor in improving well-being. As Desforges et al., (1991) posited, cooperation can evoke more
positive affect and attitudes than individualistic efforts. Conversely, selfish and
uncooperative behaviors are strongly associated with life dissatisfaction, mental

dysfunction, and illness (Cloninger, 2004). The current study found that a cooperative environment was related to subjective and eudaimonic well-being. Thus, as
far as the importance of perceived cooperation at school is concerned, the current
school policy and classroom should provide more opportunities and guidance for
students to cooperate constructively with their peers and teachers.
The experience of being bullied is also an important factor affecting students’
well-being as it often leads to mental health challenges and a sense of unhappiness,
as documented in the extant literature (Henry et al., 2014; Huang, 2021; Olweus &
Breivik, 2014; Zhao et al., 2020). This research identified the experience of bullying
as a key predictor of the positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction of students in parallel with literature.
In terms of the role of parents and teachers, their supportive and instructional
strategies have also had a significant impact on improving student well-being. In line
with previous studies, parental emotional support was associated with higher students’ well-being (Caldwell et al., 2004; Lim et al., 2015; Winakur, 2011). Consistent with previous studies (Govorova et al., 2020; Suldo et al., 2009a, b), the results
of this study indicated that the teachers’ stimulation, feedback, and interest also significantly predicted students’ well-being. In light of the results, school management,
teachers, and parents should improve the school environment to enhance students’
subjective and eudaimonic well-being by increasing students’ sense of belonging,
constructing cooperation, avoiding school violence, and providing more emotional
and academic support. Furthermore, the competition for limited college places is
very fierce. The limited opportunities for further study in high-quality schools generate great academic pressure on students. This may lead to their low level of wellbeing. Thus, we recommend future studies to consider these broader policy issues
and how they might be pertinent to Chinese students’ well-being.
6.2 Subjective and Eudaimonic Well‑Being
In this study, subjective and eudaimonic well-being were both affected by the most
important factors, such as resilience, nevertheless, there are certain differences. The
results were in line with prior studies (subjective well-being in Andreou et al., 2020,
eudaimonic well-being in Di Fabio & Palazzeschi, 2015), stressing the importance of
these factors in improving student well-being. At the same time, this study revealed
some differences in subjective and eudaimonic predictors of well-being. Perceived
competitiveness was identified important variable only for eudaimonic well-being.
This finding may be understood in the light of Chinese culture wherein many students place high value on making effort to achieve better performance, that is, striving to compete is the meaning of school life (Huang & Leung, 2005). Indeed, past
studies have found that competitiveness among Chinese students can be associated
with adaptive psychological outcomes (King et al., 2012). This might explain why
eudaimonic well-being is associated with higher perceptions of competitiveness.
This study also found that the experience of being bullied and teachers’ instruction impact subjective well-being more than eudaimonic well-being. In accordance with previous studies, being bullied was detrimental to subjective well-being

(Huang, 2021; Olweus & Breivik, 2014). Bullying makes students more at risk for
negative outcomes such as emotional problems, fear, and avoidance (Vidourek et al.,
2016). In addition, the results revealed that teacher instructions with feedback, stimulation, and interest in class were important in facilitating the subjective well-being
of students. As Govorova et al., (2020) have seen, teachers could promote positive
disciplinary school climates to reduce bullying and make students happy.
6.2.1 Chinese students’ well‑being
Among Chinese students, the results of this study showed that the different aspects
of well-being were linked to various key variables. Consistent with past studies,
Chinese students’ subjective well-being was significantly associated with sense of
belonging, resilience, fear of failure (Chen, 2016; Huang, 2021; Tian et al., 2016).
For both Chinese students’ positive affect and life satisfaction, we found that school
belonging, resilience, and parents’ emotional support were the top predictors. This
might be closely associated with the collectivistic context of China where emotions
were more associated with the assessment of social worth and the belonging to the
relationship with others (Mesquita, 2001).
In addition to school belonging, self-perception of competence and general fear
of failure played a key role in Chinese students’ negative emotions. The possible reason is in relation to the academically oriented educational system in China. Students
would frequently experience negative feelings, such as anxiety and worry if they
have a low perception of competence (Ping, 2010). General fear of failure would
lead to strategies of failure avoidance such as procrastination, which is accompanied
by negative emotions (Haghbin et al., 2012a, b; Huang, 2021).
Different from previous studies that only focused on the subjective well-being of
Chinese students (Zhou & Peng, 2018), this study also considered eudaimonic wellbeing. In line with Western studies (e.g., Di Fabio & Palazzeschi, 2015; TuominenSoini et al., 2012), the present study suggested that work mastery, competitiveness,
fear of failure, and resilience are important predictors of Chinese students’ eudaimonic well-being. One possible reason is that work mastery orientation would help
Chinese students build a sense of control that is associated with higher meaning
in life (Shek, 2001). In line with previous research, competitiveness was also substantially associated with students’ meaning in life (e.g., Hoferichter & Raufelder,
2017). Competition satisfies the drive to win, provides a chance to enhance abilities,
and motivates people to strive for greater performance (Robert and Brown, 1995).
Therefore, students with a high level of competitiveness may have a clearer sense of
purpose or meaning in life, resulting in greater eudaimonic well-being.
Fear of failure was found to be a top predictor of well-being. It was positively
associated with negative affect and negatively associated with eudaimonic wellbeing. This might be especially relevant in the Chinese cultural context where highstakes testing is prevalent and where there is very intense competition. High-stakes
testing has been found to be a significant source of stress and worry, which may
cause higher levels of fear of exam failure (Banks & Smyth, 2015). Chinese students
have a heightened fear of being negatively assessed. Such concerns are associated
with negative emotions such as shame and inferiority, or a sense of failing to live up

Past studies have found that resilience was strongly associated with Chinese students’ optimism and well-being (Zeng & Kern, 2019). This study not only found
the associations between resilience and different types of well-being among Chinese
student, but also revealed that resilience was one of the most important factors as
compared to other factors.
6.3 Implications
This research contributed to the literature in several ways. First, it addressed the
limitations of previous studies that only included a limited set of explanatory constructs for understanding students’ well-being. This study suggested that a comprehensive framework in well-being research allowed us to know how different groups
of factors predicted subjective and eudaimonic well-being. Second, current research
has enriched the literature methodologically by using machine learning approaches
as part of the well-being research. To date, machine learning has been recognized
as a powerful approach for large-scale educational data analysis, and it has been
increasingly used to understand academic achievement (Chen et al., 2021; Çınar
et al., 2020; Gabriel et al., 2018). However, the use of machine learning techniques
in well-being research is relatively rare. Our study showcased the utility of machine
learning in understanding students’ well-being.
This study also has important practical implications. Theoretically, this research
contributes to the existing literature by focusing on Chinese students. Compared to
Western, Educated, Industrial, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) countries (Henrich
et al., 2010), less research has focused on Chinese students’ well-being.
Practically, the findings of our study might have a contribution to the design of
effective school interventions aiming at improving the top predictors of Chinese
students’ subjective and eudaimonic well-being. 15-year-old Chinese students are
transitioning from middle school to high school. They must compete fiercely to get
admission to better high schools. Academic pressure leads to poor levels of wellbeing notwithstanding their worldwide success (Schleicher, 2019). Therefore, for
Chinese education practitioners, it is of utmost importance to understand and implement the factors that have the greatest impact on students’ well-being. The results
of this study would be informative for providing an evidence base for understanding
and improving Chinese student well-being. For example, this research highlighted
the critical roles of resilience and a sense of school belonging, which could become
targets of intervention efforts (Albott et al., 2020; Allen et al., 2021). Several studies
have shown how belonging or resilience can be improved using rigorous interventions (e.g., Allen et al., 2018; Brunwasser et al., 2009; Lambert et al., 2013; Layous et al., 2017). A sense of belonging could be enhanced by encouraging positive
relationships between students and others, such as their classmates, instructors, and
school personnel (see Allen et al., 2021 for a review).
In addition, the findings may inform interventions that use guided self-help
ideas that could improve students’ overall well-being (Cuijpers et al., 2010; Kählke

et al., 2019). Such self-help interventions are cost effective and efficacious and hold
immense in improving students’ well-being (Bennett et al., 2019).
6.4 Limitations and Directions for Future Research
Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, given the nature of the crosssectional data, no causal conclusions can be drawn from the results. Experimental or
longitudinal research is needed to examine causal relationships. Second, we used the
operationalization of PISA for key constructs. However, some of these operationalizations emphasized the individual aspect more but paid less attention to the social
context. For example, the resilience process has been considered to be strongly
shaped by both the individual and the environment (Ungar & Theron, 2020). As this
study relied on an existing dataset, some constructs may not be measured in detail.
For example, only individual resilience and general meaning in life were focused
on. Although resilience has often been studied as an individual trait, resilience can
also be understood as a complex interaction between the individual and the environment. (Ungar et al., 2013). Therefore, resilience should be assessed as the quality of
interaction between individuals and the environment (Ungar, 2012). Future studies
could further focus on the person-environment interaction process of resilience, and
its effects on students’ well-being rather than just treating it as an individual difference variable. Moreover, future studies may involve samples across different cultural
backgrounds.
This study operationalized eudaimonic well-being as meaning in life, which was
how PISA defined it (OECD, 2018). However, past studies have shown that wellbeing is a multi-faceted construct that comprises other dimensions such as self-discovery, development of one’s potential, and the pursuit of excellence among others
(Ryff & Singer, 2008; Waterman et al., 2010). The relative importance of predictors
may vary across different dimensions of eudaimonic well-being. Thus, we suggest
future studies to include a more comprehensive measure of eudaimonic well-being.
Last, this study only focused on the direct predictive effects of the independent
variables on well-being. A machine learning approach does not allow us to dig into
specific theoretical mechanisms to explain how the variables are associated with
each other as machine learning is not designed to answer such questions. To unpack
specific theoretical mechanisms that could link predictors to well-being, classical
statistical analyses such as mediational analyses or structural equation modelling
might be needed.

7 Conclusion
Using a machine learning approach, we employed a large-scale international educational dataset to explore the core predictors of Chinese students’ subjective
and eudaimonic well-being. The relative importance of factors drawn from three
domains (i.e., background, schooling, and non-cognitive/metacognitive factors)

potential targets for intervention efforts.
