A B S T R A C T

Grounded in Self-Determination Theory (SDT), this study explored the similarities and differences in life goals
among adolescents with varying socioeconomic status (SES), immigrant backgrounds and gender. Multi-group
confirmatory bifactor analyses and latent mean difference tests were conducted on data from a representative
sample of 3460 Italian adolescents. Our findings showed for the first time that the bifactor structure of life goals
was invariant across different sociocultural and economic conditions. However, these conditions differentially
influenced the importance adolescents placed on the various goals. Disadvantageous contexts increased the
priority adolescents gave to extrinsic over intrinsic goals, with varying effects based on the type of threat,
whether economic (low SES) or social (immigrant background). Boys were more extrinsically oriented and
prioritized wealth, whereas girls were more intrinsically oriented but also valued an appealing image. While
supporting the universality of the structure of life goals postulated by SDT, our findings revealed that sociocultural and economic conditions can lead to specific and different configurations of goals. Understanding these
differences in this formative period is fundamental because it can help identify risky situations and promote
healthier and more valuable aspirations in adolescents, enhancing their well-being and benefiting the entire
community.

1. Introduction
Within the Self-Determination Theory (SDT) framework, the Goal
Content Theory (GCT, Ryan & Deci, 2017) defines life goals or aspirations as the long-term aims people value and strive for. They organize
individuals' attitudes, emotions and behaviours, and, during adolescence, they can be guiding principles around which identity is established (Ahn & Reeve, 2021). GCT focuses on the contents of life goals
and structures them into two broad dimensions, intrinsic or extrinsic,
which affect differently people's integration and well-being. Intrinsic
goals are humans' innate tendencies towards growth and integration
with others and the environment, they allow people to satisfy their basic
psychological needs (e.g., relatedness, autonomy, and competence as
proposed by SDT; Ryan & Deci, 2017) and thus are inherently satisfying
to pursue and bolster well-being (Bradshaw et al., 2023). Extrinsic goals
focus on instrumental outcomes, do not satisfy basic psychological

needs, can frustrate them, and are related to lower well-being (Bradshaw
et al., 2023; Dittmar et al., 2014).
The GCT theorizes a structure of life goals with two general orientations that are reflected in specific aspirations and posited it as universal
(Bradshaw, 2023; Ryan & Deci, 2017). However, no study hitherto has
empirically examined the invariance of this structure across different sociocultural and economic conditions (gender, socioeconomic and immigrant background). This is a key aspect to investigate, because according
to SDT (Ryan & Deci, 2017), individuals' psychological functioning and
development result from the interplay between innate growth tendencies
and social environment, and the theory of personal values (Sagiv &
Schwartz, 2022) indicates that culture shapes value development and
expression. As young individuals grow up and become aware of their
gender, ethnic, and socioeconomic status within society, they may
perceive and conceptualize goals in different ways (Huijsmans et al.,
2021). In addition, while research indicates substantial variation in the

To address these gaps, this study, utilizing a large and representative
sample of Italian adolescents, aims to examine the differences in life
goals across socioeconomic and immigrant backgrounds and across
genders, focusing both on the structure of extrinsic and intrinsic life
aspirations as well as on the relevance of the various goals. Adolescence

well-being, as well as the social cohesion and prosociality of broader
communities. In fact, focusing on extrinsic aspirations has been found
(Kasser, 2016) to be negatively related to well-being (e.g., self-esteem
and life satisfaction), and positively related to ill-being (e.g., anxiety
and depression), and negative social attitudes and behaviours (antisocial
behaviours, fewer ecological engagement and cooperation). Conversely,
intrinsic aspirations are associated with mastery goal orientations
(Janke & Dickhauser, 2019) and with prosocial and pro-environment
behaviours, and predict various well-being outcomes, such as vitality,

Additionally, they are mostly based on data from university students'

Conflicting with the hypothesis of universality of the structure of life
goals, some studies have evidenced that aspirations, particularly
extrinsic ones, may hold different meanings for people living in
wealthier versus poorer contexts (Grouzet et al., 2005; Kasser, 2016). In
poorer countries, wealth aspirations were more closely related to
intrinsic goals: for disadvantaged people, striving for financial success
may reflect intrinsic goals such as protecting one's family. Unfortunately, most of the previous research conflated specific goals into the
two higher-order categories of intrinsic and extrinsic aspirations,
potentially obscuring different configurations of specific goals. The
recently introduced bifactor structure appears promising for investigating these potential differences in the structure of life goals, as it allows for examination of variations in individuals' overall intrinsicextrinsic orientations as well as in specific aspirations. Using a bifactor
structure combined with Latent Profile Analysis, Bradshaw et al. (2021)
identified subgroups of individuals who pursued specific combinations
of aspirations that included both intrinsic and extrinsic goals. However,
the bifactor model has been empirically tested in only one study, and its
consistency and generalizability across diverse sociocultural and economic conditions remain entirely unexamined.
In addition to the potential impacts of sociocultural and economic
conditions on the structure of life aspirations, their impact on the relative
importance that individuals assign to various goals warrants attention
(Manganelli et al., 2021). GCT posits that disadvantaged contexts hinder
the fulfillment of basic psychological needs, leading adolescents to seek
temporary satisfaction through material goods, thereby fostering extrinsic
aspirations (Bradshaw, 2023; Ryan & Deci, 2017). Conversely, needsupportive and safe environments facilitate intrinsic aspirations by
providing adolescents with the resources for prolonged periods of selfexploration and identity formation, along with experiences of autonomy
and relatedness. Consistent with this, some studies showed a negative
correlation between adolescents' extrinsic goals and family SES (Kasser
et al., 1995; Ku, 2015). However, other studies found a positive relationship between SES and extrinsic aspirations and no significant links to
intrinsic aspirations (Ahn et al., 2022; Zawadzka et al., 2022).
Adolescents with immigrant backgrounds often face challenging
social and cultural environments, characterized by heightened social
exclusion (Cavicchiolo et al., 2022, 2023; Plenty & Jonsson, 2017) and
experiences of victimization (Alivernini, Cavicchiolo, et al., 2019;
Bianchi et al., 2021; Strohmeier et al., 2011). Additionally, while navigating the developmental transition to adulthood, they are exposed to
potentially conflicting cultural values stemming from their familial
heritage and the host culture (Huijsmans et al., 2021; Phinney et al.,
2000), as well as language barriers and experiences of discrimination
(Albarello et al., 2023; Alivernini, Manganelli, et al., 2019). These adversities can hinder the fulfillment of their basic psychological needs of
relatedness, autonomy, and competence (Kunyu et al., 2021), prompting
them to prioritize values of security and conformity to foster a sense of
stability and acceptance in a new societal context (Bobowik et al., 2011).
However, no study to date has examined differences in life goals between immigrant and native adolescents.
Gendered role expectations and opportunities may also influence
goals: females are often encouraged to prioritize benevolence, whereas
males are expected to prioritize power (Sagiv & Schwartz, 2022).

1.1. The goal content theory of life aspirations
The GCT defines three specific life aspirations as specific facets of
each of the two general orientations (Ryan & Deci, 2017). Regarding
intrinsic aspirations, personal growth is defined as striving for autonomy, self-regard, and personal insight. Aspirations for meaningful relationships regards having good friends and strong familial and intimate
bonds. Aspirations for community contribution, involve striving to help
better the world and the lives of others. Regarding extrinsic aspirations,
wealth aspiration is defined as the pursuit of a great deal of money and
material possessions. Popularity aspiration involves people's interest in
notoriety and being admired. Appealing appearance aspiration centers
around looking attractive in terms of body or clothing.
The first theoretical structure theorized for life goals was the hierarchical structure proposed and tested by Kasser and Ryan (1996):
intrinsic and extrinsic general orientations are higher order factors that
account for the specific life goals, which are lower order factors and
depend on the general orientations. In a recent study, Bradshaw et al.
(2021) proposed a different structure, based on bifactor models: the
intrinsic and extrinsic general orientations are global constructs that
exist as unitary dimensions and coexist with the six specific goals. The
global factors indicate the quantity or “level effects” of intrinsic and
extrinsic aspiring, while the six aspirations indicate the specific contents
of goals. The results of this study provided a first empirical support for
proposed bifactor model and highlighted the utility of this model in
exploring how individuals prioritize specific aspirations relative to
others, despite their general orientations.
1.2. Life aspirations across different sociocultural and economic
conditions
The GCT posits the universality of the structure of life goals, suggesting
that this structure remains consistent across cultures, countries, and individual characteristics (Bradshaw, 2023; Ryan & Deci, 2017). Supporting
this proposition, two studies showed that the hierarchical structure of life
goals was invariant across gender (Costa et al., 2020; Ryan et al., 1999)
and some studies replicated this structure across different countries (e.g.,
Costa et al., 2020; Nishimura & Suzuki, 2016; Ryan et al., 1999; Schmuck
et al., 2000; Utvær et al., 2014). Using multidimensional scaling analyses,
Grouzet et al. (2005) observed that the organization of the specific

Research on adults shows that women lean towards intrinsic aspirations,
while men emphasize extrinsic aspirations, particularly wealth (Kasser
et al., 1995; Rijavec et al., 2011). However, the few studies on adolescents yield mixed results: Duriez et al. (2013) found boys endorse more

Overall, existing studies fail to provide definitive information on differences and similarities in the structure and relevance of adolescents' life
goals across various sociocultural and economic conditions. This gap is
significant, as understanding these differences in this formative period
could help promote more valuable and healthy aspirations in adolescents,
enhancing their well-being and benefiting the entire community. The
current study aims to provide an initial contribution to address these unresolved issues.

3.2.1. Life aspirations
The participants completed a brief version of the Aspirations Index
(AI; Kasser & Ryan, 1996) consisting of 23 items, which assessed the
three extrinsic values of wealth, appealing appearance, and popularity,
and the three intrinsic values of personal growth, community contribution, and meaningful relationships. The respondents rated the items
on a five-point scale based on personal importance, ranging from 1 (not
at all) to 5 (very important). In this study the internal consistency of the
scale was good, with Cronbach's alphas ranging from: 0.72 to 0.91.
3.2.2. Students' socio-economic status (SES)
Adolescents' SES was assessed using the National Index of Students'
Socio-Economic Background, which incorporates three indices to reflect
the social, educational and economic components of SES (Schulz et al.,
2016). The “highest occupational status of parents” index was derived
by coding the students' answers to open-ended questions about their
parents' jobs (International Labour Organisation, 1990) and then scoring
these codes with the International Socioeconomic Index of Occupational
Status (Ganzeboom et al., 1992), which was developed taking into
consideration the average occupational group income. The “highest
educational level of parents” index used students' reports of their parents' educational levels, aligned with the six categories of the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED-2011; UNESCO,
2012). The “home literacy resources” index was based on students' reports of book quantities at home, ranging from “0 to 10 books” to “more
than 500 books.”. Final SES scores were computed by the IEA using
factor scores from a principal component analysis of these indices
(Cronbach's alpha = 0.67). Students were categorized into low, middle,
and high SES groups using tertiles of the SES scores.

2. The present study
Guided by GCT and prior research, the study had two objectives:
OB1: To test the invariance of the structure of life goals across SES,
immigrant backgrounds and gender, using a bifactor-model with two
general factors (intrinsic and extrinsic orientation) and six specific
aspirations.
OB2: To explore the relevance of various life goals across those sociocultural and economic conditions through latent mean difference
tests, using the bifactor model described above.
Our research employed innovative methods to gain new insights into
life aspirations. The use of a bifactor model allowed us to examine
variations in specific aspirations alongside general orientations for the
first time. Latent mean difference tests ensured reliable comparisons by
accounting for the invariance of the life goals' structure and controlling
for measurement error. Additionally, utilizing a large and representative
sample provided more precise estimates of group differences and facilitated analysis across various SES levels and across native and immigrant
adolescents, which is often limited in smaller convenience samples. To
the best of our knowledge, no prior study has examined differences in
the structure and in the relevance of life goals across such diverse groups
of adolescents.

3.2.3. Immigrant background
Immigrant background was defined in accordance with the classification of the Organization for Economic Co-Operation and Development
(OECD, 2014): native adolescents were born in Italy and had at least one
parent born in Italy; immigrant adolescents had both parents born
abroad.
3.3. Analysis

3. Materials and methods

Analyses were conducted in three steps using Mplus 8.7. First, a
confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to test the bifactor model of
the AI. Second, measurement invariance of the AI was examined across,
SES (low, middle and high SES), immigrant background (native vs.
immigrant) and gender (girls vs boys). Finally, latent mean difference
tests were conducted to assess group differences in each type of life
aspiration. A detailed description of the analyses and the Mplus syntax is
provided in the Appendix.

3.1. Sample and procedures
The data analyzed in the present study came from 3460 Italian eighth
grade students who participated in the International Association for the
Evaluation of Educational Achievement International Civic and Citizenship Education Study 2016 (IEA ICCS; Schulz, Ainley, et al., 2018),
that aimed to investigate the extent to which young people are prepared
to undertake their roles as citizens. Participants were a nationally
representative sample of eighth grade students and were sampled using
the IEA sampling design. A detailed description of the sampling procedures and sample characteristics is provided in the Appendix.
Questionnaires were administered in classrooms during a regular
school day to collect the data. Each school obtained informed consent
and parental permission. All procedures were performed in compliance
with the assessment protocol approved by the IEA (Schulz, Carstens,
et al., 2018). The study was not preregistered.

Low SES adolescents had significantly lower scores on the intrinsic
general orientation and significantly higher levels of appearance and
wealth aspirations, compared to both middle SES and high SES adolescents. No significant differences were found between middle SES and
high SES adolescents. The only significant difference between native and
immigrant adolescents was on the extrinsic general orientation, with
immigrant adolescents scoring higher. The difference in meaningful
relationships was nearly significant, suggesting immigrant adolescents
might place less importance on this aspiration than native peers.
Regarding gender differences, females showed a stronger endorsement
of intrinsic general orientation, and a weaker endorsement of extrinsic
general orientation compared to males. Additionally, females placed
significantly more importance on having an appealing appearance. The
difference on wealth aspirations was nearly significant, suggesting that
males might value this aspiration more than females.

While the structure of life goals remained stable across diverse sociocultural and economic conditions, our findings revealed that these
conditions can differentially influence the importance adolescents place
on general extrinsic and intrinsic orientations, as well as specific goals.
Specifically, our findings support the predictions of GCT (Ryan & Deci,
2017) regarding the role of SES, but also show its differential influence
on general orientations and specific aspirations. Adolescents from
economically disadvantaged backgrounds exhibited lower levels of
intrinsic orientation while concurrently placing greater importance on
wealth and appearance, yet their overall level of extrinsic orientation
was unaffected. Conversely, more affluent family contexts fostered
greater intrinsic orientation among adolescents without directing them
towards specific intrinsic goals. Considering both the quantity of general
orientations and the content of specific goals was therefore crucial, as
focusing solely on one aspect or using a single index, as done in previous
research, would not have captured these differences. This might also
help explain the mixed results about the influence of SES on life goals
obtained in previous studies (Ahn et al., 2022; Kasser et al., 1995; Ku,
2015; Zawadzka et al., 2022).
For the first time, our findings revealed significant differences in life
goals between immigrant adolescents and their native peers. As predicted by GCT, immigrants, often facing adverse social and cultural
contexts, exhibited higher overall level of extrinsic orientation, even if
they were not particularly interested in specific extrinsic goals. Contrary
to expectations, they did not show lower levels of intrinsic orientation,
except in the domain of meaningful relationships. A possible explanation for this diminished importance may relate to the specific challenges
that immigrants often encounter in terms of social exclusion
(Cavicchiolo et al., 2022, 2023; Plenty & Jonsson, 2017), victimization
(Alivernini, Cavicchiolo, et al., 2019; Bianchi et al., 2021; Strohmeier
et al., 2011), discrimination and language barriers (Kunyu et al., 2021).
These challenges can hinder the fulfillment of the need for relatedness,
potentially leading immigrants to view meaningful relationships as less
attainable and, consequently, to place a lower value on them. Under
these circumstances, there may be a stronger emphasis on securing basic

5. Discussion
This study explored differences in life goals across sociocultural and
economic conditions, focusing on both the structure and relevance of
these goals. Using a bifactor model, we provided empirical support for a
theoretical structure of life goals in which the overarching dimensions of
extrinsic and intrinsic orientations coexist with the specific contents of
aspirations. This structure aligns with GCT and addresses the limitations
of previous studies that focused on either specific aspirations or general
orientations. Furthermore, by showing the invariance of this structure
among adolescents of different SES, immigrant backgrounds, and
gender, our findings support the universality of the life goals' structure
as proposed by GCT and indicate that the conceptualization of life goals,
the meaning of different goals and the interpretation of the various indicators used to measure them is the same among adolescents from
different sociocultural and economic backgrounds.

(Sheldon, 2004). Since these findings represent the first insights into the
characteristics of life aspirations among native and immigrant students,
further research is needed to deepen our understanding of these differences. Taken together, the results about SES and immigrant background
show that adverse contexts can increase the priority adolescents give to
extrinsic over intrinsic goals, with varying effects based on the type of
threat, whether economic or social.
Our findings also highlighted that gender differences play a role and
clarified that they can influence both the general orientation as well as
specific goals. The differences on general orientations align with findings on adults (e.g., Rijavec et al., 2011) and seem to reflect the differential roles and expectations that boys and girls are socialized into
(Sagiv & Schwartz, 2022). Females are often encouraged to prioritize
caring, assistance, and benevolence, which may explain the greater
intrinsic orientation found in girls. Conversely, males are expected to
prioritize power and status, which may explain the higher extrinsic
orientation found in boys. Additionally, boys placed more importance
on wealth and less on appealing appearance, whereas girls exhibited the
opposite pattern. These opposing preferences of specific aspirations can
be interpreted through an evolutionary perspective, suggesting that men
and women seek partners with distinct yet complementary attributes
(Sheldon, 2007). Although males may not prioritize personal appearance for themselves, they prefer potential partners who value good
looks. Conversely, although females may not prioritize personal wealth
for themselves, they prefer partners who aspire to financial success. Our
results suggest that adolescents may be striving to achieve the attributes
desired by their potential mates. Finally, the combination of a higher
level of intrinsic orientation and emphasis on appealing appearance in
girls suggests that individuals may differ in how they prioritize specific
aspirations over others, despite their general orientation. These findings
align with a recent profile analysis (Bradshaw et al., 2021) and suggest
that, for girls, having an appealing image may serve as a means to
achieve intrinsic goals. Bradshaw (2023) recently proposed that a goal
appearing manifestly extrinsic could have intrinsic underpinnings. For
example, “earning to give” illustrates how a seemingly extrinsic aspiration, such as seeking high wages, can be fundamentally rooted in
intrinsic values. These findings highlight again the importance of
exploring variations in general orientations alongside specific
aspirations.
Despite these significant findings, some limitations need to be
acknowledged. Although our research was grounded in the wellestablished theoretical framework of SDT, our cross-sectional design
limits the ability to establish causal relationships, necessitating longitudinal studies to corroborate our findings. Additionally, while our
sample was large and nationally representative it only included 8thgrade Italian adolescents. Further research is needed to generalize
these findings to other age groups and different countries.
In conclusion, our study significantly contributes to the literature by

uncovering similarities and differences in life goals among adolescents
from various sociocultural and economic conditions. Our research provides the first evidence supporting the universality of a bifactor structure
of life goals as postulated by GCT. By leveraging the possibilities offered
by this model and its invariance, a large sample, and latent mean analyses, we revealed that sociocultural and economic conditions can lead
to important differences in life goals, that would likely have remained
hidden using different approaches. Our findings showed that adolescents from disadvantaged economic or social backgrounds, and boys,
prioritized extrinsic goals over intrinsic goals, but with specific and
differential patterns of preferences. This kind of orientation can have
serious consequences such as negative social attitudes and behaviours
and reduced well-being (Bradshaw et al., 2023; Ryan & Deci, 2017),
exacerbating disadvantaged conditions and further distancing adolescents from intrinsic goals and well-being. Our contribution to the understanding of the link between sociocultural and economic conditions
and specific configurations of life goals is therefore fundamental for
identifying risky situations and informing interventions to foster aspirations that are healthier and more valuable for individuals and society.
This is particularly relevant during adolescence, a formative phase when
adult aspirations and hierarchies of values are established.

A.2. Analysis
Analyses were conducted using appropriate sampling weights (Schulz, Carstens, et al., 2018) in Mplus 8.7 employing the “Type = complex”
approach (Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2017). This method calculates standard errors and performs a chi-square test of model fit while accounting for
stratification and non-independence of observations resulting from the hierarchical structure of the data (students nested within classes). The very
small amount of missing data (0.2 % to 2.5 %) was handled with Full Information Maximum Likelihood method in Mplus. The Mplus sintax is provided
in section 4 of this Appendix.
A preliminary Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed on the AI to test the bifactor model, which included two general factors (intrinsic
and extrinsic orientations) and six factors for specific aspirations. The bifactor model was selected consistently with the research objectives, as it
enables an assessment of whether two overarching constructs, represented by the general factors, function as unified dimensions underlying responses
to all items while coexisting with more specific facets (specific factors) that capture the variability in item responses not explained by the general
factors. In this model, each item simultaneously loaded onto both the general factor and the specific factor it was designed to measure, with crossloadings and correlations between factors constrained to zero.
Model fit was evaluated using common fit indices and the chi-square test (which can however be oversensitive in samples as large as ours).
Additional CFAs were conducted to test alternative models of the AI (higher-order model, two-factor model and six-factor model) and to confirm that
the bifactor model provided the best fit.
Next, we tested the measurement invariance of the AI across, SES (low, middle and high SES), immigrant backgrounds (native vs. immigrant) and
gender (girls vs boys). This was done to examine the invariance of the bifactor model across groups (OB1) and as a prerequisite for reliable measurement of life goals differences between groups (OB2). A hierarchical series of multi-group CFAs was performed imposing increasingly restrictive
equality constraints on the model's parameters for testing the measurement invariance at the configural, metric and scalar level (Meredith, 1993). As
recommended by Chen (2007), the fit of the nested models was compared using changes in CFI, RMSEA, and SRMR, as these indices have varying
sensitivities to sample size and noninvariance across the different steps of invariance testing. The more stringent criteria (Chen, 2007) were adopted to
establish measurement invariance: ΔCFI <0.010; ΔRMSEA <0.015; ΔSRMR <0.030. This approach also helps account for the unequal sample sizes in
native vs. immigrant background comparisons. In line with the recommendation of Yoon and Lai (2018) for testing measurement invariance with
unbalanced samples, we also conducted an additional series of multigroup CFAs using a random subsample of the native group matched in size to the
immigrant group (n = 329).
Finally, latent mean difference tests were conducted to examine differences in each type of life aspiration across groups (OB2). Group variances
were constrained to 1 to interpret results in terms of Cohen's d.
