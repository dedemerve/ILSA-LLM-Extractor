1 | INTRODUCTION
As science-related fields greatly contribute to the prosperity of our society, there is a broad consensus that there should be sufficient talent to be encouraged to choose science-related careers.
Unfortunately, promoting students' aspirations for STEM-related careers is a challenge in many
countries. Most economies, particularly the developed countries in the OECD, are facing the
issue of insufficient STEM graduates (e.g., Archer et al., 2012; Jeffries et al., 2020; National Science Board, 2015) and underrepresented groups in STEM disciplines (e.g., Steegh et al., 2021;
Wade-Jaimes & Schwartz, 2019). Previous studies found that, though some students had an
interest in or were good at science, they lacked aspirations to enter science-related disciplines
(e.g., Jenkins & Nelson, 2005; Scholes & Stahl, 2022), especially underrepresented groups like
females (e.g., Beckmann, 2021; Scholes & Stahl, 2022) and ethnic minorities (e.g., Archer,
DeWitt, & Osborne, 2015; Wong, 2015). These issues attract broad attention in both research
and practice to identify key factors for enhancing students' science-related career aspirations.
In the past decades, factors related to students' career aspirations have been widely examined. Particularly, researchers have developed and utilized the conception of science capital,
which refers to science-related resources and dispositions, to explain the difference in students'
science career expectations (Archer, Dawson, et al., 2015; Du & Wong, 2019; Gonsalves
et al., 2021). Informed by science capital theory, researchers investigated the relationships
between career aspiration and several key factors, utilizing regional data (e.g., DeWitt
et al., 2016; Du & Wong, 2019; Moote et al., 2021). However, due to the limitations of conventional statistical techniques in handling high-dimensional data and the challenges associated
with collecting comprehensive international data incorporating contextual factors alongside

2 | THEORETICAL P ERSPECTIVE AND I NFLUENTIAL
FACTORS
Prior studies have shown that science capital is a strong conceptual framework for investigating
students' science-related identity and career aspirations (Moote et al., 2020). In this section, we
first briefly introduce the science capital framework. Then, we accordingly review the influential factors of students' expectations of science-related careers from the perspective of science
capital, which ensures a comprehensive inclusion of meaningful and relevant factors for model
training and testing.

2.1 | Science capital as a theoretical lens
The concept of capital is derived from Bourdieu (1984) sociology theory, in which the differences and inequalities in social life could be explained as the product of the interaction of habitus, capital, and field. Habitus is an internal set of dispositions that are acquired over time
during the process of socialization. It shapes people's sense of what is desirable and possible for
them (e.g., the feeling that I want and can be a scientist). Capital refers to the social, cultural,
and economic resources that people have and can be used to support their development. As for

Informed by the work of Bourdieu, researchers extended the notion of capital into the field
of science education (Archer et al., 2012; Archer, Dawson, et al., 2015; Claussen &
Osborne, 2013; DeWitt et al., 2016). They have recognized the significance of science-related
habitus and capital in gaining a deeper understanding of why some people choose to pursue science while others do not (e.g., Archer, DeWitt, & Osborne, 2015; Moote et al., 2020). These
science-related habitus and capital were termed “science habitus and capital,” or, for short, “science capital.” In this study, we also adopted this conceptualization (i.e., science capital) to collate all kinds of socialized and embodied science-related attitudes, beliefs, dispositions, and
economic, social, and cultural resources. In other words, the current study referred to science
capital as all science-related resources and dispositions that can affect science learning, and
used it as a lens to identify the key factors for students' science-related career expectations i a
broad context. According to Archer, Dawson, et al. (2015), science capital can be conceptualized
as scientific forms of cultural capital (CC) and habitus, science-related behavior and practices
(BP), and science-related forms of social capital (SC), which generally correspond to questions
of “what science you know” (and “how you think”), “what you do,” and “who you know,”
respectively (DeWitt et al., 2016). In each dimension, there are several sets of factors found to
be influential on students' career expectations.

2.2 | Influential factors of science-related career expectations
As the value of capital is not inherently unchangeable but is shaped by field (Bourdieu, 1984),
the conceptualization of science capital in our research should highlight those science-related
resources that are empirically endowed with the most value to the field of science education
(DeWitt et al., 2016) and particularly related to science-related career expectations. In the following, we briefly review the influential factors of science-related career expectations. As Moote
et al. (2021) indicated, because of the conceptual closeness, some factors can be interpreted as
comprising aspects of both habitus and capital. For instance, talking regularly with others about
science at home reflects both habitus (a socialized disposition to value and talk regularly
about science) and social capital (knowing someone who knows or values science). In this
study, we would introduce the influential factors from both the habitus and capital perspectives
and discuss their potential interplays, if applicable.

2.2.1

| Scientific forms of cultural capital and habitus

Cultural capital (CC) refers to the understanding/knowledge, literacy, and other cultural advantages that can be used to promote one's success in a science-related field. Habitus is an internal
set of dispositions that shapes people's sense of how science is desirable and possible for them
(e.g., the feeling that I want and can be a scientist). CC and habitus are closely related to each
other, featuring “What science do you know” and “How do you think (about science)” (DeWitt
et al., 2016). One of the most influential scientific forms of CC is science literacy (also termed science performance or achievement in some studies). In prior research, it was usually shown that
students with higher science performance were more likely to choose a science-related or STEMrelated career (e.g., Cairns & Dickson, 2021; Holmes et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2017), while several

the relationships between epistemic beliefs and career expectations. Although it was posited that
knowledge about science itself was important to students' science career expectations, previous
studies have found that the positive link between epistemic beliefs and career expectations was
largely explained by achievement and motivation (Guo et al., 2022). To identify more accurate
effects and their relative importance, these mixed results call for research to get a more holistic
picture by taking a broad range of related factors into consideration.
Science dispositions, as the manifestation of habitus, are essential to students' sciencerelated career expectations. Researchers have found that science dispositions like self-efficacy in
science, achieving motivation, environmental awareness, enjoyment of science, and instrumental motivation were generally positively associated with students' science-related career expectations (e.g., Cairns & Dickson, 2021; Mujtaba et al., 2018; Sheldrake, 2020; Uitto, 2014; Yang &
Gao, 2021). It should be noted that science disposition constructs were often studied as the
mediator between career aspirations and other factors like teachers' support (Lv et al., 2022),
parents' involvement (Dotterer, 2022), and science learning experience (Kang &
Keinonen, 2017). Moreover, the relationships between science dispositions and aspirations were
found to be moderated by factors like awareness or knowledge of science careers (Kang
et al., 2023) and self-concept beliefs (Sheldrake, 2016).
These studies indicated that other related variables should be accounted for jointly when
explaining the relationships between science disposition constructs and career expectations
(Archer, DeWitt, & Osborne, 2015). As disposition constructs are closely related to each other,
classical statistical approaches (e.g., regression analysis) may suffer from multicollinearity problems when simultaneously investigating their relationships with career expectations. The highly
correlated factors (e.g., similar motivation constructs like science enjoyment and interest) make
it difficult for the models to estimate the unique contribution of each variable to the outcome,
thus leading to unstable and unreliable regression coefficients and inaccurate predictions (Hair
et al., 2018). Besides, when comparing their relative importance (or effect size) in predicting
outcome variables, classical statistical techniques rely on a null hypothesis based on the p-value,
which might be overly influenced by sampling and measurement errors (Krueger, 2001). Innovative methods like machine learning approaches would be helpful in addressing these issues
and providing a more reliable relative importance of each factor simultaneously (e.g., Chen
et al., 2021; Chen, Sakyi, & Cui, 2022; Wang et al., 2022).

2.2.2

| Science-related behaviors and practices

Science-related behaviors and practices (BP) refers to the learning experiences and activities
that promote one's science-related career expectations. BP relates to the question of “what you
do” makes you build science-related career aspirations. In previous studies, consumption of
science-related media (e.g., TV programs, the web, and magazines or books) (e.g., Chen, Chiu,
et al., 2022; Cohen et al., 2021), visiting informal science learning contexts like visiting science
centers or museums (e.g., Hinojosa et al., 2021), and attending science clubs (e.g., DeWitt
et al., 2016; Tomperi et al., 2022) or workshops (e.g., Salvado et al., 2021) were found to be crucial science capitals for career aspirations. These activities can increase students' knowledge
(both scientific knowledge and knowledge about science careers) and interest in science, which
in turn contribute to students' science identity and career aspirations.

Formal science learning experience, which refers to students' science learning experience in

expectations. Previous researchers showed that students' perceptions of science teaching behaviors
(e.g., inquiry-based learning and teacher-direct teaching) were crucial factors affecting students' science learning motivational constructs and science-related career expectations (Kang &

in education were another set of influential factors. In the information age, ICT use in education is

practices, it is rarely known what factors were more influential for career aspirations.

2.2.3

| Science-related forms of social capital

Science-related forms of social capital (SC) refer to social networks and relationships that facilitate individuals' science participation. For “who you know” related to science, the main influential social capital that was investigated in the existing literature included knowing someone
working in a science job (DeWitt et al., 2016), parental scientific knowledge or education (Starr
et al., 2022), and parental support of science learning (Lv et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020). It was
assumed that with more familial and social connections to science, students were more likely to
have more knowledge, interest, and aspirations towards science careers (Holmes et al., 2018).
As significant others for students' science learning, science teachers are another essential
source of social capital. If their science teachers were qualified and provided adaptive support
rather than unfair treatment, students would have better engagement and achievement
(Chen & Cui, 2020; Molinari & Mameli, 2018), and be more likely to maintain their sciencerelated career expectations (Starr et al., 2022). Thus, it should also be investigated whether
science-teacher-related factors like the total number of science teachers at school, the proportion of fully certified science teachers, teacher fairness, and perceived teacher support in science
class were key predictors of students' science-related career expectations.
In summary, prior research has identified a diverse range of factors that play a significant
role in shaping students' science-related career expectations, including various interactions
among these factors. However, most existing studies have only examined a limited number of
variables when exploring the relationships between these factors and career expectations. As a
result, there is a lack of comprehensive research that investigates a wide range of science capitals from both a global and regional perspective to identify the most crucial features associated
with students' science-related career aspirations. Moreover, previous studies have predominantly assumed linear relationships between factors and outcomes, with limited exploration of
how science capitals may uniquely contribute to students' career expectations in distinct ways.

3 | R ESEAR CH GOALS A ND RATIONALES
Through the lens of science capital, this exploratory study aims to answer two questions:
(1) among broad contextual factors, what factors or resources are more effective in predicting
students' career aspirations, both at a global scale and in a specific region? and (2) How do they
relate to career aspirations in distinct ways? By leveraging the strengths of machine learning
techniques and utilizing the dataset effectively, our study contributes to the existing literature
in several ways. First, this study utilized a wide range of PISA 2015 variables as a proxy for science capital. These variables were measured from different perspectives (i.e., students, teachers,
principals, and parents) and covered almost all essential dimensions affecting students' science
learning outcomes, making this exploratory research a reliable and comprehensive examination
of science capitals for career aspirations.
Second, given the high-dimensional nature and the large size of the dataset used, a machine
learning approach with cross-validation was utilized to identify the crucial science capitals,
which enhanced the validity of the findings. Through this approach, the high prediction performance achieved would offer robust empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness and importance of the identified factors in predicting students' career expectations. Third, the use of
accumulated local effects (ALEs) plots helps to uncover the relationships between these key factors and students' science-related career expectations. Understanding these relationships has
theoretical and practical implications for researchers and policymakers alike.
Finally, this study conducts a large-scale analysis with international samples, incorporating
both global and regional analyses. The synergistic combination of these analyses enhances the
generalizability of our findings while also revealing contextual specifics. By initially analyzing
the entire PISA dataset, we maximized the sample size for model training, reduced the issue of
overfitting, and improved prediction accuracy. Admittedly, when treating all countries as a
whole at this stage, we might miss the subtle contextual differences between areas. Still, on the
other hand, our results may help examine to what extent findings from regional studies
(e.g., parents' expectations are important for students' career aspirations) were tenable at the
global level (e.g., DeWitt et al., 2016; Du & Wong, 2019). The findings help underscore resources
that may be generally effective yet remain underappreciated within the broader contextual
landscape. Moreover, an independent regional analysis using data from Hong Kong is conducted to provide further contrast and insights. The synergy between global and regional analyses offers valuable references for more nuanced investigations, elucidating how the results of
a region could resemble or differ from the global situation.

4.2 | Students' science-related career expectations
In this study, students' science-related career expectations refer to students' aspirations to choose
careers “whose realization requires further engagement with the study of science beyond compulsory education, typically in formal tertiary education settings” (OECD, 2016a, p. 282). Thus, it
includes both careers in science (i.e., doing science research) and careers from science
(i.e., occupations that require specific scientific knowledge and skills) (Wong, 2015). For the data collection, PISA 2015 asked students to answer a question (ST114) about “what kind of job do you
expect to have when you are about 30 years old” (OECD, 2016a, p. 282) and coded their answers
into four-digit International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO) codes (International
Labour Office, 2012), in variable OCOD3. According to the framework of PISA 2015 (OECD, 2016a),
four groups of occupations were included in this study: (1) science and engineering professionals,
like physical, earth science, life science, mathematicians, and engineering professionals; (2) health
professionals, like doctors, nurses, and veterinarians; (3) ICT professionals, like software and applications developers and analysts; and (4) science technicians and associate professionals, like physical,
life science, engineering science, medical, and pharmaceutical technicians. We recoded students
with the above science-related career aspirations as 1, otherwise (e.g., musicians, sports agents, car
guards) as 0. In the sample used in this study, there are 141,132 students who expected a sciencerelated career while 313,056 students chose non-science-related occupations (1 = 27.15%,
0 = 60.30%, Na = 12.54% [65146]). The detailed coding table is presented in Appendix 1.

4.4 | Analytic strategy
4.4.1

| Random forest

Random forest (RF) is a popular supervised machine learning algorithm for classification. We
chose RF to predict students' career expectations because of its capacity to address the complexity (e.g., both categorical and continuous features) and large volume of our data, resulting in
robust, generalizable findings for us to understand how people make career decisions. As its
name implies, RF consists of a great number of (usually from 300 to 500) individual decision
trees that work together as an ensemble. Technically, the RF algorithm learns the data by following steps. First, the RF algorithm draws a certain number of bootstrap samples
(i.e., randomly draw a sample from the data source with replacement). Then, for each bootstrap
sample, the RF algorithm grows a decision tree, thus creating a “forest” (ensemble) of
uncorrelated decision trees. In the context of our study, these decision trees are models that represent the best way to classify students who have science-related career expectations from those
who do not, within each bootstrap sample data, by making splits based on the randomly
selected features. The random subsampling of both observations (students) and features enables
a comprehensive examination of data from different perspectives and captures the diverse interactions among variables at different levels. When classifying new data samples, each decision
tree in the RF makes a class prediction, and the class with the majority vote across all
decision trees would be the prediction of the whole RF model.
Specifically, the decision tree in RF imitates human decision-making. With the selected features (e.g., science enjoyment and literacy), how the decision tree makes classifications at each
node is just like how we, as humans, make a decision (e.g., whether to choose a science career)
according to our answers to a series of questions (e.g., How good are students' scientific literacy?
How do students enjoy science learning, etc.). Thus, the whole learning process of RF is like
conducting hundreds of investigations on how selected variables could interact and relate to
people's decision and then synthesizing the results. By employing RF to learn the data and
interpreting how it predicts students' career aspirations based on features, we can gain insights
into what factors are key and how they play a role in their career decision-making.
Furthermore, besides the common merits of the machine learning approach (e.g., the capacity to deal with high-dimensional data and its multicollinearity for specific cases), RF has several other advantages for data analysis. As the RF was built by randomly selecting the data and
variables so that no single tree sees all the data, it can reduce overfitting (i.e., the model is too
closely aligned to the training dataset, making the model and results less applicable to external
test datasets). This serves our goals of finding the global and regional influential factors and
their general relation patterns. The ensemble of decision trees, combined with bootstrap

Finally, RF can be directly used for feature selection. There are several different methods or
metrics for determining the variable importance in RF (e.g., permutation importance and Gini
importance, Strobl et al., 2007). This study would identify the key features based on mean decrease
accuracy (MDA) and further rank their relative importance based on mean decrease Gini (MDG)
(Han et al., 2016; Kuhn, 2008). MDA indicates how much accuracy the model loses by excluding
each feature. Technically, to calculate MDA, the algorithm would record the prediction accuracy on
the out-of-bag portion of the data for each tree before and after permuting each predictor variable,
and then the difference between the two accuracies is averaged over all trees and normalized by the
standard error (Han et al., 2016). The more accuracy suffers, the more important a feature is for successful classification. The MDG coefficient measures how each variable contributes to the homogeneity of the nodes and leaves in the resulting random forest, which indicates each feature's unique
contribution to classifying or predicting the outcome (Strobl et al., 2007). The higher the value of
the MDG, the greater the importance of the feature in the model.

4.4.2

| Accumulated local effects plot

To make the predictive modeling by RF more interpretable, the ALEs plot, a popular method
for explainable machine learning (Molnar, 2022), was used to visualize the relationships
between science capitals and students' science-related career expectations. By calculating differences in predictions based on the conditional distribution of the features, the ALE plot can give
us the pure effect of a feature on the outcome, which would not be confounded by the effects of
other correlated features (Apley & Zhu, 2020). There are several advantages of the ALE plot
(Apley & Zhu, 2020) in interpreting machine learning classifiers. First, compared to other
methods like the partial dependence plot, ALE plots are unbiased as they work with correlated
features. Second, ALE plots are computationally fast. Finally, the ALE plot provides a clear-cut
interpretation of the feature effects. It presents the relative effect of changing the feature on the
prediction conditional on a given value (Molnar, 2022), and the ALE curve represents how
the prediction would be different as the predictor changed.

4.4.3

| Analysis procedure

The data analysis was performed with R-4.3. For the global analysis, we first randomly split the
original dataset into two parts: the training dataset (80%) and the test dataset (20%). By keeping
the test dataset separate, we prevented information leakage (i.e., information from the test dataset
leaks into the training process) and ensured that the model's evaluation was based on independent data. Missing values exist for the PISA data due to respondents failing to give a response or
questions that are not applicable to the respondents (OECD, 2017b). Data of students who did
not tell their career aspirations (n = 65,146) or with missing values over half of our selected features (n = 40,252) were deleted for data analysis (i.e., a total of 105,398 students were dropped,
the missing rate of cases was 20.29%). The missing rate of values in the remaining dataset is
29.72%. After that, we applied the k-nearest neighbor (KNN) imputation to impute the missing

Several indicators were used for model evaluation. AUC is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, which plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate at different thresholds. The higher the AUC score, the better the overall capability of discriminating
between students with and without science-related career expectations. As AUC does not
depend on the threshold and is not sensitive to imbalances in the dataset, it is a commonly used
indicator for validating and selecting the optimal classifier model. Furthermore, the
sensitivity—the percentage of how many actual positives (i.e., students who have sciencerelated career aspirations) that are correctly detected, the specificity—the percentage of how
many actual negatives (i.e., students without science-related career aspirations) that are correctly identified, and the accuracy—the percentage of how many students were correctly
classified—were also employed for model evaluation. The scores of these metrics all range from
0 to 1, with a higher value indicating better performance.
Ten-fold cross-validation was used to evaluate the model and select features (Wong &
Yeh, 2020). Specifically, this method involved randomly shuffling the data and then splitting it into
10-folds. During each iteration, nine folds were used for training the model, while the remaining
fold was then used for testing, resulting in 10 different AUC scores for model evaluation. To find
the optimal feature size for predicting students' career expectations, we performed 10-fold crossvalidation in two steps. In the first step, we trained and tested the model using recursive feature
elimination with feature sizes ranging from the top 5 k (i.e., 5, 10, 15, … 85) to 88 features and plotted their predicting accuracy. Informed by prior researchers (e.g., Zheng et al., 2023), the turning
point of the feature number, indicating diminishing improvements or convergence in the model's
performance, was regarded as the optimal feature number. It was observed that the turning point
fell within the range of 15–25 features. Subsequently, the accuracy scores of the model using the top
15 to top 25 features were also calculated via 10-fold cross-validation. As shown in Figure 1, the
accuracy tended to converge after the 25th feature. Thus, the top 25 features would be regarded as
key for identifying students' science-related career expectations in our study.
After that, we proceeded to fit and evaluate the RF model again, using only the top 25 features. The model's performance was evaluated on the test dataset, thereby validating the effectiveness of the selected key variables. Finally, the ALE plots were created for each key feature
to display their relationships with students' science-related career expectations, which could
provide further insight into how these capitals contributed to students' career aspirations. The
regional analysis using the Hong Kong dataset was conducted separately, following the above
procedures. The 10-fold cross-validation indicated that the top 10 features were crucial for
predicting Hong Kong students' career expectations (see Figure 1).

5.3 | Accumulated local effects plot
The ALEs of each key capital on students' science-related career expectations are shown in Figure 2.
Both in global and Hong Kong analyses, ALE plots revealed four main patterns of relationship. The
first pattern revealed that some factors (e.g., 1. INSTSCIE, 3. JOYSCIE, and 25. HISCED) have a generally positive effect on students' science career expectations. As these capitals continuously contribute to students' career aspirations, we named them “sustainably supportive” capitals or increasing
capitals. The second pattern is like a sigmoid curve, in which predictors (e.g., 6. PRESUPP,
8. PVMATH, and 12. PQGENSCI) showed a partial positive association with the outcome. Within a
certain range, the capitals would positively relate to students' science career aspirations, while outside the range, the trends of students' science career aspirations reach a plateau or even decrease as
the capitals increase. These factors were called “local booming” capitals or S-shaped capitals.
The third pattern draws an inverted U curve concerning the relationships between capital
and career aspirations. With the increase of predictors (e.g., 4. INTBRSCI, 9. SCIEACT, 10.
HOMESCH, 15. SCIEEFF, 17. SMINS, and CURSUPP), students' science-related career expectations would first rise and then fall gradually. For these capitals, as we need to manage to make
them stay at an optimum range for getting the maximum outcome, we call them “optimumseeking” capitals or inverted-U-shaped capitals. The last pattern showed a negative relationship
between predictor (21. unfairteacher) and outcome. We name this kind of factor “impeding” or
decreasing capitals. It should be noted that, while the decreasing and increasing capitals show a
generally linear relationship with students' career aspirations, the S-shaped and inverted-Ushaped capitals might have moderation effects on or non-linear relationships with career aspirations. As students with different levels of these capitals, the increase of these predictors would
have a different effect on predicting students' career expectations. Table 4 presents how different forms of capital were labeled based on their ALE plots (Figure 2). It is interesting that the
regional analysis for Hong Kong presented qualitatively the same relationship patterns, which
supported the reliability of the global results. However, the different slopes displayed in the
Hong Kong analysis indicated the different effect sizes in specific contexts.

want to be the best whatever they do, MOTIVAT, 7th) and self-efficacy (SCIEEFF, 15th) in science, which is consistent with studies of Kang and Keinonen (2017) and Mujtaba et al. (2018).
This result supports prior researchers' claims that educators should take strategies like providing students opportunities to discuss socio-scientific issues (Gulacar et al., 2022) and conduct
inquiry-based learning (Kang & Keinonen, 2017), which can make students feel the relevance
and enjoyment of science, to enhance students' science aspirations. Besides, as ICT professionals were a kind of science-related career, ICT interest (INTICT) also ranked 13th and shared
similar importance with science self-efficacy in the global analysis.
It should also be noted that, while INSTSCIE and JOYSCIE were increasing capitals that
have an overall positive main effect, INTBRSCI, INTICT, and MOTIVAT showed a local positive relationship with career aspirations. The ALE plots indicated that regarding interest in
broad science topics and ICT and achieving motivation, it would be more effective to implement interventions on students with low-to-moderate levels for improving their career aspirations since the RF model was more sensitive to their changes within this interval (see
Figure 2 [4, 13, and 7]). Although these results generally echoed previous studies' findings
that achieving motivation (Yang & Gao, 2021) and interest in broad science topics (Cairns &
Dickson, 2021) and ICT (Liong et al., 2023) were positively linked to students' career expectations, our findings reveal a more complex relationship. Specifically, the ALE plots indicate
that having moderate achieving motivation is enough to carry science career aspirations.
Thus, educators do not have to make students too ambitious and want to be the best at whatever they do. Making students more interested in broad science topics may help them find
and build a science-related career, but being keen on “broad” everything is not the necessary
habitus of a prospective scientist.
SCIEEFF were inverted-U-shaped capitals, which indicated that students' science-related
career aspirations would first increase and then decrease gradually with the rise of science selfefficacy (see Figure 2 [15]). This result, on the one hand, supports previous researchers' findings
that students' self-efficacy was positively related to career aspirations (Sheldrake, 2020;
Uitto, 2014), as the curve for SCIEEFF generally increased. On the other hand, however, this
study indicates there may be a drop in students' career aspirations for the high self-efficacy
group (above 2 on the IRT-derived scale). One possible explanation is that, for students with
quite high efficacy, some of them may be overconfident (Sheldrake, 2016) or presumably also
be confident in other subjects; then, their career decisions were allowed to be guided by interests and values, but not limited by self-efficacy (Wang et al., 2017). Above all, the ALE plots of
capitals suggest the necessity of investigating the non-linear relationships between capitals and
career aspirations and their interaction effects with other factors.
As for what science you know, consistent with prior research, both the results of global
analysis and Hong Kong analysis supported that science literacy (PVSCIE, 5th) and math literacy (PVMATH, 8th) were key culture capitals for students' science-related career expectations
(see Table 4). It is common sense that having basic science and math literacy is required for
almost all science-related careers. The ALE plots of PVSCIE and PVMATH also show that they
are local booming (S-shaped) capitals in which their positive relationships with career expectations were more salient within the score range from about 400 to 650. The wide “booming”
range supports the positive relationships between literacy and career expectations found in previous studies (e.g., Cairns & Dickson, 2021; Holmes et al., 2018). The reason why the relationships between academic performances and career aspirations changed within different intervals

6.2 | Science-related behaviors and practice: What you do?
Among all the “what you do” variables, the global analysis identified students' experience
(PRESUPP, 6th) and present engagement (SCIEACT, 9th) in broad science activities
(e.g., reading books or watching TV programs about science, taking apart technical devices, and
attending science club) as strong indicators for students' career aspirations. Our findings reconfirmed the importance of providing students with opportunities to engage in science activities
during their childhood and middle school years (Cohen et al., 2021; Dou et al., 2019). Although
science dispositions and literacy may benefit from these activities, the high ranks indicate that
broad science activities have made other unique contributions to career expectations. One possible valuable capital students gain is knowledge about science and science careers (especially the
daily life of science-related occupations and the transference of science qualifications). As previous studies revealed, broad science activities provided opportunities for students to learn every
aspect of science so that students became more informed and were more likely to find an occupation they liked (Kitchen et al., 2022; Tomperi et al., 2022). Furthermore, the ALE plots show
that how engagement in broad science activities contributes to career expectations is not simply
the more, the better (see Figure 2 [6]). Future studies could investigate how to combine broad
science activities with formal science learning to maximize students' learning outcomes.
Learning time-related factors (17th, 22nd, 24th in Table 3) were found to be another set of
key factors of science-related career expectations. The global analysis indicated that both the
additional science learning time (ST071Q01NA, 22nd) and the in-school science learning time
(ST059Q03TA, 24th) ranked as key variables and were of very similar variable importance. This
may be attributed to the fact that students who have science career aspirations are more likely

included in our research, it was ICT use outside of school for schoolwork (HOMESCH) the

indicated that simply increasing students' exposure to ICT use (e.g., using ICT for leisure and
social interaction) is insufficient to enhance students' ICT or other science-related career expectations. Rather, effective integration of ICT into school learning is more impactful

appropriately designed, learning with digital devices helps students become more interested
and engaged in their work (Huang et al., 2021; Tofel-Grehl et al., 2017). Similar to the relationship between ICT use and academic achievement (Zhu & Li, 2022), the ALE plot (see Figure 2
[10]) also indicates a non-linear relationship between ICT use and career expectations. This plot
highlights the benefits of ICT use as an extension and complement of school learning but also
warns against the excessive use of ICT in schoolwork. To achieve optimal learning outcomes,
educators should take advantage of the complementarity between traditional teaching
approaches and ICT-based learning in different blended settings (Bhutoria & Aljabri, 2022).

6.3 | Science-related forms of social capital: Who you know?
In terms of social capital, the global analysis identified teachers and parents as the most imperative people “who you know (related to science).” Out of all the teacher-related factors (e.g., F42,
IBTEACH, inquiry-based teaching; F45, PERFEED, perceived teachers' feedback; F88,
EC012Q06NA, teacher recommend me to attend additional science instruction) that have been
included in this study, it is teachers' unfairness (unfairteacher, 21st, see Table 3) ranked among
the top 25 features. Unsurprisingly, the ALE plot indicates teachers' unfairness was negatively
related to students' career aspirations (see Figure 2 [21]). Previous studies have found that if students were unfairly treated by teachers (e.g., given less attention and encouragement, but
harsher discipline or even ridicule), they would have lower learning interest and engagement
(e.g., Molinari & Mameli, 2018), worse science performance achievement (Chen & Cui, 2020),
and involuntarily, do not identify with science (Penuel & Watkins, 2019). Thus, our result
claims the significance of teachers' alertness to their unfair behaviors in science teaching,

Concerning the characteristics of parents, the global analysis revealed the importance of
parents' expectations on their children (enter a science-related career, PA032Q03TA, 2nd; continue further science study, PA032Q05TA1, 14th), views on science (PQGENSCI, 12th), emotional support (EMOSUPS, 23rd), and economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS, BFMJ2,
hisei, and HISCED, 11th, 18th, 19th, and 25th, respectively, see Table 4). As previous studies
showed, parents' expectations and views on science would influence their children via daily
interaction (Caspi et al., 2020; DeWitt et al., 2013; Jones & Hamer, 2022; Simunovic &
Babarovic, 2020). If parents value science and expect their children to enter science-related disciplines, they will talk more about science with their children and provide them with more
encouragement and support to learn science (Cian et al., 2022; Muenks et al., 2020). Our study
reconfirmed the positive relationships between students' career aspirations and parents' value
of science (Caspi et al., 2020; Hernandez et al., 2016). Besides, our findings echoed prior
researchers' results that parents' emotional support (e.g., being interested in school activities,
supporting children's educational efforts and achievements, supporting students when they are
facing difficulties at school, and encouraging them to be confident) was beneficial for students'
school learning (Dotterer, 2022) and could strengthen their career expectations (Lv et al., 2022;
Starr et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020). Parents' emotional support is “sustainably supportive” capital, so literally, its importance for students' science-related learning and career expectations
(in which difficulties and failures might accompany the authentic science inquiry) cannot be
overemphasized.
Finally, our global analysis reconfirmed the importance of ESCS on students' career aspirations found in prior research (Liu & Schunn, 2020; Sheldrake, 2020). However, when explaining
the effect of ESCS-related factors, we must be cautious about their relationships with other factors. For example, parents' highest education and occupation status (BFMJ2, hisei, and
HISCED) are subscales of economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS), and their effects might
be partially overlapped with their views on science (PQGENSCI). Moreover, parents with
higher ESCS are more able to help students use ICT out of school for learning (HOMESCH),
engage in broad science activities (SCIEACT and PRESUPP) (Liu & Schunn, 2020), and develop
more informed scientific knowledge (Sheldrake, 2020; Zhang et al., 2019). Fortunately, parents'
expectations (PA032Q03TA, 2nd; PA032Q05TA1, 14th), views on science (PQGENSCI, 12th),
and emotional support (EMOSUPS, 23rd) were of the same or even more importance to these
unmalleable factors (ESCS, 11th, BFMJ2, 18th, hisei, 19th, and HISCED, 25th, see Table 4).

6.4 | The applicability of the global key factors and regional specifics
The comparison between global and regional results helps us distinguish between findings that
might be generally reliable and those that might be context specific. On one hand, the regional
analysis revealed that, even when modeling the contextual specifics for a particular region, most
(9 out of 10) key factors for Hong Kong students were within the 25 key factors identified in the
global analysis. Moreover, the similar relationship patterns observed in the global and regional
analyses support the generalizability of how resources relate to students' career expectations.
These results align with vocational theories, highlighting that some factors, such as interests
and perceived values, have cross-cultural influence, and certain relationship patterns of career
choice, such as literacy serving as a “critical filter,” are generally applicable (Fouad, 2007;

On the other hand, however, regional results also indicate that not all key capitals or
resources identified by the global analysis are essential or prevalent in a specific region. Contextual specifics were evidenced by the Hong Kong analysis. The different relative importance of
Hong Kong key capitals supports prior studies' findings that, while some factors (e.g., interest
and self-efficacy) are consistently related to students' science career aspirations, their effect sizes
may vary in different contexts (Guo, 2022; Sheu & Bordon, 2017). The distinct key features
observed in the Hong Kong analysis also echoed previous results that, some factors or resources
may be crucial in certain regions but not necessarily essential in other contexts (Du &
Wong, 2019). It is worth noting that, regarding the types of shared key capitals between global
and Hong Kong results, habitus and culture capitals (CC) seem to consistently appear as the
most dominant and generally applicable factors for students' career aspirations. While the main
science-related behaviors and practices (BP) remain relatively stable, the social capitals
(CC) can vary greatly. For instance, although the absolute ranking of the shared key factors differed significantly between the global and regional analyses, many conclusions drawn from the
relative importance of the global results were also applicable to the Hong Kong analysis for factors related to CC (e.g., perceiving science as relevant and interesting being the most important
among all the disposition factors) and BP (e.g., science experiences and learning times were
identified as key among all the science-related behaviors and practices). However, this was not
the case for SC, as the Hong Kong analysis highlighted different parental characteristics compared to the global results.
These findings help elucidate the mixed results regarding the similarity or difference of
influential factors for career aspirations in different regions (e.g., Du & Wong, 2019;
Guo, 2022; Sheu & Bordon, 2017). Our results suggest that when considering the generalizability or contextual specifics of science capital, it is necessary to discuss them by type. For
educators and researchers, our findings indicate that, to enhance students' career aspirations,
improving their CCs and habitus (e.g., science literacy and internal dispositions like perceiving science as valuable and interesting) is crucial and impactful. While promoting key
science-related behaviors and practices is generally effective (e.g., ensuring suitable learning
times and science activities), social capitals vary significantly depending on your context. Our
results suggested that teachers and parents were who you know affecting students' career
expectations, but the characteristics of parents and science teachers can vary in different
regions for different people.

decent prediction performance. That means, by getting information on these key capitals, we
can make reasonably accurate predictions of whether a student would like to have a sciencerelated career in the future, regardless of their countries or other backgrounds. This provides
strong empirical support for their effectiveness in predicting students' career expectations. Generally, our global findings indicate an optimistic scenario, as most key capitals are accessible

8 | L I M I T A T I O N S A N D I M P L I C A T I O N S F O R FU T U R E
STUDIES
This exploratory study has several limitations that might have implications for future studies.
First, although we used a large international sample to train our model and obtained a good
prediction performance, the causal relationship between capital factors and career expectations
cannot be concluded as the PISA 2015 data is cross-sectional. Future studies could validate the
effectiveness of the identified factors with longitudinal data. Second, albeit the RF algorithm
was suitable for our study, it is valuable to explore whether alternative powerful machine learning techniques would yield the same set of key capital factors while achieving a similar or even
better prediction performance. The techniques employed in this research offer insights derived
from the data; however, they did not explicitly test specific hypotheses. While our findings echoed with previous studies, we have not thoroughly examined the extent to which they may be
influenced by biases inherent to the algorithm or overfitting of our model. Future investigations
can employ alternative statistical methods to rigorously scrutinize the hypotheses derived from
this study. Third, due to our research scope, we only used the ALE plot to reveal the main
effects of factors on career aspirations. It is still unknown how key capital and habitus factors
interplay with each other in developing students' science-related career aspirations. By
employing adapted or novel data science techniques, future researchers can investigate the indirect, hierarchical, and interactive relationships between capital factors and career aspirations.
Fourth, this study investigated capital for broad science-related careers. Future studies can
focus on capital specific to different occupations. Finally, different countries have different economic and social cultures. Educators and researchers from different areas can explore the variations of key factors within their local contexts and compare them to the global situations found
in this study or with reference to other societies. By comparing the results from different areas,
we might gain deeper insights into the effects of science capital on science learning and career
aspirations.
