Understanding the relationship between teacher collaboration beliefs and
job satisfaction via collaborative attitudes: A moderated mediation model
of classroom self-efficacy from PISA 2022☆
Yang Zhang *, Denis Djekourmane ** , Yiheng Qin

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Teacher collaboration beliefs
Teacher job satisfaction
Teacher collaborative attitudes
Teacher classroom self-efficacy
Moderated mediation analysis

Job satisfaction is vital for teacher retention and educational quality. This study explores how teacher collaboration beliefs impact job satisfaction through the mediation of collaborative attitudes and the moderation of
classroom self-efficacy and gender. Using PISA 2022 data, including 40,349 respondents, the moderated mediation analysis showed no direct effect of collaboration beliefs on job satisfaction but revealed an indirect positive
link through collaborative attitudes. Collaborative attitudes were also strong indicators of job satisfaction.
Moreover, classroom self-efficacy and gender moderated these relationships. These results offer theoretical and
practical insights, emphasizing the importance of fostering collaborative attitudes and self-efficacy to enhance
job satisfaction.

Teacher job satisfaction has emerged as a central concern in
contemporary educational research, particularly in an era of rapid
technological change, increasing teacher retirements, global teacher
shortages, and persistent debates surrounding the professionalization of
teaching (UNESCO, 2023). As the primary agents of educational policy
implementation, teachers' satisfaction with their work and workplace
conditions critically shapes teaching quality, student learning outcomes,
and the overall effectiveness of education systems (Admiraal & Kittelsen
Røberg, 2023; Avola et al., 2025; OECD, 2010). In recent decades,
comprehensive empirical research has shown that teachers' job satisfaction has a substantial impact on burnout rates and their intentions to
remain in or exit the profession (Chen & Huang, 2022; Kaasila et al.,
2023; Santos et al., 2023). Furthermore, it is positively associated with
supportive school climates, student motivation, and educational equity.
Scholars have identified a wide range of determinants of teacher satisfaction, commonly categorized into individual, organizational, and societal domains. Individual factors include demographic attributes such
as gender, age, teaching experience, and self-efficacy (Cha et al., 2025;
Klassen & Chiu, 2010), as well as emotional exhaustion, perceptions of
the school climate (Atmaca et al., 2020; Schulze-Hagenest et al., 2023),
affective states, motivation, engagement (Al’Abri et al., 2022), and

overall well-being (Aziku & Zhang, 2024; Luque-Reca et al., 2022).
Organizational and contextual factors such as distributed leadership,
collegial relationships, workload, and opportunities for professional
development (Amorim Neto et al., 2017; Collie et al., 2024; Coppe et al.,
2022, 2024), along with societal variables including remuneration,
educational policy, and professional prestige, also play pivotal roles in
shaping teacher job satisfaction (Haerens et al., 2022; Toropova et al.,
2021).
In the extant literature, scholars have increasingly recognized the
importance of teachers' collaboration beliefs as a critical determinant of
job satisfaction (Bryk & Schneider, 2002; Conradsen et al., 2024; Dyk
et al., 2022; Hargreaves, 2012; Tsuyuguchi, 2023). Such beliefs form the
psychological and sociocultural foundation of collaboration, influencing
how teachers interact, share knowledge, and perceive professional relationships within schools (Hargreaves, 2019). A strong belief in
collaboration fosters open professional dialogue, social readiness,
work–life balance, and shared professional values, while also enhancing
teachers' capacity for stress management, flexibility, and sensitivity to
diversity and inclusion (Bati, 2024; Zakariya & Wardat, 2024). When
teachers trust their colleagues, they are more likely to engage in
collaborative planning, share instructional materials, exchange

Collaboration beliefs are also associated with positive emotions such as

Wang et al., 2025). Recent studies have advocated for team-based

a growing body of attention, previous studies often focused on a single or

psychological needs satisfaction (Hargreaves & O’Connor, 2018; Bai &

of PISA 2022, teacher job satisfaction was conceived as a multidimensional construct and includes satisfaction with the teaching profession
(SATTEACH) and satisfaction with the current job environment (SATJOB). To date, empirical evidence that considers the two dimensions in
explaining how teachers' beliefs about collaboration shape their satisfaction remains scarce. This points to the need for more nuanced and
global investigations. Our study integrates these dimensions and intends
to provide a more comprehensive perspective and achieve a more holistic understanding of teachers' satisfaction in their work.
Furthermore, teacher collaborative attitudes are considered a crucial
psychological construct in understanding job satisfaction. Social cognitive theory and expectancy-value theory suggest that beliefs in collaboration reflect teachers' deep cognition and value-oriented evaluations
regarding the advantages and efficacy of working with colleagues,
whereas collaborative attitudes encompass the emotional and behavioral dispositions through which these beliefs manifest in daily professional practice (Ajzen, 2005, 2024; Bandura, 1986; Eccles & Wigfield,
2020; Erdmann et al., 2023). In other words, beliefs signify the internal
conviction regarding the value of collaboration, whereas attitudes
convert this conviction into willingness, motivation, and proactive
participation in collaborative endeavors (Anselmann & Vetter, 2022;
Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Teachers with positive collaborative attitudes
are more likely to work together on lesson plans, group programs,
reflective dialogue, and collective problem-solving (Chien et al., 2018;
Salleh, 2016). Conversely, teachers with strong beliefs but weak
collaborative attitudes may experience cognitive dissonance or frustration due to interpersonal or organizational constraints, which can
reduce job satisfaction over time (Hart, 2014; S. Liu et al., 2025). Positive collaborative attitudes also boost perceived self-efficacy and
collegial support, contributing to higher job satisfaction (Zhang & Wang,
2022). Hargreaves and O’Connor (2018) highlighted how collaboration
beliefs and collaborative attitudes facilitate a shift from professional
collaboration to collaborative professionalism. The extant research has
also indicated that collaborative attitudes can serve as a psychological
bridge linking beliefs to job satisfaction (Cheng et al., 2023; Edinger &
Edinger, 2018; Kaminskiene et al., 2022). Although the previous study
has highlighted certain aspects of understanding, there is still room for
improvement. Social cognitive theory provides tools for understanding
how teachers' beliefs about collaboration can be translated into proactive attitudes that foster job satisfaction (Aricak et al., 2023; Bandura,
1986). This paper aims to enhance our understanding beyond simple
interpretation by testing collaborative attitudes as a mediating variable
between collaboration beliefs and job satisfaction.
In addition, based on Bandura's social cognitive theory, scholars have
noted that teachers' self-efficacy, as a motivational factor, is positively
related to teaching practices, including lesson planning, assessment
activities, classroom instruction, and communication with colleagues,
parents, and students. (Fives & Gill, 2015; Jang et al., 2023). Classroom

self-efficacy is considered to be the belief and confidence individual
teachers have about their capabilities to be efficacious in classroom
teaching and management, resulting in satisfaction (Huang et al., 2020;
Zhang, Cheng, & Wang, 2023). This notion implies that teacher classroom self-efficacy acts as a filter, a framework, and a guide, enabling
them to identify real issues related to student behavior, administration,
and the classroom ecosystem, and to implement appropriate
enquiry-based teaching methods that promote academic achievement
and job satisfaction (Ismayilova & Klassen, 2019; Perera et al., 2022a).
Furthermore, previous studies have also revealed that teachers with
high self-efficacy are more confident in their capacity to engage in
collaborative practices and are more likely to exhibit positive collaborative attitudes, which in turn enhance their proactive participation in
daily teaching activities (Buonomo et al., 2020; Kaya et al., 2024; Liu &
Yin, 2024). To further elucidate these more complex pathways, and
building upon the well-established importance of teachers' classroom
self-efficacy, the present study examines whether this construct moderates both the direct and indirect relationships proposed in the theoretical model linking teacher collaboration beliefs and collaborative
attitudes to job satisfaction.
The current study leverages data from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022, organized by the Organization
for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), to explore the
links between teacher collaboration beliefs and job satisfaction. Specifically, we first examine how collaboration beliefs relate to job satisfaction through collaborative attitudes, and second, we test whether this
relationship is moderated by teachers' classroom self-efficacy and
gender. The findings are expected to provide insightful empirical evidence for stakeholders and policymakers on supporting teachers’
collaborative attitudes, enhancing instructional effectiveness,
improving academic performance, and ultimately promoting teacher
satisfaction with their work and environment, which is essential for
retention and recruitment (Admiraal & Kittelsen, 2023). To achieve this
goal, we address the following two research questions.
Question 1. How do teacher collaboration beliefs relate to teacher job
satisfaction through collaborative attitudes?
Question 2. How does teacher classroom self-efficacy moderate both
the correlations between teacher collaboration beliefs and job satisfaction and collaborative attitudes and teacher job satisfaction?
1. Literature review
1.1. Teacher collaboration beliefs and job satisfaction
Teacher job satisfaction is known as the level of teachers' overall
positive emotional, cognitive, and affective evaluation of fulfilment
experienced towards their teaching profession and working environment (Buonomo et al., 2020). The components of job satisfaction
encompass both intrinsic and extrinsic factors. The intrinsic elements
include points such as a sense of achievement, autonomy in teaching,
and recognition of professional value, whereas the external factors
pertain to salary, school climate, leadership support, interpersonal relationships, student behaviors, and outcomes (Zhang, Cheng, & Wang,
2023; Zhang & Wang, 2022). By extension, teachers' satisfaction with
their job is often reflected in their actions and behaviors towards
instructional tasks, organizational commitment, emotional or social
well-being, and intention to leave or remain in the teaching profession
(Admiraal & Kittelsen Røberg, 2023; Wang, 2024; Wang et al., 2024;
Zakariya & Wardat, 2024; Zhang & Wang, 2022). As a multidimensional
psychological construct, teacher job satisfaction is considered by researchers a critical factor in shaping teachers’ motivation, performance,
and long-term career goals and development (Kaasila et al., 2023; Santos
et al., 2023; Yang & Ling, 2023). Satisfied teachers are more likely to
exhibit engagement, responsibility, and effectiveness. On the other

be emotionally drained, miss work, and leave (Atmaca et al., 2020;

a wide range of beneficial professional and institutional outcomes,
including higher teaching performance, increased commitment, stronger collaborative engagement, lower levels of stress and attrition, and
improved student learning, ultimately promoting sustainable school

the existing information is limited because researchers have tended to

through the psychological branch, like collaboration beliefs holds significant value at both the individual and group levels, influencing
teacher effectiveness, student learning outcomes, and overall school
improvement.
Teacher collaboration beliefs refer to instructors' cognitive and affective cognitive judgements and value orientations regarding the
legitimacy, utility, and professional effectiveness of colleagues to
improve partnerships, collaborative networks, teaching quality, and
learning outcomes (Avalos, 2011; Hargreaves, 2012; Harvey, 2008).
Teacher beliefs about collaborative networks and partnership relations
shape teachers' ideological perceptions, norms, values, empirical practices, and technical methods used in collaborative activities and thereby
influence improvements in teacher knowledge, organizational learning,
and holistic student growth (Muehlbacher et al., 2024). Beliefs in
collaboration also help teachers to make epistemic evaluations and
question the nature, the value, and the validity of teaching knowledge or
information presented (Buehl & Beck, 2014; Djekourmane et al., 2026;
Jelen, 1990). Collaboration beliefs include perceptions of how collaboration supports instructional improvement, collective problem-solving,
and shared professional responsibility (Liu & Yin, 2024; Zhang &
Wang, 2022). Scholars have revealed that the extension of collaboration
beliefs manifests through teachers' expectations of co-learning, mutual
support, constructive feedback, and material and knowledge sharing
among peers in various professional contexts (Liu et al., 2025). As a
foundational root of teachers' belief systems, collaboration beliefs play a
determinant role in the formation and transformation of teachers’ social
spaces and professional identities. With this nature, collaboration beliefs
influence whether teachers view collaboration as a meaningful and
worthwhile endeavor or unnecessary in case of disbelief (Conradsen
et al., 2024; Dyk et al., 2022; Haukås & Drange, 2024). This notion
implies that, when teachers acknowledge the value and necessity of a
collective workforce, they are more likely to engage in cooperative
initiatives, contribute to professional learning communities, and promote a collaborative school culture (Andersen & Jaeger, 2015; Hsiung
Chang et al., 2025). Thus, beliefs in collaboration function as a psychological impetus that fosters positive collegial relationships, cohesion,
and the professional development of educators.
Previous studies have demonstrated a close relationship between
teacher collaboration beliefs and their job satisfaction. For instance,
Edinger and Edinger (2018) found that teachers who strongly endorse
collaboration are more likely to report higher levels of professional
satisfaction. Similarly, De Weerdt et al. (2024) and Haukås and Drange
(2024) highlighted that trust and mutual reliance within a collaborative
teaching environment enhance both personal fulfilment and professional commitment. Hargreaves and O’Connor (2018) argued that
collaboration beliefs extend beyond formal activities such as data discussions or team meetings to shape teachers' everyday professional lives.
Furthermore, collaboration beliefs can also reduce isolation and
improve the affective and behavioral mechanisms associated with job
satisfaction. Specifically, when teachers perceive collaboration as
valuable, they are more likely to tend to demonstrate positive attitudes,
engage in supportive interpersonal relationships, and participate in open
dialogue with colleagues (Bati, 2024; Hargreaves, 2012, 2025; Martinez
et al., 2024; Vettori et al., 2022). These social and emotional dynamics

contribute to a sense of community and professional belonging, which
has been shown to buffer stress and reinforce commitment to teaching,
resulting in satisfaction (Muehlbacher et al., 2024). Bryk and Schneider
(2002) also emphasized that teachers who value ethical responsibility
and institutional leadership can build strong social bonds and gain
intrinsic rewards that enhance well-being. Recent empirical studies
further reinforce these findings. Liu et al. (2025) identified a significant
positive link between teachers' beliefs in collective effort and job satisfaction, while analysis of the PISA 2018 dataset by Jang et al. (2023)
revealed that collaboration positively predicts both job satisfaction and
self-efficacy among teachers in Korea. This substantial body of literature
points out the importance of teacher collaboration beliefs in fostering
professional relationships, strengthening collaborative school cultures,
and enhancing teacher well-being and job satisfaction—factors essential
for teacher retention (Santos et al., 2023). However, the complexity and
evolving nature of these relationships indicate that further investigation
is necessary to deepen understanding and inform strategies to address
challenges in the teaching profession.
1.2. Teacher collaboration beliefs , collaborative attitudes and job
satisfaction
Teacher collaborative attitudes constitute the manifestation of
teachers' internal beliefs about professional collaboration. Grounded
upon Bandura's social cognitive theory, teachers' core beliefs provide the
cognitive and value foundations that shape how they perceive and
interpret the meaning, purpose, and significance of collaborative practices (Almulla & Al-Rahmi, 2023; Lent et al., 1994; Aricak et al., 2023;
Bandura, 1986). These beliefs inform teachers' affective evaluations and
behavioral intentions towards collaboration, which collectively form
their collaborative attitudes (Abakah, 2023; Ajzen, 2005; Brandt &
Sleegers, 2021; Holzberger & Prestele, 2021). Scholarly, the construct of
teachers' collaborative attitudes comprises affective, cognitive, and
behavioral dimensions, all of which influence their motivation, willingness, and persistence in engaging in collaborative endeavors
(Buchholz & Hartig, 2019; van Vuuren et al., 2021). In practice, such
attitudes are reflected through daily professional activities, including
co-learning, co-teaching, collective lesson planning, peer mentoring,
and participation in professional learning communities (Ajzen, 2024;
Geletu & Mihiretie, 2023). Teacher collaborative attitudes thus operate
as affective and evaluative expressions of their underlying beliefs,
shaping their judgments of approval or disapproval and guiding their
collaborative behaviors (Sommer et al., 2023; van Vuuren et al., 2021).
In essence, positive collaborative attitudes yield substantial professional
and organizational value: they foster teachers' professional growth,
strengthen school organizational effectiveness, and enhance collegial
relationships and emotional support, thereby contributing to higher
levels of job satisfaction.
As discussed above, teacher collaboration beliefs play a foundational
role in shaping their collaborative attitudes. From a social cognitive
psychology perspective, individual teachers' beliefs about identity,
meaning, and social norms function as cognitive and affective foundations that influence how they interpret and evaluate professional interactions (Abakah, 2023; Brandt & Sleegers, 2021; Holzberger &
Prestele, 2021). Such beliefs act as interpretive filters that shape
teachers' perceptions of their social environment, often producing
shared attitudes among those who hold common professional ideologies
or moral principles (Leibovitch et al., 2025; Sawada et al., 2025). When
teachers believe that collaboration is integral to effective teaching,
student progress, subject innovation, and the fulfillment of their professional responsibilities, these beliefs guide their planning, experimentation, and pedagogical decision-making (Hargreaves, 2025). They
then tend to engage in reflective inquiry, collective learning, and
knowledge construction processes, adapting curriculum, pedagogy, and
educational practices to contextual needs. Moreover, belief in the value
and legitimacy of collaboration shapes teachers' affective dispositions

Bandura's social cognitive theory provides a comprehensive explanation for the relationship between teachers' collaboration beliefs,
collaborative attitudes, and job satisfaction. Teachers' beliefs about
collaboration represent pivotal cognitive structures that shape their
perceptions of professional practice (Ding et al., 2018; Turetsky et al.,
2021). However, such beliefs alone may be insufficient to directly influence job satisfaction unless they are accompanied by positive attitudes towards collaboration. Accordingly, empirical research has
consistently identified teachers' collaborative attitudes as key mediating
variable of the mechanisms linking beliefs to job satisfaction (Kaya et al.,
2024; Liu et al., 2021). Positive collaborative attitudes motivate teachers to engage in cooperative practices and to perceive collaboration as
beneficial rather than burdensome, thereby fostering stronger professional relationships, enhancing school functioning, and ultimately promoting greater job satisfaction (Liu & Yin, 2024). Moreover, previous
studies have demonstrated that trust-based relationships, the density of
academic ego networks, and collective problem-solving beliefs are
strong predictors of collaborative attitudes, which, in turn, increase
teachers' motivation, benefit student learning, and enhance overall
satisfaction (Cheng et al., 2023; Edinger & Edinger, 2018; Kaminskiene
et al., 2022). For instance, Chang et al. (2025) found that teachers'
positive beliefs and attitudes towards collegial interaction and teamwork significantly influence their sustainable career development, while
Li et al. (2025), drawing on survey data from 581 Chinese university
teachers, reported that perceived organizational support and
self-efficacy are closely related to job satisfaction. Similarly, Hendrickx,
Thurlings, and Den Brok (2025) highlighted that positive collaborative
attitudes contribute to healthier workplace climates and more
constructive communication, enhancing teachers' affective well-being.
Kõiv et al. (2019) further showed that teachers' psychological empowerment, reflecting sociocultural trust within schools, strengthens their
confidence in school leadership. Céspedes Carreño's (2024) recent study
revealed the opposite direction when it indicated a significant correlation between teachers' social well-being, teacher attitudes towards citizenship, and their trust in institutions. These findings suggest that
collaboration beliefs and attitudes are central psychological mechanisms
shaping teachers' satisfaction and workplace experiences, but they
remain inconsistent. Moreover, existing studies did not capture the
multidimensional and cross-cultural nature of these pathways, particularly through large-scale international datasets. To address this gap, the
present study seeks to provide both theoretical insights and empirical
evidence to inform targeted interventions and policy initiatives aimed at
strengthening teacher collaboration, fostering professional development, improving instructional quality and student outcomes, and ultimately enhancing teacher job satisfaction.

educational context, teacher classroom self-efficacy is commonly
defined as teachers' confidence in their ability to design and deliver
effective instruction that supports diverse student needs (Granziera &

a strong sense of self-efficacy enables teachers to cope more effectively
with instructional and behavioral challenges and is strongly associated
with supportive leadership, positive school culture, and productive

that teachers with higher classroom management self-efficacy typically
adopt constructivist epistemological beliefs, which contribute to the
enhancement of pedagogical content knowledge. Similarly, Granziera
and Perera (2019) highlighted that high self-efficacy fosters greater
professional commitment, a stronger sense of belonging, effective information sharing, collective reflection, and ultimately higher job
satisfaction.
In addition, self-efficacy is also critical for encouraging shared responsibility, collaborative work norms, student-centered instruction,
positive teacher-student relationships, and teacher well-being (Chan
et al., 2020; Perera et al., 2022b; Vangrieken et al., 2017). Empirical
studies also suggest a reciprocal relationship between teacher
self-efficacy and collaborative attitudes. For example, Von Suchodoletz
et al. (2018), in a longitudinal investigation of preschool teachers, found
that collaborative practices such as coaching and induction significantly
strengthened teachers’ self-efficacy over time. Similarly, a study by
Huang et al. (2024) involving 3095 lower secondary teachers in
Singapore revealed that teachers who demonstrated stronger
self-efficacy were more likely to endorse collaborative behaviors and
experience higher job satisfaction. Despite these significant insights,
recent research has not fully explored the moderating role of teacher
classroom self-efficacy in the relationship between collaboration beliefs,
collaborative attitudes, and job satisfaction. Given that self-efficacy influences how teachers interpret their capabilities in goal-setting,
instructional decision-making, assessment, and classroom management, it may shape the extent to which collaboration beliefs translate
into attitudes and ultimately teacher contentment. Consequently,
examining teacher self-efficacy as a moderating variable is crucial for
advancing theoretical comprehension and guiding interventions
designed to improve teacher collaboration and satisfaction.
1.4. The present research
Given the global teacher shortage and the growing need for shared
knowledge, responsibility, and decision-making in schools (OECD,
2018), understanding how teachers’ collaboration beliefs relate to their
job satisfaction has become increasingly important. However, to date,
there is a scarcity of recent studies that employ updated international
large-scale assessment data (ILSAs) to examine the relationship between
collaboration beliefs and job satisfaction through the mediating role of
collaborative attitudes and investigate whether teacher classroom
self-efficacy moderates these relationships. Addressing this gap offers a
valuable opportunity to enhance theoretical insights and inform
evidence-based interventions aimed at improving teacher well-being
(Huang et al., 2020). Furthermore, although prior research has examined gender differences in job satisfaction (Snow et al., 2021; Redmond
& McGuinness, 2019), we did not locate recent studies that use gender as
a moderating variable in the analyzed relationships.
Drawing on Bandura's social cognitive theory and previous empirical
work on teacher collaboration, the present study moves beyond
simplistic interpretations of collaboration to explore its deeper psychological mechanisms. Specifically, using data from the PISA 2022, this
study investigates how teacher collaboration beliefs impact two key
dimensions of job satisfaction—satisfaction with the teaching profession
(SATTEACH) and satisfaction with the current job environment

2. Methods
2.1. Data and analytical sample
The present study used a cross-nationally representative dataset of
teachers participating in PISA 2022. The PISA test is an age-based
assessment designed by the OECD for which the target population was
aged around 15 years old (Spaull, 2019). The PISA 2022 round included
the participation of 81 countries and economies. Generally, a list of the
15-year-old students of each sampled school was generated after the
school selection. For teachers, PISA randomly selected about 20 teachers
from the selected schools (OECD, 2023). For the missing values in the
dataset, we used pairwise deletions to clean them, and we included only
the non-missing pairs of variables in the analysis. The percentage of
missing values excluded from the analyzed variables ranged from 4.45 %
(teacher collaborative attitudes) to 21.22 % (teachers collaboration
beliefs), presented in Fig. 2. Consequently, the included analytic sample
was solely the well-matched 40,349 teachers and schools from the
teachers' questionnaires, which tested their subjective self-satisfaction
and teaching practices from 17 countries and districts (OECD, 2023).

2.3. Data analysis
To rationally handle our nested data structure, this study employed a
moderated mediation analysis, which is an approach when the impacts
of the independent variable on the dependent variable through mediating factors are considered to be moderated (Hayes, 2022). We
analyzed a sample including 40,349 participants from 17 countries/economies. Table 5 presents the list of all 17 countries/economies
and the percentage of participants in the sample.
Fist, we used CFA to ensure that our constructs were valid then, we
included the mediator and the moderating variable, based on our hypothesis and purposely tested the relationships between the latent variables. We also employed Monte Carlo bootstrapping test for the
confidence intervals (CIs) for all the links. We used STATA 15.1 for all
data preparation and analysis in this study. To test the CFA and
moderated mediation effects of the SEM, we used the adjusted chisquare statistics method and maximum likelihood estimation with
robust standard errors, considered a powerful multivariate data analysis
process in social sciences (Mueller, 1999). This process automatically
corrects standard errors and model fit indices, ensuring the results
remain robust and benefiting accuracy and efficiency when estimating
causal effects in the latent variables (Bollen, 1989; Hayes, Andrew 2022;
Mayer et al., 2016; Sengewald & Mayer, 2022).

3. Results

Table 7 presents the results of the correlations between different
variables tested to explore whether or not our latent constructs are
suffering from the effects of multicollinearity or the linear relation
among two or more variables (Olaluwoye et al., 2025). The pairwise
correlation between the key variables shows positive relationships,
meaning that as one variable's standard deviation increases, so does the
other. Teacher collaboration beliefs and teacher classroom self-efficacy
exhibit the highest correlation value (r = 0.2298), indicating a weak to
moderate association. The other relationships are relatively weak, with
values ranging from 0.1140 to 0.2046, indicating limited linear relationships between these variables. Though there is a lack of standardized diagnostic tools for multicollinearity, or the linear relation
among two or more variables, and the consensus among scholars
regarding accepted threshold value, researchers tended to admit that for
the variance inflation factor (VIF), cutoff values ranging from 2 to 10
should be considered tolerable or acceptable (Kim, 2019; Schroeder
et al., 1990). For the pairwise correlations test, researchers suggested
that, if threshold collinearity is between 0.5 and 0.7, the threshold is
suitable; if this value is higher, it may suggest the existence of multicollinearity concerns (Dormann et al., 2013; Otse et al., 2025).

4. Discussion
In this study, we found that there is no direct correlation between
teacher collaboration beliefs and teacher job satisfaction. However, the
relationship exists indirectly through the mediating role of the teacher's
collaborative attitudes. We further reveal that the effect of teacher
collaboration beliefs on teacher job satisfaction is better when teachers
possess a higher level of collaborative attitudes and self-efficacy about
classroom practice. Moreover, teacher gender played a significant
moderating role between the focal input antecedent variables and the
output variables, with a more pronounced benefit for women. The
findings align with existing literature (Hargreaves, 2012; Orakci et al.,
2023; Redmond & McGuinness, 2019; Zhang, Cheng, & Wang, 2023).
These results also provide a more holistic understanding of the intersecting links between teacher collaboration beliefs and teacher job
satisfaction.
As a result of globalization, continuous space compression, rapid
technological advancement, rising uncertainty and complexity in education systems, and persistent issues related to teacher shortages,
retention, and well-being, scholars have emphasized the importance of
teachers' beliefs in working collaboratively and collectively to solve
problems (Briskin et al., 2009; Hargreaves, 2025). Such collaboration is
considered essential not only for meeting the ongoing demand for professional development but also for improving the quality of instruction
and student engagement, ultimately leading to greater student success
and satisfaction (Hargreaves & O’Connor, 2018; Gutu Sakketa, 2023;
Hargreaves, 2019; Hermans & Dimaggio, 2007; UNESCO, 2020). Based
upon the previous research that closely looks at the relationships of
teacher collaboration beliefs and job satisfaction, our findings revealed
that teacher collaboration beliefs are not directly associated with job
satisfaction, but the link is supported by teacher attitudes in collaboration and their self-efficacy. This finding aligns with social cognitive
theory, which believed that individuals' self-efficacy and attitudes are
pivotal in converting their beliefs into behavioral, cognitive, and
non-cognitive outcomes (Bandura, 1997; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020;
Granziera & Perera, 2019). The result implies that teachers who promote cooperation but have negative attitudes towards colleague interaction or believe they are ineffective in collaborative settings may
experience decreased job satisfaction. This also further underscores the
intermediary function of psychological elements in influencing
work-related teacher effectiveness and satisfaction outcomes.
Teachers may encounter challenges such as isolation, inadequate
support from school administrators, loneliness, and a diminished sense
of belonging and satisfaction. This empirical and statistical evidence

self-recognition with the collectivist culture that prioritizes collective
effort, sharing knowledge, kind interactions, and teamwork with colleagues and peers, which impacts their satisfaction (Hsiung et al., 2025).
In particular, our results show that teacher collaborative attitudes

partially mediate the link between their collaboration beliefs and their
job satisfaction, making up about 12 % of the total effect of their belief in
working together. These positive, intricate interplays suggest the
transformative force of fostering a collaborative attitudes as motivational behaviors among instructors, helping them to set expectations and
values for achievement-related performance and choice (Eccles & Wigfield, 2023). This also implies that interventions or policies that target
the enhancement of teacher job satisfaction should not only focus on the
psychological and abstract beliefs in collaboration but also purposely
consider the behavioral attitudes necessary for effective collaboration
and satisfaction. In other words, if teacher collaboration beliefs act as
the ground, teacher attitudes towards collaboration serve as the vehicle
(Conradsen et al., 2024; Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975; Orakci et al., 2023),
which job satisfaction may be considered the resulting outcome within
an educational ecosystem where interdependence, collective goals, and
shared responsibility are valued (Edinger & Edinger, 2018; Lee & Shin,
2017). Educational reformers and stakeholders should foster an environment that encourages, internalizes, and integrates collaborative
confidence in teaching practices.
This examination of the indirect pathways provides more theoretical
insights on the mechanism trough which teacher collaboration beliefs
can contribute to the betterment of their job satisfaction. While collaboration beliefs are found to not impact teacher job satisfaction directly,
the findings demonstrated that it is also beneficial by improving their
attitudes on instructional collaboration and capacities, which subsequently enhances teaching job satisfaction. Because scholars have
demonstrated that if predictor variables do not have a linear effect on
the predicted variables, the connection may depend on another variable
in the model or be conditional upon it (Hayes, 2022; Shmueli et al.,
2019). Therefore, the findings suggest that school leaders should still
pay more attention to affording sufficient possibilities and opportunities
for teacher development, advantageous collaboration beliefs, and attitudes that lead to positive effects on job satisfaction (Yoon & Kim, 2022).
Our analysis also finds that the direct and indirect connections between teacher collaboration beliefs and teacher job satisfaction are
amplified or weakened when teachers possess a high level of classroom
self-efficacy. Specifically, when teachers have confidence, and belief in
collaboration inside their working ecosystem, and when they feel not
being isolated and detached from others, not prisoned in solitude and
loneliness, past studies reported that this drive to the adoption of
constructive behaviors and practices resulting in positive outcomes and
satisfactions (Friedman, 2003; Vangrieken et al., 2017). Collaboration
beliefs, such as trust, joining the workforce and collective effort
commitment, is essentially embodied in the teaching profession settings,
and it is more effective when teachers exhibit a significant amount of
self-efficacy in the classroom (Duan et al., 2024). This implies that when
teachers possess a high level of self-efficacy and engagement toward
their classroom teaching, they are likely to derive more benefit from
collaboration beliefs to job satisfaction than from conducting the link
between collaborative attitudes and job satisfaction. In other words,
when teacher classroom self-efficacy weakens the positive relationship
between teacher collaborative attitudes and teacher job satisfaction, it
could be because highly self-efficacious teachers rely more on their
personal capabilities and less on collaborative attitudes for professional
achievement, reducing its impact on job satisfaction.
Conversely, teachers with a low level of classroom self-efficacy may
derive greater advantage from collaborative attitudes, since it offers
support and knowledge-sharing that directly bolster their confidence
and job satisfaction. Regarding these findings, we suggest that educational administrators and stakeholders develop teachers’ psychological
motivation and confidence in their classroom teaching through supportive policies and interventions. Some possible strategies are teacher
induction programs, shared leadership, autonomy, and shared responsibilities to encourage teachers to work together more effectively
and boost their classroom self-efficacy, which is a key factor in job
satisfaction (OECD, 2024; Poulou et al., 2019).

Interestingly, our study's results also reveal that gender significantly
moderated the relationship between teacher collaboration beliefs and
job satisfaction with notorious variations. In detail, compared to male
teachers, teacher collaboration beliefs exhibit a stronger positive association with job satisfaction among female teachers. Likewise, collaborative attitudes show a positive effect on job satisfaction for both females
and males when conditioned with gender as a background variable,
further underscoring the gap in job satisfaction between genders, though
the coefficients are negligible. The findings are in agreement with the
gender-based job satisfaction literature (Andrade et al., 2021; Redmond
& McGuinness, 2019). The findings imply that female teachers derive
greater job satisfaction from collaboration beliefs and collaborative attitudes than their male counterparts. Theoretically, the finding aligns
with existing literature indicating that women often place higher value
on intrinsic job characteristics, enjoyable work, and relational and
collegial aspects in their working environments and tend to show greater
job satisfaction than men, who value extrinsic aspects such as salary and
bonus pay or promotion in their work (Grönlund & Öun, 2018; Redmond
& McGuinness, 2019). For female educators, collaboration may serve as
an existential affirmation of belonging and mutual growth resulting in
satisfaction, whereas for male teachers, professional satisfaction may
draw more from individualistic or personal perception (Kifle & Hailemariam, 2012). The results call for reflection on the philosophical nature
and complex understandings of the teaching profession as embodied not
only in knowledge production and transmission but in shared beliefs and
attitudes.
Practically, the study provides valuable insights into gender-based
job satisfaction, enabling educational systems and organizations to
transcend universal approaches and adopt a more nuanced, tailored, and
human-centered perspective on professional well-being and satisfaction
(Byrd & Sparkman, 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Zhang & Wang, 2022).
Moreover, comprehending teacher collaboration beliefs and attitudes
interacting with gender in fostering job satisfaction helps address
sub-aspects related to satisfaction like a safe working environment,
remuneration, workload, the chance of promotion, and developing a
sense of belonging and achievement (Miao et al., 2017; Redmond &
McGuinness, 2020). Fostering collaborative environments may also be
particularly effective in avoiding male-dominated conception, segregation, and discrimination in the labor market while prioritizing equality
in the teaching profession with the contented female and male worker.
These results underscore the importance of gender-responsive job
satisfaction in the context of educational leadership, as well as in beliefs
and attitudes related to teacher collaboration.
5. Limitation and future directions
Despite its contributions, the current study has some limitations
worth acknowledging, and further research would better our understanding of teacher collaboration belief, teacher job satisfaction, teacher
collaborative attitudes, and teacher classroom self-efficacy by considering these limitations. First, the study is based on the self-reported
quantified teacher variables from PISA 2022. There is a possibility
that teachers overestimate their perceptions of job satisfaction, which
makes the interpretation challenging regarding the objectivity and efficacy of the data. We suggest that future studies could utilize additional
qualitative methods, like observation and the collection of class diaries,
to test teacher job satisfaction more holistically. Moreover, researchers
could incorporate other stakeholders’ perceptions to gain more insight
about teacher job satisfaction. Second, we were unable to interpret our
results as causal inference findings due to the cross-sectional nature of
our data. We also suggest that future research integrate longitudinal and
experimental data to examine further research on teacher collaboration
beliefs and teacher job satisfaction. Third, past studies suggested that
there is no confusion between beliefs and attitudes, which supports our
arguments for developing collaboration beliefs and collaborative attitudes constructs; researchers also underlined the continuous ambiguity
12

between the two concepts (Ajzen, 2005; Boutyline & Vaisey, 2017).
Further, a qualitative approach or field studies are needed by integrating
data from interviews and observations of behavior to capture the
interaction between collaboration beliefs and collaborative attitudes.
Finally, the research insights are driven by collaborative attitudes as a
mediating variable moderated by classroom self-efficacy and gender.
Future studies would expand our understanding by including more
conditioning variables at the teacher level, like teaching experiences,
age, level of education, ethnic group, and rural or urban factors. Despite
these limitations, the study is still meaningful for its theoretical and
practical contributions to the explored topic.

6. Conclusions

References

This study examines the correlation between teacher collaboration
beliefs and teacher job satisfaction, as well as teacher collaborative attitudes. The study acknowledges the importance of teacher collaboration beliefs in improving job satisfaction through the development of
collaborative attitudes and self-efficacy in the classroom. We also
showed that teacher classroom self-efficacy and teacher gender play
crucial mediating roles in the relationships between teacher collaboration beliefs, collaborative attitudes, and job satisfaction. For gender, we
have revealed that, when conditioning it with collaboration beliefs and
collaborative attitudes, female teachers show greater job satisfaction
compared to their male colleagues. These results have both theoretical
and practical implications for a more profound understanding of the
critical role of teacher collaboration beliefs in fostering job satisfaction.
With this cross-national data, these findings provide accurate, comparable, timely relevant, and robust international comprehension. Based
on this evidence, we underscore that school leaders and policymakers
adopt a collaborative framework in leadership and organization and
provide sufficient assistance to teachers by supporting their active and
effective engagement in collaborative confidence and behaviors. In so
doing, the efforts are likely to enhance collaborative attitudes, classroom
self-efficacy, and ultimately teacher job satisfaction. Moreover, interventions aimed at improving beliefs in collaboration, collaborative
behaviors, and job satisfaction should take into account the significant
role of teacher gender to ensure balance in job satisfaction. Our study's
findings have the potential for developing targeted professional development and teacher training programs, integrating educational technologies, and augmenting teachers' positive attitudes and collaboration
beliefs to make schools more receptive and attractive to retain teachers,
which is crucial for educational success and satisfaction. The study's
insights also advance our knowledge from a conceptual perspective
about the process and extent to which teacher collaboration beliefs
contribute to teacher job satisfaction and the conditions that amplify or
diminish the pathways. Overall, the current study offers a comprehensive international analysis of teachers' collaboration beliefs, their
collaborative behaviors, and their job satisfaction to assist countries and
economies in reviewing or developing policies that foster effective
strategies and interventions, ensuring that teachers are equally content
with both their work and work environment.
