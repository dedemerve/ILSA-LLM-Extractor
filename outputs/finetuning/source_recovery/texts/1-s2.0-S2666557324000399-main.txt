Pre-service English teachers‘ approaches to technology-assisted teaching 
and learning: Associations with study level, self-efficacy and value beliefs 
Andreas Hülshoff *, Regina Jucks 
University of Münster, Institute of Psychology in Education and Instruction, Fliednerstraße 21, 48149 Münster, Germany   
A R T I C L E I N F O  
Keywords: 
Media in education 
Teacher professional development 
A B S T R A C T  
Information and communications technology (ICT) plays an important role in teaching and learning English as a 
Foreign Language (EFL) at school. However, there are still relevant research gaps regarding (prospective) 
teachers’ attitudes and approaches towards ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning. The present study examined 
possible different groups of pre-service teachers based on patterns regarding their approaches to ICT-assisted EFL 
teaching and learning and associations with study level and value and self-efficacy beliefs based on a sample of 
pre-service EFL teachers from various universities in North Rhine-Westphalia (Germany). Results from a cluster 
analysis identified three distinct clusters of pre-service teachers who differed significantly in their transmissive 
and constructivist approaches to ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning. Cluster allocation varied significantly 
depending on participants’ study level. Further cluster comparisons also partly indicated significant associations 
between participants’ transmissive and constructivist beliefs and their value beliefs regarding ICT-assisted EFL 
teaching and learning. Participants’ self-efficacy beliefs regarding ICT-assisted EFL teaching did not vary 
significantly between different clusters of pre-service teachers. Possible implications are discussed conclusively.   
1. Introduction 
Although by no means an end in itself or a guarantee for high quality 
teaching and learning, the integration of information and communica­
tions technology (ICT) into teaching and learning is essential in an 
increasingly digitized world [31,54,91,92] and has putatively been 
further catalyzed since the (emergency) remote teaching during the 
COVID-19 pandemic [44]. However, while matters of ICT-assisted 
teaching and learning are often primarily approached in a rather 
generic way (see e.g. [103] for an overview), it is also important to 
consider subject-specific particularities because the use of ICT for 
teaching and learning may vary between disciplines [41,42]. For 
example, the (school) subject of English as a Foreign Language (EFL) has 
a special relationship with digitization: As a lingua franca, English plays 
an important role for globalization [2,69] and therefore also for tech­
nological development. Even more importantly in the present context, 
ICT offers novel opportunities for making and maintaining intercultural 
contact [12,59] as well as for developing and enhancing functional 
communicative skills in the context of EFL teaching and learning [90, 
112]. 
In general, it has been shown that many teachers learn how to 
integrate ICT into teaching and learning on the job and/or through 
formal in-service professional development activities but many teachers 
still report that they do not feel well prepared for ICT-assisted teaching 
[78]. All the more it is important that proper preparation for 
ICT-assisted teaching already starts during initial teacher education [43, 
54]. This requires not only promoting prospective teachers’ competence 
for ICT-assisted teaching but also taking a closer look at pre-service 
teachers’ attitudes and beliefs regarding the use of ICT for teaching 
and learning since such or related views have shown to be strong pre­
dictors of their prospective use of ICT for such purposes [87,88,97]. 
Findings from previous research suggest that there are different clusters 
of pre-service teachers regarding their views on ICT-assisted teaching (e. 
g. [88]). Prospective teachers’ study level should be taken into account 
here, too, as pre-service teachers’ views and knowledge on ICT-teaching 
have shown to evolve during teacher education [9,48]. Moreover, 
findings from previous research indicated that profiles of teachers based 
on their actual use of ICT for teaching at a later professional stage 
(in-service teaching) may be interlinked with their more general trans­
missive and constructivist approaches towards teaching and learning 
[106]. While generally there is research on pre-service EFL teachers’ 
views on ICT-assisted teaching (e.g., [37,118]), the existing research

1.1. ICT integration in EFL teaching and learning in the school context 
Ever since the nineteenth century, teaching and learning English as a 
foreign language has been an inherent part of key school subjects in 
many countries [45]. In Germany, the important role of the English 
language as a lingua franca [5,13] is inter alia reflected in the curricular 
embedding of EFL already in primary education [74] and in a broad 
range of offers regarding content and language integrated learning 
(CLIL) in secondary education [10]. According to the Common European 
Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFRL), central goals of EFL 
teaching and learning include the promotion of students’ methodolog­
ical and other interdisciplinary skills as well as their functional 
communicative 
and 
intercultural 
competence [17]. 
In North 
Rhine-Westphalia (Germany), where the present study was performed, 
the respective statewide regulations (e.g., [73,75]) are largely based on 
these goals, which are again further specified through school-specific 
curricula. 
These goals are based on several major linguistic and cultural para­
digm shifts from the past few decades, such as moving from prescriptive 
to descriptive grammar as well as moving from regional studies to 
intercultural competence [11,19,115]. This has led to increased atten­
tion toward students’ effective opportunities to practice speaking 
foreign languages in the classroom as well as to more inductive didac­
tical approaches toward the exploration of linguistic structures and 
regularities and to an increased importance of intercultural encounters 
in the context of EFL teaching and learning. Such goals may be effec­
tively achieved with ICT, e.g., by facilitating access to content from 
Anglophone contexts through online resources as well as new opportu­
nities with regard to making and maintaining intercultural contact [12, 
59]. For instance, eTwinning programs may contribute relevantly to EFL 
students’ development of global competence [12]. Moreover, digital 
tools or strategies can be helpful for developing and enhancing func­
tional communicative skills [90,112]. For example, e-mail exchange 
programs with people who learned English as a first language may help 
improve EFL students’ scores in grammar, vocabulary and accuracy 
[90]. Also, findings by Tsai [112] indicated that the use of digital ap­
plications based on augmented reality (AR) and automatic speech 
recognition (ASR) technologies may positively contribute to the devel­
opment of the speaking- and listening-related communicative skills of 
EFL learners. At the same time, challenges that potentially arise from 
such new opportunities (e.g., matters of digital security; critical recep­
tion of digital content and devices), also need to be considered in the 
school context [86]. 
Regarding the specific area of foreign language teaching, results from 
the 2018 International Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS) 
showed that the share of year-8 students in Germany who reported that 
ICT was used in at least some of their lessons per week (42.6 %) was well 
below the international average (69.2 %) [98]. On top of that, according 
to students’ self-reports, ICT was used more often in school subjects such 
as computer science (60.3 %), in the humanities and social sciences 
(47.9 %) and in science (47.6 %) than in foreign languages (42.6 %) in 
the German ICILS-2018-subsample. Lower frequencies of ICT use were 
found in subjects such as German (38.7 %) and mathematics (31.2 %) in 
the German ICILS-2018-subsample [98]. Earlier results from a study by 
Howard et al. [42], however, suggest that this might depend on the 
specific didactical purpose the use of ICT refers to. Their findings e.g. 
showed that ICT was used less in the subject area English for the purpose 
“data and visualization” (which might be interpreted as more closely 
associated with transmissive teaching and learning) than in the subject 
areas mathematics and science by the teachers in their sample from 
Australia. However, ICT was e.g. used relevantly more often in English 
than in mathematics for the purpose of working collaboratively (which 
might be interpreted as more closely interlinked with constructivist 
teaching and learning), while the difference regarding this purpose be­
tween English and science was rather small [42]. 
1.2. (Pre-service) teachers’ (ICT-related) attitudes 
(Pre-service) Teachers’ attitudes are a crucial prerequisite for the 
successful integration of ICT in EFL teaching and learning, since they 
have been shown to be relevant predictors of their teaching and 
teaching-related intentions and in this regard also potential (indirect) 
predictors of their students’ learning gains and motivation [23,40,65, 
100,101,106]. (Prospective) Teachers’ attitudes (or beliefs) can be 
interpreted as individual, mental and rating appraisals of intellectual 
constructs (see also [71]). They comprise cognitive but also affective 
aspects and often are rather implicit in character [20]. Attitudes tend to 
be fairly stable [30], but they can change [72]. Further, attitudes are not 
necessarily consistent and coherent. In fact, they often tend to be rather 
quasi-logical [1] and, therefore differ – despite significant correlations 
that can often be found – from the concept of knowledge [76]. 
Research on learning and instruction in schools and on teacher ed­
ucation, as well as in higher education, often predominantly focuses on 
(pre-service) teachers’ attitudes with regard to their approaches to 
teaching [81,110]. A prominent distinction between teaching ap­
proaches is often made between transmissive or teacher-focused (some­
times also referred to as “traditional”) approaches and constructivist 
(conceptual change) or student-focused approaches [49,80,81]. These 
styles mainly differ in the respective proportion of teacher and student 
activity in the context of teaching and learning processes: Transmissive 
teaching styles primarily focus on teaching activities and the trans­
mission of declarative knowledge [51,81,111]. By contrast, primarily 
constructivist or student-focused teaching more strongly encourages 
students’ active and explorative learning processes [51,81]. 
The distinction between such approaches is a matter of didactics, but 
it also implies certain epistemic assumptions: Primarily student-focused 
approaches are often based on the idea that knowledge is constructed by 
people – individually or collaboratively within social groups [83]. Ac­
cording to such approaches, learning environments should enable stu­
dents to learn in an inquiry-based and problem-solving manner, and 
learning paths should ideally remain as undetermined as possible be­
forehand [83]. Here, the teacher serves as “learning facilitator” ([83], p. 
1353, see also [84]). By contrast, more traditional teacher-focused ap­
proaches center on the concept of teachers as “knowledge transmitters” 
[83,84]. 
Yet, the use of these approaches may also rely on the nature of 
respective subjects [80]. So-called “soft domains” such as the humanities 
and social sciences are often said to be shaped more strongly by 
constructivist approaches, whereas “hard domains,” such as mathe­
matics and science, tend to lend themselves to transmissive approaches 
[80]. However, such distinctions between disciplines are not always 
straightforward as the research findings by Howard et al. [42] on the use 
of ICT by English, mathematics and science teachers in Australia which 
we referred to in Section 1.1 of the present paper suggest. 
All the more it is important to note that transmissive and construc­
tivist teaching styles do not necessarily rule out one another: (Pre-ser­
vice) Teachers may, for example, generally prefer student-oriented 
approaches, but they may at times still be drawn to teacher-focused 
methods and in that – at least partly – agree with both approaches 
[49]. Therefore, (pre-service) teachers’ attitudes toward transmissive 
approaches, on the one hand, and toward constructivist styles, on the 
other, are often considered two distinct phenomena rather than two 
poles of one and the same phenomenon [7,82]. Although remarkably on 
the rise in the past few decades, arguments in favor of constructivist 
teaching and learning should not be absolutized, since open,

Also relevant to this area of inquiry is the domain of (pre-service) 
teachers’ ICT-related value beliefs [55,89], since positive attitudes in 
this area are relevant predictors for the use of ICT in schools and 
classrooms [4,14,79,104]. Following expectancy-value theory [25], 
Quast et al. [89], for example, distinguished between interest, utility, 
relevance and assumed costs as four key dimensions of teachers’ atti­
tudes toward the use of ICT in contexts of learning and instruction. 
Initial findings indicate that the effective integration of digital media in 
the classroom is more strongly driven by the perceived value of ICT 
rather than hindered by its assumed costs [89]. 
Along with value beliefs, another aspect to consider in this context is 
(pre-service) teachers’ self-efficacy beliefs with regard to ICT-assisted 
teaching and learning. According to Bandura [6], self-efficacy refers to 
individuals’ beliefs concerning their capability to plan and carry out 
certain actions in order to reach subjectively important goals, which, in 
turn, has impacts on decision-making, effort and perseveration. Specif­
ically, with regard to teachers’ integration of ICT in learning and in­
struction, 
Doll and Meyer [21] distinguished 
between four 
self-efficacy-related domains, namely learning-related knowledge 
(about digital media), technical knowledge (about digital media), 
diagnosing with digital media/digital diagnosing and teaching with 
digital media/digital teaching, refining the “Computer Technology 
Integration Survey” [116]. In general, self-efficacy beliefs often corre­
late with individuals’ skills, but self-efficacy beliefs and (perceived) 
competence may also be (partly) discrepant [46,93] and, beyond that, 
can be affected by mastery and failure, feedback as well as affective and 
physiological states ([6,85]; see also [21]). Prior empirical evidence has 
indicated that positive self-efficacy beliefs or similar constructs are 
specifically relevant for both EFL teaching and learning [15] as well as 
for ICT-assisted teaching and learning [32,89,96]. 
From a general (i.e. non-subject specific) perspective, findings from 
prior research have shown that pre-service teachers often hold rather 
positive views on ICT-assisted teaching and acknowledge the signifi­
cance of the integration of ICT into teaching and learning [33]. Findings 
from subject-specific research imply that this seems to hold true for the 
realm of EFL in particular, too [118] – especially when specific training 
and preparation is provided [35]. Moreover, in comparison with 
in-service teachers, prospective teachers often tend to rate their own 
skills for ICT-assisted teaching higher [64], which might be due to an 
overall higher familiarity with ICT in younger generations but also an 
expression of overestimating their skills (see also [87]) due to a rele­
vantly lower amount of school-practical experience. Earlier findings by 
Yüksel and Kavanoz [118], in turn, showed that pre-service EFL teachers 
in particular do not necessarily feel confident to fully live up to the 
potentials of ICT when teaching. 
1.3. Clustering or profiling approaches and associations between 
approaches to teaching, study level and ICT- and classroom-related value 
and self-efficacy beliefs 
Clustering or profiling approaches etc. can be powerful tools that 
contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of phenomena in 
(prospective) teachers’ professional practices and development and they 
have been applied to the realm of ICT in education many times (e.g. [22, 
44,48,56,61,63,99,102,107–109,113]). In a recent study that focused – 
as the present research does – on initial teacher education in Germany, 
Pozas et al. [88] examined possible clusters of pre-service teachers based 
on participants’ attitudes towards ICT, their ICT self-concept, their ICT 
self-efficacy and their concerns about ICT. Based on this approach, two 
distinct and opposed clusters of pre-service teachers were identified: a 
“Can-do-ICT 
type” 
(cluster 
1) 
and 
a 
“Discouraged-ICT 
type”/”Possibly-can’t-do-ICT type” (cluster 2). Pre-service teachers of 
the “Can-do-ICT type” reached relevantly higher values regarding their 
ICT-related attitudes, self-concept and self-efficacy and relevantly lower 
values regarding ICT-related concerns than pre-service teachers of the 
“Discouraged-ICT type” [88]. However, the study did not explicitly 
involve the examination of pre-service teachers’ beliefs in the in the 
context of transmissive and constructivist or related paradigms. A study 
including a clustering approach comprising such or at least very closely 
related (and other) factors is one by Vettori et al. [114]. They identified 
three distinct profiles of pre-service teachers, namely (1) “adaptive view 
on learning”, (2) “lacking self-determination and anxiety” and (3) “lack 
of metacognitive approach”. However, this study does not specifically 
refer to ICT-assisted teaching and learning. A profiling approach based 
on teachers’ approaches to teaching in the context of ICT-assisted 
teaching and learning can be found in a study by Tondeur et al. [106], 
who explored teachers’ educational belief profiles in the context of 
ICT-assisted teaching in Belgium. Their study referred to the concept of 
constructivist and traditional teaching approaches, identifying four 
distinct profiles based on a sample of 574 (in-service) elementary school 
teachers in Flanders: (1) teachers with a constructivist and traditional 
teaching profile, (2) teachers with a constructivist teaching profile, (3) 
teachers with a traditional teaching profile and (4) teachers with an 
undefined profile [106]. However, this study refers to in-service teach­
ers. Moreover, none of these three studies [88,106,114] refers to EFL 
teaching and learning in particular. 
Examining possible groups of pre-service teachers in the context of 
ICT-assisted teaching based on patterns regarding transmissive and 
constructivist beliefs with an EFL-specific focus, the present study thus 
addresses an important research gap. At the same time, a closer look at 
the potential interrelatedness between (pre-service) teachers’ attitudes 
and beliefs regarding ICT-assisted teaching and their transmissive and 
constructivist approaches is required: Constructivist teaching and 
learning styles have often been assumed to be positively associated with 
the classroom use of ICT [26,77,83]. There is a large body of empirical 
evidence supporting this assumption (e.g. [8,38]), inclusive of literature 
specifically focussing on EFL teaching (e.g. [36]). A reason for this might 
be the potential of the classroom use of ICT for students’ individual or 
collaborative learning and designing their own learning paths [83]. 
Respective approaches to teaching are therefore likely to correlate 
positively with teachers’ or prospective teachers’ attitudes towards 
ICT-assisted teaching since those are usually closely connected with 
actual practices of ICT-assisted teaching (e.g. [40]).This is likely to 
comprise both, value and self-efficacy beliefs, which have been assumed 
to be positively associated with one another since positive value beliefs 
are likely to result in higher effort leading to higher achievement and 
more positive views on one’s respective skills [95]. 
When such questions are being addressed, the study level of pre- 
service teachers also needs to be taken into account, since evidence 
from prior research suggests that the amount of teacher education one 
receives relates to effectiveness as a teacher [58]. Further, more 
advanced pre-service teachers often have more profound pedagogical 
and didactical knowledge at their command [57]. Therefore, distinc­
tions should be made between bachelor and master students enrolled in 
teacher education programs [34]. Specifically regarding constructivist 
and transmissive approaches to teaching, bachelor students, who have 
just started teacher education, are perhaps still likely to be more shaped 
in their views on school teaching and learning by rather transmissive 
approaches they experienced during their own school days, although 
this might have started to change during the past few years [49]. In 
teacher education (and other domains), currently, constructivism is the 
dominant didactical paradigm [49]. Indeed, longitudinal evidence from 
a study by Eßling et al. [27] showed that pre-service teachers’ 
constructivist views on teaching and learning increased and trans­
missive views on these matters decreased during the course of a bachelor 
teaching degreee program.

1.4. Research questions and hypotheses 
So, while there are relevant research findings on pre-service teach­
ers’ views on ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning in general, previous 
research on more specific matters around profiles of (pre-service) 
teachers in the context of ICT-assisted teaching and/or (pre-service) 
teachers’ transmissive and constructivist or related beliefs to our 
knowledge tended to be conducted primarily in a more generic, i.e. non- 
subject-specific manner. Addressing the resulting research gap, we 
therefore investigated the following research questions (RQ) in the 
present study with an EFL-specific focus: 
1. Which different clusters of pre-service EFL teachers based on pat­
terns regarding their transmissive and constructivist approaches to­
wards ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning can be identified in the 
underlying sample and how do these clusters differ from each other 
regarding these approaches?  
2. Does cluster allocation vary significantly along participants’ study 
level?  
3. Do pre-service EFL teachers’ value and self-efficacy beliefs regarding 
ICT-assisted EFL teaching vary significantly between the identified 
clusters? 
We formulated two hypothesis sets:  
1. Cluster allocation of pre-service teachers based on patterns regarding 
their constructivist and transmissive approaches to ICT-assisted EFL 
teaching and learning varies significantly depending on participants’ 
study level, with bachelor students showing a higher affinity towards 
transmissive and a lower affinity towards constructivist teaching 
approaches than master students.  
2. Participants’ value and self-efficacy beliefs regarding ICT-assisted 
EFL teaching vary significantly between different clusters of pre- 
service EFL teachers, with pre-service teachers with the highest af­
finity towards constructivist approaches to ICT-assisted EFL teaching 
and learning showing on average significantly higher values 
regarding value and self-efficacy beliefs expect for lower values 
regarding assumed costs. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Participants 
We invited pre-service teachers in teacher education programs from 
universities in North Rhine-Westphalia (Germany) through various on­
line platforms and mailing lists to take part in an online survey about 
their attitudes toward ICT in EFL teaching and learning. Data collection 
was carried out via SoSci Survey [62]. Participation was voluntary and 
based on participants’ informed consent. Participants received a small 
monetary compensation in case they chose this option and provided 
necessary data for the transaction as well as informed consent regarding 
the collection and processing of the required data. Data collection took 
part from 25 October through 30 November 2022. Research data were 
collected fully anonymously. In total, 150 people provided informed 
consent in participation and took the online survey. After excluding 
cases with missing values or more than 100 malus points for too quick 
responses as well as participants who did not eventually provide consent 
to analyze their data, the total sample included N = 125 pre-service 
teachers (85.6 % female, 12.8 % male, 1.6 % diverse; mean age =
24.10 years, SD = 3.06). The sample comprised 46 (36.8 %) bachelor 
students and 79 (63.2 %) master students enrolled in their respective 
teacher education programs. Overall, 120 pre-service teachers (96.0 %) 
reported that they had learned German as a first language (L1). Partic­
ipants’ attitudes towards ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning in the 
investigated sample did not vary significantly depending on their re­
ported sex/gender identity (based on analyses comparing only female 
and male participants to each other due to the low number of partici­
pants in the underlying sample who identified as diverse). We therefore 
did not control for potential sex and/or gender dimensions in our further 
analyses. Participants were, on average, studying EFL in their fifth to 
sixth semester (M = 5.89, SD = 3.59). Regarding the type of school they 
aimed to teach at, 95 pre-service teachers (76.0 %) studied EFL in order 
to become teachers at Gymnasien und Gesamtschulen (grammar and 
comprehensive schools), 4 pre-service teachers (3.2 %) studied EFL for 
Haupt-, Real-, Sekundar- und Gesamtschulen (general secondary schools, 
general modern schools, and comprehensive secondary schools), 5 
pre-service teachers (4.0 %) were aiming for Berufskollegs (vocational 
schools) and 21 participants (16.8 %) studied EFL in order to become 
teachers at Grundschulen (primary schools). 
2.2. Measures 
Three groups of variables were used as measures: 
Approaches to teaching 
Participants’ attitudes with respect to approaches to teaching in the 
context of ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning were measured with 
adapted scales from the Approaches to Teaching Inventory-Revised 
(ATI-R, [111]) and its German translation by Lübeck [66] with 22 
items representing the two scales “information transmission/teacher 
focus” (11 items; α = 0.82; e.g. addressing participants’ views on 
whether ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning should mainly focus on 
the transmission of the teacher’s knowledge to the students) and “con­
ceptual change/student focus” (11 items; α = 0.70; e.g. addressing 
participants’ views on whether the teacher should purposely provoke 
discussion and debate in ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning). 
Value beliefs 
Participants’ value beliefs concerning the use of ICT for EFL teaching 
and learning were measured with adapted versions of four scales by 
Quast et al. [89]; (see also [24,94]): The scales “interest” (3 items; α =
0.78; e.g. addressing whether using ICT for EFL teaching and learning is 
interesting from the participants’ point of view), “utility” (3 items; α =
0.73; e.g. addressing participants’ views on whether the use of ICT in 
EFL teaching and learning supports students’ competence develop­
ment), “relevance” (3 items; ɑ = 0.80; e.g. addressing participants’ 
views on whether it is important to use ICT for EFL teaching) and 
“assumed costs” (3 items; α = 0.74; e.g. addressing participants’ views 
on whether the use of ICT in EFL teaching costs effort). 
Self-efficacy 
Participants’ self-efficacy with regard to the use of ICT in the context 
of learning and teaching EFL was measured with adapted versions of the 
scales “learning-related knowledge” (3 items; α = 0.84; e.g. addressing 
participants’ views on whether they are able to accompany students’ ICT 
use in the context of EFL teaching and learning based on media theory), 
“technical knowledge” (3 items; α = 0.74; e.g. addressing participants’ 
views on whether they are able to solve technical problems that arise 
during the use of ICT in the context of EFL teaching and learning), 
“digital diagnosing” (3 items; α = 0.69; e.g. addressing participants’ 
views on whether they are able to use an electronic portfolio in the 
context of EFL teaching and learning in order to collect data on the 
students’ learning progress) and “digital teaching” (3 items; α = 0.84; e. 
g. addressing participants’ views on whether they are able to adapt the 
use of ICT for EFL teaching and learning to the students’ prior ICT- 
related skills) from the SWIT (“Selbstwirksamkeit von Lehrerinnen 
und Lehrern im Hinblick auf die unterrichtliche Integration digitaler 
Technologie” [Teachers’ self-efficacy with regard to ICT inclusion in 
teaching and learning environments]) by Doll and Meyer [21]; derived 
from the “Computer Technology Integration Survey” [CTIS] by Wang 
et al. [116]. 
The response format was set from 1 (do not agree at all) to 7 (fully 
agree) for all items. Rounded on the second decimal place, internal 
consistency was at least acceptable (α ≥0.70) for all but one (digital 
diagnosing) of the used scales (Table 1).

2.3. Analyses and assumptions 
After descriptive analyses of the data, we ran a two-step cluster 
analysis (log-likelihood measure, Schwarz’s Bayesian information cri­
terion (BIC), maximum number of clusters: 15). According to findings 
from Shapiro-Wilk tests, data were normally distributed for information 
transmission but not for conceptual change. However, the two-step 
cluster analysis is considered robust with regard to such violations of 
assumptions [117]. There was no significant correlation between in­
formation transmission and conceptual change (p = .82). The cluster 
analysis was carried out twice, and variables were included in two 
different orders to rule out sequence effects. To check for relevant mean 
differences between the identified clusters (RQ 1 and RQ 3), we ran two 
one-way multivariate analyses of variance (MANOVA). Exploratory data 
analyses were carried out to test assumptions: Natural outliers spotted 
through visual box-plot interpretation remained within the sample due 
to the limited value range. According to results from Shapiro-Wilk tests, 
normality could not be assumed in all cases. However, the MANOVA is 
considered robust with regard to such violations of assumptions [29]. All 
bivariate correlations between dependent variables had coefficients of r 
< 0.80 (Table 8 in the appendix), so that multicollinearity could be ruled 
out [18]. All Mahalanobis distance measures were below the relevant 
cutoff values of 13.82 (RQ 1) and 26.12 (RQ 3). According to visual 
scatterplot interpretation, linearity could not be assumed for all con­
stellations among the respective dependent variables. Also, results from 
Levene’s tests indicated heteroscedasticity for some variables, and ho­
mogeneity of the covariance matrices could not be assumed in all cases 
according to box test results. However, the MANOVA is also considered 
robust for violations of these kinds of assumptions [3]. Following the 
MANOVAs, analyses of variance (ANOVAs) and post-hoc tests were 
conducted for in-depth cluster comparisons with regard to the respective 
dependent variables. Since all clusters comprised more than 30 cases, 
the central limit theorem could be applied when normality was not 
given. Where homoscedasticity could not be assumed, we considered the 
robust Welch output and Games-Howell- instead of Bonferroni-corrected 
post-hoc tests. We conducted χ2 tests to check for possible different 
cluster distributions depending on participants’ study level (RQ 2). Zero 
cells had an expected count below 5. All statistical analyses were carried 
out with SPSS (Version 29; [47]). 
3. Results 
3.1. Descriptive results 
Participants reported scores above the scale center for all constructs 
with regard to their approaches to teaching. Agreement with conceptual 
change (student focus) was on average higher (M = 6.00, SD = 0.49) 
than with information transmission (teacher focus) (M = 4.37, SD =
0.90) (Table 1). Participants also reported high scores in areas of value 
beliefs. Again, all scores were above the scale center (Table 1) – except 
for the scale “assumed costs” (M = 3.51, SD = 1.34), the only negatively 
keyed scale, i.e., here, high scores do not represent ICT-related affinity. 
The highest score was reached for “interest” in the use of digital media 
for EFL teaching and learning (M = 6.38, SD = 0.73), followed by high 
scores for “utility” (M = 5.90, SD = 0.89) and “relevance” (M = 5.84, SD 
= 1.07). Compared with the overall results for the pre-service teachers’ 
value beliefs, scores for participants’ self-efficacy were slightly lower, 
but still above the scale center. The highest value was reached for 
“digital teaching” (M = 5.49, SD = 1.09), the lowest for “technical 
knowledge” (M = 4.29, SD = 1.46) (Table 1). 
3.2. Identified clusters 
The cluster analysis identified three different clusters depending on 
the participants’ approaches to ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning 
(Table 2), with a silhouette measure for cohesion and separation (0.5) 
indicating a good cluster quality and with an appropriate ratio of 
smallest to highest cluster (1.39; according to Marques et al. [70], the 
ratio should be under 2.00). 
According to the results from the first one-way MANOVA, there was 
significant variance between clusters with regard to the included vari­
ables (information transmission and conceptual change) (F(2, 242) =
88.43, p < .001, η2
p = 0.59, Wilk’s Λ = 0.17). Results from ANOVAs and 
post-hoc tests indicated significant variance between all groups except 
for participants in cluster 2 and 3 regarding conceptual change (Tables 3 
and 4). According to Cohen [16], the overall effects from the ANOVAs 
for information transmission (F(2, 122)=111.51, p < .001, η2 = 0.65) 
and conceptual change (Welch(2, 71.69)=67.80, p < .001, η2 = 0.53) 
classify as large effects. 
Pre-service teachers in cluster 3 (n = 46) showed on average the 
highest values for conceptual change (M = 6.33, SD = 0.26) and the 
lowest value for information transmission (M = 3.69, SD = 0.57). Pre- 
service teachers in cluster 2 (n = 33) showed on average the highest 
values for information transmission (M = 5.50, SD = 0.42). However, 
the least constructivist focus was not found in cluster 2, but in cluster 1 
(n = 46, M = 5.55, SD = 0.38) (see Table 5 for a comparison of the 
identified clusters). 
3.3. Cluster distribution depending on study level 
According to results from χ2 tests, the proportion of cluster alloca­
tions varied significantly between bachelor and master students enrolled 
in teacher education programs (χ2(2)=15.05, p < .001, Table 6). There 
were especially relevant differences regarding cluster-2 and cluster-3 
allocations. The relative proportion of cluster-3 pre-service teachers 
(highest affinity towards conceptual change, lowest affinity towards 
information transmission) was significantly higher in the group of 
master students, the relative proportion of cluster-2 pre-service teachers 
(highest affinity towards information transmission) was significantly 
higher in the group of bachelor students (Table 6). There also was a 
small difference regarding cluster 1 (lowest affinity towards conceptual 
change). Unexpectedly, the relative proportion of cluster-1 pre-service 
teachers was a little higher in the group of master students (Table 6). The 
overall effect classifies as medium-large (Cramer-V = 0.35).

allocation

was significant variance between clusters with regard to the included 
variables (F(16, 230) = 2.17, p < .01, η2

(2, 122) = 6.56, p < .01, η2 = 0.10), utility (F(2, 122) = 3.39, p = .04, η2 
= 0.05) and relevance (F(2, 122) = 3.80, p = .03, η2 = 0.06) depending

high value beliefs and also – in this regard differing from results by 
Yüksel and Kavanoz [118] – felt quite confident about using ICT for EFL 
teaching. In addition, they rated the assumed costs of ICT integration as

Findings from a cluster analysis indicated a fair fit for a 3-cluster 
solution with a good cluster quality. Pre-service teachers in cluster 3 
showed the highest affinity towards constructivist approaches to ICT- 
assisted EFL teaching and learning as well as the lowest affinity to­
wards transmissive approaches. Strikingly, however, the highest affinity 
towards transmissive teaching approaches (in cluster 2) did not go along 
with lowest affinity towards constructivist ICT-assisted EFL teaching and 
learning, which was found in cluster 1. In that, one group of pre-service 
teachers’ with a clearly constructivist profile (cluster 3) was identified, 
in some regard similar to a cluster found by Tondeur et al. [106] in a 
sample of in-service teachers (with no subject-specific focus, however). 
Participants in cluster 2 in the present sample can at least partly be 
described as similar to the group of in-service teachers’ with both a 
constructivist and traditional profile in the study by Tondeur et al. [106] 
as they showed at least moderate affinity towards both constructivist 
and transmissive approaches to ICT-assisted EFL teaching. However, it 
needs to be noted that participants in this cluster in the present sample – 
although not reluctant towards transmissive teaching approaches – 
showed a higher affinity towards constructivist teaching approaches as 
values for conceptual change were higher than for information trans­
mission in all of the three clusters that were identified in the sample of 
the present study. Pre-service teachers in cluster 1 have a rather unde­
fined profile and appear therefore at least in some regard similar to 
in-service teachers’ with an undefined profile regarding constructivist 
and traditionalist views on teaching found in the study by Tondeur et al. 
[106]. Despite these similarities, key differences in both, methodolog­
ical approaches (e.g. different foci regarding school types; qualification 
level of participants) and empirical findings (e.g. number of identified 
clusters) between the study by Tondeur et al. [106] and the present 
research need to be acknowledged. After all, the findings of the present 
research also underpin what previous research already suggested: 
(Pre-service) Teachers’ transmissive and constructivist views do not 
necessarily strictly rule out one another [7,49]. 
Findings from χ2 tests were partly hypothesis-confirming: The pro­
portion of cluster-3 pre-service students (highest affinity towards con­
ceptual change, lowest affinity towards information transmission) was 
more than twice as high in the group of master students as the respective 
proportion in the group of bachelor students. At the same time, the 
proportion of cluster-2 pre-service teachers (highest affinity towards 
information transmission) was about three times as high in the group of 
bachelor students as the respective proportion in the group of master 
students. Unexpectedly, however, the proportion of cluster-1 partici­
pants (lowest affinity towards conceptual change) was a little higher in 
the group of master students than in the group of bachelor students. The 
findings of the present research thus at least partly support the prior 
evidence-based [27] assumption that pre-service teachers’ transmissive 
and constructivist beliefs evolve during the course of teacher education 
(and therefore differ on average between bachelor and master students). 
However, while we compared bachelor and master students in a 
cross-sectional design, the study by Eßling et al. [27] referred exclu­
sively to trajectories during the course of a bachelor teaching degree 
program, in turn based on a longitudinal research design. 
Findings from cluster comparisons regarding participants’ value and 
self-efficacy beliefs were partly in line with our hypotheses: As expected, 
pre-service teachers with the highest affinity towards constructivist 
approaches to ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning (cluster 3) reached 
on average the highest values for relevance and interest. However, 
participants in cluster 2 reached the same average for interest. More­
over, it is them (pre-service teachers in cluster 2), and not participants in 
cluster 3, who rated utility of ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning the 
highest. A possible explanation for this might be that some digital tools 
or ICT-assisted strategies specifically support transmissive frontal 
teaching (e.g. certain uses of electronic whiteboards) [83]. Moreover, 
there was unexpectedly no significant variance in assumed costs and 
self-efficacy beliefs between clusters. A reason for the latter might be, 
that self-efficacy or similar beliefs with regard to ICT-assisted teaching 
are more closely related to other factors – e.g. experience with the actual 
use of ICT for teaching [50]. This would also explain that values for 
self-efficacy beliefs were lower than those for value beliefs in the un­
derlying sample of the present study as the sample comprises pre-service 
teachers with an overall by definition rather low amount of 
school-practical experience. In light of the present study’s findings, the 
role of assumed costs in the context of ICT-assisted teaching should be 
further examined. This is in line with previous findings by Cheng et al. 
[14] and Quast et al. [89] who found that assumed costs were rather 
surprisingly no relevant predictors of teachers’ ICT integration (when 
other factors were controlled). 
4.2. Limitations and possible directions for future research 
From a methodological point of view, the following aspects need to 
be considered: In the present study, internal consistency of most of the 
used scales indicated good construct validity. However, it should be 
noted that Cronbach’s alpha was – differing from tests in previous 
research [21] – below 0.70 for the scale “digital diagnosing” so that 
internal consistency might here be considered too low (for a critical 
discussion see inter alia [105]). Probably even more importantly, 
self-reports on attitudes are highly subjective and may be biased through 
social desirability and other factors [52,60,67]. Future research may, 
therefore, wish to also take more implicit attitude measures into account 
[20,68]. With regard to statistical validity, multiple testing in the pre­
sent study may have led to type 1 error inflation [28]. Through 
Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc testing – where applicable –, the likeli­
hood of such errors could be reduced [28]. At the same time, due to the 
rather small sample size, partial false-negative findings cannot fully be 
ruled out and precision might be compromised. Realizing a larger 
sample size for such research thus seems favorable in the future. In terms

allows for conclusions that are rather speculative regarding potentially 
underlying cause-and-effect relationships of the study’s findings. Future

tional methodological approaches (e.g., longitudinal, qualitative, 
quasi-experimental) and non-convenience sampling procedures. 
4.3. Potential implications for the practice of teacher education 
From our point of view, the following conclusions may be derived 
from the present study’s findings for the practice of teacher education: 
(1) In at least some regard, bachelor students – in comparison with 
master students – are slightly more likely to show a higher affinity to­
wards information transmission in ICT-assisted EFL teaching and 
learning and a lower affinity towards constructivist approaches. (2) 
However, the findings also suggest: This should not be over-generalized 
and teacher educators should especially keep in mind that their students 
might as well show both transmissive and constructivist beliefs. (3) 
Further, the findings suggest that pre-service EFL teachers with mod­
erate transmissive and rather low constructivist beliefs (in the sense of 
cluster 1 identified in the present study) are more likely to show lower 
amounts of interest, perceived relevance and perceived utility regarding 
ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning. (4) Pre-service teachers’ trans­
missive and constructivist beliefs do not appear primarily suitable for 
predicting or explaining pre-service EFL teachers’ amount of self- 
efficacy regarding ICT-assisted EFL teaching and learning. Taking this 
into account when tailoring ICT-related learning opportunities for 
(initial) teacher education might help teacher educators and policy 
makers to better understand pre-service EFL teachers views and learning 
processes around the integration of ICT into teaching and learning EFL.
