Decoding divides: The role of socioeconomic status and personality traits 
in AI divides and educational inequality

H I G H L I G H T S
• Digital literacy, not AI usage intensity, mediates personality-performance links.
• A new digital skills divide emerges, driven by personality traits instead of SES.
• SES advantages on performance operate independently of AI engagement.

A B S T R A C T
This study investigates whether the integration of AI in primary education relates to the digital divide and ed­
ucational inequality, thereby examining the mediating roles of AI usage and digital literacy. Using survey and 
national registry data from 4497 Grade 6 students in the Netherlands (2022–2023), we examined relations be­
tween family socioeconomic status (SES) and personality traits (openness, extraversion, perseverance) via AI 
usage and digital literacy to academic performance. Our analyses revealed that digital literacy, not AI usage, 
positively mediated the relationship between all three personality traits and academic achievement. Family SES 
demonstrated a strong, direct relationship with performance but was not positively mediated by either AI usage or 
digital literacy, indicating that SES advantages persisted regardless of classroom AI implementation we observed. 
These findings suggest that as technology access becomes ubiquitous, a skill-based gap is evident that correlates 
with personality traits. This highlights the need for educational policies to move beyond providing mere access 
to AI and focus on cultivating digital skills tailored to diverse student personal attributes to ensure educational 
equality.
1 . Introduction
In primary education, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has enabled adap­
tive learning that can address diverse students’ needs with real-time, 
tailored interventions and feedback (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022; Zhai et al., 
2021). If AI is deployed in a manner that is both inclusive (ensur­
ing all students have access to AI tools) and equitable (providing 
personalized adaptations based on each student’s unique features and 
abilities), AI could potentially reduce educational inequality by offering 
tailored learning experiences (Organisation for Economic Co-operation 
and Development, 2024). Systematic disparities in access to, usage of, 
and benefits from digital technologies (known as the digital divide 
(Van Dijk, 2020)) represent a significant barrier to achieving this po­
tential. The AI-driven digital divide extends this concept to AI-enabled 
tools, where greater complexity and rapid technological evolution raise 
thresholds of usage and skills required for effective utilization of the 
AI tools (Clemence et al., 2026). Evidence from digital divide research 
suggests the persistence of disparities in digital technology usage and 
skills among students across educational levels, resulting from societal 
inequalities in positional characteristics (e.g., SES) as well as personal 
attributes (e.g., personality traits) (Van Dijk, 2020). Recent studies indi­
cate that these disparities have already manifested in the domain of AI, 
particularly following the introduction and rapid adoption of generative 
AI tools (Daepp & Counts, 2024). Such an AI-driven digital divide may

Therefore, we addressed this gap within the framework of digital

digital literacy, and how these two factors are associated with academic 
performance among primary school students.
1.1 . The digital divide theory
The digital divide theory provides a comprehensive framework for 
understanding how societal inequalities manifest in the context of in­
formation and communication technologies (ICTs) (Van Dijk, 2020). 
At its core, the theory posits that inequalities in both positional so­
cietal factors (e.g., SES) and personal characteristics (e.g., personality 
traits) create a fundamental divide in how individuals engage with and 
benefit from digital technologies (Van Dijk, 2020). The divide is con­
ceptualized across three levels: physical access (first level), skills and 
usage (second level), and outcomes (third level) (Lythreatis et al., 2022). 
Regarding skills and usage, a distinction can be made between general 
digital literacy versus the use of AI. In the current study, we focus on 
the second-level digital divide, examining AI usage intensity and digi­
tal literacy as key measures of usage and skills, respectively. We define 
AI usage intensity as a combination of the extent and frequency of stu­
dents’ engagement with AI learning tools, across different tool types and 
usage contexts. Digital literacy encompasses the ability to understand, 
integrate, communicate, and create information safely through digital 
technologies (UNESCO Institute for Statistics, 2018).
Typically, individuals with more advantaged positional features and 
favorable personal characteristics enjoy more extensive physical access 
to digital devices (DiMaggio et al., 2004), develop superior digital skills 
(Hargittai, 2002), engage in more effective usage (Van Deursen & Van 
Dijk, 2014a), and consequently reap more beneficial outcomes from 
digital technologies (Van Deursen & Helsper, 2015).
In primary education, the digital divide relates to students’ learning 
experiences and academic achievement across all three levels (physi­
cal access; skills and usage; outcomes) (Tang et al., 2025). On the first 
level, although the divide in hardware access in primary school class­
rooms in developed economies is largely bridged over time (Hohlfeld 
et al., 2008, 2017), disparities remain in the quality and utilization of 
digital resources (Van De Werfhorst et al., 2022). Additionally, on the 
second level, there are substantial variations in primary school students’ 
digital skills and their ability to effectively use educational technolo­
gies (Dolan, 2016; Hohlfeld et al., 2017). The variations in skills and 
usage result in the third-level divide, where differences in outcomes 
from using educational technologies are evident among students (Zhao 
et al., 2022). Effective use of educational technologies leads to enhanced 
learning and better preparation for future opportunities (Dolan, 2016). 
However, these benefits are unevenly distributed among primary school 
students, as reflected in their learning experiences and outcomes (Wang 
et al., 2024).
Despite extensive research on digital divides in education focusing on 
traditional ICTs (Dolan, 2016; Wang et al., 2024), the relationship be­
tween AI technologies and digital divides in education remains largely 
unexplored (Mirazchiyski, 2025; Tang et al., 2025). AI tools differ sig­
nificantly from traditional ICTs in their complexity and rapid evolution 
(Seshia et al., 2022). The complexity and evolving nature of AI create a 
dual burden whereby students must develop competencies in technolo­
gies whose functional requirements are themselves subject to continuous 
change. This complexity and evolution of AI tools may thus create 
unique challenges in educational settings. Understanding AI-specific di­
vides is crucial as they could significantly influence educational equity 
and outcomes.
1.2 . The role of SES in the digital divide and educational inequality
SES stands as a crucial positional factor in digital divide theory, serv­
ing as a primary determinant of an individual’s capacity to utilize and 
benefit from digital technologies (Van Dijk, 2020).
In primary education, at the first level of the digital divide, pri­
mary schools in higher SES areas and students from higher SES families 
have access to more advanced digital learning tools and tech-rich home 
environments (Dolan, 2016; Gonzalez-Betancor et al., 2021; Liu, 2021).
At the second level (level of interests in the current study), SES 
significantly shapes students’ digital competencies and usage. Children 
from higher SES backgrounds often benefit from greater exposure to 
technology, more opportunities for guided learning, and higher lev­
els of parental digital literacy, leading to more advanced digital skills 
(Hohlfeld et al., 2017; Van De Werfhorst et al., 2022). The skills advan­
tage translates into distinct usage patterns. Higher SES students tend to 
engage in more sophisticated, creative, and collaborative digital learn­
ing activities, while their lower SES peers may be limited to more basic, 
leisure-oriented usage (Ren et al., 2022).
The third level of the digital divide manifests in learning outcomes, 
reflecting the cumulative impact of disparities in both access and skills. 
The combined effect of unequal access to technology (first level) and 
divergent digital competencies and usage patterns (second level) con­
tributes to significantly different learning experiences and educational 
attainment among students from various SES backgrounds (Van De 
Werfhorst et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024).
SES-based digital divides in primary education hold additional sig­
nificance given the well-established relationship between SES and aca­
demic performance. Research across disciplines consistently shows a 
positive relationship between family SES and academic performance 
(Blanden et al., 2023).
Despite extensive research on both digital divides and SES-academic 
performance relationships, empirical evidence remains limited on how 
the introduction of AI in primary education relates to the established 
SES-performance patterns. There is a risk that AI learning tools could 
further widen the digital divide and exacerbate educational inequality. 
The benefits of AI tools in enhancing learning outcomes require proper 
usage, skills, and integration into broader learning strategies (Kabudi 
et al., 2021; Zhai et al., 2021). Students from lower SES backgrounds, 
who often lack the necessary skills and opportunities, may not fully uti­
lize AI tools, potentially missing out on these benefits (Hannan & Eynon, 
2025). As a result, educational inequalities could be exacerbated, as stu­
dents from lower SES backgrounds may fall further behind compared to 
their higher SES counterparts who are better positioned to exploit AI’s 
educational potential.
1.3 . The role of personality traits in the digital divide and educational 
inequality
Within the digital divide framework, personal categorical differences 
(e.g., differences in personality traits) are also key origins of disparities 
in engagement with digital technologies (Van Dijk, 2020). Different per­
sonality traits, as a core aspect of personal categorical differences, may 
contribute considerably to digital divides at the second (usage and skills) 
and third (outcomes) levels (Scheerder et al., 2017).
Personality traits are enduring patterns of thoughts, feelings, and be­
haviors that distinguish individuals from one another. They are typically 
measured using models such as the “Big Five” (openness, conscientious­
ness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism) (Roberts & Yoon, 
2022).
Studies among adult populations have shown that adults with higher 
openness, extraversion, and perseverance (a facet of conscientiousness 
in the Big Five model) are more willing and motivated to use digital 
technologies more frequently and effectively (Ali et al., 2020; Correa 
et al., 2010; Joshi et al., 2023; Maran et al., 2022). As students share 
fundamental psychological characteristics with adults, and there is no 
evidence indicating that other personality traits might correlate with AI

usage and digital skills, we focus our investigation on these three specific 
traits (openness, extraversion, and perseverance).
While the aforementioned research has established personality ef­
fects on technology usage patterns, it has largely overlooked how these 
traits are correlated with digital skills, the second key component of 
the second-level digital divide (Scheerder et al., 2017; Van Dijk, 2020). 
This research gap may exist because traditional ICT tools were often 
relatively simple and static. Mastering them typically required procedu­
ral learning rather than a strong individual disposition for continuous 
adaptation or critical thinking. In contrast, the rapidly evolving and 
generative nature of AI presents a different challenge (Clemence et al., 
2026). It necessitates greater user autonomy for acquiring and updating 
knowledge and requires enhanced critical evaluation of AI-generated 
content (Clemence et al., 2026; Glickman & Sharot, 2025). These abil­
ities (autonomy and critical evaluation) are core components of digital 
literacy (Bawden, 2008) and are closely linked to traits like openness, ex­
traversion, and perseverance (Maran et al., 2022). The demanding skill 
set required by AI, together with its alignment with these specific person­
ality traits, suggests a need to reassess how personality traits correlate 
with digital skills in the context of AI.
Mirroring the scarcity of research in adult populations, educational 
research also lacks empirical evidence connecting students’ personality 
traits to digital skills and AI usage. Nonetheless, emerging evidence in­
dicates that personality traits are critical in technology adoption and 
directly influence academic performance. One strand of research from 
educational economics demonstrates that students with higher levels 
of perseverance (Brandt et al., 2021), openness (Meyer et al., 2019), 
and extraversion (Meyer et al., 2023) tend to achieve better academic 
outcomes. A second strand of research from developmental psychology 
shows that personality traits also shape important aspects of technology 
adoption for younger populations. Research highlights the importance 
of openness and extraversion in children’s innovation ability (Rawlings 
et al., 2022), and perseverance is crucial for sustained engagement with 
complex learning tasks that demand iterative problem-solving and adap­
tation (Duckworth et al., 2007), which are essential for engaging with 
AI.
In sum, research findings across disciplines among different pop­
ulation groups suggest that openness, extraversion, and perseverance 
could significantly influence students’ adaptability, effective use of, and 
benefits from AI learning tools in education. Therefore, AI usage and 
skills could potentially mediate the relationship between these person­
ality traits and academic achievement. It is then warranted to examine 
their relationships with students’ usage of and skills in AI learning tools, 
and how the differences in usage and skills contribute to inequality in 
academic performance.
1.4 . The present study
While existing research provides valuable insights into the digital di­
vide and its implications for education, several critical gaps remain in 
our understanding of how AI relates to the divide. First, numerous stud­
ies have examined the digital divide in (primary) education, including 
many prompted by COVID-19 after 2020 (Liu, 2021; Wijayanti et al., 
2024), but they predominantly focus on traditional ICT-driven divides 
rather than the AI-driven ones (Wang et al., 2024). AI, being more com­
plex and rapidly evolving than conventional ICT, may create unique 
challenges such as inequitable access to advanced tools and resources, as 
well as continuous acquisition and updating of skills (Wang et al., 2025). 
Even in developed countries where the first-level divide (physical ac­
cess to basic digital hardware and internet connection) is largely bridged 
(Eurostat, 2024), the second-level (skills and usage) and third-level (out­
comes) divides are still prominent (Van De Werfhorst et al., 2022), 
especially concerning AI-related tools (Appel et al., 2025; Chatterji et al., 
2025; Daepp & Counts, 2024). Understanding the second-level divide is 
particularly crucial as it forms the basis for comprehending the third-
level divide in AI-enhanced education (Van Dijk, 2020). In other words, 
disparities in AI skills and usage patterns may directly influence the in­
equalities in outcomes derived from AI technologies. However, research 
on AI skills and usage disparities in education remains limited, leaving 
the nature and extent of AI-driven educational inequalities unclear.
Second, beyond the digital divide phenomenon itself, how AI integra­
tion in education relates to educational inequalities remains ambiguous 
and under-explored, despite the potential advantages and relevance of 
AI in primary education (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022). Research has not 
established how the introduction of AI in primary education will al­
ter the well-established relationship between family SES and academic 
outcomes (Blanden et al., 2023).
Third, there is a significant gap in our understanding of how per­
sonality traits relate to digital proficiency, particularly in the context of 
AI. In digital divide theory and empirical research on adult populations, 
there is a lack of studies exploring how personality traits predict digital 
skills, a crucial component of the second-level divide (Van Dijk, 2020). 
The research gap becomes even more pronounced in the field of educa­
tion. While educational research has examined the second-level digital 
divide, educational studies have overlooked the role of personality traits 
in AI contexts, especially regarding AI learning tools (Mirazchiyski, 
2025). The neglect of personality factors is particularly significant given 
the established importance of personality in predicting academic per­
formance (Almlund et al., 2011). Understanding how personality traits 
associate with both the digital skills and AI usage in educational settings 
is crucial, as AI has the potential to amplify the effects of personality on 
educational outcomes through the second-level divide.
To address the identified research gaps, we investigate the follow­
ing research question: To what extent do AI usage intensity and digital 
literacy mediate the relationship between family SES and academic per­
formance on the one hand and between personality traits (openness, 
extraversion, and perseverance) and academic performance on the other 
hand?
Based on digital divide theory and existing empirical evidence, we 
hypothesize:
H1: The relationship between family SES and academic performance 
is positively mediated by a) AI usage intensity and b) digital 
literacy.
H2: The relationship between personality traits (openness, extraver­
sion, perseverance) and academic performance is positively me­
diated by a) AI usage intensity and b) digital literacy.
To test these hypotheses, we employ a unique dataset combining sur­
vey responses and national administrative records from 4497 Grade 6 
students in the Netherlands during the 2022–2023 academic year. We 
conduct mediation analyses using PROCESS macro Model 4 to examine 
whether AI usage and digital literacy mediate the relationships between 
individual characteristics (family SES and personality traits) and aca­
demic performance. The remaining sections of this paper are structured 
as follows: Section 2 describes the method, including the sample, pro­
cedure, and measures; Section 3 presents the results of the analyses; 
Section 4 discusses the findings and their implications; and Section 5 
concludes.
2 . Method
2.1 . Participants
The sample was drawn from the 2022–2023 wave of the Education 
Monitor Limburg (in Dutch: Onderwijs Monitor Limburg, OML) sur­
vey. The OML targeted Grade 6 students (“group 8” in the Dutch 
education system, equivalent to U.S. 6th grade) in the province of 
Limburg. Limburg is a southern province in the Netherlands with lower 
average educational attainment and concentrated areas of additional 
governmental funding, providing substantial SES variation relevant for 
studying educational inequalities (Monfrance et al., 2025). Despite 
these regional differences, digital infrastructure in Limburg aligns with

the national standard, with near-universal classroom technology ac­
cess achieved through national digitalization policies (Kerssens & Van

cational policies, including curriculum standards and digital learning 
tool implementation, are set centrally and applied uniformly across 
provinces. The OML covers about 90% of regional schools, providing 
near-census coverage of the student population. Before excluding cases 
with missing data, the initial sample comprised 6772 students, rep­
resenting approximately 75% of all Grade 6 students in the province 
that academic year. After excluding participants with missing data on 
key variables (the three personality traits, AI usage variables, digital 
literacy, and family SES), the final analytic sample consisted of 4497 
students. Most missing observations lacked data on all variables, sug­
gesting that missingness resulted from entire schools not participating 
rather than individual students skipping specific items. Because no data 
were collected from non-participating schools, a formal selectivity anal­
ysis comparing responding and non-responding institutions was not
feasible.
The final sample was approximately balanced in terms of sex (52% 
male). The average age was 11.9 years (𝑆𝐷= 0.47).
2.2 . Procedure
Data collection was conducted under a legally binding coopera­
tion agreement between the participating school boards and Maastricht 
University, with the protocol approved by the local ethics committee 
(ERCIC-092-12-07-2018). Parents were informed about the study via 
written school communications with sufficient advance notice to ex­
ercise their right to withdraw their child. Passive consent was used 
following approval by the local ethics committee as appropriate for this 
minimal-risk educational survey research, and the withdrawal rate was 
negligible.
The OML survey was administered to students and teachers in-school 
under supervised conditions during the 2022–2023 academic year be­
tween March and June. The survey provided measures for personality 
traits, AI learning tool usage, digital literacy, and covariates includ­
ing traditional information and communication technology in education 
(ICT‑Ed) usage, IQ, oldest child status, age, and sex. Following data 
collection, the survey data were linked via a secure, encrypted identi­
fier to national registry data incorporated by Netherlands Cohort Study 
on Education (Haelermans et al., 2020), to obtain students’ primary 
school exit test scores and information on their parents’ SES. The linkage 
was performed by Statistics Netherlands (Centraal Bureau voor de 
Statistiek; CBS) using encrypted identifiers, and the linked dataset was 
pseudonymized. Access was restricted to authorized research team mem­
bers via CBS Remote Access. The analysis was conducted via Remote 
Access on CBS secure local machine to ensure data security and privacy 
compliance.
2.3 . Materials and measures
The following sections describe the measures used in this study. 
Descriptive statistics and correlations for all measures are presented in 
Table 1 in the Results section.
2.3.1 . Registry-based measures
SES. 
SES was measured by a composite index derived from national 
tax registry data, combining 50% parental-average wealth percentile and 
50% parental-average income percentile, where both components were 
ranked relative to the Dutch working population and the parental av­
erage was calculated as the mean of father’s and mother’s percentiles. 
As a robustness check, we re-specified SES using alternative measures 
including parental average wealth, parental average income, and high­
est parental education (see Appendix C.2 for details). These alternative 
specifications did not alter the substantive findings.
Academic performance. 
We measured academic performance using stu­
dents’ end-of-primary-education exit test scores from national registry 
data (2022–2023). Dutch law grants schools autonomy in selecting 
among approved test providers (Eurydice, 2025). Schools in our sample 
used five certified test providers with different scales and distribu­
tions, making raw scores non-comparable. Provider-specific raw-score 
distributions were heterogeneous and non-normal. We therefore con­
verted scores to national within-provider percentiles, which aligned 
scales while preserving students’ relative standing in each provider’s 
scoring system. Percentiles were treated as continuous variables rang­
ing from 0 to 100. Unlike z-score standardization, percentile conversions 
were more robust to skewness and heavy tails in the observed dis­
tributions. To absorb any remaining systematic differences between 
instruments, we controlled for test-provider identifiers in all models. As 
a robustness check, we also repeated all mediation analyses using z-
score standardized outcomes; results were substantively unchanged (see 
Appendix C).

2.3.2 . Survey-based measures
AI usage intensity. 
We define AI usage intensity as the extent of stu­
dents’ engagement with AI learning tools across different tool types, 
usage contexts (at home, at school, or both), and frequency of use (cat­
egorical options: multiple times per day, once per day, twice per week, 
once per week). We constructed this measure by combining informa­
tion from two complementary sources. Teachers reported which AI tools 
were used in their classrooms and where students were assigned to use 
them (at school only, at home as homework, or both), creating a set 
of tool-context indicators such as ’Gynzy at school only’ or ’Snappet at 
both school and home.’ Students self-reported their overall frequency of 
digital educational tool usage, providing individual-level engagement 
data. To create a composite index capturing this multi-dimensional in­
formation from both teachers and students, we required a method that 
could handle both the extensive margin (variety of tool-context combi­
nations) and the intensive margin (whether specific combinations, such 
as Gynzy at home, empirically contributed to actual usage frequency). 
We adopted CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018), a gradient boost­
ing algorithm specifically designed for categorical features, because our 
tool-context indicators are inherently categorical (tool type × context 
combinations), and standard methods like ordinary regression or princi­
pal components analysis would impose inappropriate assumptions about 
distances between categories. Unlike ordinary methods that treat cate­
gories as arbitrary numbers or require manual dummy coding, CatBoost 
handles categorical variables natively through ordered target statis­
tics, preventing information loss and reducing bias. This supervised, 
data-driven weighting approach trained the model to predict students’ 
self-reported frequency from their tool-context indicator profiles. The 
model’s learned feature-importance values quantified how much each 
configuration contributed to observed engagement frequency. Critically, 
each student received their own AI usage intensity score, computed as 
the weighted sum of whichever tool-context indicators applied to that 
individual student (for instance, a student using Gynzy at school and 
Snappet at home would receive weights only for those two combina­
tions). This approach captured both the extensive margin (variety of 
tools used) and the intensive margin (contexts that empirically mattered 
more for predicting actual engagement), yielding individual-level varia­
tion that reflected heterogeneous patterns of AI integration. Compared to 
simple equal-weight counts, this method better captured heterogeneous 
AI adoption patterns while remaining interpretable (see Appendix A.1 
for full technical details). The complete questionnaire items for AI learn­
ing tools usage are provided in Appendix A.4. Fig. A.1 shows that the 
resulting index provided smoother, more differentiated variation than 
simple counts or coarse context categories, enabling more precise detec­
tion of individual differences in engagement that may relate to academic 
outcomes.
Digital literacy. 
We operationalized digital literacy using a Dutch 
translation of the PISA 2018 ICT Familiarity Questionnaire (nine items, 
see Appendix A.2 for the full questionnaire), which spans digital com­
petency and digital autonomy (Organisation for Economic Co-operation 
and Development, 2019). All items were rated on a five-point Likert 
scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.”
Exploratory factor analysis indicated a single underlying dimen­
sion for the nine items, with the scree plot showing a dominant first 
eigenvalue (3.81). We therefore estimated a composite score of digital 
literacy for each student using a Graded Response Model (GRM), an 
Item Response Theory (IRT) model for ordered responses (Embretson 
& Reise, 2013). This approach yielded personal scores across all nine 
questions on a common scale, with IRT diagnostics indicating the high­
est measurement precision around the moderate ability range. Digital 
literacy scores were standardized to 𝑀= 0, 𝑆𝐷= 1 for analysis. We 
used this composite measure rather than modeling subdimensions (e.g., 
operational, informational, critical) because the single-factor structure 
empirically supported aggregation.
Personality traits. 
We measured three personality traits relevant to 
educational and digital engagement: Perseverance, Extraversion, and 
Openness. They were assessed using Likert-type questions adapted from 
the Big Five Inventory-2 (BFI-2; Soto and John (2017)) and translated 
into Dutch. All items were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging 
from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” Similar to digital literacy, 
we constructed a composite score for each trait using a GRM to utilize its 
advantage in modeling Likert-type data. All personality trait scores were 
standardized to 𝑀= 0, 𝑆𝐷= 1 for analysis. The complete questionnaire 
items are provided in Section A.3.
Covariates. 
To account for potential confounding variables, we in­
cluded several covariates. We measured IQ using the Non-Scholastic 
Cognitive Abilities Test (NSCCT), a standardized assessment of cogni­
tive skills not tied to school curricula (Van Batenburg & Van der Werf, 
2004). The results of the OML survey provided the NSCCT scores, which 
were derived from a 2-parameter logistic (2PL) Item Response Theory 
(IRT) model and standardized to 𝑀= 0, 𝑆𝐷= 1. (Traditional) ICT-
Ed Usage was assessed through a teacher-reported questionnaire on the 
frequency of using non-AI digital tools, such as publisher’s digital re­
sources and open-source software. This was operationalized as a sum 
score of the different tools used. Finally, demographic variables includ­
ing Oldest Child Status (a binary variable), Sex, and Age were obtained 
from registry records by the OML project. Oldest Child Status and Sex 
were coded as binary variables (1 = yes and male, respectively), while 
Age was measured in years.
2.4 . Analytic strategy
We tested our hypotheses using mediation analyses with the 
PROCESS macro 5.0 (Model 4) for R, an ordinary least squares (OLS) 
regression-based path analysis tool for estimating direct and indirect 
effects (Hayes, 2022).
Model specification. 
To test our mediation hypotheses, we specified a 
parallel mediation model examining the indirect relationships between 
four independent variables (family SES, openness, extraversion, and per­
severance) and academic performance through two parallel mediators 
(AI usage intensity and digital literacy). We note that all mediation 
effects in the current study decompose correlational pathways and do 
not imply causation. The model follows Hayes’ conceptual framework 
(Hayes, 2022) with three key pathways for each independent variable: 
(a) the relationship between the independent variable and each mediator 
(path 𝑎), (b) the relationship between each mediator and the dependent 
variable controlling for the independent variable (path 𝑏), and (𝑐′) the 
direct relationship between the independent variable and the dependent 
variable controlling for both mediators. The indirect effect through each 
mediator is calculated as the product of the respective paths 𝑎 and 𝑏 (i.e., 
𝑎× 𝑏). The model tests H1 by examining SES pathways through both 
digital factors, and H2 by examining personality trait pathways through 
both digital factors (see Fig. 1 for the complete model visualization).
Statistical approach. 
The model was estimated using ordinary least 
squares regression with robust standard errors clustered at the school 
level to account for potential within-school correlations in the data. All 
GRM-derived variables (digital literacy, openness, extraversion, perse­
verance, IQ) were on standardized scales (𝑀= 0, 𝑆𝐷= 1) by default. 
Missing data were handled using listwise deletion, resulting in a fi­
nal analytic sample of 4497 students with complete data on all key 
variables. The school-level clustering of missing data does not compro­
mise the validity of our findings given the substantial remaining sample 
size and broad school representation. Balance tests on participating 
versus non-participating schools conducted on the CBS secure local ma­
chine showed no significant differences in SES composition or academic 
performance, suggesting limited representativeness bias. The model in­
cluded all control variables (student sex, age, IQ, traditional ICT-Ed

4 . Discussion
4.1 . Summary of key findings
This study investigated how AI-related digital divides mediated the 
relationships between student characteristics and academic performance 
among 4497 Grade 6 primary school students in the Netherlands. Our 
findings align with the core tenets of digital divide theory while chal­
lenging some conventional assumptions and previous empirical results 
regarding the primary drivers of the digital divide in technology-rich 
contexts.
First, we examined the role of family SES. Contrary to our hypothe­
sis, the results provided no support for the expected positive mediation 
of a) AI usage intensity and b) digital literacy between family SES and 
academic performance. H1 was not supported; neither AI usage intensity

4.2 . Interpretation of findings
The primacy of digital literacy over AI usage in predicting academic perfor­
mance. 
Our finding that digital literacy significantly correlates with 
academic performance (𝐵= 1.78) while AI usage intensity does not 
suggests that skills matter more than usage frequency in AI-enhanced 
education. In AI-integrated classrooms, AI exposure is routine; the real 
divide is not whether students use AI tools, but how effectively they do 
so. Our digital literacy measure captures AI-relevant competency and 
autonomy (comfort with new digital tools, problem solving, helping oth­
ers, and the ability to find, install, and learn software independently), 
so it tracks who can convert AI exposure into academic gains. Even with 
a null AI-usage result, this can still be treated as an AI-related divide 
because the inequality lies in who can use AI effectively in AI-enriched 
classrooms. This result aligns with previous research (Hohlfeld et al., 
2017; Wang et al., 2024) indicating that exposure to smart learning 
tools alone is insufficient for academic benefits. The pattern reinforces 
a fundamental aspect of digital divide theory: while bridging gaps in 
access and basic usage is necessary, it is far from sufficient for achiev­
ing digital equity (Van Deursen & Helsper, 2015; Van Deursen & Van 
Dijk, 2014a). The more consequential divide emerges in the realm of 
skills and effective use (Van Dijk, 2020), where students’ underlying 
digital competencies (their abilities to critically evaluate, navigate, and 
employ digital technologies) prove more predictive of educational out­
comes than mere usage intensity. As AI tools become more sophisticated 
and ubiquitous in educational settings (Chen et al., 2020), the ability to 
effectively navigate, evaluate, and utilize these technologies becomes 
a meta-skill that amplifies advantages in traditional academic domains 
(Su et al., 2023).
A new personality-associated digital skills divide. 
The SES-digital liter­
acy relationship was statistically significant but practically negligible 
(𝐵= −0.00), whereas the personality-digital literacy associations were 
positive and substantively larger (openness: 𝐵= 0.19, extraversion: 
𝐵= 0.17, perseverance: 𝐵= 0.05). In this Dutch sample with high base­
line digital access, personality traits thus showed stronger correlations 
with digital skills than SES background. Together with the significant 
and positive indirect effects through digital literacy (openness: 𝐵= 0.33, 
extraversion: 𝐵= 0.30, perseverance: 𝐵= 0.08), our results indicate that 
personality traits have emerged as important predictors of who develops 
the digital competencies needed for academic benefits in AI-enhanced 
environments.
Several explanations may account for why digital literacy mediates 
the relationship between personality traits and academic performance 
to varying degrees. The three personality traits (openness, extraversion, 
and perseverance) appear to relate to a proactive learning orientation 
and sustained engagement necessary to develop robust digital literacy

in the context of using complicated AI learning tools. Students high in 
openness may be more willing to explore new digital environments and 
experiment with unfamiliar technologies (Kashdan et al., 2004). Those 
high in extraversion may be more likely to seek help, collaborate with 
peers, and engage actively with digital learning platforms (Borg et al., 
2021). Students with greater perseverance may demonstrate the sus­
tained effort required to master sophisticated digital skills that extend 
beyond basic operational competencies (Duckworth et al., 2007). These 
individual differences correlate with meaningful academic advantages 
through digital literacy (indirect effects: openness = 0.33, extraversion 
= 0.30, perseverance = 0.08). In a 25-student classroom ranked by 
openness, one standard deviation in openness (comparing a student at 
position 4 to one at position 13) translates to 0.33 percentile points 
higher academic performance through digital literacy alone (roughly 
0.08 rank positions in a standard 25-student classroom). Openness and 
extraversion support digital literacy development more effectively than 
perseverance.
The findings reveal a pattern in the nature of digital divides in the 
context of AI-enhanced education. While digital divides have mostly 
been linked to disparities in “positional categorical inequalities” like 
SES (Scheerder et al., 2017; Van Dijk, 2020), our study provides empiri­
cal evidence that “personal categorical inequalities” such as personality 
traits serve as important predictors of the digital skills divide (Scheerder 
et al., 2017). To the best of our knowledge, this represents one of 
the first empirical investigations in educational research to show that 
personality traits mediate academic performance through digital lit­
eracy in AI-enhanced learning environments. Even in environments 
where technological access is equitable, substantial differences in dig­
ital engagement and benefits may persist based on personal attributes 
(Scheerder et al., 2017).
Non-mediation of SES-academic performance relationship. 
Our results 
showed that SES maintained strong direct effects on academic perfor­
mance (𝐵= 0.23) with no positive mediation through AI usage intensity 
(𝐵= 0.00) or digital literacy (indirect 𝐵= −0.01). This finding contrasts 
with our expectations based on previous research (Zhang et al., 2024) 
and indicates that SES advantages on academic performance show as­
sociations through pathways beyond the classroom AI usage and digital 
literacy measures we observed.
In the context of a developed country like the Netherlands, where 
near-universal access to technology in schools has been largely achieved 
(Kerssens & Van Dijck, 2023), differences in SES background may no 
longer be the primary factor in whether and how children engage with 
digital technologies in educational settings. The provision of technology 
through schools and the institutional integration of AI tools may have 
effectively neutralized some of the traditional pathways through which 
SES relates to educational technology use. The result shows a relative 
success in addressing the SES-driven first-level divides (access and basic 
usage) and second-level divides (operational use of technology) up until 
the 2022–2023 school year.
The relationship between SES and digital literacy. 
The relationship 
between family SES and digital literacy was found to be statisti­
cally significant but practically negligible (𝐵= −0.00, 𝑆𝐸= 0.00, 
95% 𝐶𝐼[−0.01, −0.00]). This near-zero finding, which indicates that SES 
shows no meaningful relationship with digital literacy in our sample, 
contradicts the conventional expectation from digital divide litera­
ture that higher SES should correlate with higher digital literacy. This 
deviation from the expected positive relationship reflects two com­
plementary mechanisms operating in the Dutch educational context. 
First, compensatory educational policies in the Netherlands provide 
schools serving lower-SES populations with additional resources for dig­
ital literacy through weighted student funding mechanisms (Kerssens 
& Van Dijck, 2023; Ladd & Fiske, 2011), actively reducing SES-based 
skill gaps. Second, higher-SES parents impose stricter limits on chil­
dren’s recreational technology use (Livingstone & Helsper, 2008; Röhlke, 
2025), paradoxically constraining informal digital skill development 
through unrestricted exploration that lower-SES children may gain 
through less-monitored access. Together, these mechanisms effectively 
level digital literacy across SES groups in this high-resource educational 
setting.
Differential effects of personality traits on AI usage patterns. 
Extraversion 
and perseverance were related to AI usage intensity in opposite direc­
tions (extraversion: 𝐵= −0.04, 95% 𝐶𝐼[−0.07, −0.01]; perseverance: 𝐵=
0.03, 95% 𝐶𝐼[0.01, 0.06]), though the resulting usage differences did not 
correlate with academic outcomes. In practical terms, more extraverted 
students used AI tools slightly less frequently, while more perseverant 
students used them slightly more, but neither pattern was correlated 
with their grades. These results might reflect that socially oriented 
students could favor peer- or teacher-mediated activities (Borg et al., 
2021) over solitary interactions with AI tools, whereas perseverant stu­
dents may sustain independent, task-focused engagement with AI tools 
(Duckworth et al., 2007) that aligns with classroom AI activities. The di­
vergence of different personality traits’ effects on AI usage highlights the 
importance of designing AI-supported tasks that accommodate both col­
laborative use for socially oriented students and sustained, self-directed 
work for perseverant students, even though such usage variations do not 
directly impact achievement in our sample.
4.3 . Implications of the study
Theoretical implications. 
This study makes two key contributions to dig­
ital divide theory in the context of AI-enhanced education. First, our 
findings demonstrate that digital divide theory remains highly appli­
cable in the age of AI, with the second-level digital divide persisting 
even after first-level divides related to access have been largely ad­
dressed through policy interventions (Van De Werfhorst et al., 2022;
Van Dijk, 2020). Bridging access gaps is insufficient; the more conse­
quential divide emerges in skills and effective use (Van Deursen & Van 
Dijk, 2014b). Our results highlight that in AI-enhanced educational en­
vironments, individual differences in digital literacy continue to serve 
as critical mechanisms through which broader inequalities manifest.
Second, our findings reveal that personality-driven digital divides, 
representing personal categorical inequalities in the digital divide frame­
work, deserve greater theoretical attention and empirical investigation 
than they have received in the past few decades (Van Dijk, 2020). 
While SES-initiated digital divides have dominated research and policy 
discourse over the past decades (Scheerder et al., 2017), our results sug­
gest that personal characteristics, particularly personality traits, show 
equally important correlations with the second-level skills divide in the 
age of AI. The personality-driven pathway to digital inequality has been 
relatively overlooked in digital divide theory, despite its potential to 
create persistent educational advantages that operate independently of 
socioeconomic factors.
Practical and policy implications. 
The findings of this study carry signifi­
cant implications for educational policy and practice. Our results suggest 
that policy efforts must fundamentally shift from simply providing AI 
tools and ensuring basic access toward cultivating the foundational dig­
ital skills that enable effective engagement with these technologies. This 
approach aligns with contemporary educational frameworks, such as 
the Dutch government’s comprehensive approach to basic skills (ba­
sisvaardigheden), which positions digital literacy alongside traditional 
competencies in language, mathematics, and citizenship as essential 
foundations for educational success (SLO, 2024), and European digital 
skills initiatives (European Commission, 2024).
The identification of personality traits as key predictors of digital 
skills carries two significant implications for educational interventions. 
First, it highlights the need to move beyond one-size-fits-all approaches 
and instead provide differentiated, heterogeneous support tailored to 
students’ differentiated personality profiles and individual learning ap­
proaches (Boekaerts, 1996; Borg et al., 2021). For instance, students

Second, beyond simply accommodating these differences, interventions

Schools may consider using diagnostic assessments of students’ open­
ness, extraversion, and perseverance to inform targeted digital literacy 
development. While our study does not directly test specific interven­
tions, our findings suggest that personality-responsive digital literacy 
instruction might be beneficial: students low in openness may benefit 
from guided exploration of digital literacy concepts with explicit ratio­
nales; those low in extraversion might respond better to small-group 
digital literacy training formats; those low in perseverance may need 
milestone-based digital skill development with incremental challenges. 
Teacher professional development focused on personality-responsive 
digital literacy pedagogy could help educators support students in 
developing the digital competencies that, as our findings show, me­
diate the relationship between personality and academic outcomes in 
AI-enhanced contexts.
Our findings also reveal important limitations regarding the scope of 
digital factors in addressing educational inequalities. The persistence of 
strong direct SES effects on academic performance, despite no positive 
mediation through AI usage or digital literacy, suggests that SES advan­
tages continue to operate through pathways that are largely independent 
of the digital factors we measured. This finding indicates that digital 
literacy and AI usage interventions alone may be insufficient to close 
SES-based achievement gaps and educational inequalities. In contrast 
to these systemic SES-driven inequalities that require broader societal 
interventions, personality-driven digital divides offer more tractable tar­
gets for educational policy, as differentiated pedagogical approaches 
within existing school structures can directly address disparities in dig­
ital skills by tailoring support to individual personality profiles and 
cultivating learning-related dispositions.
4.4 . Limitations
This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, 
the cross-sectional nature of our data prevents definitive causal con­
clusions. While our mediation analysis identified digital literacy as a 
key correlational pathway linking personality to achievement, the cross-
sectional design does not establish temporal precedence. Alternative 
causal orderings, such as bidirectional relationships or common an­
tecedents, cannot be ruled out. Longitudinal studies would be needed 
to assess developmental predictions, and causal effects require interven­
tion studies. Second, our findings were drawn from Limburg, a single 
province in the Netherlands. Beyond the Netherlands, our findings, 
particularly the minimal SES to digital literacy relationship, likely re­
flect the country’s relatively equitable digital infrastructure and mature 
school choice system (Eurostat, 2024; Kerssens & Van Dijck, 2023). 
Educational systems with greater technology access disparities or less 
developed digital infrastructure may observe stronger SES-driven digi­
tal divides than we detected. Third, our measures, while theoretically 
grounded, face certain constraints. The data were collected in Spring 
2023, just before generative AI became widely integrated into educa­
tional contexts, meaning our findings on AI usage do not reflect the inte­
gration of generative AI tools into educational contexts (OpenAI, 2022). 
Furthermore, our reliance on teacher-reported AI usage likely captures 
institutionally sanctioned “technologies for education” but overlooks in­
dividual, out-of-school engagement with general-purpose “technologies 
in education” (Bauer et al., 2025). This measurement limitation is crit­
ical because the autonomous use of “technologies in education” may 
represent the primary pathway through which new digital divides and 
educational inequalities emerge, yet our study provides no information 
on this potentially crucial dimension of AI engagement. Additionally, 
our AI usage measure relies on survey reports rather than objective be­
havioral data such as usage logs or system-generated activity records, 
which would provide more precise and unbiased measurements of actual 
engagement with AI tools. Our null AI usage finding may partly reflect 
this measurement constraint, and enhanced measurement approaches 
incorporating objective behavioral data might yield different results re­
garding AI usage effects. Using perseverance as a facet rather than the 
full conscientiousness dimension also limits direct comparability with 
studies that use complete Big Five inventories. Finally, because both per­
sonality traits and digital literacy were measured via student self-reports, 
the correlation between these constructs may be partially inflated by 
common-method bias, such as response styles or social desirability. 
Future research using performance-based digital literacy assessments or 
independent teacher ratings would mitigate this limitation and provide 
stronger evidence for the personality-digital literacy relationship.
Fourth, our study focuses exclusively on Grade 6 students in their 
final year of Dutch primary education and does not examine whether 
the relationships examined in this study vary across different devel­
opmental stages. Younger primary school students may show different 
patterns, where personality traits may be less stable and digital experi­
ence more limited, as may older secondary school students, where digital 
literacy patterns and educational contexts differ substantially. Future 
research should examine the personality-digital literacy-achievement re­
lationships across age groups to understand how developmental stage 
moderates the patterns we observe.
4.5 . Directions for future research
The limitations of this study highlight several important directions 
for future research. To address the issue of causality, longitudinal studies 
are needed to track the developmental trajectories of digital literacy and 
its relationships with personality and academic achievement over time. 
Experimental interventions could also be employed to test whether tar­
geted pedagogical strategies can mitigate personality-based differences 
in digital engagement and skill development.
To enhance the generalizability of our findings, future research 
should include cross-national studies that examine how digital di­
vides manifest across diverse educational and technological contexts. 
Furthermore, there is a pressing need to refine measurement approaches. 
Future work would benefit from incorporating behavioral measures or 
performance-based assessments of AI skills to strengthen validity (e.g., 
Jin et al., 2025). It is also crucial to develop more precise instruments 
capable of capturing individual-level AI engagement and differentiating 
between the use of institutionally provided “technologies for education” 
and general-purpose “technologies in education.” A deeper understand­
ing of the distinct inequality risks posed by each is essential for designing 
effective and equitable educational policies. Finally, as AI continues to 
evolve, ongoing research will be necessary to reconceptualize digital 
literacy and investigate the novel pathways through which individual 
characteristics shape learning outcomes in the digital age. 
5 . Conclusion
The integration of AI into primary education does not simply bridge 
old divides; it is associated with new ones, presenting a dual challenge to 
educational equity. Our findings reveal that as disparities in technologi­
cal access narrow, the digital divide is re-emerging as a skills-based gap 
correlated with students’ personality traits. For the first time, we pro­
vide empirical evidence that digital literacy serves as a key mechanism 
through which personality traits (openness, extraversion, and perse­
verance) correlate with academic advantages in AI-enhanced learning 
environments. This personality-driven divide is particularly concerning 
because it is rooted in stable individual differences, potentially creating

persistent inequalities that are harder to address than resource-based 
gaps.
Simultaneously, our results confirm that traditional socioeconomic 
divides persist with formidable strength. Family SES continues to 
demonstrate a strong direct correlation with academic performance, 
showing associations through channels beyond the digital factors we 
measured. This starkly demonstrates that AI is not a silver bullet for 
counteracting educational inequality. While AI transforms the mech­
anisms of digital disadvantage, it leaves the foundational pillars of 
socioeconomic inequality untouched.
These findings carry a clear mandate for policy and practice. Efforts 
to ensure equitable outcomes in a time of rapid AI development must 
adopt a two-pronged strategy: first, cultivating digital literacy through 
pedagogical approaches that are responsive to diverse student per­
sonalities, and second, redoubling efforts to dismantle the systemic 
socioeconomic barriers that digital advancements alone cannot erase.

A.1 . AI usage intensity construction: CatBoost weighting approach
Traditional measures of AI usage in educational settings typically 
rely on simple frequency counts or binary usage indicators, which fail to 
capture the nuanced patterns of institutional AI integration and the dif­

student A using multiple AI tools sporadically may not experience the 
same educational impact as student B who uses a single tool intensively 
in critical learning contexts. Yet, the binary or count-based measures 
treat these usage patterns equivalently.
The limitations on measurement are particularly problematic in 
heterogeneous educational environments where AI tools are deployed 
with varying intensity and contexts across different learning scenarios. 
This section details our machine learning approach to constructing a 
weighted composite measure that reflects both the extensive margin

superficial AI exposure and meaningful AI engagement in educational 
contexts. The resulting metric provides a continuous measure that bet­

compared to conventional counting approaches.

A.2 . Digital literacy questionnaire items
Digital literacy was measured using a Dutch translation of the PISA 
2018 ICT Familiarity Questionnaire (Kastberg et al., 2021), comprising 
9 items that assess two key dimensions: digital competency and digital 
autonomy. All items used a 5-point Likert scale with responses ranging 
from 1 = helemaal niet (not at all) to 5 = helemaal wel (completely), with 
all items positively coded so that larger scores indicate higher digital 
literacy. Each item is listed below in its original Dutch form, followed 
by an English translation in parentheses.
Items 1–6 assess digital competency:
1. Ik werk graag met digitale leermiddelen (I enjoy working with 
digital learning tools).
2. Ik heb er geen problemen mee om met nieuwe digitale apparaten 
te werken die ik nog niet ken (I have no problems working with 
new digital devices that I don’t know yet).
3. Als vrienden of familie nieuwe apparaten of apps willen kopen 
geef ik altijd advies (When friends or family want to buy new 
devices or apps, I always give advice).
4. Ik kan goed overweg met de digitale apparaten thuis (I can handle 
the digital devices at home well).
5. Als ik een probleem tegenkom met een van mijn digitale appa­
raten dan kan ik dat zelf oplossen (If I encounter a problem with 
one of my digital devices, I can solve it myself).
6. Als anderen problemen hebben met hun digitale apparaten dan 
kan ik hen hiermee helpen (If others have problems with their 
digital devices, I can help them with this).
Items 7–9 assess digital autonomy:
7. Als ik nieuwe software of apps nodig heb dan zoek ik zelf uit 
welke ik nodig heb (If I need new software or apps, I find out for 
myself which ones I need).
8. Als ik nieuwe software of apps nodig heb installeer ik dat zelf (If 
I need new software or apps, I install them myself).
9. Ik zoek en lees extra informatie over digitale apparaten zodat ik 
niet afhankelijk ben van anderen (I search for and read additional 
information about digital devices so that I am not dependent on 
others).

all SES measures, conclusions remain consistent with the main analy­

pattern aligns with main analysis findings and suggests that SES operates 
through direct pathways rather than digital literacy mechanisms.

C.6 . Robustness check 6: digital literacy components analysis
To examine the sensitivity of our digital literacy mediation find­
ings to measurement specification, we conducted alternative analy­
ses by decomposing the composite digital literacy measure into its 
two constituent dimensions: digital competency and digital autonomy. 
These parallel mediation models test whether the overall media­
tion effect of digital literacy operates uniformly across its technical 
proficiency component (digital competency) and its self-regulation 
and autonomy component (digital autonomy), or whether differ­
ent aspects of digital literacy account for the observed mediation
effects.
The alternative PROCESS macro specifications employed the same 
Model 4 parallel mediation framework and covariates as the main anal­
yses. However, they replaced the composite digital literacy measure 
with its two constituent dimensions (digital competency and digi­
tal autonomy) alongside AI usage intensity, yielding three separate 
mediators tested simultaneously. This approach examines the robust­
ness of our findings against measurement choices and provides insight 
into which specific mechanisms account for the observed mediation
effects.
Results demonstrate that digital competency and digital autonomy 
both serve as significant mediators, while AI usage intensity again shows 
minimal mediation effects. As shown in Fig. C.8 and Table C.8, digi­
tal competency shows significant indirect effects for openness (indirect 
effect = 0.201, 95% CI [0.043, 0.364]), extraversion (indirect effect =
0.198, 95% CI [0.043, 0.364]), and perseverance (indirect effect = 0.056, 
95% CI [0.010, 0.121]). Digital autonomy also shows significant indirect 
effects for openness (indirect effect = 0.124, 95% CI [0.013, 0.247]) and 
extraversion (indirect effect = 0.084, 95% CI [0.009, 0.176]). Family SES 
shows significant negative indirect effects through both digital compe­
tency and digital autonomy. These findings suggest that technical digital 
skills and autonomous digital usage, rather than machine learning-
derived usage patterns, primarily account for the observed mediation 
effects.

C.7.1 . Structural equation modeling versus PROCESS: methodological 
comparison
The main analyses used the PROCESS macro (Model 4) rather than 
SEM for two primary methodological reasons. First, our study focuses on 
testing specific, theory-driven mediation pathways rather than assess­
ing the global fit of a complex theoretical model. The PROCESS macro 
is explicitly designed for this path-by-path approach, allowing precise 
examination of individual indirect effects with bootstrap confidence in­
tervals (Hayes, 2022). Second, our key variables are operationalized as 
observed composite scores derived from psychometric scaling (IRT and 
CatBoost), not latent constructs requiring measurement model specifica­
tion, making the more parsimonious PROCESS approach both suitable 
and statistically powerful for our clearly defined single-mediator models.
However, SEM offers several complementary advantages that pro­
vide valuable robustness validation. SEM enables simultaneous esti­
mation of all pathways, reducing concerns about multiple testing and 
providing global model fit assessment through established indices (CFI, 
TLI, RMSEA, SRMR). Additionally, SEM can explicitly model measure­
ment error and test structural invariance across different specifications, 
offering a more comprehensive evaluation of model adequacy.
In our case, SEM serves as a robustness check rather than the primary 
analytical approach because: (1) it confirms that conclusions hold under 
simultaneous pathway estimation, (2) it demonstrates adequate model 
fit despite the complexity of modeling all relationships simultaneously, 
and (3) it validates that the theoretical framework adequately represents 
the observed covariance structure among variables.

C.7.2 . SEM model specification and fit
We specified a structural equation model that parallels the PROCESS 
macro analyses, incorporating the same predictor variables (openness, 
extraversion, perseverance, family SES), mediators (AI usage intensity, 
digital literacy), outcome variable (academic performance), and con­
trol variables. The measurement model treated personality traits and 
digital literacy as latent constructs with their constituent observed indi­
cators, while maintaining AI usage intensity and academic performance 
as observed variables consistent with their operational definitions.
Model fit was evaluated using multiple established indices. As shown 
in Table C.9, the model achieved acceptable fit on most criteria: RMSEA 
= 0.077 (90% CI [0.074, 0.080]) falls within the acceptable range (≤
0.08), and SRMR = 0.060 indicates good fit (≤ 0.08). However, CFI 
= 0.840 and TLI = 0.779 fall slightly below conventional thresholds 
(≥ 0.90), reflecting the complexity of simultaneously modeling multi­
ple theoretical constructs and their interrelationships. The significant 
𝜒2 statistic (1,975.112, 𝑝< 0.001) is expected given the large sample 
size (N = 4, 497), where even trivial model misspecifications become 
statistically detectable.

C.8 . Synthesis of robustness checks and implications
The mediation patterns remain stable regardless of how AI usage in­
tensity and digital literacy are measured. AI usage intensity remains a 
null mediator, while digital literacy remains a positive mediator for per­
sonality pathways and a statistically significant but practically negligible 
negative mediator for SES pathways. Replacing the CatBoost-weighted 
AI usage index with a simple sum score produces virtually identical 
patterns of non-significant mediation, confirming that the limited medi­
ating role of AI usage reflects genuine differences in how AI engagement 
translates into academic benefits rather than an artifact of measure­
ment complexity. Likewise, across income, wealth, and education-based 
operationalizations of SES, digital literacy consistently emerges as a sig­
nificant mediator for personality-academic performance relationships 
while AI usage shows minimal mediation effects. These patterns per­
sist whether SES is conceptualized through economic resources (income, 
wealth) or cultural capital (education).
GRM-derived and EFA-derived versions of both personality traits 
and digital literacy yield virtually identical mediation patterns. The 
EFA-derived personality traits replicate the finding that digital literacy 
mediates the personality-achievement relationship across all three traits 
(openness, extraversion, perseverance), while AI usage intensity shows 
non-significant mediation in both specifications. The EFA-derived digital 
literacy measure similarly produces consistent mediation effects across 
all four predictors, with bootstrap confidence intervals that exclude zero. 
This convergence across different psychometric modeling approaches 
provides strong evidence that the mediating role of digital competencies 
is robust to methodological choices in scale construction.
