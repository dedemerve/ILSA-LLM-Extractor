Concerns about teacher attrition and associated teacher shortages are widespread. This multilevel study
explores the relative importance of three main mechanisms through the investigation of relevant
teacher, school, and country characteristics: (A) selection mechanisms related to who becomes a teacher;
(B) early career support resources related to entering the profession; and (C) long-term support resources related to developing a teaching career. Using the 2018 Teaching And Learning International
Survey (TALIS), retention intention is analysed using information from 11 613 early-career teachers in
3998 schools in 31 countries. Results underline the importance of selection processes at individual and
country level.

1. Introduction
Teacher shortages are high on the agenda of policymakers
worldwide. Many countries are expected to suffer dramatic shortfalls in the teaching staff in the coming years, especially in secondary education (Sutcher et al., 2019; UNESCO Institute for
Statistics, 2016). Key demographic drivers for the shortage are the
rise in student enrolments and the increasing number of teachers
reaching retirement age (Ingersoll, 2001). Despite strenuous policy
efforts to attract and retain teachers, the supply of teachers is
further affected by a decline in the relative attractiveness of the
teaching profession as well as the large number of qualiﬁed
teachers leaving the profession prematurely also known as teacher

Some countries seem to experience epidemic proportions of ECT
attrition. A longitudinal study from the United States, for example,
shows that 45% of teachers leave the profession within 5 years of
entry (Ingersoll et al., 2018). Similar high ECT attrition rates are
reported for the United Kingdom (Department for Education, 2019),

Finland are often referred to as examples of educational systems
that manage to retain their teachers (Sutcher et al., 2019). Even
though exact ﬁgures on ECTs are lacking for these countries, ECT
attrition rates are expected to be well below 20% (Goodwin et al.,
2017; Lanas, 2017).
The large international differences in ECT attrition suggest that
some teacher policies are more effective than others in retaining
qualiﬁed teachers. There remains much uncertainty, however,
regarding the relevance of individual and contextual characteristics
for improving ECT retention. Indeed, although there is a vast body
of literature on ECT retention and attrition, most studies concern
(1) isolated pieces of research (Paniagua & S
anchez-Martí) that (2)
rarely take the multilevel nature of education systems into account
(Lindqvist et al., 2014). Moreover, much of the current research
originates from the US, the UK, and Australia, which may create
important knowledge gaps as policy contexts are more diverse than
those represented by these post-colonial democracies. In addition,
previous research produced an extensive list of potential risk and
protective factors for teacher retention, which can be grouped into
teacher, school, and country characteristics. Most studies pay
particular attention to the relationship between individual teacher
characteristics and factors that contribute to teachers leaving or
staying, such as perception of stress and performance efﬁcacy
(Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2016). However, surprisingly few studies have
assessed teacher characteristics in the wider school or country
context. To date, research has identiﬁed inﬂuential factors within
the schools, including leadership style (Player et al., 2017), school
identiﬁcation (Chan et al., 2008), school culture (Cohen et al., 2009),
induction and mentoring programs (Ingersoll & Strong, 2011), and
organizational structure (Ellis et al., 2017; Ingersoll, 2001). Country
factors such as salary and working conditions have been reported
as important factors in the decision to leave the teaching profession
(den Brok et al., 2017; Imazeki, 2005). Nonetheless, only a few
studies have assessed individual and school-level predictors
simultaneously (e.g., Liu, 2007; Miller & Youngs, 2021). We found
only one study that also included country-level data to contextualize the ﬁndings at individual and school-level: Qin investigated
teacher transfer and attrition intentions in relation to working
conditions using the 2013 Teaching And Learning International
Survey data (Qin, 2021). However, the mechanisms that underpin
these ﬁndings for working conditions are still not fully understood.
The available literature demonstrates a pressing need for a
comprehensive study synthesizing relevant predictors on the individual, school, and contextual level simultaneously. We
contribute to this objective in three ways. At the theoretical level, we
develop a framework to investigate the relative importance of
multidimensional mechanisms related to ECT retention intention.

1.1. Literature review
In the empirical part of this paper we focus on teachers' retention intention. A much-debated issue within attrition research is
the connection between one's intention to leave the profession and
actually leaving. Although intention has been shown to be a good
predictor for the actual decision to leave or stay at a future time
(Gersten et al., 2001; Sun & Wang, 2017), previous turnover
research has suggested moderating effects of the risk context and
personality traits in this relationship (Allen et al., 2005). In order to
provide a versatile empirical instrument, we have structured the
theoretical framework in such a way that it builds on both effective
ECT retention and ECT retention intention research ﬁndings. The
empirical literature encompasses a seemingly endless list of potential ‘risk’ factors (Burke et al., 2013). The problem with such an
extensive list is twofold: (1) no single study will ever manage to
include all these indicators simultaneously in one analysis and (2)
empirical indicators are but an operationalization of the more
fundamental mechanisms that are at play. Indeed, too strong a
focus on empirical predictors distracts attention from what really
matters: the underlying mechanisms. So, what is needed is a
framework that allows to organize predictors in such a way that it
helps policy makers to focus their actions to combat ECT attrition
rates.
Our framework synthesizes three processes emerging from the
vast body of literature on factors inﬂuencing ECT retention and ECT
retention intention. These processes correspond to key policy ﬁelds
and include indicators that can be used for speciﬁc policy measures.
As illustrated in Fig. 1, we distinguish between (A) selection
mechanisms related to who becomes a teacher (the selection process); (B) early career support resources related to entering the
profession (the entrance process); and (C) long-term support resources related to developing a career in teaching (the support and
development process). For each mechanism, we distinguish between characteristics at the individual, school, and country level.
We include both novel indicators, as well as well-established predictors to investigate the consistency of previous ﬁndings in this
large-scale multilevel study design. The proposed mechanisms are
not exhaustive and there may be other ways to structure the variables. For example, we deliberately choose not to include potential
mediating factors such as job satisfaction, self-efﬁcacy, and health.
Although previous research has established the importance of
these factors in explaining ECT retention (intention) (e.g., Kelly
et al., 2019; Schwarzer & Hallum, 2008; Skaalvik & Skaalvik,
2016), they may also mediate the effects of the more exogeneous
factors that we study in this paper. Moreover, in anticipation of new

1.1.1. Becoming a teacher?
One of the key objectives in retaining qualiﬁed teachers is
attracting skilled and motivated candidates to become teachers.
Not everybody matches up to the variety of personality traits, and
the intellectual, social, and emotional skills demanded by the
teaching profession. Although views on teaching differ across cultures and school systems (Frost, 2012), the European Commission's
Thematic Working group on Teacher Professional Development
agrees on a number of core teaching competence requirements
(similar descriptions can be found for other OECD regions).
Teachers are expected to be knowledgeable experts with a deep
and broad understanding of their class subjects, as well as
education-related topics. They are efﬁcient educators with strong
interpersonal, reﬂective, and inquiring attitudes. In addition,
teachers are expected to be lifelong learners who are oriented towards professional development, collaboration, diversity, and inclusion. Further core requirements refer to teachers' capacity of

student teacher and ECT (Wilkins & Comber, 2015). Compared to
ﬁrst-career teachers, second-career teachers experience higher job
satisfaction and lower job stress a decade after graduation (Troesch
& Bauer, 2017).
Third, most previous research has emphasized the importance
of intrinsic, altruistic, and extrinsic motivations in starting a career
in teaching (e.g., Bergmark et al., 2018; Brookhart & Freeman, 1992).
However, much less is known about the relation with intentions of
leaving the profession prematurely. Intrinsically motivated behaviours commonly refer to actions that are primarily for the pleasure
and satisfaction derived from performing them (e.g., educating
students). Closely related to this, altruistic motivation refers to
actions intended to enhance the welfare of others without concern
for oneself (e.g., wanting to contribute to society). Extrinsic motivation is commonly related to characteristics of the job itself and
focuses on the goal-driven reasons for individual behaviour (e.g.,
job beneﬁts). The teaching profession has been characterized by
high levels of intrinsic and altruistic motivation (Manuel & Hughes,
2006), which have been associated with a greater commitment to
teaching later in the career (Watt & Richardson, 2008). However,
there remains relevant individual-level variation among teachers
and it is not clear how teaching motivation develops over time. For
example, it can be hypothesized that when a loss in intrinsic and
altruistic motivations occurs, it may be more difﬁcult to recover
from, leaving frustrated ECTs to turn their back on their profession.
Fourth, related to the concept of extrinsic motivation, teacher
salary is an important factor in choosing or leaving any job (e.g.,
Ingersoll, 2001). High teacher salaries have been found to be
effective in attracting and retaining teachers (Hendricks, 2015).
Teacher salary satisfaction has been widely investigated in ECT
retention intention research, but less attention has been paid to the
wider policy context of income comparisons. As teacher salaries are
often regulated at the country level and -in some countries- at
school level, a potential effect of individual perceptions of
compensation on ECT retention intention may differ depending on
the overarching teacher reimbursement context (European
Commission/EACEA/Eurydice, 2019; OECD, 2019a).
Finally, one of the most important selection processes to become
a teacher is regulated at the country level. In addition to diploma
requirements, some educational systems control the supply of new
teachers through policy measures aimed at screening, selecting, or
appointing teacher candidates. A number of assessment strategies
can be employed to examine candidates' teaching motivations,
subject knowledge, or basic skills (e.g., literacy), including
competitive exams and interviews. Stricter entry requirements for
teacher trainings can have two main effects on ECT retention. First,
the selected teachers will better ﬁt the demands of the teaching
profession and are therefore more likely to become successful
teachers. Second, being labelled as ‘suitable for the teaching profession’ by a test or selection procedure may have a ‘labelling effect’
that makes aspiring teachers feel special and willing to live up to
this ﬁrst positive assessment. Previous research has established an
association between teacher quality and national teacher selection
mechanisms (Ingvarson & Rowley, 2017), but, to the best of our
knowledge, the link between teacher retention and entry requirements has never been investigated within the wider framework of teacher and school characteristics.
In order to fully grasp the relative importance of selection processes for ECT retention intention, we also consider a number of
inﬂuential indicators that were already identiﬁed in previous
teacher retention intention research. Work time is included as
studies have shown that part-time teachers have higher earlycareer attrition rates than full-time teachers (e.g., Boe et al.,
2008). ECT retention may also be inﬂuenced by various selection
effects of matching a speciﬁc ECT into a speciﬁc school. Among a

number of school characteristics, previous research suggests that
high-income schools with a homogenous student population are
more effective in attracting high-skilled teachers and are better at
retaining them (Barbieri et al., 2011; Lankford et al., 2002).
Furthermore, teachers' perception of the societal status of their
profession may play a role in the process of becoming a teacher, as
well as in the decision to leave the profession. Teachers who believe
the profession is highly valued in society are more conﬁdent and
committed to their profession, which may potentially reduce the
intention to leave the profession (e.g., Hargreaves, 2009). A comparison of teachers' status in 35 countries around the globe reveals
large international variation in the societal appreciation of the
teaching profession (Dolton et al., 2018). Because of this, we
consider an individual-level and a country-level indicator to gauge
a potential relationship between teachers’ societal status and ECT
retention intention.
In sum, for part A ‘Becoming a teacher’, we expect higher ECT
retention intentions when ECTs perceive teaching as the ﬁrst career
choice; have non-teaching professional experiences; report high
levels of teaching motivation; have higher salary satisfaction levels;
work full-time; and perceive strong societal appreciation for their
profession. At the school level, we hypothesize that ECT retention
intention will be lower in schools with lower salary satisfaction
levels and more disadvantaged student population. At the country
level, we expect to ﬁnd higher ECT retention intentions when
countries offer higher starting salaries for ECTs, require aspiring
teachers to pass a competitive exam before entering teacher
training programs and teachers report a strong perceived societal
appreciation from teachers' work.
1.1.2. Entering the profession
The early stages of the teaching career can be an overwhelming
and stressful period. Teachers are required to be experts in their
subject matter and pedagogy, as well as being captivating performers, classroom managers, administrative workers, and much
more (European Commission, 2013b). It seems difﬁcult for one individual to master every aspect of this varied list, let alone at the
very beginning of the career. Although the development of these
competences is supposed to be a permanent endeavour throughout
teachers' career as a professional (European Commission, 2013b), it
can put a lot of pressure on new teachers. ECTs often experience a
‘reality shock’ or a difﬁcult transition from the relative comfort of
being student teachers to the many responsibilities in their own
classroom (Kelchtermans, 2019). Supporting new teachers during
the early formative stages of their career has been the topic of
considerable debate among scholars and policy makers. In the
following paragraphs, we outline the most important support resources in this early phase and their relation to ECT retention.
A ﬁrst important resource to support teachers during the early
stages of their teaching career is to provide adequate preparation
during the teacher training. Previous research suggests that new
teachers who feel prepared also intend to stay in the profession for
longer periods of time (Darling-Hammond et al., 2002; DeAngelis
et al., 2013), potentially because they are better equipped to
handle challenges in the early stages of their career. The perceived
quality of the pre-service training has been associated with
perceived quality of support, and attrition intention among
Australian ECTs (Kelly et al., 2019). However, to our knowledge,
these factors have never been examined simultaneously to gauge
international differences in ECTs retention intentions.
The second resource included in the framework refers to early
career support measures. Induction and mentoring programs have
been widely acknowledged as measures to help new teachers to
adjust to their working environments and thus enhance ECT
retention (intention) (Ingersoll & Strong, 2011). However, the

2. Methodology and methods
2.1. Data
Data were derived from the most recent wave of the Teaching
And Learning International Survey (TALIS), which was conducted in
2018 among 48 countries and economies. TALIS is a large-scale
survey of teachers, schools, and their learning environments
organized by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and
Development (OECD). TALIS 2018 primarily focused on lower secondary education (ISCED 2), which was used for the purpose of this
study. Schools were selected using systematic two-stage random
sampling with probability proportional to size within explicit strata
by country (OECD, 2019b). Separate questionnaires were completed
by the principal and randomly selected teachers. The current study
focuses on European countries, as well as a selection of highachieving Asian and North-American educational systems according to recent PISA results (OECD, 2019d). After deleting cases which
had missing values for one of the variables included in the analyses,
our ﬁnal country selection for the multilevel analyses amounts to
31 countries/regions (See appendix). Following the TALISmethodological design, the two regional educational systems in
Belgium were analysed separately (Flemish community and
French-speaking community). In the countries included in this
study, principals’ participation ranged between 71% in Denmark to
100% in 8 countries, including Austria. Overall teacher participation
by country was very high and ranged from 62% in Denmark to
almost 100% in Singapore (OECD, 2019d).
ECTs were deﬁned as teachers with no more than 5 years of
teaching experience, consistent with previous research (Hong,
2012; Rosenholtz & Simpson, 1990). Teachers older than 45 years
were not included in our analyses in accordance with previous
studies on the retirement intention of older employees (Van
Droogenbroeck & Spruyt, 2014; Schreurs et al., 2011). As a result,
our study population consisted of 11 613 ECTs from 3998 schools in
31 OECD countries. Appendix A1 provides an overview of the study
population by country or region.

1.1.3. Developing a career
Support for ECTs is not limited to the policy measures specifically targeting new teachers. ECTs may also beneﬁt from general
support available for all teachers. Additionally, the decision to leave
or remain in teaching can be inﬂuenced by the professional prospects for ECTs. What kind of resources are available to support ECTs
in the later stages of their career? Factors related to school culture,
school leadership and professional development are ﬁrmly
anchored in most teacher policies (European Commission/EACEA/
Eurydice, 2018) and their relevance has been well-established in
previous ECT retention intention research. However, there remains
uncertainty about the relative importance of teacher, school, and
country factors related to these indicators, and whether the other
mechanisms of becoming a teacher and entering the profession
might not be more inﬂuential for ECT retention intention.
First, the perception of the school culture has been reported to
inﬂuence ECTs commitment to teaching and the decision to leave
the profession (Allensworth et al., 2009). Speciﬁcally, schools
characterized by mutual respect, collaborative cultures and common educational objectives are more successful in retaining earlycareer teachers (Long et al., 2012). The ﬁt between ECTs and the
school environment has been identiﬁed in recent longitudinal
research from the United States as an important factor for ECT
turnover and attrition decisions (Miller et al., 2020).
Second, school leadership plays an important role in teachers'
perceptions of the school culture as well as on teachers’ decisions to
leave (Burkhauser, 2017). Supportive and communicative leadership has been associated with lower teacher turnover (Kraft et al.,
2016; Rothmann & Fouch
e, 2018). Qualitative research indicates
that ECTs experience higher levels of support when school leaders
provide strategic guidance for them and try to accommodate
ongoing learning-opportunities (Sullivan & Morrison, 2014). ECT
retention is expected to be higher when school leaders show they
care about ECTs challenges and also provide the necessary tools to
overcome them.

increase teacher resilience and self-efﬁcacy, and along this way to
enhance ECT retention intentions. Different types of in-service

Gender was operationalized using a dummy variable, contrasting female (coded 1) and male (coded 0) teachers. Age was treated

2.3.1. Becoming a teacher
Initial outlook: Teaching ﬁrst choice (Level 1) builds on teachers'
answers of whether or not teaching was their ﬁrst career choice
(Yes ¼ 1; No ¼ 0). Prior work experience: Lateral entry teachers (Level
1) is a dummy-variable building on past and present work experiences. All non-educational experiences exceeding one year were
considered a proxy for lateral entry (coded 1). Work time: full-time
(Level 1) accounts for differences in the work duration. Full-time
work (coded 1) was deﬁned by working more than 90% of fulltime working hours in all teaching employments combined. Parttime work (coded 0) refers to work hours between 90% and less
than 50% of full-time hours. Intrinsic and altruistic motivation: the
social utility motivation scale (Level 1) from the Factors Inﬂuencing
Teaching Choice frame-work (FIT-Choice) was grand mean centred
to control for ECT's intrinsic and altruistic motivations in general
(Watt & Richardson, 2008). Teachers responded on a 4-point Likert
scale (anchored 1 ¼ “not important at all’ to 4 “of high importance”)
to three statements (e.g., “Teaching allowed me to inﬂuence the
development of children and young people”; OECD, 2019b).
Extrinsic motivation: the personal utility motivation scale (Level 1)
from the FIT-choice framework was grand mean centred to account
for ECTs initial extrinsic motivation to become a teacher. A 4-point
Likert scale (anchored 1 ¼ “not important at all’ to 4 “of high
importance”) was constructed using statements such as “Teaching
offered a steady career path” (OECD, 2019b). Teacher salary: Salary
satisfaction (Level 1 & 2) provides a subjective measure for extrinsic
motivation. Teachers were asked whether they were satisﬁed with
the salary they receive for their work. Answers were recorded using
a 4-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly
agree. To disentangle the within-group and between-group relationships of teachers' salary satisfaction with retention intention,
we group mean centred ECTs' at the individual and school level.

As explained earlier, one objective of this paper is to redirect the
focus from the relevance of speciﬁc separate indicators towards a
more general assessment that aims at synthesis. In order to get a
more comprehensive understanding of the importance of the three
theoretical parts in explaining retention intention, we examined
the explained variance and ICCs between models. The variance
decomposition of the unconditional model (model 0 with 31
countries) indicated that 3.6 percent of the variance in retention
intention could be explained by differences between schools and
11.3 percent by differences between countries. Model 8 (with 31
countries) explained 14.3 percent of the total variance in retention
intention. Conversely, model 9 (with 25 countries) combined all
three mechanisms and explained 17.2 percent of the total variance
in retention intention. Speciﬁcally, 14.0 percent of the variance in
retention intention at the teacher level, 13.1 percent of the variance
at the school level and 48.9 percent of the variance at the country
level in comparison with the unconditional model of the restricted
sample (model 0R*).

the school level and 36.9 percent of the variance at the country
level. The total explained variance of mechanism A was 14.7
percent. For mechanism B ‘Entering the profession’, the total
explained variance was 6.1 percent. For mechanism C ‘Developing a

4. Discussion and conclusion
Against the background of current and coming teacher shortages this paper studied the retention intention among early career
teachers (ECT). In order to bridge the gap between theory and
policy, we have synthesized the relevant teacher, school, and
country characteristics in three main mechanisms: (A) selection
processes related to who becomes a teacher; (B) early career support resources related to entering the profession; and (C) long-term
support resources related to developing a career in teaching. The
usefulness of this framework was assessed based on new data from
the most recent wave of the Teaching And Learning International
Survey (TALIS) 2018. These data enabled us to study predictors at
the individual, school, and country level. The combination of both
factors (three theoretical mechanism and three different levels of
analysis) renders this study one of the most comprehensive study
on ECT retention intention thus far.

suggested that personality traits, the risk context and the motives
underlying the intention are important factors to understand the
actual decision to leave the profession (Allen et al., 2005;

believe that ECT retention intention is a good proxy measure when
an objective measure for ECT retention is lacking. As a consequence,

Second, in this paper, for reasons explained earlier, we deliberately did not include every possible inﬂuential factor. We chose to
assess the three main mechanisms using a selection of relevant
indicators after carefully reviewing different theoretical concepts
and operationalizations of these concepts.2 With regard to potential mediating factors such as job satisfaction, self-efﬁcacy, and
health, we performed additional robustness checks, controlling the
presented models for self-reported job satisfaction and mental
work-related health. These results show (1) substantial and theoretically consistent signiﬁcant effect parameters for job satisfaction
and mental health but also (2) that the main conclusions for the
variables we focus on remain when controlling for these mediators.
Third, even though the 2018 TALIS data are a rich data source,
they also have some limitations. Due to the cross-sectional nature
of the data, this study cannot make any claims on the causal relationships between teacher, school, and country characteristics,
and ECT retention intention. Our investigation relied strongly on
subjective measures, which may not fully correspond with the
objective reality. That said, it can also be argued that the personal
interpretation of this reality may be more inﬂuential in highly
subjective processes such as deciding to leave the profession. The
present study included 31 countries, the majority of which European countries or countries with high-achieving educational systems. In order to assess if the patterns that we found also hold in
other countries, further international research is required.
Despite these limitations, this study contributes to the ﬁeld of
ECT retention in three main ways. First, to the best of our knowledge this is the ﬁrst study that investigated how diverse teacher,
school-, and country-level characteristics are related to retention
intention among ECTs. Not only do we provide a framework which
can be easily translated to diverse policy contexts and used in new
retention (intention) research, our results clearly demonstrate the
importance of the selection process of teachers. More speciﬁcally,
we identiﬁed two inﬂuential teacher characteristics (i.e., teaching
as a ﬁrst career choice and intrinsic motivation) and two important
country-level characteristics (i.e., higher starting salaries and a
competitive exam) which (1) are clearly related to higher retention
intention, and (2) can be easily targeted by educational policies.
Finally, the present study ﬁlled a fundamental data gap and
established a new freely-available database containing countrylevel information on teacher training and support policies.

In sum, our results demonstrate that selection processes related
to becoming a teacher provide an important explanation for the
variation in ECT retention intentions and that this variation can be
largely explained by teacher- and country-level predictors. Indeed,
the results from our multilevel linear regression analyses corroborate the relevance of looking into teacher, school, and country

seems to explain most of the differences in ECT retention intention,
our ﬁnal model was most effective in explaining the variation in
ECT retention intention, demonstrating the importance of considering the three mechanisms together. Still, further validation of this
framework is required to determine whether similar results are
found with other data sources and alternative measures.
What policy implications do our results have? Building a stable
teaching force seems to depend largely on the teachers’ proﬁle.
Selecting aspiring teachers who dream of a career in teaching, and
who are driven by intrinsic and altruistic motivations, increases the
likelihood of ECTs wanting to remain in the profession for longer
periods of time. In this way, our results underline the importance of
increasing the attractiveness of the teaching profession among
young people. Promoting the merits of the profession early on may
induce a professional calling and generate teachers that retain for
the long run. Our ﬁndings also support the prioritization of
developing an economically attractive teaching profession because
even intrinsically motivated people care about their pocketbooks.
We found some indications that it may be a good idea to
administer aptitude tests before entering pre-service teacher
training to shed some light on these key drivers among aspiring
teachers. However, caution is warranted when attaching formal
entry requirements to these tests. A too strict interpretation of
motivational drive may unnecessarily reduce the often already
limited number of potential teacher candidates. Motivations have
also been found to change over time (Watt & Richardson, 2012), so
it is possible that aspiring teachers become more intrinsically
motived during their training or while performing their job. Hence,
we suggest that policy efforts should target pre- and in-service
teacher training to further cultivate intrinsic and altruistic motivations. Our results do indicate that a skill-based selection of
teacher candidates may be an effective measure in retaining ECTs as
we ﬁnd longer expected teaching careers for ECTs who passed a
mandatory competitive exam. Competitive exams have a number of
policy implications. The very best candidates are selected
enhancing the overall educational quality (Corcoran & O'Flaherty,
2018). Labelling effects may provide a powerful stimulus on its
own, further inﬂuencing ECT retention (Graham & Williams, 2009).
High recruitment requirements are an acknowledged method to
increase the attractiveness of the teaching profession (European
Commission, 2013a). This confronts policy makers with a
paradox: on the one hand competitive exams seem to improve the
quality of teachers and prolongs the length of teacher careers, on
the other hand it reduces the number of potential teachers that can
apply for a teaching job in the short term. In times of teacher
shortages, a choice between quality or quantity is a challenging one
to make.
