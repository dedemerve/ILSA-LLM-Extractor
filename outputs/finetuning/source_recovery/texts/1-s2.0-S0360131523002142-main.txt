How well does the digital home learning environment predict ICT 
literacy and ICT self-efficacy? Comparing the predictive power of 
adolescent and parent reports 
Martin Senkbeil 
Leibniz Institute for Science and Mathematics Education, Kiel, Germany   
A R T I C L E I N F O  
Keywords: 
ICT literacy 
ICT self-efficacy 
Adolescent and parent reports 
Home learning environment 
Parental mediation 
A B S T R A C T  
Although the home environment is considered as a key factor in the acquisition of children’s ICT 
literacy, only few studies have examined the relationship between ICT-related parental beliefs 
and practices and children’s ICT literacy. Moreover, most of these studies, predominantly based 
on the construct of parental mediation, use self-assessments which are not considered valid in­
dicators of actual ICT literacy. By investigating different aspects of a digital home learning 
environment, we extended prior research by (a) using an objective test of ICT literacy to examine 
this question. In addition, (b) we included children’s ICT self-efficacy as a second outcome var­
iable, which is an important motivational variable for self-directed and lifelong learning, and (c) 
to gain a comprehensive understanding of ICT-related parental beliefs and practices, both 
adolescent and parent perspectives were considered and examined for their predictive power. 
Using data from 422 children (aged between 11 and 14 years) and their parents, adolescent and 
parent reports showed medium correlations. The results show that parental ICT values (beliefs 
about the importance of being able to work with ICT) positively predict ICT literacy while 
monitoring/supervision (e.g., controlling children’s online activities) negatively predicts ICT 
literacy. According to predictive power, after controlling for gender and students’ educational 
level, adolescent reports predicted ICT self-efficacy (27%) better than parent reports (9%), while 
the opposite was found for ICT literacy (adolescents: 8%; parents: 18%).   
1. Introduction 
For most of the young people in developed countries, using digital media plays a central role in everyday life. They have grown up 
with this technology and are characterized by their familiarity with information and communication technologies (ICT) (Fraillon, 
Ainley, Schulz, Friedman, & Duckworth, 2019). In OECD countries 91% of 15-year-olds report access to a smartphone, 74% have 
access to a portable laptop, and on average they spend almost two and a half hours online outside of school (Burns & Gottschalk, 2019). 
Thus, young people are often assumed to show great willingness and ability to use ICT but, for example, the International Computer 
and Information Literacy Study (ICILS) 2018 demonstrates that more than one quarter of students in all but one country lack basic ICT 
knowledge and skills (33% in the case of Germany) (Fraillon et al., 2019). This is a crucial aspect because ICT literacy is considered a 
key competence that plays an important role in many educational and workplace settings (Siddiq, Hatlevik, Olsen, Throndsen, & 
Scherer, 2016). Since young people use ICT more often at home than at school (Fraillon et al., 2019), children’s home environment

Chaudron, 2016). Correspondingly, research has shown that young people are exposed to computers at home from an early age 
(Chaudron, Di Gioia, & Gemo, 2018) and that ICT-related home factors were more predictive for ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy in

Furthermore, parents try to support the acquisition of their children’s ICT literacy by using a wide range of mediation strategies. 
However, little is known about how home factors, especially parents’ beliefs, and behaviors, affect children’s ICT literacy (Cabel­
lo-Hutt, Cabello, & Claro, 2018; Nikken & Schols, 2015; Rodriguez-de-Dios, van Oosten, & Igartua, 2018). Insofar as studies have been 
conducted on this issue, ICT literacy was used almost exclusively on the basis of self-assessed skills (Haddon, Cino, Doyle, Livingstone, 
Mascheroni, & Stoilova, 2020). However, self-ratings are not considered valid indicators of actual ICT literacy (Palczynska & Rynko, 
2021). 
Against this background, the present study’s aim was to extend the field of research in several aspects by adapting the framework of 
the home learning environment (HLE) to the field of ICT (Bonanati & Buhl, 2021; Toth et al., 2020): First, we use an objective test to 
investigate to what extent different aspects of digital HLE are related to ICT literacy. Second, children’s ICT self-efficacy is included as a 
second outcome variable since it represents an important person characteristic for continuous lifelong learning with digital media 
which has rarely been studied (Aesaert & van Braak, 2014; Bonanati & Buhl, 2021). Third, given a lack of studies that include both 
children’s and parents’ perspectives, which is necessary for a comprehensive understanding of ICT-related parental involvement 
(Beyens & Valkenburg, 2019; Symons, Ponnet, Emmery, Walrave, & Heirman, 2017), the present study investigates both perspectives 
for differences in their reports as well as in their predictive power with regard to ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy. 
2. ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy 
ICT literacy is understood as a meta-competence that helps people to acquire important competencies and skills for educational and 
work situations and to achieve private goals (Fraillon et al., 2019; Siddiq et al., 2016). More recent conceptualizations of ICT literacy 
integrate technological and cognitive aspects to define ICT literacy. For instance, the Educational Testing Service (ETS) describes ICT 
literacy as “using digital technologies, communication tools and/or networks to access, manage, integrate, evaluate and create in­
formation in order to function in a knowledge society” (p. 16; see also Senkbeil, 2022; Siddiq et al., 2016). This means that ICT literacy 
requires technological literacy as well as higher-order cognitive skills (e.g., problem-solving skills), for example, when searching for 
trustworthy and useful information on the internet (Senkbeil and Ihme, 2017; van Laar, van Deursen, van Dijk, & de Haan, 2017). 
Furthermore, in this technologically driven world, students need to acquire new knowledge and skills in a self-regulated way over 
the life span. Since lifelong learning – defined as a „self-motivated act to expand one’s own knowledge” (Kaur & Beri, 2016, p. 1365) – 
is increasingly enabled through digital media, ICT self-efficacy is an important driver that helps students to deal with technological 
progress (Bonanati & Buhl, 2021; Goldhammer, Gniewosz, & Zylka, 2017). Correpondingly, not only objective skills impact the 
effective use of digital media, but also subjective competence beliefs as they determine whether and how individuals use them (Chen, 
Hsiao, Chern, & Chen, 2014; Moos & Azevedo, 2009). Thus, „although related, competences and competence beliefs should be 
differentiated, as they independently influence learning, motivation, and performance” (Ulfert-Blank & Schmidt, 2022, p. 2). 
According to the expectancy-value model (EVM; Eccles & Wigfield, 2002), self-efficacy has been shown to affect effort, persistence, 
and the use of learning strategies in diverse areas. Individuals with high self-efficacy, defined as the perceived abilities of individuals, i. 
e. what individuals believe they can do with the abilities they possess, are more likely to engage and persist longer when faced with 
difficult and time-consuming tasks than those persons with low self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997). Numerous studies demonstrated these 
relationships in the area of digital learning (see Hammer, Scheiter, & Stürmer, 2021 for a summary). 
3. Home learning environment (early adolescence) 
To better understand how the home environment is related to children’s ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy, we focus on the role of 
parental involvement in children’s ICT-related activities, i.e., factors that are changeable and can be modified through interventions 
(Gonzalez-deHass, Willems, Powers, & Musgrove, 2022; Gruchel, Kurock, Bonanati, & Buhl, 2022). The conceptualization of home 
learning environment (HLE) provides a useful framework for this issue. The HLE is one of the contexts in which young children develop

important competencies and which influence their long-term development (into adolescence), i.e., they acquire skills, and behaviors 
that have the potential to influence their motivations toward learning, and their subsequent academic achievement. The HLE can be 
understood as a combination of educational resources and activities, e.g., including various activities that promote children’s learning, 
although learning is not the focus of the activity (Sammons, Toth, Sylva, Melhuish, Siraj, & Taggart, 2015). 
According to the widely used framework of Kluczniok and colleagues (2013), the HLE can be understood as a structure-process 
model of educational quality that encompasses three different dimensions: (a) structural characteristics, (b) parental beliefs, and (c) 
educational processes (see Fig. 1). The structural characteristics include longer-term and less changeable aspects, for example educa­
tional background, or availability of learning materials, e.g. digital media. Parental beliefs refer to a wide range of different types of 
beliefs, e.g., values towards a certain domain, or aspirations regarding their child’s academic career. Educational processes refer to the 
educational interactions that the child experiences in interacting with his or her family, especially with his or her parents (Kluczniok, 
Lehrl, Kuger, & Rossbach, 2013; Toth et al., 2020). Within this model, educational processes constitute the main factor assuming that 
they display a direct influence on child development while structural characteristics and parental beliefs are intermediated by 
educational processes, and thus, influence child outcomes indirectly (Kluczniok et al., 2013; see Fig. 1). 
Since the focus of the present study relies on early adolescence (10–14 years), age-group-specific characteristics in the home 
learning environment are to be taken into account. Research has shown that involvement changes over time as the child develops and 
time spent on direct involvement activities decreases (Sammons et al., 2015; Toth et al., 2019). Parents tend to have more supervisory 
and advisory roles to create the conditions under which academic success can be fostered, e.g., by „enhancing motivation, or struc­
turing and monitoring free time and homework time” (Boonk, Gijselaers, Ritzen, & Brand-Gruwel, 2018, 2022; Sammons et al., 2015; 
Toth et al., 2020). Correspondingly, Bradley et al. (2000) who investigated the home learning environment in early adolescence 
identified the following important educational processes for this age group (see also Bradley, Pennar, Fuligni, & Whiteside-Mansell, 
2019): (a) instructions (parental help, and support, e.g., during homework; interactive parent-child activities such as shared reading 
activities); (b) motivation (e.g., encouragement, such as praising their children’s performance and efforts), (c) parental supervision (e.g. 
making sure that the child completes homework), and (d) regulative activities (e.g., establishing rules for behavior). Research within the 
HLE framework has demonstrated the effectiveness of the mentioned HLE components in early adolescence with regard to academic 
success and motivation (Lehrl, Evangelou, & Sammons, 2020; Niklas, Cohrssen, Lehrl, & Napoli, 2021; Sammons et al., 2015; Toth 
et al., 2019). 
4. Digital home learning environment (early adolescence) 
Although there is a growing body of research examining HLE or parental involvement within the field of ICT (Gonzalez-deHass 
et al., 2022; Niklas et al., 2021), a conceptualization that provides an overview of several different dimensions has been lacking. For 
example, the well-researched construct of parental mediation explores parental strategies (e.g., instructional support, rules) for 
regulating children’s ICT use to maximize the opportunities and minimize the risks that digital media may pose to their children (Chen 
& Shi, 2019; Livingstone, Olafsson, Helsper, Lupianez-Villanueva, Veltri, & Folkvord, 2017; Nikken & Schols, 2015). However, 
parental mediation is considered a fuzzy construct due to substantial conceptual and methodological differences in identifying stra­
tegies (Bonanati & Buhl, 2021; Symons et al., 2017) which does not account for additional ICT-related HLE components beyond 
parental practices either (e.g., parental beliefs and values; Nichols & Selim, 2022). Only recently, a conceptualization for the digital 
home learning environment was presented using the HLE framework to systemize different components of the ICT-oriented home 
situation (Bonanati & Buhl, 2021). This concept focuses on an adaptation of the educational processes in early adolescence identified 
by Bradley et al. (2000) with reference to results from parental mediation research. 
The conceptualization presented in this study combines the digital HLE model with the mentioned structure-process model 
(Kluczniok et al. (2013) by adapting the three dimensions of (a) structural characteristics, (b) parental beliefs, and (c) educational 
processes (instructions, motivation, parental supervision, and regulative activities) to the field of ICT (see Fig. 2 for the relevant 
constructs of this study). We describe each component of the digital HLE and the relationships of each component with ICT literacy and 
ICT self-efficacy based on the available research literature. If no or only very few ICT-related results are available, findings from

4.1. Structural family characteristics: ICT access 
Structural characteristics are often operationalized in digital media research as ICT access. ICT access at home refers to the op­
portunities parents create for their children to develop ICT literacy by providing them with the necessary digital devices in the sense of 
learning materials (Aesaert et al., 2015). Although almost every household has digital devices, there may still be a difference between 
digital devices being present in the home and the child having access to them (e.g., access to a computer at any time, owning a 
computer). Numerous studies investigating adolescents (Grades 5 to 9; age: 10–16 years) reported positive and direct associations of 
ICT access with ICT literacy (Senkbeil et al., 2013, 2022; Fraillon et al., 2019; Hatlevik et al., 2018) as well as with ICT self-efficacy 
(Aesaert & van Braak, 2014; Tsai & Tsai, 2010; Zhong, 2011), although in the vast majority the correlations are low (r ≤0.15). 
4.2. Parental beliefs: parental ICT values 
Research on parental involvement demonstrated that parental values and expectations seem to be the most important positive 
predictors of students’ academic self-efficacy and achievement (Boonk et al., 2018, 2022; Erdem & Kaya, 2020; Fan &Williams, 2010. 
This relationship can be explained with the EVM (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002). Parents have child-specific beliefs about how they value a 
domain for their child, which influence their parenting behavior by imparting their own beliefs and values through direct instruction 
or by acting as role models (e.g., engaging in valued activities). Additionally, parents act as ‘interpreters of reality’ by conveying 
messages to their children about their value of various domains (Hammer et al., 2021). That means that children perceive, interpret, 
and internalize their parents’ values, which in turn influence their own expectation of success (Jacobs & Eccles, 2000) as well as their 
academic engagement and effort (Fan & Williams, 2010). 
With regard to ICT, parental ICT utility values (conceptualized as „parents’ beliefs about the general importance and usefulness of 
being able to work with a computer”; Aesaert et al., 2015, p. 59) were significantly associated with students’ (Grades 5 to 7; age: 10–14 
years) ICT literacy (r = 0.16) as well as ICT self-efficacy (0.10 ≤r ≤0.24; Aesaert & van Braak, 2014; Bonanati & Buhl, 2021; Hammer 
et al., 2021). Moreover, in contrast to the assumptions of the HLE model, parental values does not only seem to be indirectly (via 
parental parenting processes) but also directly associated with both variables (Boonk et al., 2018, 2022; Fan & Williams, 2010). Thus, it 
is assumed that parental ICT values are also directly positively related to both ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy. 
4.3. Educational processes: ICT-related parental mediation practices 
4.3.1. Instructions: Instructional support 
Parental instructions are labeled as instructional support within the parental mediation framework and refer to practices such as 
helping the child to find specific information, supporting the child when asked for advice or help, or suggesting ways to use the Internet 
safely (Livingstone et al., 2017; Nikken & Schols, 2015). According to HLE research, instructional or learning support (e.g., computing 
activities, shared reading activities) positively affects the children’s motivational beliefs (e.g., their self-efficacy) and academic 
achievement (Boonk, Gijselaers, Ritzen, & Brand-Gruwel, 2022; Gonida & Cortina, 2014; Sammons et al., 2015). 
With regard to the digital HLE framework, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, only three studies have investigated this issue 
among adolescents (Grades 5 to 7; age: 10–14 years) and found no significant correlations with ICT literacy (Aesaert et al., 2015; Zhu, 
Yang, MacLeod, Yu, & Wu, 2019) and even a negative correlation with ICT self-efficacy (Bonanati & Buhl, 2021). The authors conclude 
that instructional support may be perceived as parental control by the child, especially if parents frequently interrupt their children’s 
digital media activities. Nevertheless, it can be assumed that instructional support enhances the child’s actual ICT literacy and ICT 
self-efficacy in dealing with everyday problems (e.g., searching for specific information on the Internet) when parents’ guidance is 
autonomy supportive, e.g., requests for parental support are child-initiated, allowing the child to solve problems on his or her own) 
(Livingstone et al., 2017; see also Beyens & Valkenburg, 2019). 
4.3.2. Motivation: Psychological support 
Motivation was predominantly investigated in parental mediation research using the construct of psychological support 
(encouragement) (Dürager & Sonck, 2014; Vekiri, 2010). Parental involvement research shows that when parents provide encour­
agement and express positive values (e.g., praising children’s progress and efforts), children develop a preference for challenging tasks 
and are more likely to have positive beliefs about their academic abilities (Fan & Williams, 2010; Gonzalez-deHass et al., 2005, 2022). 
Moreover, parental encouragement is positively correlated with academic achievement. Especially, adolescents seem to benefit from 
academic encouragement (Boonk et al., 2018; Hill & Tyson, 2009). 
With regard to ICT literacy, research has shown that encouraging adolescents (fifth and sixth graders) to explore and learn things on 
the internet on their own is associated with higher confidence in their ICT skills (Vekiri, 2010; r = 33, p < .01) and more learning 
opportunitities to gain ICT skills, which in turn should lead to deeper engagement with the internet and higher ICT skills (Livingstone 
et al., 2017; Mascheroni et al., 2016). Accordingly, psychological support is expected to be positively associated with ICT literacy and 
ICT self-efficacy. It should be mentioned that both processes (instructional support, psychological support) are often combined into one 
dimension, namely active mediation, within the parental mediation framework, rather than conceptualized as separate dimensions 
werden (e.g., Cabello-Hutt et al., 2018; Dürager & Sonck, 2014; Sciacca, Laffan, O’Higgins Norman, & Milosevic, 2022).

4.3.3. Parental supervision: ICT-related monitoring/supervision 
Parental supervision is often operationalized in ICT research through the practice monitoring/supervision. Monitoring refers to 
parents’ checking children’s online activities (e.g., email messages, websites visited) after use. Supervision is a very similar concep­
tualization and refers to „parental activities whereby the child is allowed to go online alone but … under the direct supervision of a 
nearby parent” (Nikken & Jansz, 2014, p. 259). In adolescence, monitoring/supervision (e.g., checking children’s online activities) can 
undermine the child’s need for autonomy and competence, especially when it involves personal domain issues and is done without the 
child’s consent (Steinfeld, 2021; Valkenburg, Piotrowski, Hermanns, & de Leeuw, 2013). Thus, parents’ excessive participation in 
extracurricular activities with their adolescent children might be viewed as controlling and, therefore, have a negative relationship 
with student motivation (Fan & Williams, 2010). 
This argumentation corresponds with key assumptions on parental involvement referring to parental control defined as pressuring 
the child to think, feel, or behave in particular ways (Joussemet, Landry, & Koestner, 2008). Parental control impedes or prevents the 
child’s possibility to solve challenges and problems on their own, and the positive feeling of taking initiative, which in turn might 
deprive them of feelings of autonomy (Dinkelmann & Buff, 2016). Furthermore, a direct negative influence of parental control on 
children’s learning was assumed by causing anxiety that undermines working memory (Grolnick, 2003). Correspondingly, negative 
effects of parental control were found with regard to competence beliefs (Boonk et al., 2022; Grolnick & Pomerantz, 2009) and ac­
ademic achievement (Boonk et al., 2018; Erdem & Kaya, 2020; Hill & Tyson, 2009). With regard to ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy, to 
the author’s best knowledge, this issue has not yet been investigated (Haddon et al., 2020). Thus, following the mentioned psycho­
logical effects of parental control, negative relationships of monitoring/supervision can also be expected with ICT literacy and ICT 
self-efficacy. 
4.3.4. Regulative activities: rules 
Regulative activities of the parents are labeled as rules (and conceptualized as restrictive mediation) within parental mediation and 
refer to practices such as restricting time and content of digital media use (Chen & Shi, 2019). Regarding self-determination theory 
(Ryan & Deci, 2000), rules can be understood as structure (or behavioral control) defined as “parents’ organization of children’s 
environment to facilitate children’s competence. When parents are structuring, they highlight the relations between actions and 
outcomes through clear and consistent guidelines, expectations, and rules for children” (Grolnick & Pomerantz, 2009, p. 167; see also 
Joussemet et al., 2008). It is argued that providing structure allows adolescents to work semi-autonomously and understand the value 
and benefits of education for their future (Hill & Tyson, 2009). Moreover, when children have a say in the rule, the likelihood that 
children will gradually accept and internalize their parents’ values and rules over the course of family socialization increases (Beyens & 
Valkenburg, 2019). 
Due to this argumentation positive relationships between structure (in terms of rules) and academic achievement and motivation 
are to be assumed, but parental involvement research indicates no or at best very weak positive relationships (Boonk et al., 2018; Hill & 
Tyson, 2009). Also, the only study known to the authors that examined the association of ICT rules with ICT literacy and ICT 
self-efficacy among sixth graders found no significant correlations (Aesaert et al., 2015). In line with these findings, rules are expected 
not to be correlated with ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy. 
Summarizing the research on digital home environment, two difficulties have become apparent. First, to our knowledge, only very 
few studies have examined the relationships of more than two digital HLE components with ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy (Aesaert 
et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2019: three components), thus limiting the robustness and interpretation of the results, e.g., by possibly 
omitting important digital HLE components (Gruchel et al., 2022). Second, and even more important, ICT literacy was assessed by 
using subjective self-assessments. According to a recent review which identified 14 studies (Haddon et al., 2020), only two studies were 
based on an objective test (Aesaert et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2019). Although self-assessments of ICT literacy provide some insight into 
the true skills level, they are rather imperfect proxies for the true ICT competence level as numerous studies have shown (see e.g., 
Goldhammer, Naumann, & Kessel, 2013; Helsper, Schneider, van Deursen, & van Laar, 2020; Palczynska & Rynko, 2021 for a summary 
and detailed discussion). Thus, this research could lead to a deeper understanding to explain the relationships of different digital HLE 
aspects with ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy. 
5. Comparison of adolescent and parent reports 
Another important issue relates to the question whether studies should rely on parent or child reports of parenting behavior 
(Buijzen, Rozendaal, Moorman, & Tanis, 2008; Korelitz & Garber, 2016). While the opinions of researchers on this issue are mixed 
(Beyens & Valkenburg, 2019), with regard to digital HLE components and parental mediation, respectively, there seems to be 
consensus that it „is necessary to identify both the parent’s and the child’s perspective” (Symons et al., 2017, p. 96) to gain a 
comprehensive understanding of ICT-related parental beliefs and practices (Beyens & Valkenburg, 2019; Bonanati & Buhl, 2021; 
Gruchel et al., 2022; Symons et al., 2017). However, only few studies have examined parent-child agreement on this issue. These 
studies found discrepancies between the two sources regarding mediation strategies, e.g., inconsistent factor loadings, low correlations 
between parent and child reports (Shin, Huh, & Faber, 2012; Sonck, Nikken, & de Haan, 2013). Furthermore, children often report 
lower levels of parental engagement than their parents (Symons et al., 2017). 
While these dicrepancies have long been explained by bias reflected in both the parent and adolescent reports (e.g., social 
desirability) or measurement error (Buijzen et al., 2008), incongruence between adolescent and parent reports is now widely 
acknowledged as valid but different perspectives about parental engagement. Additionally, differences in perceptions of parental 
mediation may also develop as children mature, with certain parental efforts becoming more or less meaningful to children than others

for prediction depending on the outcome variable under investigation (Korelitz & Garber, 2016). Thus, the goal of this study is to 
identify the potential specific value of each approach. 
6. Research goals and hypotheses 
The present study aims to investigate the contributions of six different digital HLE components (ICT access, parental ICT values, 
instructional support, psychological support, monitoring/supervision, and rules) to ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy. To gain a 
comprehensive understanding of ICT-related parental beliefs and practices, this study considers both children’s and parents’ per­
spectives for differences in their reports as well in their predictive power with regard to both outcome variables. 
Thus, a first goal is to examine the extent to which both perspectives agree or disagree with regard to the different digital HLE 
components (with the exception of ICT access, which was not taken into account because it is not a psychological variable and was only 
collected from adolescents). According to Multitrait Multimethod (MTMM)-Analysis, these correlations (heteromethod-monotrait 
correlations) can be interpreted as so-called validity coefficients (Campbell & Fiske, 1959) and should be larger than all other cor­
relations between different methods (heteromethod-heterotrait correlations) assuming that the same construct is measured to a 
considerable extent. As a necessary prerequisite for computing these correlations, we first establish whether or not the two perspectives 
share the same factor structure (Kunter & Baumert, 2006). According to previous research (Beyens & Valkenburg, 2019; Shin et al., 
2012), at least moderate correlations (.30 ≤r ≤0.50) are expected between adolescent and parent reports. 
•Hypothesis 1 (H1): The assumed five-factor model of the digital HLE components can be confirmed for adolescents (H1a) as well as 
for their parents (H1b). 
•Hypothesis 2 (H2): Adolescent and parent reports correlate moderately to highly with each other. These correlations (hetero­
method-monotrait correlations) are higher than all other correlations between different methods (heteromethod-heterotrait 
correlations). 
The second and major goal is to examine the extent to which ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy can be predicted by the different 
digital HLE components from both perspectives. Since our objective is to examine and compare the differential effects of each HLE 
component from both perspectives (rather than testing the HLE model as such), all digital HLE components are used as direct pre­
dictors of both outcome variables (see also Sections 4.1 and 4.2) without treating parental mediation practices as mediators. As a basic 
assumption of this study, a first subgoal is to investigate whether the associations between the different digital HLE components and 
ICT literacy or ICT self-efficacy turn out according to the considerations presented in Section 4. 
•Hypothesis 3 (H3): ICT access, parental ICT values, instructional support, and psychological support are positively correlated with 
ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy, while rules is uncorrelated and monitoring/supervision is negatively correlated with both var­
iables. All hypotheses apply – with the exception of ICT access – to both the adolescent (H3a) and the parent perspective (H3b). 
•Hypothesis 4 (H4): As parents and children may both have valid but different perspectives about parenting behavior (Beyens & 
Valkenburg, 2019), digital HLE components are expected to contribute to the prediction of ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy from 
both adolescent (H4a) and parent (H4b) perspectives. Additionally, we exploratively examine whether the child or parent 
perspective has greater predictive power with respect to the dependent variables when both perspectives are used simultaneously 
(H4c). 
7. Materials and methods 
7.1. Sample and procedure 
The present study included a convenience sample and involved 16 schools (33 grade 6 classes) in northern Germany and was 
conducted in 2019. Thirty-seven percent of the students attended an upper secondary school (academic track Gymnasium), 63% 
attended a basic or intermediate secondary school (vocational track). Before the researchers contacted the schools through their 
principals and informed them of the purpose of the study, approval was obtained from an ethics committee and a data protection 
committee so that the study was conducted in compliance with the APA ethical principles. The parents of the participating schools and 
classes received a consent form for their child with information about the study. In addition, they were asked to complete a ques­
tionnaire themselves. Participation was voluntary for parents and children and could be withdrawn at any time. With consent of their 
parents, each student completed the ICT literacy test and questionnaire at school. Parents completed the digital HLE questionnaire at 
home. The parents’ and children’s questionnaires were linked via an identification code. 
The sample consisted of a total of 553 students (54.1% female) aged between 11 and 14 years (M = 12.60, SD = 0.62) and 449 
parents. Because of missing data with regard to central constructs (e.g., ICT literacy test, digital HLE questionnaire), some students

7.2. Instruments 
7.2.1. Digital HLE components 
The described digital HLE components were assessed by six different indicators. 
•Regarding ICT access to digital media, students were asked if they own a computer (desktop, notebook, or tablet) or if they could 
use a computer at home at any time (0 = no, 1 = yes; M = 0.96, SD = 0.19). 
With regard to parental ICT values and parental mediation strategies, 24 items were used, which can be clearly assigned to one of 
the following five dimensions. They were either adapted from previous studies (Bonanati & Buhl, 2021; Dürager & Sonck, 2014; 
Nikken & Jansz, 2014) or self-constructed. 
•Instructional support was assessed through seven items. The items reflect different situations in which parents actively support their 
child in dealing with specific applications. 
•Parental ICT values were operationalized by four items relating to different aspects and situations in which ICT knowledge is useful, 
e.g., at school or in later workplaces. 
•Psychological support was measured with four items and refers to verbal encouragement and positive reinforcement from parents 
when the child explores new, particularly learning- or information-related uses of digital media. 
•To measure rules, seven items were developed that refer to parental rules such as time or content restrictions on digital media use 
and to additional explanations and justifications of parental rules. 
•For monitoring/supervision, three items were used that describe common parental practices to control their children’s digital media 
use. 
The parent and child questionnaires contained the same set of questions referring to the digital HLE components. The participants 
were requested to respond on a four-point rating scale, asking how strongly they would agree with different statements (from 1 =
strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree). 
A preliminary study was conducted to investigate the postulated five-dimensional structure of the scale. For organizational reasons, 
the sample included 140 students (57.9% female; 20.7% academic track Gymnasium) aged between 11 and 14 years (M = 12.68, SD =
0.61) from six schools (six classes) in Northern Germany, but no parents. The measurement model was examined with confirmatory 
factor analyses (CFA) using Mplus 8.4 (Muthen & Muthen, 2012) with the weighted least squares mean and variance (WLSMV) 
estimator for categorical data. The goodness-of-fit was determined according to the following fit statistics cut-offs: ≤0.07 for the root 
mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), ≥0.95 for the confirmatory fit index (CFI), and ≤0.08 for the standardized root mean 
square residual (SRMR) (Hooper, Coughlan, & Mullen, 2008). 
The CFA showed that the proposed five-factor model did not provide a good model fit for the preliminary study data, χ2 (161, N =
140) = 374.658, p < .001, RMSEA = 0.063, 90% CI [0.050; 0.075], CFI = 0.918, SRMR = 0.088. Therefore, three items with low factor 
loadings (<0.50) were finally eliminated. After excluding (to ensure a short and precise measurement instrument that is suitable in 
terms of time and cost when used in large-scale assessments) another seven items with largely redundant content and item in­
tercorrelations of >. 70, the five-factor model fitted the data well, χ2 (67, N = 140) = 74.994, p = .24, RMSEA = 0.029, 90% CI [0.000; 
0.060], CFI = 0.989, SRMR = 0.053. All factor loadings were significant and above 0.40. Thus, the final model of the digital HLE 
encompassed 14 items with the same questions for adolecents and parents (see Appendix A for the items of the final model).

7.2.2. ICT literacy 
The measurement of ICT literacy was based on a multiple-choice test: the Test of Technological and Information Literacy (TILT) for 
Grade 6 (Senkbeil et al., 2013). The TILT-Grade 6 is comprised of 30 items and part of the competence measurement in the German 
National Educational Panel Study (NEPS; Blossfeld, Roßbach, & von Maurice, 2011). The test is based on the definition of ICT literacy 
of the ETS (2002) and is operationalized according to aforementioned facets of technological and information literacy (accessing, 
creating, managing, and evaluating information; see also Senkbeil, 2022). With the exception of a few items that address factual 
knowledge (e.g., computer terminology), the items ask students to accomplish computer-based tasks. To do so, realistic problems 
embedded in a range of authentic situations were presented to the students. To make the design as realistic as possible, screenshots as 
prompts are integrated into the task stimulus if possible. Each item requires a multiple-choice response that asks test-takers to identify 
a correct solution from up to four response options. In Fig. 3 an example item is presented assessing the facet managing. In the present 
study, competence scores were estimated as weighted maximum likelihood estimates (WLE; M = 0.00, SD = 0.76), and the WLE 
reliability of the test was 0.70, indicating an acceptable reliability of measurement (see Senkbeil et al., 2013 for further psychometric 
properties of the test). 
7.2.3. ICT self-efficacy 
For the assessment of ICT self-efficacy, items were constructed comprising technological and information-related aspects according 
to the conceptualization of ICT literacy (ETS, 2002; see also Senkbeil and Ihme, 2017). The resulting scale included ten items (e.g., “I 
am able to sort data according to different criteria in a table”). Participants indicated how well they thought they could do each of the 
computer-based tasks using a four-point rating scale (1 = I cannot do this at all, 4 = I can do this very well by myself; M = 3.08, SD = 0.62). 
The reliability of the ICT self-efficacy scale was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.84). 
7.3. Data analyses 
All analyses were conducted using structural equation modeling techniques with Mplus 8.4. To investigate H1 and H2, CFAs with 
the weighted least squares mean and variance (WLSMV) estimator for categorical data (items of parental ICT values and mediation 
strategies) with robust standard errors and mean-variance-adjusted χ2-test statistics were used (Rhemtulla, Brosseau-Liard, & Savalei, 
2012). As in the preliminary study, the goodness-of-fit was determined according to RMSEA, CFI, and SRMR. Since all data were 
obtained in a classroom setting, the nested structure of the data was taken into account, specifying class membership as the cluster 
variable (Mplus option TYPE = COMPLEX).

To examine the predictive validity of the adolescents’ and parents’ reports (H3 and H4), Mplus was used to estimate correlation and 
regression coefficients. With regard to H4, three models were used for predicting ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy: Model 1 (M1) 
included only adolescent reports as predictors (H4a), Model 2 (M2) included only parent reports as predictors (H4b), and Model 3 (M3) 
encompassed the predictors of both perspectives (H4c). M1 and M2 include all digital HLE components as predictors. In M3, the 
number of predictors were reduced due to the high number of independent variables, as each irrelevant regressor decreases the 
precision of the estimated coefficients (Kline, 2016; Olusegun, Dikko, & Gulumbe, 2015). Therefore, in a stepwise approach, predictors 
that did not significantly contribute to M1 or M2 were removed from the analysis of M3 (see also Aesaert et al., 2015). 
To minimize bias in parameter estimates due to omitted variables, two control variables were included in the analyses. Omitted 
variable bias occurs when an omitted third variable affects both the independent and dependent variable (Wilms, M¨athner, Winnen, & 
Lanwehr, 2021). Gender (male: M = 0.47, SD = 0.50) and students’ educational attainment (as an indicator of educational level; 
Senkbeil and Ihme, 2017) were included as control variables because these variables are correlated with ICT literacy and/or ICT 
self-efficacy (Hatlevik et al., 2018; Siddiq & Scherer, 2019) and are also associated with parental mediation practices (Ferreira, Ponte, 
& Castro, 2017; Nikken & Jansz, 2014). With regard to students’ educational attainment, two levels were differentiated: a basic or 
intermediate secondary school (vocational track) and upper seondary school (M = 0.42, SD = 0.50). 
Overall, there were few missing data (<2% for each variable) except for two scales in the student questionnaire: parental ICT values 
(9.0%) and monitoring/supervision (6.9%). Thus, the full information maximum likelihood estimation (FIML) approach in Mplus was 
used to handle missing data (Lim & Cheung, 2022). Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and internal consistencies of 
study variables) were generated using SPSS 26.0. 
8. Results 
8.1. H1: factorial structure of the adolescent and parent reports 
With regard to the adolescent reports, the CFA of the 14 digital HLE scale items revealed that the proposed five-factor model fitted 
the data well, χ2 (67, N = 422) = 120.282, p < .001, RMSEA = 0.043, 90% CI [0.031; 0.056], CFI = 0.985, SRMR = 0.046. All factor 
loadings were significant (ps < .001) and above 0.50 (see Fig. 4a). The majority of the correlations among the dimensions were low to 
moderate (0.11 ≤r ≤46) except for three strong correlations (0.58 ≤r ≤75). With regard to the parent reports, the CFAs revealed the 
same pattern of results, i.e. a good fit for the five-factor model, χ2 (67, N = 422) = 154.969, p < .001, RMSEA = 0.056, 90% CI [0.044; 
0.067], CFI = 0.971, SRMR = 0.051. All factor loadings were significant (ps < .001) and above 0.50 (see Fig. 4b). The majority of the 
correlations among the dimensions were low to moderate (−0.04 ≤r ≤49) with two exceptions (r = 0.62). In summary, these analyses 
indicate, in line with H1, that adolescent and parent reports share a common conceptual structure. 
8.2. H2: correlations between adolescent and parent reports 
As expected, significant and moderate correlations (0.28 ≤r ≤0.44, ps < .001) were found between adolescent and parent reports 
regarding the five digital HLE components (Table 1). Furthermore, they (monotrait-heteromethod correlations) turned out to be higher 
(with one exception: r = 0.29 between monitoring/supervision and rules) than the correlations concerning different constructs 
(heterotrait-heteromethod correlations: −0.03 ≤r ≤0.27). These result support the assumption that the same construct is measured to 
some extent by adolescent and parent reports. Consistent with expectations, adolescents and their parents differed in their reported 
levels of parental ICT values and mediation practices (Symons et al., 2017). Paired-samples t-tests indicated, corresponding with 
previous research (Beyens & Valkenburg, 2019), that parents reported a significantly higher level for each digital HLE component than 
their children (see Table 2). Cohen’s d values pointed at low to moderate (0.26 ≤d ≤0.64 for instructional support, psychological 
support, and rules) as well as large standardized mean differences (0.78 ≤d ≤0.97 for parental ICT values, and 
monitoring/supervision). 
8.3. H3: correlations between digital HLE components and ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy 
Table 3 shows, as assumed, that the digital HLE components were each differentially related to ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy 
from both the adolescents’ and the parents’ perspectives. However, with regard to adolescent reports, not all correlations were 
consistent with those hypothesized in H3a. As expected, positive correlations with ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy were found for 
parental ICT values and psychological support (although no significant correlation with ICT literacy: r = 0.10, p = .08), negative 
correlations were found for monitoring/supervision, and no correlations were found for rules. Contrary to H3a, instructional support 
was unrelated to ICT literacy as well as ICT self-efficacy. 
A very similar pattern of correlations emerged for parental reports. Eight out of ten correlations were consistent with H3b. Again, as 
expected, positive correlations with ICT self-efficacy and ICT literacy were obtained for parental ICT values and psychological support 
(again with the exception: psychological support and ICT literacy were uncorrelated), while negative correlations were obtained for 
monitoring/supervision and no correlations for rules. However, contrary to H3b, instructional support was only correlated with ICT 
literacy, but not with ICT self-efficacy. Furthermore, in line with H3, ICT access was weakly positively correlated with both outcome 
variables. 
In summary, the results provide substantial support for H3a and H3b, although not all correlations were consistent with the hy­
pothesized relationships. Interestingly, although the correlation patterns of the adolescent and parent reports were largely consistent

8.4. H4: predicting ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy 
Table 4 shows the results of the regression analyses predicting ICT self-efficacy and ICT literacy (see Section 8.3). With regard to 
adolescent reports (M1, H4a), ICT self-efficacy and ICT literacy were predicted by perceived parental ICT values. As the standardized 
regression coefficients show, these were particularly important for predicting ICT self-efficacy (β = 0.32 vs. β = 0.25 for ICT literacy). 
Monitoring/supervision was another significant predictor but it had negative effects on both variables (β = −0.40 and β = −0.26, 
respectively). In summary, the mentioned digital HLE components were strong predictors of ICT self-efficacy even beyond control 
variables, explaining a high proportion of additional variance (ΔR2 = 0.27), while the proportion of additional variance in predicting 
ICT literacy was low (ΔR2 = 0.08). Summarizing, the results support H4a that digital HLE components significantly contributed to 
explained variance of both ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy from the adolescents’ perspective. 
With regard to parental reports (M2, H4b), the opposite pattern of results emerged: Of all digital HLE components, only moni­
toring/supervision predicted ICT self-efficacy. Accordingly, their proportion of additional variance (predictive validity) was low (ΔR2 
= 0.09). In contrast, four out of six digital HLE components predicted ICT literacy and accounted for 18% additional variance beyond 
control variables. While the signs of the regression coefficients for instructional support, parental ICT values, and monitoring/su­
pervision are consistent with those of the bivariate correlations (see Table 3), the negative sign and significant regression weight for 
psychological support (although uncorrelated with ICT literacy) indicate classic suppression (Kline, 2016). According to Pandey and 
Elliott (2010), a suppressor variable correlates with other independent variables but not with an outcome variable, removes irrelevant 
variance in one or more other predictors, and increases the predictor’s regression weight, thus increasing overall model predictability. 
A suppressor variable may also receive non-zero r 
egression weight with a negative sign. To determine if psychological support was a suppressor variable, the regression model was 
also run without the psychological support variable (M2a). The comparison of M2 and M2a (see Table 4) showed that the inclusion of 
psychological support (M2) increased the regression coefficient of parental ICT values (from β = 0.25 to β = 0.38) and the overall 
predictive power of the model (from R2 = 0.31 to R2 = 0.35). Therefore, it can be concluded that psychological support acted as a 
suppressor or enhancer by improving the relationship of another independent variable (parental ICT values) with an outcome variable 
(ICT literacy) (Pandey & Elliott, 2010). 
When adolescent and parent reports are used simultaneously (M3, H4c), significant differences between the two perspectives were 
obtained in terms of their predictive power. ICT self-efficacy was exclusively predicted by the adolescent reports (|0.37| ≤β ≤0.41|), 
that is, by the digital HLE components already mentioned in M1. With regard to ICT literacy, the results were exactly the opposite. Only 
the digital HLE components from parent reports (mentioned in M2) proved to be significant predictors (|0.17| ≤β ≤|0.36) with 
psychological support acting again as a suppressor. The regression coefficients agreed with those from M2 except for minor deviations. 
ICT access did not contribute substantially to variance explanations of ICT literacy or ICT self-efficacy. 
9. Discussion 
This study sheds light on an important part of research on home environment with regard to adolescent digital media use by 
examining the contribution of different digital HLE variables to ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy (Bonanati & Buhl, 2021; Niklas et al., 
2021). Using the HLE framework (Bradley et al., 2000, 2019; Kluczniok et al., 2013), it was possible to integrate well-studied concepts 
of ICT research such as the parental mediation framework (Chen & Shi, 2019; Symons et al., 2017) into a broader theoretical model. 
This allowed to emphasize previously underexplored but important components, such as parental ICT values or encouragement 
(Bonanati & Buhl, 2021; Hammer et al., 2021) and to investigate their relationships with ICT-related characteristics in terms of lifelong 
learning. For example, parental ICT values proved to be the strongest predictor for both outcome variables in the present study. Thus, 
the results extend previous findings of research on parental mediation and (digital) home learning environment. In particular, 
consideration of numerous digital HLE aspects allows for a better understanding of how they relate to the outcome variables studied 
(Bonanati & Buhl, 2021; Hatlevik et al., 2018). 
9.1. Agreement between adolescent and parent reports 
The first goal of this study dealt with the question to what extent both perspectives agree or disagree with regard to the various 
digital HLE components (with the exception of ICT access). In other words, it was investigated whether the adolescent and parent 
reports tap the same constructs or whether the perceptions of ICT-related parenting behavior reflect different, perspective-specific 
constructs. Assuming adolescent and parent reports measure the same construct, both should (a) have the same factor structures, 
(b) be highly correletad with each other with regard to identical digital HLE components and (c) show similar correlations with the 
outcome variables (see e.g., Kunter & Baumert, 2006). 
Consistent with H1 and H2, adolescent and parent reports shared the same factor structure and correlated moderately in terms of 
the measured digital HLE components (mean correlation: r = 0.35). These were higher than those reported in previous parental 
mediation studies (Beyens & Valkenburg, 2019: r = 0.22; Buijzen et al., 2008: r = 0.28; Shin et al., 2012: r = 0.16) and in parental 
involvement studies (Korelitz & Garber, 2016: 0.22 ≤r ≤29). The relatively high agreement between adolescents and parents in their 
reporting suggests that the same construct was measured to some extent from the different perspectives, as indicated by the shared

variance proportions (between R2 = 7.8% for parental ICT values and R2 = 19.4% for instructional support). However, despite their 
shared variance proportions, the results clearly demonstrate that both perspectives reflect correlated but distinct constructs with 
specific perspectives as shown, for example, by the differential associations with ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy. 
It is important to note that the limited overlap is probably better explained by specifics of perspectives than by biases reflected in 
both parent and adolescent reports (Buijzen et al., 2008; Korelitz Garber, 2016). With regard to parental mediation, it was argued that 
the perceived use of mediation was related to different perceptions of the amount of internet use, the type and moments of help needed 
as well as different interpretations of the underlying activities: „Parents might, for example, think that they actually helped their child 
to use the internet in a safe manner every time the child needed assistance, whereas children might experience many more occasions 
when they felt they needed help. … For example, parents might see all kinds of talking to their children about the internet as forms of 
active mediation, whilst their children may have thought of only some specific kinds of interaction with their parents. In addition, since 
parents deliberately applied mediation, they may also remember their practices better than their children, and consequently report a 
higher prevalence of mediation“ (Sonck et al., 2013, p. 109). Thus, it makes sense to establish the specific value of each approach, 
which may depend on the construct in question. In summary, the present study replicates and complements previous findings that both 
perspectives need to be measured to gain a comprehensive understanding of ICT-related parental involvement (Beyens & Valkenburg, 
2019; Symons et al., 2017). 
9.2. Predicting ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy 
First of all, the results demonstrate that measuring ICT literacy using an objective test and using self-assessments (in this study: ICT 
self-efficacy) lead to different associations with digital HLE components. For example, although psychological support is positively 
related to ICT self-efficacy (r = 0.33, p < .001 and r = 0.13, p < .001), it is not positively related to ICT literacy. These findings are in 
line with previous findings that self-assessments are not considered valid indicators of actual ICT skills (Palczynska & Rynko, 2021). 
Both ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy could be predicted by digital HLE components. In line with H4a (adolescent reports), 
parental ICT values and monitoring/supervision explained additional variance in ICT self-efficacy (27%) as well as in ICT literacy (8%) 
after controlling for gender and students’ educational level. Regarding parent reports (H4b), only a small amount of additional 
variance in ICT self-efficacy (9%) could be explained by one parental practice, namely monitoring/supervision. In contrast, instruc­
tional support, monitoring/supervision, and, as the most powerful predictor, parental ICT values predicted a relatively large amount of 
incremental variance in ICT literacy (18%). Moreover, the results showed, contrary to H3, that psychological support is not associated 
with ICT literacy, but acts as a suppressor by increasing the regression coefficient of parental ICT values and the overall model pre­
dictability. Consequently, there is irrelevant variation or error in parental ICT values that is not associated with ICT literacy but with 
psychological support (r = 0.62, see Fig. 4). Thus, it is possible that psychological support (verbal encouragement), which can be 
understood as a component of parental ICT values but is uncorrelated with ICT literacy (r = 0.04), was partialized out of parental ICT 
values, increasing its regression weight. It should be noted that the negative regression weight of psychological support cannot be 
interpreted since “a suppressor is not defined by its own regression weight but rather by its effects on other variables in a regression 
system“ (Conger, 1974, p. 37). In summary, these results suggest that perceptions of parental ICT values and associated parental 
behaviors are far more important for the acquisition of ICT literacy (possibly caused by internalization of parental values) than 
psychological support in the form of verbal encouragement. 
The results also show that not all digital HLE components for which it was hypothesized were positively associated with ICT literacy 
and ICT self-efficacy (H3). This applies for both adolescent and parent reports. While rules, in line with H3, was uncorrelated with ICT 
literacy as well as ICT self-efficacy, contrary to expectation, instructional support was uncorrelated with both variables with one 
exception as a significant predictor of ICT literacy from the parent perspective (β = 0.17, p < .05). While this significant relationship 
corresponds with the assumption that instructional support enhances the child’s actual ICT literacy in dealing with everyday problems 
(e.g., searching for specific information on the Internet) (Livingstone et al., 2017), it cannot be ruled out that instructions (e.g., 
frequent interruptions by the parents) undermine the child’s need for autonomy and thus does not promote ICT self-efficacy (Bonanati 
& Buhl, 2021). Additionally, ICT access only plays a minor role in predicting the outcome variables. First, ICT access when needed 
seems to be ubiquitous for adolescents, and second, ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy do not depend on ICT availability itself but on the 
purposes for which digital media are used (Senkbeil and Ihme, 2017; Bonanati & Buhl, 2021). 
The most important result is that – despite the differences mentioned above – from both the adolescents’ and the parents’ 
perspective, parental ICT values positively predict ICT literacy and monitoring/supervision negatively predicts ICT literacy, even when 
relatively strong control variables are included (e.g., the students’ educational level). That parental ICT values are the strongest 
predictor of both ICT self-efficacy (β = .41; adolescents’ perspective) and ICT competence (β = 0.36; parents’ perspective) is consistent 
with key findings of parental involvement research, which showed that parental expectations, beliefs, and values are more predictive 
of higher achievement and academic self-efficacy than behavioral forms of involvement (e.g., academic support and encouragement) 
(Boonk et al., 2018, 2022; Erdem & Kaya, 2020). 
Monitoring/supervision might play a negative role in shaping the child’s development of both ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy 
undermining the child’s need for autonomy and competence. This practice might be viewed as controlling and, therefore, have a 
negative relationship with student motivation (Fan & Williams, 2010). Furthermore, it might prevent the possibility of solving 
challenges and problems on their own (sensu parental control), which should have a negative impact on ICT literacy (Dinkelmann & 
Buff, 2016). 
However, it must be emphasized that the results illustrate relationships but not causal mechanisms, i.e. the mentioned relationships 
could also work in the opposite direction as well. This applies in particular to monitoring/supervision in the sense of parental control

(and instructional support, see above). For example, with regard to parent involvement research, the negative association between 
parental control and academic performance has been explained by attributing high levels of parental control primarily to students’ 
academic failure. Parents of students who have academic problems at school may be more controlling (Erdem & Kaya, 2020). Such an 
explanation is also conceivable for the presented data: When parents recognize that their children have low ICT skills, they can opt to 
supervise them more in order to ensure their safety online. It can be concluded that „attempts at disentangling the direction of effect 
are futile with correlational research. Longitudinal, natural, and experimental designs provide the best context for social scientists to 
infer causality or direction of effect“ (Hill & Tyson, 2009, p. 759, p. 759). In summary, the present study demonstrates that the digital 
HLE components are associated with ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy, but the causal mechanisms remain unclear according to the 
cross-sectional design. 
Regarding the variance explained by the simultaneous use of adolescent and parent reports (H4c), it can be concluded that parents’ 
reports of ICT self-efficacy and adolescents’ reports of ICT competence each do not contribute to the explanation of the dependent 
variable beyond the other perspective. ICT self-efficacy can be fully predicted by adolescents’ reports alone and ICT literacy can be 
fully predicted by parents’ reports alone so that the predictive power of each perspective is highly dependent on the particular variable 
under investigation. With regard to ICT self-efficacy, the result is in line with expectancy-value theory (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002), since 
children’s motivations (e.g., self-efficacy) are not only influenced by parental behavior toward their children but also by the child’s 
perception of this behavior. Parent reports represent more distal influencing factors, while child reports represent more proximal 
influencing factors on motivations (e.g., self-efficacy). Thus, the influence of parental support occurs via children’s perceived parental 
support, i.e., perceptions and interpretations of parental support seem to be crucial for children’s self-efficacy (Dinkelmann & Buff, 
2016). With regard to ICT literacy, the result corresponds with several studies showing that only parent reports predicted academic 
achievement, whereas children’s perceptions of parents’ behavior did not (Choe, 2020; Dinkelmann & Buff, 2016). This could be 
explained by the fact that perceptions of teachers’ behavior become more important for academic achievement during adolescence, so 
adolescents may underestimate the influence of their parents on their performance (Diaconu-Gherasim, Brumariu, & Hurley, 2022). 
10. Limitations and directions for future research 
Shortcomings and limitations of this study - beyond the mentioned causal mechanism problem - could lead to possible implications 
for future research. First, the focus in this study was only on certain components of digital HLE. Thus, other components need to be 
considered further that might be associated with young people’s ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy (e.g., parental ICT skills and self- 
efficacy, parental worry and coping strategies in the face of online risks; Livingstone et al., 2017; Sciacca et al., 2022). 
Second, this study is limited to young adolescents in Germany. The results cannot be generalized to other age groups because 
parental involvement changes over time as the child develops (Sammons et al., 2015). 
Third, longitudinal data are needed to demonstrate the impact of digital HLE components on children’s ICT literacy and ICT self- 
efficacy. Those studies should start in elementary school over a longer period of time (e.g., until the end of lower secondary school) in 
order to additionally investigate reciprocal processes between parents and their children, e.g., the interplay between digital HLE, ICT 
literacy and ICT-related motivations (Hill & Tyson, 2009). 
Fourth, the variable-centric approach used in most studies allows only rudimentary conclusions on how parents can simultaneously 
maximize the benefits and minimize the risks of their children’s digital media use. As the various educational processes are practiced 
together, a person-centred approach could provide new insights into the effectiveness of the digital HLE by identifying typical digital 
HLE profiles (Livingstone et al., 2017). 
Fifth, in order to achieve unbiased and robust results, control variables should be taken into account to a greater extent than in 
previous studies. Although the students’ educational level could be used in this study, additional cognitive variables (e.g., intelligence) 
and more precise parents’ social background variables (e.g., occupational status) should be included. 
11. Conclusion 
The present study demonstrates that digital HLE components – from both adolescent and parent perspectives – seem to be 
important predictors of the children’s ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy, e.g., parental ICT values toward digital media. It should be 
taken into account that this does not seem to apply to all types of parental beliefs and practices (Bonanati & Buhl, 2021). It was also 
shown that the predictive power of each perspective with regard to ICT literacy and ICT self-efficacy is highly dependent on the 
variables examined, so that no general and conclusive statements about the predictive power of adolescent and parent reports can be 
made. Thus, the recommendation to consider both perspectives (Symons et al., 2017) is underlined by the presented results. According 
to the presented results, parents should be encouraged to support their children in using ICT responsibly, e.g., by imparting their own 
beliefs and values through direct instruction or by acting as role models. As already implemented in other domains, special inter­
vention programs (e.g., Family Literacy Programs), in which parents and children learn together, could therefore be beneficial for the 
development of ICT-related skills and motivational beliefs (Gruchel et al., 2022).
