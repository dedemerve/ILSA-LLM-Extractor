A B S T R A C T
The breadth and depth of students' knowledge play an important role in their learning. However, at the start of 
compulsory education individual differences in knowledge can be immense, particularly between learners from 
different socio-economic backgrounds. As knowledge acquisition is cumulative, achievement gaps between more 
and less advantaged students can quickly increase when these lacunae in knowledge are not explicitly and 
systematically remedied. In this article, we argue that knowledge-rich curricula in compulsory education can 
help promote deep thinking and complex skill acquisition for all students by systematically building their 
knowledge of the world. First, the importance of knowledge for deep, meaningful learning and complex skill 
acquisition is discussed. Subsequently, a selection of knowledge-rich curricula and their impact on achievement 
is addressed in detail. The article concludes with a discussion on the implications for curriculum building, and 
the adoption of knowledge-rich curricula with a view to improved learning for all students.
Educational relevance: Students' knowledge plays an important role in their learning. At the start of compulsory 
education individual differences in what students already know can be immense, particularly between students 
from different socio-economic backgrounds. Adopting knowledge-rich curricula in compulsory education can 
help improve learning for all students by systematically building their knowledge of the world, leading to deep, 
meaningful learning and a solid foundation for complex skill acquisition.
1. Introduction
Proficiency in reading and math not only enables students to effec­
tively acquire knowledge and perform calculations, but also allows them 
to participate actively in economic, political, social, and cultural life 
(Castles et al., 2018; Hirsch, 2016). However, at the start of compulsory 
education, individual differences in knowledge can already be immense, 
particularly between students from different socio-economic back­
grounds. Knowledge acquisition is a cumulative process, and the 
breadth and depth of students' knowledge play an important role in their 
further learning and acquisition of complex cognitive skills (Surma 
et al., 2025). Initial differences between more and less advantaged 
students can quickly increase when these gaps are not explicitly and 
systematically remedied.
Knowledge gaps often form even before formal literacy and 
numeracy learning, highlighting the need to provide high-quality edu­
cation to children from a young age onwards (Adlof et al., 2010; Hjet­
land et al., 2020; McGee et al., 2002; Ritchie & Bates, 2013). Recent 
empirical studies have shown that systematic and cumulative work on 
foundational knowledge can have a major impact on strengthening

2. The importance of knowledge and its crucial role in the 
curriculum
2.1. Knowledge acquisition is a cumulative process
What you know determines what you see (Kirschner, 1991; Kirschner 
et al., 1997). If someone who has never been exposed to chemistry walks 
past a lab, they will probably see a lot of glassware, some flames, baths, 
and so forth, but will have no idea what is going on. They know little to 
nothing and ‘see’ very little. If that person has taken high school 
chemistry, they will see a distillation setup and know that someone is 
busy separating one liquid from another via evaporation and conden­
sation (e.g., alcohol from an aqueous solution). If that person is a 
chemist, they will see a distillation setup with a water bath set at 78.2C 
and know that ethanol (drinkable alcohol) is probably being distilled. In 
other words, all three people look at the same thing, but due to their 
prior knowledge, they notice different things. It is the job of the teacher 
and the curriculum to help students gain the knowledge they need to 
really see, understand, and interpret the world around them. However, 
over the past few decades, some erroneous ideas concerning knowledge 
acquisition and the curriculum have had a profound influence on edu­
cators' thinking. Tracing back to early Spencerian lines of thinking, there 
was a widespread belief that children learn more from experience, dis­
covery, and natural self-development than from what they are formally 
taught (Bruner, 1977), more from the concrete than from the abstract 
(Spencer, 1939), more when content was encountered in discrete 
developmental stages than at fixed moments within the curriculum 
(Piaget, 1952), more when individualised and preferred learning styles 
were taken into account in teaching practice (Kolb, 1985) and so forth. 
These ideas became educational dogmas that dominated early theories 
of education, and were prevalent in teacher education (Hirsch, 1996; 
Menter, 2016). The influence of ideas is very difficult to establish, but it 
seems likely that they contributed to beliefs that early childhood edu­
cation should particularly attend to children's nature and preferences, to 
their modes of learning and stages of cognitive developmental needs 
(Geary, 2006, pp. 312): 
The function of schools is then to provide the raw materials for the 
minds of children to consume, much like parents provide the food 
children consume for their bodies to grow. If this assumption is 
correct, then teachers should not be in control of educational 
activities nor should school boards or other adults control the con­
tent of what children are exposed to in these classrooms.
The formal curriculum and the role of knowledge (i.e., explicit 
learning of vocabulary and acquisition of world knowledge) were 
therefore considered less vital than fostering a nurturing, personalized, 
experience based learning environment where children engage in dis­
covery based methods with learning materials of direct relevance and 
utility to their lives when they felt ready for it (Hirsch, 1996). These 
ideas turn out to be inconsistent with what scientific advancements of 
the study of the human mind have revealed, but this lack of empirical 
support has done little to dampen its popularity (for critiques, see e.g. 
Kirschner et al., 2006; Pashler et al., 2008; Sugarman, 1987). However, a 
recent renewal of appreciation for the role of knowledge in human 
cognition, building on David Ausubel's (1968) view that people learn 
new things by building on their own current knowledge, is emerging 
both in the learning sciences and curriculum. To understand why, we 
need to look at better-informed theories of how we learn and acquire 
knowledge.
2.1.1. Human working memory capacity is limited
Working memory (WM) is a central component of human cognition. 
It is responsible for the temporary storage and manipulation of infor­
mation required for reasoning, comprehension, and learning (Baddeley, 
2000). However, WM is also extremely limited in capacity and duration. 
Classic research by Miller (1956) suggested that individuals can hold 
approximately seven (plus or minus two) items in WM, though more 
recent studies suggest the true average is closer to four discrete units, 
particularly when rehearsal strategies are restricted (Cowan, 2001). 
Furthermore, information in WM typically decays within 15 to 30 s if not 
actively maintained (Brown, 1958; Peterson & Peterson, 1959). These 
constraints raise a significant question: how do individuals manage to 
process and retain complex or lengthy information (e.g., sentences, 
problem-solving steps, visual configurations) when WM capacity is so 
limited?
Humans possess two interrelated cognitive mechanisms that help to 
address this challenge, namely chunking and schemas. Chunking is one 
of the most effective strategies for bypassing WM's limited capacity. 
When discrete bits of information are grouped into larger units, they are 
treated as a single item in WM (Miller, 1956). For example, the number 
sequence 1–9–4-5-2-0-2-4 can be encoded as two familiar years, “1945” 
and “2024”, reducing the number of items to be remembered from eight 
to two. This process relies heavily on prior knowledge, since a chunk is 
only meaningful if the individual can relate it to a familiar concept 
stored in long-term memory (LTM) (Gobet et al., 2001). In this sense, 
chunking is not a standalone process but one that is closely dependent on 
what we already have retained in our LTM, namely the activation of 
relevant schemas.
Schemas are cognitive structures that represent organized knowl­
edge about objects, situations, or events (Bartlett, 1932; Rumelhart, 
1980). They function as mental frameworks that allow individuals to 
interpret and structure incoming information efficiently. When new 
information aligns with such a schema, it can be encoded and remem­
bered as part of a larger integrated chunk, rather than as isolated items.
Take, for example, the common schema of a dog as a pet which in­
cludes expectations such as that dogs: live in our homes, are friendly and 
loyal, bark and wag their tails, enjoy playing fetch, and need food, 
walks, and affection. This framework or schema allows us to recognize 
and interact with dogs without needing to consciously process each new 
encounter from scratch. When we see a Labrador chasing a ball in a park, 
we don't see just “an animal running”; instead, the pet-dog schema is 
activated, and the behaviour is immediately recognized as playful, safe, 
and familiar.
However, our understanding of dogs doesn't stop at the pet schema. 
We also have broader schemas that place dogs within larger biological 
categories. In a related and possibly higher level schema dogs are

recognized as mammals who have body hair (i.e., fur), are warm- 
blooded, have back-bones (i.e., spines), bear live young, and produce

such as cats, mice, horses, or even humans. We understand that, like

regular nourishment and care.
Beyond this, we may activate an even more general schema: dogs as 
animals. Here, dogs are schematized as living beings that move, breathe, 
eat, reproduce, and respond to their environment and, as such, are part 
of ecosystems, have basic needs, and are distinct from inanimate objects 
or machines. These layers of schemas—dog as house pet, as mammal, 
and as animal—are not separate from one another but nested within a 
hierarchy. Depending on the context, different levels of the hierarchy 
may be activated.
Schemas are powerful in WM because they can be activated as single 
units, despite encompassing substantial detail (Sweller, 1988), which 
significantly reduces cognitive load. For example, an experienced pro­
grammer may recognize a code segment as a sorting algorithm, treating 
it as one conceptual unit. Similarly, fluent language users chunk words 
into phrases and phrases into coherent sentences by drawing on lin­
guistic schemas. In both cases, schemas reduce the number of inde­
pendent items that need to be held in WM and allow for faster, more 
efficient processing. Finally, in narrative comprehension, individuals 
rely on story schemas to chunk and integrate events into a coherent 
structure, linking current input with previously acquired knowledge.
Schemas, however, do not only support capacity but also contribute 
to the duration of memory retention. Structured, coherent information is 
easier to rehearse and maintain in WM, and if some parts are forgotten, 
schemas help reconstruct missing elements by filling in likely details 
based on past experience (Alba & Hasher, 1983). In this way, schemas 
enhance both the stability and resilience of working memory contents.
The interaction between WM and LTM is central to these processes. 
WM constantly draws upon LTM to make sense of new inputs, organize 
them into meaningful structures, and guide processing.
Knowledge acquisition, thus, is a cumulative process, and the 
breadth and depth of students' knowledge (i.e., their cognitive schemas) 
play an important role in their learning and their acquisition of complex 
cognitive skills such as critical thinking, problem-solving, and reading 
comprehension (Surma et al., 2025). However, this also implies 
achievement gaps between more and less advantaged students early in 
development can quickly increase. Research indicates that a systematic 
build-up of knowledge from an early age onwards and over an extended 
period has a protective, positive impact on language development, 
numeracy skills, and socio-emotional outcomes (e.g. Chambers et al., 
2016; Hjetland et al., 2020; Jenkins et al., 2018; Ritchie & Bates, 2013). 
Knowledge building should therefore play a central role in the curricu­
lum, not solely to promote meaningful learning for all students (Ausubel, 
1968), but equally as a solid foundation for acquiring complex cognitive 
skills.
2.2. Knowledge and the acquisition of complex cognitive skills
2.2.1. Knowledge and reading comprehension
It is important to underline that reading comprehension is multidi­
mensional, and our ability to comprehend what we read is dependent 
upon a wide range of knowledge and skills, often depicted in five areas, 
the so called ‘big five’: phonics, phonemic awareness, vocabulary, 
fluency, and comprehension. (National Research Council, 2000; RAND 
Reading Study Group, 2002). However, as Catts and Kamhi (2025) have 
argued, this depiction can give teachers a false impression, as if the 
comprehension component were comparable to the other skill-like 
components of reading, and can be taught in a similar way, namely by 
practicing it as a generic skill that can then be applied in different 
reading contexts, usually through reading strategy instruction.
However, we know from research that complex cognitive skills, such 
as reading comprehension (and for example, critical thinking) are not 
generically applicable in new contexts, but are domain-specific and 
therefore require a solid knowledge base (Catts & Kamhi, 2025; Surma 
et al., 2025). For this reason, the effects of generic reading strategy in­
struction on students' reading comprehension quickly reach a minimal 
plateau and have little further impact (Elleman, 2017; Rosenshine & 
Meister, 1994; Stevens et al., 2019; for a full overview see Willingham, 
2023). What does help students progress is instruction in knowledge 
(Peng et al., 2023). This is not surprising, as comprehension strategies 
must activate students' knowledge which simply cannot be achieved if 
this knowledge is lacking (Hattan, 2024; Hattan et al., 2024; McCarthy 
& McNamara, 2021). Others therefore contend that a more promising 
approach to boost reading comprehension consists of content-rich in­
struction dedicated to cumulatively building students' knowledge with 
the aid of a knowledge-rich curriculum (Cabell & Hwang, 2020; Catts, 
2022; Cervetti & Hiebert, 2019; Hirsch, 2003). A more detailed analysis 
on the role of knowledge in different reading theories is beyond the 
scope of this article, but can be found in a recent overview by Hattan and 
Kendeou (2024).
2.2.2. The role of prior knowledge in mathematics
Perhaps no other field of human knowledge is more interwoven and 
path-dependent than mathematics. Without mastering the cardinality 
principle and number symbols, children can't understand the four 
arithmetic operations; without mastering these, they can't understand 
fractions, the decimal number representations, and so on. Later, without 
a training in simple number problems and in the so-called word prob­
lems, young students can't understand algebra, which is a precondition 
to the study of functions, which in turn is necessary for understanding 
function limits, sequences, series, derivatives, and so on. All this is 
obvious simply from the structure of mathematics. But we now know 
much more due to rigorous research in the last decades about predictors 
of success in elementary mathematics. Although other variables may 
play a confounding role, three cases are worth noting.
First, it is now well established that children who start school behind 
their peers in number knowledge, namely, not understanding the car­
dinal principle, tend to lag behind their peers years later. It is also 
established that acquiring this knowledge, although informally, as early 
as at 3 or 4 years of age, constitutes a foundation for success that can 
percolate throughout primary school or even later (Geary, 2022).
Second, research also points out the extreme importance of under­
standing rational numbers for performance in various later content do­
mains, such as algebra (Van Hoof et al., 2017), and even in high-school 
mathematics (Siegler et al., 2012).
Third, an interesting dependence on prior knowledge has also been 
found in both procedural and conceptual knowledge. Contrarily to what 
was previously believed by some currents of thought, research indicates 
a bidirectional dependence: prior conceptual knowledge supports the 
development of new procedural knowledge and prior procedural 
knowledge supports the development of new conceptual knowledge 
(see, e.g., Rittle-Johnson et al., 2015).
2.2.3. The role of prior knowledge in science, history, and geography
Natural and social sciences are not as internally interdependent as 
mathematics. However, prior semantic knowledge is equally necessary 
for an organized understanding of concepts and phenomena. In Physics, 
for example, a lack of necessary prior knowledge can dramatically 
hinder students' progression (see, e.g., Schmitt & Spatz, 2024). The 
importance of the necessary domain-specific knowledge to master sci­
ence concepts is much larger than usually realized and includes not only 
factual but also schematic conceptual knowledge (Duncan, 2007). 
Consider, for instance, the physical explanation of the apparently simple 
atmospheric rainbow. To understand it, students need knowledge of 
many physical concepts. They need to know how light travels, what light 
colour decomposition is, what light reflection and refraction are, and 
how the refraction index depends on the wavelength of light. They also

systematically over time, and not on a case-by-case basis.
2.3. The crucial role of knowledge in the curriculum
In order to more broadly discuss the role of knowledge in the cur­
riculum, it is essential to first clarify the concept ‘curriculum’. In the 
narrow sense, curriculum can be defined as all the content to be taught 
and learned, the what of education. However, it can also be conceptu­
alized more broadly by including the how (in what way and with what 
activities), the when (in what order and within what time frame) and the 
why (the underlying philosophy) of education (Priestley & Xenofontos, 
2020). At the policy level, curriculum can be defined as the expectations 
for education, elaborated in goals and policy documents, which in turn 
are translated into curriculum materials, such as textbooks (Stein et al., 
2007). Furthermore, the curriculum operates across multiple levels of 
activity (Priestley et al., 2021): ranging all the way from (a) the supra 
level (transnational discourse about curriculum by e.g. the OECD (Or­
ganization for Economic Co-operation and Development) over (b) the 
macro (national level of curriculum frameworks and standards, e.g. the 
English national curriculum) and (c) the meso level (local curriculum 
agencies, textbook publishers, educational designers) to the (d) micro 
(schools and teachers designing educational programs and lesson plans) 
and (e) nano level (individual teachers designing teaching activities to 
promote student learning). Frequently, there are discrepancies between 
curricular intentions, implementation and what is ultimately enacted 
and learned in the classroom (Bauersfeld, 1979), and namely teachers 
play a crucial role in the translation of curricular standards to schools 
and their enactment in individual classrooms (Stein et al., 2007).
Moreover, curricula are never neutral as they start with the funda­
mental why question, namely Why schooling? As many aspects of edu­
cation are anchored to the answer to this question, any shift in this 
rationale triggers changes across various dimensions of the curriculum, 
including content, standards formulation, and assessment methods (Van 
den Akker, 2003). While many different goals in education can be 
distinguished, our rationale is straightforward: if we want to promote 
deep thinking and meaningful learning in all students, and build a solid 
foundation for their complex cognitive skills to develop, then knowledge 
building should be at the centre of the curriculum; that is, it must be 
knowledge-rich. Surma et al. (2025) define a knowledge-rich curriculum 
as follows:
[…] a plan for learning over time that is concept-led and knowledge- 
led (Oates, 2011), which encompasses a wide range of specified 
knowledge, and provides ample depth and opportunities to engage with 
that knowledge (Rata, 2021). It sets high expectations for all students 
and systematically builds their knowledge of words and the world 
(Hirsch, 2016). It aims at a broad and steady foundation for complex 
thinking skills, such as critical thinking and reading comprehension, but 
also knowledge building that is further amplified and deepened by those 
complex skills. A comprehensive knowledge-rich curriculum covers 
subjects and concepts that go beyond children's day-to-day experiences 
and is based on the ‘best’ disciplinary knowledge available at that time 
(Young & Lambert, 2014). It ensures that every child has access to a 
broad and solid knowledge base in school, even if it has not been 
(partially) acquired from an early age onwards outside school.
3. Knowledge-rich curricula and student achievement
Let us now turn to concrete examples of knowledge-rich curricula at 
the micro and meso level (Section 3.1), and macro level (Section 3.2) and 
further examine the relationship between knowledge-rich curricula and 
student achievement.
3.1. Knowledge-rich curricula at the micro and meso level
Whereas older experimentally-designed interventions that focused 
on building domain and topic knowledge over a relatively short period 
of time tended to show small or negligible effects on standard measures 
of student achievement (e.g. Connor et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2021; See 
et al., 2015), recent longitudinal empirical research appears to corrob­
orate the promising findings at the curricular macro level as described 
above. The results suggest that systematically building foundational 
knowledge from an early age onwards through a knowledge-rich cur­
riculum and effective pedagogy can indeed boost overall student 
achievement, while at the same time reducing differences between more 
and less advantaged learners thus promoting equitable opportunities for 
all. The key seems to reside in sustained implementation, so the 
increased duration gives room for larger effects to accumulate.
An experimental study conducted by Kim et al. (2024) in 30 
elementary schools (N = 2870 students) in the United States found that a 
knowledge-rich curricular intervention that spirals science topics and 
vocabulary from grades 1 to 3 can not only improve treatment students' 
science vocabulary knowledge across all three grades, but can also boost 
Grade 3 science reading (ES 0.14), overall reading comprehension (ES 
0.11) and mathematics achievement (ES 0.12), as compared to control 
students. Moreover, the impact of the intervention, which built back­
ground and vocabulary knowledge, had positive transfer effects on 
reading comprehension (ES 0.12) and mathematics achievement (ES 
0.16) up until the end of Grade 4.
A related mixed methods study by Mosher et al. (2024), also in the 
United States with Grades 1 and 2 students (N = 2156) revealed that 
consciously and cumulatively building students' domain-specific vo­
cabulary knowledge, combined with a pedagogy in which teachers (N =
48) provided students with ample opportunities to engage with words 
and to connect those words to overarching topics might be one of the key 
elements to promote reading comprehension transfer through sustained 
content literacy interventions. Moreover, Relyea et al. (2024) found that 
classroom-based content literacy interventions in which content and 
vocabulary knowledge was built in thematic units through interactive 
read-alouds and concept mapping, had similar positive effects for Grades 
1 and 2 English learners (N = 1236) and their English-proficient peers 
(N = 4161) alike on domain-specific vocabulary and argumentative 
writing, and improved English learners' oral proficiency as well, which 
in turn positively predicted their reading proficiency.
Another recent large-scale experimental study capitalized on a lot­
tery for Kindergarten places at one of 9 Core Knowledge Charter schools, 
and examined the long-term effects of a knowledge-rich curricular 
intervention on the achievement of 2360 students from Kindergarten to 
8th grade (Grissmer et al., 2023). The long-term effects of the curricu­
lum, which included language arts, history, geography, mathematics, 
science, music, and visual arts (Core Knowledge Foundation, 2010) were 
assessed via state tests in 3rd through 6th grade in reading and mathe­
matics and in science in 5th grade. While there was no significant effect 
in mathematics, students in the Core Knowledge schools scored higher in

In sum, Kim et al. (2024), Mosher et al. (2024), Relyea et al. (2024), 
and Grissmer et al., 2023 all provide convincing evidence that a distinct 
approach to instruction with regard to the comprehension component of 
reading is needed. This promising approach to boost reading compre­
hension consists of content-rich instruction dedicated to cumulatively 
building students' knowledge with the aid of a knowledge-rich curricu­
lum (Cabell & Hwang, 2020; Catts, 2022; Cervetti & Hiebert, 2019; 
Hirsch, 2003). The results of these recent long-term studies have shown 
that this approach might also boost achievement in other areas such as 
mathematics and science. This is in line with recent overview studies 
highlighting the positive effects of integrated literacy and content-area 
instruction on both vocabulary and comprehension in elementary 
school in general (Hwang et al., 2022; Hwang, Cabell, et al., 2023) and 
the reciprocal relations between domain knowledge and reading in the 
elementary years in particular (Hwang, McMaster, et al., 2023). In other 
words, a more solid knowledge-base is essential to improve students' 
reading comprehension (knowledge begets reading), while at the same 
time improved reading comprehension and effective instruction can 
help students to deepen and broaden their knowledge in other areas as 
well (reading begets knowledge). This is important for all students, but 
particularly for those from less-advantaged backgrounds, as their access 
to a shared knowledge base in school is essential, not only to foster 
lifelong literacy, but also to participate actively in democratic society 
(Hirsch, 2006, 2009, 2011).
Combined with the insights from earlier work by Grissmer et al. 
(2010), Chambers et al. (2016), and Jenkins et al. (2018), who found 
that balanced pre-school curricula with sufficient focus on systemati­
cally and explicitly teaching and practicing knowledge and sub-skills 
within specific developmental domains, have positive effects on pre- 
schoolers' cognitive development, these findings raise interesting ques­
tions on how knowledge-rich preschool curricula might be shaped and 
balanced and connected to primary curricula with a view to promoting 
achievement for all students from an early age onwards. Let us now 
consider two examples of knowledge-rich curricula at the macro level to 
shed further light on this issue.
3.2. Knowledge-rich curricula at the macro level
3.2.1. The case of Portugal
The Portuguese education system has witnessed notable changes 
over the last 25 years, prompted by declining results in external 
assessments in TIMSS1 and PISA.2 First, TIMSS 1995 revealed that in 
Math, Portuguese 4th grade students ranked near the bottom of the 
table, with only two countries, Iran and Iceland, behind. Subsequently, 
the PISA 2000 results indicated that Portuguese students scored below 
the participant countries' average and well below the OECD average in 
all three areas (Literacy, Math, and Science). This led to a shift in 
educational policy.
Ensuing reforms in the Portuguese school system were at times 
contradictory and served different purposes, but until 2015 essentially 
went in the same direction: increased attention was being dedicated to 
the results. Three periods can be distinguished (Crato, 2020): 
• a pragmatic period, from 2002 to 2011, during which policy makers 
almost unanimously underlined the need for a better-defined cur­
riculum and more rigorous assessment;
• a knowledge-rich period, from 2011 to 2015, during which curricular 
reforms were intentionally oriented towards a more structured and 
more demanding curriculum, centred on knowledge rather than 
competencies;
• a competency-based period, from 2016 until early 2024, during which 
curricular reforms were intentionally oriented towards a less struc­
tured curriculum, with less evaluation, and centred on competencies 
rather than knowledge.
The main characteristics of the curriculum during the knowledge- 
rich period can be summarized as follows (Crato, 2020):
First, the commonly accepted essential subjects, such as mathe­
matics, history, and sciences were prioritized, with particular attention 
paid to reading and elementary mathematics. Second, the curriculum 
was organized in different subjects according to their internal coherence 
based on the disciplines. Third, the curriculum was organized in a pro­
gressive way, translating the internal coherence and logic of the disci­
plines. Fourth, the curriculum set ambitious standards, was rich in 
knowledge, and envisioned a deep understanding of the concepts and 
procedures with a view to helping students grasp the structure of the 
different subjects. Fifth, the curriculum was specific, so it could be easily 
translated into assessment and auxiliary materials.
However, a subsequent political turn in 2015 led to a coalition 
government that abruptly changed the course of educational policy: two 
of the four standardised assessment stages and the entrance exam for 
new teachers were abolished. Rather than focusing on subject knowl­
edge and subject coherence, the new ministry adopted a focus on mul­
tidisciplinarity, practical skills and competencies, and diminished 
assessment. The new government created a set of vaguely formulated 
and less-structured “Essential Learnings” (“Aprendizagens Essenciais”), 
culminating in 2021 with the elimination of all other curricular pro­
grams and standards (Despacho n.◦6605-A/2021). The country's edu­
cation policy changed completely, both in administrative orientation 
and public perception, and entered a third phase that lasted from 2016 
to early 2024, characterised by a competency-driven curriculum.

3.2.2. The case of England
England was one of the pioneers in developing a knowledge-rich 
National Curriculum. introduced in 2013, following the election of the 
Conservative-Liberal Democrat government in 2010. The curriculum 
was one of a number of reforms instigated by the Education Secretary 
Michael Gove, and his Schools Minister Nick Gibb in response to what 
were seen as declining standards in the English education system (Muijs, 
in preparation).
Ever since it was first introduced in 1998, the national curriculum for 
England is structured around subjects rather than areas of learning or 
themes as in many competency-based curricula (e.g. Curriculum for 
Excellence in Scotland), and sets out a programme of study for each 
subject in each year. However, although defined in terms of traditional 
subjects, revisions to the curriculum between 1997 and 2010 had tended 
to focus more on competences rather than knowledge. From 2010 on, 
the curriculum was explicitly knowledge-rich, with Gibb in particular 
influenced by the work of E. D. Hirsch (2006) and progressive, in that 
content was sequenced to develop understanding over time (DfE, 2013).
The new curriculum was introduced rapidly, coming into effect for 
most year groups (with the exception of years 2 and 6 due to national 
exams) in September 2014. The Department for Education (DfE) issued 
guidance documents, including detailed programs of study for each 
subject at each key stage. Professional development opportunities were 
offered, though critics argued that support for teachers was insufficient, 
especially given the significant changes in core subjects like English, 
mathematics, and science. Resources, including exemplar lesson plans 
and assessment materials, were shared online, but schools often needed 
to create or adapt their resources to align with the new expectations.

The curriculum reform was accompanied by a complete overhaul of 
the assessment and accountability system: 
• Levels of attainment (used since 1989) were abolished. Instead, 
schools were required to develop their own systems for tracking 
student progress.
• New, tougher standardised tests were introduced in 2016 at the ends 
of Key Stages 1 and 2 (respectively years 1 and 2 and years 3–6 in 
primary school) to align with the revised standards.
• New, more challenging specifications were also phased in from 2015 
for the national school leaving examination, the General Certificate 
of Secondary Education (GCSE), taken at age 16, and a new scoring 
system was introduced, using numerical grade from 1 to 9, rather 
than the traditional A*-G system.
In September 2019 the school inspectorate, Ofsted (Office for Stan­

which placed a greater emphasis on curriculum than in previous years 
(Ofsted, 2019). In combination, these elements aimed to bring about a 
necessary alignment between curriculum, assessment and account­
ability, supported through professional development.
It is worth pointing out that the National Curriculum in England is 
compulsory only for local authority-run schools, which account for 
almost half of all primary schools and over 80 % of secondary schools as 
of 2024 (Department for Education, 2024). Academies, which are state- 
funded but independent, are not required to follow the national cur­
riculum, though the vast majority do, not least as they are subject to the 
same exam system and to Ofsted inspections in which the inspectorate is 
looking for a curriculum ‘at least as demanding as’ the national 
curriculum.
What has the impact of this new curriculum been? It is always hard to 
disentangle the effect of specific policy measures, and of course the 
period of the introduction of the national curriculum saw a number of 
changes to the English education system, but there have been in­
dications suggesting a positive impact.
The international comparative assessments PISA, PIRLS4 and TIMSS 
have seen a substantial improvement in the position of England relative 
to comparator countries. Of particular note, as it cancels out most of the 
impact of cultural differences, England has started to significantly 
outperform Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, the other nations 
constituting the United Kingdom which all have competency-based 
curricula (Muijs, forthcoming).

4. Implications for curriculum building
To build a knowledge-rich curriculum that promotes deep and 
meaningful learning for all students, certain key principles need to be 
prioritized, namely content-richness, coherence, and clarity (for a more 
detailed description see Surma et al., 2025). These principles can then 
provide a framework for a curriculum that systematically builds stu­
dents' knowledge and prepares them for complex cognitive tasks, and 
democratic citizenship.
4.1. Key design principles of a knowledge-rich curriculum
4.1.1. Content-richness
To ensure that every child acquires a sufficiently broad and deep 
knowledge base, the curriculum must go beyond children's everyday 
experiences. Such a knowledge-rich curriculum should be both knowl­
edge- and concept-led (Oates, 2011) which can be explored across 
various disciplines, each with their own disciplinary structure and hi­
erarchy (Crato, 2024; Young & Lambert, 2014).
When selecting content, curriculum designers should therefore 
create a plan for learning that spans within and across disciplines, 
ensuring a logical progression that supports meaningful learning. This 
approach allows existing knowledge to serve as the foundation for future 
learning (Ausubel, 1968). However, it is important to avoid the temp­
tation to include too much in the curriculum at the expense of depth, as 
this subsequently is likely to result in a curriculum that is “a mile wide 
and an inch deep” (Schmidt et al., 1997, p. 62). Given the limited time 
frame in education, when selecting content we therefore need to prior­
itize what is important and what is even more important (Wiliam, 2013), 
underlining the need for a careful analysis of the disciplinary content 
logic, and a thorough societal debate with subsequent consensus on the 
selected content among the distinct educational stakeholders.
4.1.2. Coherence
As a knowledge-rich curriculum aims for deep thinking and under­
standing, both vertical and horizontal curricular coherence is essential. 
Vertical coherence means that what students learn in one lesson, course, 
or grade level prepares them for the next lesson, course, or grade level 
(Thijs & van den Akker, 2009). For meaningful learning to take place, 
concepts must be sequenced logically and systematically over time. This 
way, prior learning is the foundation for future learning. However, while 
this might seem straightforward, there is often no one universal perfect 
sequence that applies for everyone (Wiliam, 2013). Nonetheless, for 
learning not to become fragmented and episodic, thinking about 
sequence helps determine what students need to know before moving 
on.
Second, horizontal coherence is needed to grasp the complexity of a 
concept (Thijs & van den Akker, 2009), as subjects are not isolated 
bodies of knowledge (Surma et al., 2025). Horizontal coherence means 
that connections are made across topics, subjects and disciplines at the 
same educational level to ensure that the knowledge is not 
compartmentalized (Van Merri¨enboer et al., 2024). When this is not 
done explicitly, most students will encounter difficulties to do so by 
themselves (Wiliam, 2013).
In addition, curricular coherence also entails alignment within all 
components of the educational system, including clear learning objec­
tives, learning materials, quality monitoring, pedagogy, etc. (Oates, 
2011; Schmidt & Prawat, 2006).
4.1.3. Clarity
Learning goals should be stated clearly, not as a goal in itself but as a 
means to obtain curricular coherence and alignment. With clearly 
defined learning goals teachers know about students' previous educa­
tional experiences, enabling knowledge to become shared between 
rather than restricted to individual students (Surma et al., 2025). 
Teachers are key mediating factors in translating curricular standards 
into teaching and learning activities (Stein et al., 2007). Clear learning 
goals facilitate similar interpretations, and reduce the discrepancies 
between the intended and attained curriculum (Bauersfeld, 1979; Thijs 
& van den Akker, 2009). Furthermore, clarity in learning goals impacts 
overall system alignment. With clear learning goals, assessment and 
quality monitoring become more reliable (Oates, 2011; Steiner et al., 
2018) and less subjective. However, curricular overload remains a risk. 
Too many specific learning goals could result in a reduction of the 
curriculum to a mere checklist (Surma et al., 2025). Finally, clarity is 
also important for the design and development of textbooks and digital 
learning materials (Crato, 2024; Oates, 2014). While teachers often rely 
on them, Dockx et al. (2020) highlight that the quality of learning ma­
terials can vary significantly. Too broadly formulated learning objec­
tives can then put too much power in the hands of commercial parties to 
determine educational quality.
4.2. Creating a shared knowledge base for all students
The results described in Section 3 suggest that implementing a 
knowledge-rich curriculum can improve student achievement, yet also 
be a transformative step towards ensuring all learners have access to a 
body of foundational knowledge necessary for deep learning and the 
acquisition of complex cognitive skills. This approach addresses indi­
vidual differences between more- and less-advantaged students by 
providing all students - not only those with access to enriched experi­
ences and knowledge outside of school - the possibility to systematically 
acquire and build knowledge from an early age. By offering a content- 
rich, well-sequenced and coherent curriculum, implemented by means 
of effective pedagogy in the classrooms, schools can create an environ­
ment where every student has the opportunity to succeed.
So how does it work? Knowledge significantly impacts a student's 
ability to understand both oral and written language, and schools play a 
particularly vital role in providing this knowledge to less-advantaged 
students. Knowledge is a cognitive scaffold, allowing students to inte­
grate new information into existing knowledge-frameworks (i.e., their 
cognitive schemas). We often assume a certain level of shared knowl­
edge when communicating, but for students who may have limited 
exposure to enriching experiences outside school, this assumed shared 
knowledge is often lacking - not due to limitations in ability but rather a 
gap in access (Hirsch, 2016). For less-advantaged students who may not 
encounter a wide variety of enriching content outside school, access to 
this knowledge is a key factor in succeeding at school.
Less-advantaged students may miss crucial background under­
standing that their more advantaged peers pick up through family, 
travel, or extracurricular learning. Consequently, this gap is not just 
educationally significant but has broader societal implications. Without 
access to this shared knowledge, inequality in education is likely to 
grow. When schools commit to creating a content-rich curriculum that 
intentionally builds shared knowledge, they not only improve language 
comprehension but also equip all students with a shared foundation that 
enables them to actively participate in society. As Hirsch (2009) argues,

5. Conclusion
We have argued that knowledge building should play a central role 
in the curriculum to promote deep thinking, and as a solid foundation for 
acquiring complex cognitive skills. This is supported by cognitive psy­
chological research showing that the construction of broad and deep 
knowledge structures and the way they are presented lay important 
foundations for meaningful learning, and that this knowledge plays a 
central role in acquiring complex cognitive skills such as reading 
comprehension. 
However, 
when 
implementing 
knowledge-rich 
curricula it is important to proceed with some caution. The curriculum 
should not simply provide a list of concepts and facts students should 
know, leading to ‘tick box’ behaviour. It is important to underline that 
building knowledge “[…] is not just accumulating facts; rather, children 
need to develop knowledge networks, comprised of clusters of concepts 
that are coherent, generative, and supportive of future learning in a 
domain” (Neuman et al., 2014, p. 147). Moreover, what knowledge 
matters is in many subjects a (socio-political) choice and should be 
subject to a thorough debate between distinct educational stakeholders. 
The subsequent curriculum will be a compromise, and there are no 
choices without trade-offs if we want to avoid the legitimate risk or 
curricular overload. However, it is important to make clear choices 
between what we consider important and even more important (Wiliam, 
2013), and to make those trade-offs explicit. Then, the adoption of a 
knowledge-rich curriculum that is content-rich, coherent, and clear, can 
provide a shared body of foundational knowledge to promote achieve­
ment for more and less advantaged learners, as all students acquire the 
knowledge and skills necessary to participate and thrive in school and 
democratic society.
