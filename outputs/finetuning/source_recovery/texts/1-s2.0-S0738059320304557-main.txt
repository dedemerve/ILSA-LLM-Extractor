This study provides new evidence on school efficiency for low and middle income countries. We use data from
PISA for Development (2017) for seven countries to obtain estimates on school efficiency using data envelopment
analysis, both for cognitive and non-cognitive outputs, and their determinants. We find that there is a scope to
increase efficiency by 20–22% via boosting both types of educational outputs and by reducing within-country
disparity on schools’ efficiency scores by weakening the impact of students’ disadvantages. Our results suggest that schools cognitive inequality can be reduced alongside inefficiency. Cross-country results suggest similar
drivers of efficiency across countries, at least for students’ school determinants, though we find more nuanced
results on teachers and policies determinants for efficiency.

1. Introduction
Education, understood as a process of accumulation of human capital, can be hampered by inefficient factors that prevent reaching the
maximum possible educational level for a given level of spending.
Hence, efficiency on education provision, where schools make the best
use of available inputs, had become a topic of intense debate among
educational stakeholders (De Witte and López-Torres, 2017). To
empirically contribute to this debate within the context of
resource-constrained education systems is paramount, not only because
of the few available resources which need to be efficiently administered,
but also because over the last decade there has been an increasing
educational access (Filmer et al., 2018) yielding new challenges in
educational quality and equity. This is why the analysis of school efficiency and its comparison among lower and middle income countries
(LMIC) is policy relevant. Ultimately, efficient and equitable education
systems can lead to higher levels of economic growth and social cohesion (Woessmann, 2008).
Some studies (e.g., Almlund et al., 2011; Chernyshenko et al., 2018;
Cordero et al., 2016) emphasise both the relevance of cognitive and
non-cognitive dimensions of educational results. However, within the
school efficiency literature, studies have been silent with regards to the
efficiency of non-cognitive outcomes and their relationship with
cognitive efficiency. Thus, it is necessary to incorporate the

non-cognitive dimension in efficiency analysis, particularly in LMIC
educational contexts, where the detrimental effects of school violence
and weak learning environments on students’ outcomes are well documented and compounded by the added class heterogeneity brought via
multigrade teaching (Evans and Yuan, 2018; Murillo and Román, 2011).
Moreover, even though earlier research on developed countries (Grosskopf et al., 2017; Woessmann, 2007) finds a lack of evidence for a
negative relationship between efficiency and equity, a policy relevant
question is whether this also holds for LMIC education systems since
their expansion would make schools to operate with lower student’s
inputs. Knowing if schools’ efficiency can be increased in parallel to
narrowing the gaps among sub-populations in the distribution of
educational outcomes is, therefore, crucial.
In this paper, using Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) (Thanassoulis,
2001) which mimics an education production function where school
inputs are transformed into outputs (Levin, 1974; Hanushek and
Woessmann, 2011), we estimate the efficiency scores of 1180 schools in
seven low and middle income countries from three different regions
(Ecuador, Guatemala, Honduras and Paraguay from Latin America;
Senegal and Zambia from sub-Saharan Africa; and Cambodia from the
Southeast Asia) and the determinants of their efficiency levels. The
analysis is based on the 2017 PISA for Development (PISA-D). This allows us to contribute to the school efficiency literature in different ways.

Turning to work carried out in Latin America, there is the study of
Santín and Sicilia (2015) for Uruguay. The authors uses two waves of the
PISA data (2009 and 2012) to estimate the technical efficiency of Uruguayan public schools, finding a decrease in school efficiency between
the two waves and a direct relationship between a greater responsibility
of school principals in the distribution of the school budget and the level
of public schools’ efficiency. On the other hand, a close study to ours
(both from the point of view of being an international study of efficiency
and for its focus on less developed countries) is Antequera (2019) – a
comparative analysis of efficiency and its determinants for South
America. This study measures technical efficiency of primary schools in
eight countries of this region using alternatives of the DEA methodology
finding inefficiency levels around 20% and that one of the key determinants of efficiency is the level of school infrastructure.
Also from an international approach (but for OECD countries) there
is the analysis of secondary school efficiency by Agasisti and Zoido
(2018). Their study, based on a sample of 30 countries from PISA (2012)
data including 8500 schools, estimates an international efficiency
frontier at 0.73, which implies an inefficiency of 27%. Yet they find that
the degree of inefficiency varies between countries, from 15% in
Singapore to 33% in Slovenia, and their results also show a greater
dispersion within each country than between them. Cordero et al.
(2017) efficiency study of primary schools from 16 European countries
(based on PIRLS 2011) is another example of cross-country efficiency
analysis. Their study provides a methodological improvement through
the adaption of a metafrontier framework (Battese et al., 2004) to
compare and decompose the technical efficiency of primary schools
operating in heterogeneous contexts between two different levels
(school and country). Sutherland et al. (2009) study for 30 OECD
countries (PISA, 2003) find that, holding resources constant while
moving towards best practice, there is a room for efficiency improvements on PISA scores by an average of 5% for OECD countries and by
around 10% for the least efficient countries.
However, the studies listed above investigate schools’ efficiency
relying on cognitive outputs (i.e., based on test scores) and, importantly,
they do not derive a non-cognitive frontier. In the small group of studies
where non-cognitive outcomes are included (i.e., Cordero et al., 2017),
they are used as determinants of efficiency but not as outputs. The only
exception seems to be Mainardes et al. (2014) which is closer to our
study as they analyse the level of efficiency of Portuguese public universities, according to the expectations and satisfaction of their students.
So, as far as we are aware, our study is the first one which simultaneously estimates the two types of efficiency across schools, cognitive
and non-cognitive and their relationship, from an international
perspective.

1. What is the degree of cognitive and non-cognitive efficiency for LMIC
and their relationship? And does efficiency vary across countries?
2. Which generic students, teachers, schools characteristics as well as
policy factors are associated with both types of efficiency?
3. What is the relationship of equity with efficiency for the whole
sample of LMIC and between countries?
4. Are generic factors associated with efficiency fundamentally
different among the seven LMIC?
The remaining of the paper is organised as follows. The next section
contains a brief background review on efficiency in education (more
background is presented when outlining the data). In Section 3, we
describe the data and the methods used. Section 4 presents the findings
for the four research questions. Conclusions and policy implications are
included in the last section.
2. Literature review
There is an extensive body of empirical work that studies the efficiency of education at different educational levels, but largely for
developed countries based on international learning surveys (Afonso
and Aubyn, 2006; Agasisti and Zoido, 2018; Cordero et al., 2017;
Sutherland et al., 2009). An exhaustive and up to date review of the
literature on efficiency in education can be found in De Witte and
López-Torres (2017). The main methodology used by empirical research
on school efficiency is Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) (Coelli et al.,
2005; Thanassoulis, 2001). This technique, with its variants, has been
used to estimate efficiency in many countries of the world with different
socio-economic and cultural realities.
There are various country specific studies on school efficiency. For
the United States, there is the work carried out by Primont and
Domazlicky (2006) who use a two-stage DEA model to analyse efficiency
of schools in the state of Missouri and how their efficiency levels are
related to the implementation of public policies. For Italy, Agasisti
(2013) uses different alternatives of the DEA methodology to estimate
efficiency in a sample of secondary schools in using PISA (2006) data; a
notable result of this study is the leverage effects of competition among
schools. For the New Zealand case, Alexander et al. (2010) estimate the
efficiency levels of secondary schools using a double bootstrap procedure and find evidence that teaching quality influences efficiency levels.
Ramzi et al. (2016) also apply DEA in the case of the Tunisian provinces
and find that the inefficiency of education is strongly related to the
poverty level of each province. For the SSA region, Akyeampong et al.
(2018) study both the technical and cost efficiency for around 170
secondary schools in Malawi and Uganda, finding that efficiency is
largely influenced by two layers related to the structure of secondary
school supply: the typology of school types and their locations. For these
two countries, reaching both technical and cost efficiency is achievable
for all schools regardless of their type. Indeed, the authors find that
suitable policies and unique practices (where their resources are utilised
more efficiently at a lower cost) are feasible across secondary schools of
these two countries. Moreover, Burney et al. (2013) estimate the technical and allocation efficiency at different levels of Kuwait public
schools for two time periods. Here, teachers’ salary and the percentage

3. Data and methods
3.1. Data and sample
We use data from the 2017 PISA for Development (PISA-D), a
learning survey which includes students from seven low and middle
income countries/economies at grade 7 (15 years-old) or above. The
countries included in the survey are: Cambodia, Ecuador, Guatemala,
Honduras, Paraguay, Senegal and Zambia. The aim of PISA-D is to be
more relevant to education systems with larger populations of poor and
marginalised students which, in turn, is achieved through two distinctive elements (OECD, 2018).1
First, test instruments cover a wider range of performance at the
lower end of the PISA scales. Specifically, given the goal of PISA-D to

attempt to capture issues related to barriers to school attendance and
factors that may impede students’ progress through education in

physical health, travel time to school) and specific bottlenecks on education policies (e.g., multigrade teaching, repetition and teaching
absenteeism replacement policies), as well as index of family SES which
also captures student’s basic needs such as food security and quality
shelter (Ward, 2018). Moreover, PISA-D’s contextual questionnaires is
enhanced by measuring students’ engagement, health and wellbeing as
key outcomes in parallel to achievement, as well as classroom and school
processes related to these outcomes (OECD, 2018). This is important as
it allows us to jointly examine efficiency based on cognitive outcomes as
well as relevant non-cognitive outcomes.2

composed of 34,604 students, 15,169 teachers and 1299 schools. After
excluding missing values on DEA inputs, our final working sample
contains 1180 schools. The design of PISA-D, that is, a more granular
definition of student performance at the lower achievement levels and
the details on social and economic contexts, allow us to measure school
efficiency and its relationship with equity for the seven LMIC.3

3.2. Variables
We consider variables which mimic the education production function where school inputs are transformed into outputs and, subsequently, another set of environmental variables are selected to explain
the school efficiency scores (Worthington, 2001). The selection of inputs
(I) and outputs (O) for the DEA analysis is guided by previous studies on
school efficiency (e.g., Afonso and Aubyn, 2006; Agasisti and Zoido,
2018; Cordero et al., 2017; Sutherland et al., 2009). All outputs and
inputs are average values at the school level.
We define two set of outputs: (i) cognitive outputs (math, reading
and science scores), (ii) non-cognitive outputs (socialisation, and positive attitudes and feelings towards school). For cognitive outcomes, ten
plausible values are randomly selected for each student (OECD, 2018,
Chapter 9). We use the first plausible value for each subject following
previous studies (e.g., Agasisti and Zoido, 2018; Cordero et al., 2017).4
Indices for non-cognitive outcomes are derived by factor analysis using a
group of questions for each concept.5 Socialisation, as well as positive
feelings, are linked to ‘emotional stability’, one of the five components
of personality skills (Chernyshenko et al., 2018; Cordero et al., 2016),
while the index on school’s attitudes measures non-cognitive traits

Panel B of Table 1 contains the list of second stage variables, that is,
environmental factors explaining the degree of school’s efficiency which
can be thought of as the main determinants of poorer education systems.
We group them into three categories: students, teachers and schools.
First, at the student level, the gender gap is a key socio-cultural learning’s driver for LMIC (see, Deutsch et al., 2013 for Latin America) and,
likewise, a higher prevalence of student’s work (a rate of 48.3% for the
whole sample) is a main factor leading to lower learning (Sánchez et al.,
2009), whereas preschool attendance and repetition in conjunction with
longer travel time to school (which promotes lateness and truancy
among students) attempt to capture educational supply factors and
disadvantaged environments where students may be more susceptible to
fail (Grosskopf et al., 2014). As shown by Pov et al. (2020), a study of the
key factors behind learning in lower secondary rural schools in
Cambodia, absenteeism has significant negative effects among disadvantaged students. Another key determinant on achievement at the
student level is language – a third of students do not speak the test
language at home. Research (Pasquier-Doumer and Brandon, 2015;
Song et al., 2014) has shown that schools with a larger proportion of
students who do not speak the language of the test at home face additional barriers to efficiency due to language transition and wider
discrimination associated with indigeneity/ethnicity. We also insert
among students’ covariates overall health (which is a particular barrier
in LMIC affecting achievement due to hunger, chronic illness and
increasing the chances of students missing school) as well as school
safety and expectations.
Second, we include an array of teachers characteristics and for the
learning environment: gender, qualifications, professional development
undertook over the last year, payment satisfaction, whether they are
involved in teaching across grades6 and consensus on goals with the
school’s principal. Third, schools characteristics included are: school
(area) location (Cordero et al., 2017; Kantabutra, 2009) because urban
schools can be operated at lower costs with higher scores, school type
(public/private) (Cherchye et al., 2010), financial stability (measured
through the proportion of funding coming from fees and government
contributions), class size, enrolment and whether the school offers free
breakfast and lunch (around 16% in the sample). Moreover, the model
includes policy variables (see Table B1). Repetition and dropout at
secondary level is a core issue for poorer education systems7 haltering
efficiency because it slows the flow of children through the system.
Likewise, additional barriers to efficiency are teacher shortage and
attrition. Thus, we evaluate the impact of distinct repetition policies and
different policies for students at risk of dropping out (e.g., remedial
classes, the allocation of guide teachers), and teaching policies to
address absenteeism (teacher substitution, grouping classes).
Lastly, we use a range of inequality indicators to assess the efficiencyequity trade off (see Table B2). All indicators are measured at the school
level and so they encompass within-school inequality. The framework of
educational prosperity used in PISA-D (Willms, 2018a,b) defines two
concepts for monitoring inequalities in educational outcomes: “equality”-differences among sub-populations (e.g., gender, poverty,

disability) in the distribution of their educational outcomes; and
“equity”-differences among sub-populations in their access to school and
to the resources and schooling processes that affect schooling outcomes
(e.g., quality instruction, material resources, learning time). As such,
most of the indicators of Table B2 are equality measures. For example,
the different school ratios, calculated as the school mean learning scores
of disadvantaged groups over the advantaged groups (be it by gender, by
working status, etc.), are equality measures. Other indicators employed
measure the degree of heterogeneity on the distribution of learning
scores (standard deviation) and the degree of equality of condition
within a population (Gini coefficient). In line with Agasisti and Zoido
(2018) we insert a measure of inclusivity (i.e., proportion of students
who obtain a score below level 2), and a school association of performance with student family SES (correlation coefficient), and what proportion of disadvantaged students (bottom half of family SES
distribution) who are high performers (reaching level 2 or above).
3.3. Methodology

Envelopment Analysis (DEA) (Coelli et al., 2005; Thanassoulis, 2001).

1957). The starting point is an educational production function where it

estimated frontier which represents the maximum output that can be
achieved given the available resources. Each school k (k = 1, …, K) is
characterized for a combination of outputs (m = 1, …, M) and inputs
(n = 1, …, N) and a production technology, a set of all feasible

repetition and additional support for students at risk of repeating are

not in other three countries (Ecuador, Guatemala and Cambodia). As we

relief teachers is advantageous (with Honduras, Cambodia and Senegal
supporting this finding). Ability grouping either does not have an effect
on efficiency or it leads to lower school efficiency.

results qualitatively indicate similar drivers of efficiency across the
seven countries, at least for students’ school determinants. There are
some regional differences such as on school climate, which is a more
acute issue in LAC region, for instance. Similarly, multigrade teaching
and teaching quality have differential impact on efficiency between
countries, as well as repetition and teaching substitution policies.
Overall, distinct features and obstacles of education systems are reflected in more nuance country results on teachers and policy determinants for efficiency.
5. Conclusions and policy implications
An international cross-country comparative analysis of the determinants of efficiency across less developed economies is essential
because of their unique educational challenges. This requires a comparable database fitted to the realities of poorer education systems. In this
paper, we used the PISA-D dataset which allowed us to analyse the determinants of school efficiency at the secondary level in low- and
middle-income countries (LMIC). Our analysis employed the DEA
technique to come up with estimations for efficiency scores of 1180
secondary schools from seven LMIC. In doing so, we were able to expand

and equality does not hold. Estimates indicated that it is possible that
efficiency (both cognitive and non-cognitive) could be improved by
increasing equality within schools; in fact, schools showing a greater
degree of equality (be it via the distributions of test scores, or in terms of
higher inclusion) are also the more efficient schools.

5.1. Main findings
The study made a range of contributions to the literature. First, by
using non-cognitive outputs as outcomes of the educational process we
produced novel estimates for the efficiency frontier for these types of
outcomes and, furthermore, we assessed more comprehensively whether
there is a trade-off between equality and efficiency in schools. As far we
are aware, both the derivation of a non-cognitive frontier and the
detailed joint analysis of equality and efficient for LMIC, are new. Results showed that it is possible to increase cognitive and non-cognitive
educational results approximately between 20% and 22% keeping the
level of inputs constant; hence the level of inefficiency is around 20%
and there is a scope to raise efficiency for the seven countries. Greater
dispersion was found for cognitive efficiency and, in line with earlier
studies (e.g., Agasisti and Zoido, 2018), a larger variation on school
efficiency was found within each country rather than between countries.
Second, another interesting result found is the positive relationship
between levels of cognitive and non-cognitive efficiency among the
poorest countries. Plainly, in the low-income countries of our sample, an
increase in efficiency would imply a simultaneous improvement in
cognitive and non-cognitive outcomes. The relevance of these findings
for low income countries cannot be underestimated; that is, school

5.2. Policy implications and limitations
Two broad policy relevant messages emerges from our study: (i)
there is scope to improve school efficiency across LMIC education systems, and (ii) improvements on efficiency can be attained in conjunction
with boosting equity. More equitable education systems are also more
efficient. Thus, leading pathways to increasing efficiency are naturally
those which deal with cumulative and compounded disadvantages faced
by students throughout their educational journeys up to secondary
school -e.g., repetition, issues related to language spoken at home and to
school climate, an environment which is shaped by neighbours through
school location. Besides, within the layer of education processes influenced by teacher and school policies, answers on what works better are
not homogenous; for instance, some policies on school climate have a
higher leverage effect in countries from LAC (as it was also shown
through the high correlation of cognitive and non-cognitive efficiency),
other policies on teacher working conditions are relatively more efficient in either SSA or Asia countries from our sample. Plainly, educational and social approaches targeting students barriers to learning can
be seen as first stage of an overall strategy to enhance technical efficiency while, as a second stage strategy, more nuanced policies are
required within secondary schools.
The paper’s analysis has some limitations. As for most learning
surveys, a standard limitation of PISA-D is the cross-section nature of the
data and that its exclusion out of school students, a larger proportion
across poorer countries. This implies that the estimates reported should
be examined with caution and cannot be interpreted in a causal sense,
and derived estimates on efficiency measure upper bound relationships.
