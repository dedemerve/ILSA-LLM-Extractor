Given the significance of teacher quality and the pressing need to address teacher shortages
worldwide, understanding the dynamics of adolescents’ career decision-making among adolescents is crucial. This study aimed to shed light on the evolving landscape of adolescents’ teaching
career expectations and its influencing factors using the Programme for International Student
Assessment data spanning two decades. Results showed that (a) differences between boys’ and
girls’ expectations for teaching careers had narrowed in many advanced economies, with an
increasing number of boys aspiring to become teachers. (b) The disparities between high and low
socioeconomic status students’ expectations for teaching had also been reduced, while efforts
were still needed to recruit prospective teachers from diverse backgrounds. (c) Boys required
enjoyment of reading, self-efficacy, and family and teacher support to be attracted to teaching
careers, but girls were naturally inclined to be teachers. Importantly, each economy exhibited its
unique combinational pattern of pull and push factors. The findings emphasised the need to
address the human resource shortages on multiple fronts, particularly reducing gender disparities,
engaging boys in teaching careers, and promoting inclusivity in the teaching profession.

1. Introduction
Teacher quality determines students’ educational outcomes even after considering prior achievement and socioeconomic status
(SES), leading to mounting concerns about who enters teaching and why (Hanushek et al., 2019; OECD, 2018). Many studies have been
conducted to reveal the influencing factors, while most concentrate on pre-service teachers in developed economies, such as the USA
and Australia (Fray & Gore, 2018). The social status and income of teachers vary substantially among economies, and thus, factors
affecting career choices in teaching can differ in less-developed areas. Given the chronic teacher shortages worldwide, especially in
developing regions (UNESCO Institute for Statistics, 2016), conducting research in a wider range of contexts and for a more extended
period is imperative. Although adolescents’ expectations of being teachers may alter over time, the initial pool of prospective candidates is crucial to ensuring an adequate supply of qualified teachers. This is particularly significant given the persistent teacher
shortages, which can be mitigated from the beginning of the "leaking pipeline."
Adolescents’ career expectations tend to endure over time (Forgasz & Leder, 2017). However, the absence of accurate
self-assessment and limited access to labor market information hinders the development of well-informed career expectations. As a
result, adolescents are highly susceptible to influences from their contextual environment, including peers, teachers, parents, and

1.1. Adolescents’ teaching career expectations
Career expectations refer to the realistic and accessible choices that adolescents make regarding their future occupations (Arbona &
Novy, 1991). These expectations typically take shape in childhood and gradually become more focused and compromised during
adolescence and young adulthood (Gottfredson, 1981). Importantly, career expectations not only impact adolescents’ future educational and occupational achievements but also serve as predictors of their earning potential and social status in adulthood, particularly
for those from disadvantaged backgrounds (Ashby & Schoon, 2010; Greve et al., 2021). Although the present survey on adolescents
would not accurately measure the actual number of young people entering teaching, 15-year-olds were at a critical age to decide on
their streams (science/ arts/ social science) of senior secondary school study, ultimately leading to different university majors.
Research has shown that students expecting to be scientists at age 13 are 3.4 times more likely to earn science degrees in college (Tai
et al., 2006). Nevertheless, adolescents often lack sufficient self-knowledge and access to labor market information, which inhibits
their ability to make informed decisions about their future careers. In such circumstances, educational systems have the opportunity to
guide young people in accurately assessing their capabilities and unlocking their occupational potential, thereby aligning their expectations with local workforce demands.
Teaching is an esteemed profession that plays a vital role in student development, human resources planning, and national
prosperity. With a large number of experienced teachers approaching retirement age in the next decade, governments are facing
increasing pressure to recruit and train new teachers (OECD, 2021). Although a larger share of adolescents express an interest in
teaching careers compared to the proportion of teachers in the adult population (OECD, 2018), this can be attributed in part to the
familiarity with teaching as an occupation among teenagers. Unfortunately, a considerable number of them abandon their teaching
career plans before reaching the age of 23 (Sikora, 2021). Moreover, recent reports indicate that 15-year-olds who envision becoming
teachers generally exhibit lower math and reading scores than their counterparts aspiring for other occupations requiring tertiary
education (OECD, 2018). The shortage of teachers and the challenges in recruitment have raised concerns about attracting motivated
and qualified individuals to the teaching profession. Therefore, comprehending how various factors influencing adolescents’ expectations of becoming teachers have evolved over the past decades is a prerequisite for addressing this issue effectively.
1.2. Expectations to teach
Expectations regarding the outcome or success of individuals’ efforts strongly predict their performance and choices (Wigfield &
Eccles, 2000). Adolescents’ expectations to teach are affected by a variety of environmental (e.g., working conditions and salaries) and
individual (e.g., attitudes and values) factors. Previous research has shown that altruistic, intrinsic, and extrinsic values are the three
primary driving forces behind individuals pursuing teaching careers (Fray & Gore, 2018). Intrinsic value generally emerges as a
stronger predictor of career choices in teaching than extrinsic ones (Jugović et al., 2012). The relative importance of intrinsic and
extrinsic values can vary considerably depending on cultural, gender, and ability factors. For instance, in Western countries, intrinsic
and altruistic values surpass extrinsic ones in predicting individuals’ choice of teaching (Jungert et al., 2014). On the contrary, in Asian
countries, extrinsic value such as social status, salaries, and tuition waivers has a stronger influence on young people’s choice of
teaching (Gao & Trent, 2009; Liu, 2010). In the present study, we asked about their intrinsic interest in reading, one critical value to
instill among students. Specifically, we explored the evolving influence of enjoyment of reading across 39 economies over the past two
decades.
Moreover, two types of environmental factors significantly influence teaching expectations. Firstly, teaching is often viewed as an
ideal profession for maintaining a desirable work-life balance due to its flexible working hours and holidays (Gu & Lai, 2012; Pop &
Turner, 2009). This aspect appeals to individuals seeking a lifestyle accommodating personal commitments and responsibilities.
Secondly, teaching offers favourable working conditions, including job security, a stable income, and potential career advancement
opportunities (Cheung & Yuen, 2016; Sinclair, 2008). These factors contribute to the attractiveness of teaching as a profession,
ensuring financial stability and long-term prospects for individuals considering a career in education. One implication from these
considerations is that in societies with females taking the stereotypical caretaker role for children, teaching will be a preferentially
desirable career.
Furthermore, “adaptive” and “maladaptive” motives have been found to influence individuals’ choice of teaching (Fokkens-Bruinsma & Canrinus, 2012). Adaptive motives foster effort, involvement, and commitment, whereas maladaptive motives contribute
to disengagement and superficial participation in tasks (Sinclair et al., 2006). The presence of a fallback career motive is particularly
detrimental, as teaching is seen as a personal backup plan to avoid career uncertainty (Klassen et al., 2011). When individuals perceive
their ideal career choices as unattainable, teaching becomes a temporary and non-committal option associated with low engagement
and simplistic beliefs about teaching (Thomson et al., 2012). In this regard, the fallback career motive is often maladaptive and merits
further attention (Wong et al., 2014). Students with lower self-efficacy may be more likely to consider teaching as a fallback option.
Ideally, we would want students with high self-efficacy to aspire to teaching careers, but the actual scenario can vary across different
years and countries, necessitating further investigation.

1.3. Factors affecting expectations to teach
Students’ expectations to teach are profoundly influenced by their country of residence, gender, and ability. It is evident that the
popularity of teaching careers differs across countries, which can be partly attributed to variations in teacher certification requirements, employment flexibility, and principles of equality within educational systems (Watt et al., 2012). Research indicates that
boys are more responsive to salary incentives, but a higher income alone is unlikely to attract high-achieving students toward teaching
careers (OECD, 2018). Academically talented students typically aspire to professions that are respected, challenging, and have the
potential to make a significant societal impact (Han et al., 2018).
To disentangle the complex interactions among culture, gender, and ability, this study analysed the potential variations across
different economies and gender groups. Quadratic terms were utilised to explore potential nonlinear relationships between students’
ability and their expectations of pursuing teaching careers. This approach allowed us to investigate whether specific patterns emerged,
such as average-achieving students (not the highest nor the lowest achievers) primarily considering teaching as a career path.
Gender undoubtedly plays a significant role in shaping adolescents’ expectations of pursuing teaching careers, as education remains one of the most gender-segregated fields worldwide (OECD, 2021). The gender disparity in teaching gradually decreases as the
level of education rises, with figures ranging from 96 % of females at the pre-primary level to only 44 % at the tertiary level across
OECD countries (OECD, 2021). Gender stereotypes and societal perceptions of different occupations strongly influence individual
career choices, leading to teaching being predominantly viewed as a feminised profession that involves direct human contact, care, and
nurturing (Murray, 2006). In certain social contexts, the intertwining of religious purposes and gender roles further shapes individual
decisions regarding teaching, as observed in countries like Indonesia and Turkey (Akar, 2012; Aksu et al., 2010).
Establishing a gender-balanced professional environment contributes to fostering positive gender identity among adolescents and
challenging traditional gender stereotypes (Hutchings et al., 2008). Numerous studies have examined the factors influencing boys’
reluctance to pursue teaching careers. Han et al. (2020) conducted a cross-country analysis of adolescents’ teaching career expectations across 49 countries. They found that an increased representation of male teachers and higher teacher salaries could attract more
boys to consider teaching. Another study by Han and Borgonovi (2020) revealed that gender gaps in teaching career expectations
tended to narrow when teachers received competitive salaries and possessed numeracy skills comparable to those in science and
technology professions. As gender equality continues to make strides in various fields, it is anticipated that gender disparities in
adolescents’ expectations of pursuing teaching careers have been progressively reduced over the past two decades.
In addition to individual factors, significant others, as part of the contextual influences, wield a significant impact on adolescents’
expectations of pursuing teaching careers. Parental encouragement, regardless of cultural background, has been found to enhance
students’ aspirations of becoming teachers (Butt et al., 2010). Conversely, negative comments and insufficient family support can
discourage young people from choosing teaching as a career path (Kelly & Northrop, 2015).
The influence of significant others, however, can vary across economies. Research indicates that family members exert a strong
influence on students in Portugal, while their impact is relatively weaker in Sweden, where friends play a more significant role (Flores
& Niklasson, 2014). Teacher support also plays a crucial role in shaping the expectations of American college students regarding
teaching careers, but this may not hold true for Chinese students (Liu, 2010). Exploring how family and teacher support have shaped
adolescents’ expectations of pursuing teaching careers across different cultures over the past two decades is indispensable. Furthermore, we examined the impact of teacher enthusiasm on adolescents’ aspirations to become teachers, as enthusiastic teaching has the
potential to stimulate students’ intrinsic value and expectations toward teaching (Thomson & Palermo, 2014).
1.4. Social cognitive career theory
SCCT, derived from Bandura’s (1986) Social Cognitive Theory, is a prominent conceptual model that explains individual career
interests, choices, and performance. It takes a comprehensive approach to understanding career development by considering both
cognitive-person variables and contextual influences. The cognitive-person variables encompass self-efficacy, outcome expectations,
and goals, which play a crucial role in shaping career behaviours. On the other hand, contextual influences encompass demographic
backgrounds, environmental features, and special learning experiences, contributing to the individual’s career development (Lent
et al., 1994,2000). In the context of an uncertain labor market and unpredictable economic events, SCCT has evolved to encompass
dynamic adaptations in career decision-making across the lifespan. Recognizing that career development is a lifelong process, SCCT
has expanded its scope to account for the changing nature of work and the challenges individuals face in making career decisions (Lent
& Brown, 2013,2020).
The study was situated within the SCCT framework by incorporating personal inputs and contextual influences to examine the
development of teaching career expectations among adolescents. The personal variables considered in the study included gender, SES,
ability, enjoyment of reading, and self-efficacy. Self-efficacy, a key construct in SCCT, refers to individuals’ beliefs in their capability to
effectively perform tasks and achieve desired outcomes (Lent et al., 1994). In the scope of the study, self-efficacy links not only to
adolescents’ confidence in their career success but also to the possibility of adopting fallback career motives in choosing teaching as a
future career. Particularly, this study recognised the significance of contextual influences like family and teacher support. We
examined how the sources of support, referring to the encouragement, guidance, and resources provided by teachers and parents,
could impact adolescents’ teaching career expectations. By integrating both personal and contextual factors, the study would shed light
on the development of teaching career expectations reflecting the central tenets of SCCT.

1.5. The present study
Given the ongoing challenges of global teaching workforce shortages and the dynamic nature of adolescents’ expectations for
teaching careers, it is pressing to gain a comprehensive understanding of the individuals and factors influencing their career aspirations
across diverse educational systems over the recent decades. Also, the significant gender segregation within the teaching profession
worldwide compels us to examine the distinct factors that attract boys and girls to pursue teaching careers within the context of each
economy. Therefore, the present study aimed to answer:
(a) What were the trends in the proportions of adolescents expecting to pursue teaching careers over the past two decades?
(b) How did personal factors such as gender, SES, ability, enjoyment of reading, and self-efficacy influence adolescents’ expectations of becoming teachers over time?
(c) How did contextual factors including family and teacher support and teacher enthusiasm affect adolescents’ expectations of
becoming teachers over time?
(d) Did the impacts of these factors on adolescents’ teaching career expectations vary across gender groups and different educational systems?
2. Methods
2.1. Participants
This study utilised the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) data, conducted by the Organization for Economic
Cooperation and Development (OECD), which is a triennial large-scale international assessment measuring the reading, math, and
science abilities of 15-year-olds. The project was initiated in 2000 with 43 participating economies and expanded to include 78
participants by 2018. Each cycle of the assessment focused on a specific domain, with questionnaire data also collected to capture
students’ attitudes, dispositions, and background variables related to the chosen domain. Preliminary analyses revealed a close association among teaching career expectations, reading performance, and reading-related constructs. Hence, the two-decade trends in
teaching career expectations were examined based on PISA data from 2000 and 2018, encompassing 39 economies, in which data was
available in both cycles.
2.2. Measures
The PISA project employed multiple-choice and open-ended questions to assess students’ academic abilities and collect their
background information. To ensure the comparability of PISA results across economies and test cycles, appropriate scaling approaches
were implemented, following the detailed procedures outlined in the PISA Technical Report (OECD, 2019). The variables selection was
guided by and based on the SCCT conceptual framework and the availability of variables across the 2000 and 2018 PISA cycles.
2.2.1. Teaching career expectations
In the PISA project, students’ career expectations, the outcome variable, were assessed through an open-ended question: "What
kind of job do you expect to have when you are about 30 years old?" These responses were coded into four-digit classification numbers
based on the International Standard Classification of Occupations 1988 (ISCO-88, for PISA 2000) and the 2008 editions (ISCO-08, for
PISA 2018). The ISCO-88 codes were mapped to their corresponding codes in ISCO-08, allowing for conversion between the two
classification systems. This study focused on students who expressed an expectation of becoming a teacher, including primary, secondary, and special education teachers. These occupations were classified as Skill Level 4, which required a minimum of a first degree
obtained from a higher education institution through a study period of 3–6 years. For analytical purposes, teaching career expectations
were recoded dichotomously for each student.
2.2.2. Reading achievement
The reading assessment in PISA evaluated students’ reading comprehension skills, including their ability to understand, interpret,
and analyze various types of texts. Tasks simulated real-world reading situations and required students to navigate different text
formats and critically evaluate main ideas and supporting details.
2.2.3. Math and science achievement
PISA assessed students’ mathematical and scientific abilities through real-life problem-solving tasks. The math assessment covered
various topics like numbers, algebra, geometry, statistics, and probability, while the science assessment evaluated students’ understanding of scientific concepts and their ability to apply scientific inquiry and critical thinking. To represent overall science
achievement, math and science scores were averaged (abbreviated as MS) due to their strong positive correlation (r = 0.81 in 2000,
0.85 in 2018).
2.2.4. Socioeconomic status
SES, representing a student’s access to family resources and social standing, was assessed in PISA using the index of economic,
social, and cultural status (ESCS). ESCS captured the highest level of education and occupational status of parents, as well as home

2.2.5. Psychological attributes
Enjoyment of reading. The index was derived from students’ level of agreement with the following statements: “I read only if I have
to”; “Reading is one of my favourite hobbies”; “I like talking about books with other people”; “For me, reading is a waste of time”; and
“I read only to get information that I need” (5 items in 2018, α = 0.820). In PISA 2000, another four items were added “I find it hard to
finish books”; “I feel happy if I receive a book as a present”; “I enjoy going to a bookstore or a library”; and “I cannot sit still and read for
more than a few minutes” (total 9 items in 2000, α = 0.873). Higher values on the index indicated greater enjoyment of reading.
Self-efficacy. PISA 2018 measured students’ general self-efficacy by asking to what extent they agreed with “I usually manage one
way or another”; “I feel proud that I have accomplished things”; “I feel that I can handle many things at a time”; “My belief in myself
gets me through hard times”; and “When I’m in difficult situation, I can usually find my way out of it” (5 items, α = 0.783). In 2000,
PISA measured students’ self-efficacy in reading by asking their level of agreement with “I am certain I can understand the most
difficult material presented in readings”; “I am confident I can do an excellent job on assignments and tests”; and “I am certain I can
master the skills being taught” (3 items, α = 0.686). Higher values on the index suggested stronger self-efficacy.
2.2.6. Contextual influences
Parents’ career in teaching. Students also reported their mothers’ and fathers’ careers with two open-ended questions: “What is your
mother/father’s main job?”. The answers were also coded into four-digital ISCO numbers, and we used the same method to classify
students’ parents’ careers into teaching and non-teaching.
Family support. PISA 2018 asked students to report whether they agreed with “My parents support my educational efforts and
achievements”; “My parents support me when I am facing difficulties at school”, and “My parents encourage me to be confident” (3
items, α = 0.891). Similarly, PISA 2000 derived the index from the frequencies with which students’ mothers, fathers, and siblings
assisted in their schoolwork. Higher values on the index referred to stronger family support.
Teacher support. Students answered how often the following things happened in their classrooms: “The teacher shows an interest in
every student’s learning”, “The teacher gives extra help when students need it”, “The teacher helps students with their learning”, and
“The teacher continues teaching until the students understand” (4 items in 2018, α = 0.891). In 2000, another two items were added:
“The teacher gives students an opportunity to express opinions” and “The teacher does a lot to help students” (total 6 items in 2000, α =
0.865). Higher values on the index demonstrated that students received greater teacher support.
Teacher enthusiasm. Particularly, PISA 2018 asked students how they agreed with “It was clear to me that the teachers like teaching
us”; “The enthusiasm of the teacher inspired me”; “It was clear that the teacher like to deal with the topic of the lesson”; and “The
teacher showed enjoyment in reading” (4 items, α = 0.878). Higher values on the index indicated that students perceived their
teacher to be more enthusiastic about teaching. The index was only available in 2018.
2.3. Data analyses
Multilevel logistic regression models were adopted to reveal factors affecting girls’ and boys’ teaching career expectations for each
economy, respectively. Missing values in the predictors were substituted with multiple imputations, and all available plausible values
were used to estimate students’ reading and MS achievement based on Rubin’s (2004) rules. To investigate Level 1 (student level)
effects and interactions, we employed the group-mean centering method (school-mean centering in this study) to partial out Level 2
effects (Enders & Tofighi, 2007). In addition, appropriate survey weights were incorporated at each level of analysis to ensure the
representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings to the 15-year-old population within each economy.

3. Results
3.1. Trends in adolescents’ career expectations
From 2000 to 2018, there was a decrease in the proportion of adolescents who expected to pursue teaching careers (Fig. 1). In 2000,
among the 43 participating economies in the PISA study, the average percentage was 4.8 % (SD = 2.9). The economies with the highest
proportions of adolescents expecting teaching careers were Albania (13.0 %), Indonesia (10.4 %), and Luxembourg (9.7 %). On the
other hand, Bulgaria (0.0 %), Hungary (0.9 %), and Greece (1.1 %) had the smallest proportions. In 2018, the average percentage
declined to 4.3 % (SD = 3.2) among the 78 participating economies. The economies with the highest proportions were BSJZ (China,
14.0 %), followed by Ireland (12.8 %), and Azerbaijan (11.5 %). In contrast, Thailand, Indonesia, and Taipei had the lowest proportions, all approaching 0.0 % (the number of students expecting to be primary, secondary, and special teachers in Taipei, Indonesia,
and Thailand were 2, 1, and 1, respectively).
Although girls dominantly expected to become teachers, such gender disparities had generally decreased in most examined
economies (Table 1). However, in certain economies, particularly in the developing world, the gender gaps had actually expanded.
This expansion was primarily attributed to a decline in the proportion of boys anticipating pursuing teaching careers. Notable examples of such trends could be observed in Indonesia (boys: 32.6 % in 2000 to 0.0 % in 2018), Thailand (20.8 % to 0.0 %), and Chile
(30.4 % to 16.7 %).
The subsequent analyses focused on 39 economies, comprising 28 advanced and 11 developing economies, as these were the ones
for which girls’ and boys’ data on teaching career expectations in PISA 2000 and 2018 were available.

3.2. Changing importance of personal inputs
Drawing upon SCCT, this study examined the evolving impact of personal inputs (ability, socioeconomic status, self-efficacy, and
enjoyment of reading) and environmental factors (parents’ careers in teaching, family and teacher support, and teacher enthusiasm) on
adolescents’ expectations for teaching careers from 2000 to 2018. While the specific influencing factors varied across different
economies, certain patterns emerged. In general, girls and students from various socioeconomic backgrounds, with either parent
working in the teaching profession, displaying a genuine interest in reading, or benefiting from strong environmental support were
more likely to anticipate teaching careers. Interestingly, in some economies, teaching professions tended to attract students with
slightly above-average academic abilities but lower general self-efficacy when it came to career expectations.

4. Discussion
Given the significance of teacher quality and the pressing need to address teaching workforce shortages, this study aimed to
enhance our understanding of the evolving dynamics of adolescents’ teaching career expectations and the factors influencing these
expectations across diverse educational contexts. Over the past two decades, the gaps between boys’ and girls’ preferences for teaching
careers have been narrowed in more advanced economies, with an increasing number of boys aspiring to become teachers. Surprisingly, in some developing economies, these gender discrepancies widened as fewer boys anticipated pursuing teaching careers. This
highlights the growing importance of engaging boys in the establishment of an inclusive and sufficient teaching workforce.
To achieve this goal, understanding factors affecting boys’ teaching career expectations is pivotal. Guided by SCCT, our study
examined the impact of personal factors and contextual influences on adolescents’ expectations for pursuing teaching careers. The
findings revealed that boys who exhibited better reading skills, enjoyed reading, had either parent in a teaching career, and received
greater environmental support were more likely to expect teaching careers. On the other hand, girls, regardless of their academic
achievement, socioeconomic backgrounds, or other examined factors, tended to express a natural inclination to become teachers. To
address the gender gap, guiding adolescents toward a fair assessment of their capabilities, interests, and goals is indispensable,
enabling them to develop a comprehensive understanding of themselves and the diverse career opportunities available to them.
Furthermore, our analysis revealed that in certain economies, particularly among boys, those with average reading scores (Chile
and Israel), average performance in math and science (Latvia and New Zealand), and average SES (Albania and Denmark) exhibited
higher expectations for teaching than the lower/higher ones. This observation suggests teaching may not be the preferred career path
for the brightest or highest SES students who lean toward prestigious professions (e.g., doctors) or digital/technological roles (e.g., IT
work). Instead, teaching remains an attractive profession among average-ability or middle-SES students. There is no one simple
formula to improve the popularity of teaching. To sustain and elevate the desirability of teaching, developed economies should portray
it as a human-centered and professional vocation, transcending its traditional perception as a childcare-oriented role. Simultaneously,
in developing economies, teaching should be recognised as a stable and financially secure profession to improve its attrativeness.
Above all, governments must provide competitive salary packages, conducive working environments, and a respectful societal attitude
to teaching careers.
4.1. Ability and social status
In exploring who aspires to become teachers, it is inevitable to consider the influence of gender, ability, and family SES. Notably,
the impact of reading scores on career aspirations seemed to diminish, allowing for a greater number of high-achieving students in
science to express their interest in pursuing teaching professions. This trend appeared particularly prominent in developing economies.
The willingness of more capable students to join the teaching workforce holds promise for the education sector. Conversely, in some
wealthier nations, high-achieving students in math and science are enticed by more trendy and financially rewarding professions,
leading to a scarcity of highly skilled science teachers. Ensuring a continuous supply of competent educators for future generations
relies heavily on attracting and retaining prospective capable teachers, thereby preventing any leakage from the pipeline.
Offering competitive salaries and benefits packages can be crucial for attracting and retaining high-quality teachers. However,
economic incentives alone are not enough to attract those high-performing students who may have other career options available (Han
& Borgonovi, 2020). Thus, raising public recognition of the teaching profession is essential. Educating the public about the importance
of teaching, highlighting the teachers’ significant impact on society, and promoting teaching as a valuable and esteemed profession can
attract individuals with high potential (Park & Byun, 2015). In addition, enhancing teachers’ working conditions by providing
appropriate resources, reducing administrative burdens, and ensuring manageable class sizes is also beneficial for retaining capable
professionals in the field (Han et al., 2018).
Furthermore, the study’s findings indicated a decrease in the impact of SES on students’ teaching career expectations, highlighting
progress toward more equitable educational systems. Nonetheless, in economies with higher teacher income and social status, such as
Hong Kong and Denmark, students from higher SES backgrounds continuously showed a greater likelihood of considering teaching as a
career. To foster inclusivity and equity, it is crucial to attract prospective teachers from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds. Many
educational systems have successfully achieved this by breaking down barriers and providing equal opportunities to adolescents from
different SES levels to enter the teaching profession. Ongoing efforts to promote diversity and equity in the teaching profession are

4.2. Enjoyment of reading and self-efficacy
The present study investigated the influence of enjoyment of reading and self-efficacy on adolescents’ expectations to teach.
Enjoyment is a crucial aspect of intrinsic value contributing to sustaining the willingness to continue and persist in the activity (Deci &
Ryan, 2013). This study demonstrated that students with high enjoyment of reading were more likely to pursue a career in teaching.
Fostering a positive reading experience may have long-term implications for the teaching profession. Educators can consider strategies
to promote reading enjoyment among students, such as creating engaging and diverse reading materials, incorporating interactive and
enjoyable reading activities, and providing support for struggling readers. These initiatives can help cultivate a love for reading, which
may increase the likelihood of students considering teaching as a viable and fulfilling career option.
Moreover, in light of SCCT, the study emphasises self-efficacy in shaping career expectations. Self-efficacy refers to individuals’
belief in their own abilities to perform a specific task or accomplish a particular goal successfully (Lent et al., 1994). In the context of
teaching, it is noteworthy that in some economies adolescents with lower self-efficacy tended to expect to become teachers, even after
considering their academic achievement. This finding raises the possibility of fallback career motives for choosing to teach. These
students, lacking the confidence to pursue more challenging and demanding careers, may perceive teaching as an attainable occupation. Counsellors should recognize this phenomenon and aim to provide comprehensive career guidance and support to help students explore a wider range of career options. By offering exposure to diverse career paths, mentoring programs, and information
about alternative professions, students can be encouraged to pursue careers that align with their passions and abilities, rather than
viewing teaching as a fallback option.
4.3. Family and teacher support
SCCT underscores the significance of environmental influences on individuals’ career-related interests and choices (Lent et al.,
2000). Review studies also demonstrate the necessity of incorporating a wider range of theoretical perspectives to comprehensively
understand the factors that drive individuals to pursue teaching as a profession (Fray & Gore, 2018). The findings of the present study
shed light on the influential role of family and teacher support in adolescents’ decisions to pursue teaching, particularly among boys.
The support and encouragement received from family members and teachers had a considerable impact on adolescents’ career expectations related to teaching. This highlights the importance of fostering a supportive environment that nurtures and encourages
individuals to consider teaching as a viable career option.
Interestingly, the study revealed that in 2018, teacher enthusiasm surpassed teacher support in shaping adolescents’ expectations
of pursuing a teaching career. This reaffirms that the enthusiasm demonstrated by teachers in their profession can serve as a powerful
motivator for students, inspiring them to consider teaching as a potential career path (Thomson & Palermo, 2014). Teachers who
exhibit passion, dedication, and enthusiasm for their work significantly influence their students’ career aspirations. Schools, therefore,
should focus not only on providing academic support but also on cultivating a positive and enthusiastic teaching culture that inspires
students to pursue teaching as a fulfilling and rewarding profession.
Incorporating an understanding of environmental factors, such as family and teacher support, and recognizing the significance of
teacher enthusiasm, provides valuable insights into the complex process of career decision-making in the teaching profession. By
integrating these factors into educational practices and policies, we can effectively inspire and support more individuals, particularly
boys, in pursuing a teaching career. To achieve this, it is essential to provide teachers with resources and opportunities for professional
development to enhance their enthusiasm and passion for teaching. Additionally, involving families in career guidance and counselling
programs establishes a robust support system for students considering teaching as a career path. Through these concerted efforts, we
can create an environment that encourages and nurtures prospective teachers, ultimately strengthening the teaching profession and
benefiting the education system as a whole.
4.4. Limitations
One limitation of this study was its reliance on data from the PISA 2000 and 2018 assessments, which predominantly focused on
reading. Consequently, this research did not examine science-related psychological constructs, such as the enjoyment of science and
science self-efficacy. This omission raised questions about whether students with a genuine passion for science and the confidence to
educate in science were adequately represented in the study’s findings. Additionally, the representative samples of students and the
hard-to-collect standardized tests across a large number of economies in PISA were precious. Nevertheless, the examined variables
were neither designed to test nor extend SCCT. Hence, longitudinal or multi-wave studies were needed, though might not be as largescale as PISA, to delineate the possible causal relations of adolescents’ career expectations.
5. Conclusion
The present study delved into the changing landscape of adolescents’ teaching career expectations and the factors that shaped them
using PISA data. Examinations of the two-decade trends did not show strong individual conducive factors; each economy has its unique
combinational pattern of pull and push factors. The results emphasised the need to address the teaching workforce shortages on
multiple fronts, including narrowing gender disparities, engaging boys in teaching careers, and promoting inclusivity in the teaching

Attracting and retaining high-quality prospective teachers, particularly those with strong academic aptitude in developing economies, may require competitive salaries, public recognition of the teaching profession, and improved working conditions. Additionally, attracting teachers from diverse SES backgrounds is critical to fostering a representative workforce that cultivates understanding
and cultural responsiveness in classrooms. The study highlights the significance of promoting reading enjoyment and self-efficacy
among students to stimulate interest in teaching and pursue professions that align with their passions and abilities. Environmental
factors such as family and teacher support, along with the enthusiasm demonstrated by teachers, also play crucial roles in shaping
students’ teaching career expectations, especially among boys.
By implementing these findings, we can pave the way for a more inclusive and diverse teaching workforce. This requires providing
teachers with resources and professional development opportunities, involving families in career guidance programs, and cultivating a
positive and enthusiastic teaching culture. Ultimately, these endeavours will contribute to the development of a highly skilled and
dedicated teaching workforce, ensuring the availability of competent educators to shape the minds of future generations and drive
positive change in our society.
