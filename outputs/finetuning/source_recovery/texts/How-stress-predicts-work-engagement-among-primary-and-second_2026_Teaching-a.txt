How stress predicts work engagement among primary and secondary school 
teachers: The mediating role of emotional labor and burnout, and the 
moderating role of school support
Yinghua Ye , Yang Jiang , Manliu Wang *
College of Education, Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, 310058, China

A B S T R A C T
Teachers’ stress and burnout in China is problematic. Hence, this questionnaire survey investigated stress and its 
underlying mechanisms among 5400 school teachers in China. Teachers’ perceived stress was high, with student 
academic stress and technological stress especially important. Teacher stress positively predicted emotional labor 
and burnout, and negatively predicted work engagement. Emotional labor and burnout mediated teacher stress 
as a predictor of work engagement, while school support had a weaker inverse moderating effect. Accordingly, 
sources of pressure should be clarified, teacher evaluation mechanisms established, and teachers’ emotional 
labor assessed. Emotional support services should be provided to build a supportive environment.
The importance of a highly qualified and specialized teaching force 
cannot be overstated. However, the status of teacher stress and burnout 
in China is problematic, which is an obstacle to high-quality develop­
ment of the teaching community. In 2022, a large-scale survey of stu­
dents, parents, and teachers in China indicated that the burden on 
teachers had tended to increase since implementation of the “double 
reduction”, primarily in terms of increased workload and pressure (Ning 
et al., 2022). The pressure on teachers is most severe in junior high 
schools in counties and towns (Zhou et al., 2023). Stress is an important 
factor that affects teachers’ well-being and adversely affects the quality 
of their teaching (Pag´an-Garbín et al., 2024). Job stress among primary 
and secondary school teachers is significantly positively correlated with 
burnout, negatively correlated with job satisfaction (Li et al., 2022), and 
significantly negatively predicts work engagements (Li et al. ,2018). 
Professional, emotional, and other social support for teachers not only 
have a direct impact on their work and health, but also buffer the 
negative impact of stressful events on their physical and mental 
well-being (Xie et al., 2021). In addition, as technology is being rapidly 
integrated into K-12 classrooms worldwide, with rapid advances such as 
artificial intelligence, technological stress is also becoming a greater 
concern for teachers, affecting teacher burnout and turnover intentions 
(Califf & Brooks, 2020).
As the study of teachers’ emotions has expanded since the mid- 
1990s, researchers have reached the consensus that teaching is an 
emotional endeavor and labor that requires intense interpersonal in­
teractions, strong emotional commitment, and thoughtful emotional 
management (Yin, 2015). Empirical studies have found that the effort 
teachers put into emotional labor affects their commitment and 
self-esteem, that the three emotional labor strategies—superficial, deep, 
and authentic—have a differential effect on teachers’ job satisfaction, 
and that to make teaching, learning, and personal well-being more 
effective, teachers should improve their ability to regulate their emo­
tions (Yin, 2016). While previous research has explored the negative 
impact of stress on teachers’ health and teaching outcomes, few studies 
have focused on whether the strength or direction of this negative 
impact relationship is moderated by environmental factors and the 
possible mediating role of emotional labor in this process. In terms of 
teaching outcome variables, more attention has been paid to job satis­
faction, and less variables such as work engagement, while technology 
stress, as a relatively new stressor, has been explored in relatively few 
studies to determine whether it is an important component of current 
teacher stress and affects teacher burnout and job satisfaction. Based on 
the stress-strain-outcome framework (SSO) and the theory of emotional 
labor of teachers, this study focuses on teacher stress as a predictor of 
work engagement, the mediating roles of emotional labor and burnout, 
and the moderating role of school support as an environmental factor.

1. Literature review
1.1. Teacher stress and its impact on teaching outcomes
Stress is the result of an individual’s cognitive appraisal of their 
inability to meet the demands of the environment (Pag´an-Garbín et al., 
2024). Teachers face multiple demands from students, parents, col­
leagues, and school administrators, all of which can lead to stress. Some 
studies have divided stress among primary and secondary school 
teachers into five dimensions: workload, student academics, social and 
school evaluations, professional development, and student problem 
behaviors (Li et al., 2011). High levels of job stress among teachers are a 
global problem (Redín & Erro-Garc´es, 2020). In 2016, the Tencent Ed­
ucation and Max Research Institute conducted a survey of 410,000 
Chinese teachers (ranging from kindergarteners to undergraduates). The 
results showed that 46 % and 38 % of teachers said they felt “stressed” 
and “very stressed,” respectively. The 2018 Teaching and Learning In­
ternational Survey (TALIS) showed that 48 % of teachers in the OECD 
said they experienced “quite a bit” or “a lot” of stress (OECD, 2023). A 
2024 study found that 71 % of teachers in the United States intended to 
leave the profession (Ghasemi, 2025). Research has found that European 
teachers have worse health status than workers in other professions in 
terms of levels of stress, anxiety, fatigue, and sleep problems, but are 
more satisfied with their job (Redín & Erro-Garc´es, 2020). Work pres­
sure on primary and secondary school teachers in countries such as 
Japan and Egypt is also excessive (Zhang et al., 2023).
Teacher stress is the result of the interaction of factors such as 
teacher personality, values, ability, and environment (Kyriacou, 2001). 
Stress is a key factor in teacher development and has an impact on 
teaching outcomes such as job satisfaction (Woods et al., 2023), career 
commitment (Buettner et al., 2016; Collie & Mansfield, 2022), turnover 
intentions (Klassen & Chiu, 2011), work engagement (Qi et al., 2016), 
and teacher health (Luo et al., 2023). In terms of teaching outcomes, 
work engagement is a key factor directly related to teaching quality, 
which is defined as a positive, fulfilling work-related state of mind that is 
characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption (Schaufeli et al., 
2006). Previous studies examined the relationship between stress and 
teacher work engagement. Teacher stress has been linked to decreased 
negative teaching practices (von der Embse et al., 2016; Woods et al., 
2023). High levels of work pressure lead to high turnover tendencies and 
physical and mental health problems among teachers, which are not 
conducive to their professional development and the improvement of 
teaching (Luo et al., 2023). Qi et al. (2016) found that obstructive job 
demands, such as emotional demands, student misbehavior, and 
entrance exam pressure, have a negative predictive effect on the work 
engagement of primary and secondary school teachers.
Additionally, with the increasing trend of digital transformation in 
education, technological stress has gradually become an important 
aspect of teacher stress (Califf & Brooks, 2020). Technostress primarily 
refers to the inability of individuals or organizations to use technology in 
a healthy way (Estrada-Mu˜noz et al., 2021), and the various negative 
effects of technology on personal attitudes, behaviors, or physiology 
(Rey-Merch´an & L´opez-Arquillos, 2022). Technological stressors 
include different dimensions, such as techno-complexity, tech­
no-insecurity, 
techno-uncertainty, 
techno-overload, 
and 
techno-invasion (Tarafdar et al., 2007). Technostress negatively affects 
teachers’ willingness to accept and integrate technology into their 
teaching (Joo et al., 2016). Technostress is positively correlated with 
burnout and turnover intention among K-12 teachers in the United 
States (Califf & Brooks, 2020). Compulsive use of ChatGPT increases 
technostress, while technostress decreases life satisfaction (Duong et al., 
2024). The negative impact of technostress on psychological outcomes 
exceeds its impact on behavioral outcomes (Nastjuk et al., 2024). 
Hypothesis 1.
Teachers’ stress negatively predicts teachers’ work 
engagement.
1.2. The mediating role of teacher burnout
The SSO framework proposed by Koeske and Koeske (1993) is a 
commonly used model in psychology, management, and other fields to 
explain the stress process. Stress refers to environmental stimuli 
(objective events) that are perceived and interpreted by the actor as 
troublesome and potentially disruptive. Strain refers to disruptive im­
pacts on actor concentration, physiology, and emotion (i.e., negative 
reactions tied to situational stimuli). Outcome refers to the lasting 
behavioral or psychological consequences of chronic stress and strain, 
but also includes general attitudinal tendencies and behavioral in­
tentions or decisions, such as job satisfaction and turnover intentions 
(Koeske & Koeske, 1993).
Burnout has been commonly investigated strain (Cheung & Cheung, 
2012). Burnout is defined as “a state of physical, emotional, and mental 
exhaustion that results from long-term involvement in work situations 
that are emotionally demanding” (Carroll et al., 2022). Previous studies 
have focused more on emotional exhaustion in job burnout; however, a 
meta-analysis showed that anxiety, depression, tension, emotional 
exhaustion, fatigue, and psychosomatic symptoms can all be used as 
indicators of strain (Podsakoff et al., 2007). Cheung & Tang (2010)
treated employees’ physical and mental health as strain and explored the 
relationship between work stressors, health, and job satisfaction.
Research has explored the relationship between teacher stress and 
burnout. A survey of 749 Australian teachers by Carroll et al. (2022)
showed that more than half of the sampled teachers reported high or 
very high stress, with new teachers, primary school teachers, and 
teachers working in remote rural areas experiencing the highest levels of 
stress and burnout. Perceived stress significantly and positively pre­
dicted various types of burnout. A study involving 102 teachers and 
1450 students found three latent profiles of stress and coping among 
middle school teachers, namely “high stress and high coping” (66 %), 
“high stress and low coping” (28 %), and “low stress and high coping” (6 
%). Teachers with “high stress/low coping” had higher burnout and 
lower self-efficacy, as well as higher rates of depression reported by 
students (Herman et al., 2020). A survey of hundreds of primary and 
secondary school teachers in China showed that work stressors posi­
tively predicted job burnout and that social support and psychological 
empowerment played a mediating role in this predictive relationship 
(Song et al., 2024). Many studies have demonstrated negative effects of 
teacher burnout on work engagement. Burnout threatens teachers’ work 
performance and attitudes toward career development (Cacciamani 
et al., 2022). A study of 639 rural primary and secondary school teachers 
in China showed that job burnout significantly negatively predicted 
support work engagement (Chen et al., 2022). Xing (2022) pointed out 
that educators should strive to reduce occupational burnout if they wish 
to increase work engagement. 
Hypothesis 2.
Teachers’ burnout plays a mediating role in the process 
by which stress negatively predicts work engagement.
1.3. The mediating role of teachers’ emotional labor
This study adds emotional labor as a mediating variable in the SSO 
framework to better explore how stress affects strain (burnout) and 
outcome (work engagement). Teachers’ emotional labor can explain 
teachers’ emotion regulation from a sociological perspective. Emotional 
labor is usually used to describe “individuals transforming their inner 
real emotional experiences into external emotional expressions that 
meet social expectations for the purpose of communication or work 
(Wang et al., 2024).” Emotional labor strategies primarily include sur­
face acting, deep acting, and the expression of naturally felt emotions 
(Wang et al., 2024).
Existing research findings have suggested stress is a significant factor 
influencing emotional labor. When facing stress, the three emotional 
labor strategies adopted by teachers often undergo varying degrees of

change. Teachers’ occupational stress often increases the use of surface 
acting and deep acting while decreasing the use of the expression of 
naturally felt emotions (Hu et al., 2023). Zhang et al. (2025) found that 
challenge-obstacle stress was positively correlated with surface acting 
but negatively correlated with the expression of naturally felt emotions. 
Research by Shi et al. (2024) indicated that teachers experiencing higher 
levels of interpersonal stress tend to engage in less deep acting.
Teachers’ emotional labor affects their work engagement. Deep 
performance or the expression of naturally felt emotions can bring about 
a sense of accomplishment and task efficiency, thereby increasing 
teachers’ work engagement (Huang & Yin, 2024). Li et al. (2024) found 
similar results, namely, that deep acting and the expression of naturally 
felt emotions were positively correlated with work engagement. Zeng 
et al. (2025) found that deep acting and the expression of naturally felt 
emotions significantly and positively predicted teachers’ work engage­
ment, whereas surface acting significantly and negatively predicted it.
Furthermore, emotional labor and stress exhibit a reciprocal rela­
tionship: stress influences emotional labor strategies (Shi et al., 2024; 
Zhang et al., 2025), while emotional labor itself influences stress levels. 
Regarding the impact of emotional labor on stress, surface acting tends 
to increase stress, while deep acting mitigates it (Choi et al., 2019). 
Teachers exhibit similar characteristics, with emotional labor often 
contributing to stress, especially when they suppress their authentic 
emotions (Cos¸kun et al., 2025). This study focused on how stress impacts 
emotional labor, exploring how teachers’ emotional labor patterns shift 
under stress and the subsequent effects on their work outcomes. 
Hypothesis 3.
Teachers’ emotional labor plays a mediating role in the 
process by which stress negatively predicts work engagement.
1.4. The chain mediating effect of emotional labor and burnout
Most burnout studies consider emotional demands as a prerequisite 
for burnout, and emotional labor is believed to make a unique contri­
bution to burnout (Li & Zhou, 2013). Mao and Mo (2014) explored the 
role of emotional labor in different dimensions and found that deep 
acting and the expression of naturally felt emotions were negatively 
correlated with work burnout, whereas surface acting was positively 
correlated with work burnout. Previous studies have discussed the 
mediating role of teachers’ emotional labor in the relationship between 
stress and work status. Surface and deep acting behaviors mediate the 
relationship between stress arising from job insecurity and emotional 
exhaustion, and both emotional labor strategies have positive and sig­
nificant effects on emotional exhaustion (Zhang et al., 2013). Authori­
tarianism (which can also be viewed as a form of pressure) has an overall 
negative indirect impact on employees’ intention to leave, mainly 
through surface behavior rather than deep behavior or the expression of 
naturally arising emotions (Huang & Yin, 2024). Teachers often resort to 
emotional labor in the face of stress, which involves managing and 
regulating emotional expressions through surface-acting or active 
deep-acting strategies (Xia et al., 2024). Such emotional labor may 
exacerbate burnout, negatively affecting work engagement. Based on 
related theoretical and empirical studies, we hypothesized that work 
stress indirectly influences teachers’ work engagement through 
emotional labor and burnout. 
Hypothesis 4.
There is a chain-mediated effect, in which emotional 
labor and burnout serve as intermediate factors between stress and 
teachers’ work engagement.
1.5. The moderating role of school support
Many studies have indicated that social support from family mem­
bers, colleagues, friends, schools, and others can alleviate the harm 
caused by stress (Gerrig & Zimbardo, 2018, p. 373). In the 
social-support-role model, social support not only has a direct impact on 
individual work and health, but can also buffer the negative impact of 
stressful events on an individual’s physical and mental condition (Xie 
et al., 2021). People with strong social support systems are physically 
and mentally healthier, and less likely to experience emotional 
exhaustion (Wang & Xu, 2004). The more social support primary and 
secondary school teachers have, the less job burnout and emotional 
exhaustion they experience (Xu et al., 2005), and the higher their sense 
of efficacy (Huang & Wang, 2005). This study focused on the impact of 
teachers’ perceptions of school support. The concept of school support 
originates from the concept of organizational support, which refers to 
teachers’ perceptions of the value of their own contributions and 
whether the school cares about their well-being (Cherubin, 2011). 
Studies have shown that school support plays a moderating role in the 
relationship between stress and teacher work performance. The more 
resources schools provide teachers to do their jobs, the less burnout they 
experience (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). High perceived organizational 
support can strengthen the negative impact of job-person fit on occu­
pational burnout and help teachers reduce the pressure caused by 
job-person mismatch (Sun & Zhou, 2020). Support from leaders and 
colleagues moderates the relationship between teachers’ work-family 
conflict and emotional exhaustion, mitigating the impact of 
work-family stress on emotional exhaustion (Muasya, 2024). Koeske and 
Koeske (1993) explored the moderating effect of social support in an 
SSO model. The results showed that social support moderated the rela­
tionship between teachers’ emotional exhaustion and turnover inten­
tion. Specifically, when social support was low, emotional exhaustion 
led to a greater tendency to leave. The school main organization in 
teachers’ work life, organizational support provided by schools can help 
teachers effectively cope with work pressure and alleviate their mental 
health and emotional problems. 
Hypothesis 5.
school support moderates teachers’ stress as a predictor 
of emotional labor, burnout, and work engagement.
The theoretical framework of this study is shown in Fig. 1, on which 
the research questions and hypotheses were based. According to the 
theoretical framework of the present study, this study mainly includes 
five research hypotheses: teachers’ stress negatively predicts teachers’ 
work engagement (H1); teachers’ burnout plays a mediating role in the 
process by which stress negatively predicts work engagement (H2); 
teachers’ emotional labor plays a mediating role in the process by which 
stress negatively predicts work engagement (H3); there is a chain- 
mediated effect, in which emotional labor and burnout serve as inter­
mediate factors between stress and teachers’ work engagement (H4); 
and school support moderates teachers’ stress as a predictor of 
emotional labor, burnout, and work engagement (H5).
2. Method
2.1. Participants
A stratified sample of 5737 primary and secondary school teachers in 
China was selected. Stratified sampling is a widely used technique. It 
divides the population into different strata (also known as sub- 
populations) based on the principle of minimizing intra-strata differ­
ences and maximizing inter-strata differences, and then independently 
draws certain sample units from each layer for investigation and infer­
ence (Liu & Wu, 2007). The selection of strata in this study is based on 
the teachers’ schools (e.g., school location and teaching period) and 
personal characteristics (e.g., gender and teaching years), and the di­
vision of strata is based on the criteria of Lin et al. (2021), that is, 
dividing the teaching period into elementary school, junior high school, 
and senior high school, and then a certain number of samples from 
different strata are taken for the survey. The data were excluded from 
teachers who answered the questions for less than 180 s (135) or more 
than 10,000 s (10), who came from vocational high schools (119), whose 
schools were “other” (8), who were retired (2), and who had a total 
sample size of fewer than 10 (63) in the province (district and city); 5400

2.2. Measures and reliability and validity
2.2.1. Primary and secondary school teachers’ stress questionnaire
Teachers’ stress was first measured using the Primary and Secondary 
School Teachers’ Stress Questionnaire developed by Li et al. (2011), 
which consists of five factors with a total of 22 measurement items. The 
five factors are stress related to each of workload, student academics, 
social and school evaluation, professional development, and student 
problem behavior. An example item is “I feel that the teaching tasks are 
heavy.”
The Teacher Technology Stress Questionnaire used in Califf and 
Brooks (2020)
has a five-factor structure. In this study, the 
highest-loading items for each factor (five in total) were selected to form 
a single Teacher Technology Stress factor. An example item is “I feel 
constant threat to my job security due to new technologies.” Inclusion of 
this factor resulted in a teacher stress questionnaire with six factors (27 
questions), each of which was rated using a five-point scale, ranging 
from 1 = “very inconsistent” to 5 = “very consistent”; the higher the 
score, the greater the perception of teacher stress.
2.2.2. Emotional labor scale
Emotional labor was assessed using the Teachers’ Emotions Scale 
(Yin, 2015), which consists of three factors (surface acting, deep acting, 
and the expression of naturally felt emotions) with a total of 13 items, 
including “The emotions I show students or their parents come natu­
rally.” Items are rated using a five-point scale, ranging from 1 =
“completely disagree” to 5 = “completely agree.”

(2020) was used. The scale has a single-factor structure and consists of

2.2.4. Work Engagement Scale
The short version of the Work Engagement Scale developed by 
Schaufeli et al. (2006) was used, which has a three-factor structure and 
consists of nine items. Two questions with relatively high loadings on 
each factor were retained for this study, leaving six items, including “I 
am enthusiastic about my job.” The items are rated on a seven-point 
scale, ranging from 1 = “never” to 7 = “every day.”
2.2.5. School support scale
The Organizational Support Scale used in Cherubin (2011) was used, 
revised to be suitable for use in a school context. The scale has a 
one-factor structure and consists of a total of eight items, all of which 
were used in this study. The items consist of positive descriptions, such 
as “The school takes pride in my accomplishments at work.” The items 
are rated on a seven-point scale, ranging from 1 = “completely disagree” 
to 7 = “completely agree.”

structural validity and internal consistency reliability.
2.3. Procedures
The study first used a stratified sampling method to select 5400 
primary and secondary schoolteachers in China. A confirmatory factor 
analysis was used to test the structural validity of each measure, and the 
internal consistency coefficients of each measure were reported. Sub­
sequently, the study was tested for common methodological bias using 
Harman’s one-way analysis of variance. A total of 10 factors were 
extracted without rotation; the first factor explained 30.10 % of the total 
variance, which is lower than the critical value of 40 %, suggesting that 
the study did not suffer from serious common methodological bias. The 
descriptive and correlational profiles of each study variable were further 
analyzed and the mediating role of emotional labor and burnout and the 
moderating role of school support explored. Finally, the main findings of 
the study were summarized. A research discussion is presented, 
including research implications based on the empirical findings. Statis­
tical software such as SPSS, AMOS, and MPLUS were used to analyze the 
data in this study. A structural equation model was first used to examine 
the relationship between teachers’ stress and work engagement, as 
mediated by teachers’ emotional labor and burnout, using the boot­
strapping method and 5000 bootstrapped samples. The moderating ef­
fect of school support on teacher stress in predicting emotional labor, 
burnout, and work engagement was tested using a simple slope analysis. 
Bootstrapping and simple slope methods are commonly used for medi­
ating and moderating effect analyses, respectively (Shoshani, 2025; Ren 
et al., 2025).
3. Results
3.1. Descriptive statistics and correlations
Table 3 presents the descriptive statistics and correlation results for 
the research variables. The descriptive results showed that stress levels, 
in decreasing order, were represented by student academic stress, social 
and school evaluation stress, workload stress, student misbehaviors 
stress, professional development stress, and technological stress. 
Emotional labor was greatest for the expression of naturally felt emo­
tions, followed by deep acting, then surface acting. The mean scores for 
teacher’s burnout, work engagement, and school support were 3.556 out 
of 5, 4.700 out of 7, and 4.185 out of 7, respectively. The correlation 
results revealed that all three emotional labor strategies were signifi­
cantly positively correlated with total stress, with surface acting 
exhibiting the highest correlation coefficient, and the expression of 
naturally felt emotions the lowest. Burnout was significantly positively 
correlated with total stress, whereas work engagement and school sup­
port were significantly negatively correlated with total stress.
Individual selections of teacher stress were evaluated. Excluding 
professional development stress and technological stress, the proportion 
of teachers who provided responses of “fairly consistent” and “very 
consistent” for the remaining four factors ranged from about 47.6 % to 
70.1 %.

higher scores for the expression of naturally felt emotions and work 
engagement. This is consistent with the related prior results.

but did not moderate stress as a predictor of work engagement (Int =

4. Discussion
This study investigated the prevalence of occupational stress among 
5400 primary and secondary school teachers across ten Chinese prov­
inces (autonomous regions and municipalities); analyzed its effects on 
emotional labor, burnout, and work engagement; and examined the 
moderating role of school support. The following research findings were 
obtained. First, primary and secondary school teachers are under sig­
nificant stress, with student-related stress being the primary source of 
stress, and technological stress emerging as a new important component 
of teacher stress. Second, teacher stress significantly positively predicts 
emotional labor and burnout, and significantly negatively predicts work 
engagement. Third, emotional labor and burnout mediate the relation­
ship between teacher stress and work engagement, while school support 
plays a partial “reverse” moderating role. Overall, Hypotheses 1 and 4 
were confirmed, whereas Hypotheses 2, 3, and 5 were partially 
confirmed.
4.1. Clarifying the current status and enriching the theoretical structure of 
teacher stress
The finding that students’ academic performance and behavior place 
significant pressure on teachers is consistent with many previous 
studies. A study of 3976 teachers in Shanghai reported similar results, 
with more than half of the teachers feeling stressed about student 
achievement, nearly 10 percentage points higher than the OECD average 
(Zhu, 2020). A study of primary schools in deprived areas found a sig­
nificant relationship between teachers’ perceived stress and students’ 
academic achievement and behavior (Elliott et al., 2024). The reason 
why students’ academic achievement is the main source of teachers’ 
pressure may be that some schools link quantitative indicators, such as 
students’ pass rates, rankings, and scores, with teachers’ performance 
(Zhang et al., 2009).
Technical stress is a stress dimension of interest in this study. As 
digital technology continues to be applied in the education sector, it is 
essential to pay attention to address the technical stress faced by 
teachers. Although the research findings indicate that, compared to 
other forms of stress, technology stress is currently perceived as rela­
tively mild by teachers, it has the highest correlation coefficient with 
teacher surface acting when compared to other stressors. This indicates 
that the stress caused by technology leads teachers to a greater use of 
surface acting in their work. Therefore, while technical stress does not

pose a threat to teachers’ work performance, it is necessary to consider 
the increasing application of digital technology in education and the 
corresponding increase in teachers’ use of digital technology. Zhao 
(2021) pointed out that the integration of digital technology into the 
teaching profession leads to the restructuring of work through stan­
dardization and digitalization, thereby increasing the burden on teach­
ers as they adapt to this transformation. Other empirical studies have 
shown similar results, with technology stress showing a significant 
relationship with the individual dimensions of perceived stress and 
emotional exhaustion (Pag´an-Garbín et al., 2024).
4.2. Teachers’ stress significantly predicts their emotional labor, burnout, 
and work engagement
The findings on the relationship between stress and teacher burnout 
and between stress and work engagement are consistent with previous 
research findings. There was a significant positive correlation between 
teachers’ stress levels and burnout. A previous questionnaire survey of 
1821 primary and secondary school teachers showed that work stress 
was a significant positive predictor of burnout (Zhang et al., 2014). 
Given high levels of work stress, the perception of disproportion be­
tween effort and reward leads to a diminishing sense of value and 
satisfaction in the teaching profession, which in turn induces burnout. 
The pressure caused by “rationalized” teacher evaluations ignores the 
subjectivity of the teacher, and it is easy for professional enthusiasm and 
interest to be weakened (Chen, 2009). Teachers’ stress significantly and 
negatively predicted work engagement. A possible reason for this 
negative predictive relationship is that the current school and social 
demands on teachers’ work are high, exceeding the resources and 
abilities that teachers themselves possess, which, in turn, weakens 
teachers’ willingness and behavior toward work engagement and hin­
ders their professional development. A study of new-generation sec­
ondary school teachers also found that job stress significantly and 
negatively predicted work engagement (Li et al., 2018). A further study 
of special education teachers found that role stress significantly and 
negatively predicted work engagement of special education teachers 
(Wang et al., 2023).
This study is partially consistent with previous studies, in terms of 
the relationship between teacher stress and emotional labor. First, the 
relationship between stress and surface acting is consistent with the 
results of other studies. Li et al. (2022) found that job stress was 
significantly and positively related to surface acting and burnout to 
varying degrees. The underlying reason for teachers’ increased use of 
emotional labor and engagement in surface acting under pressure may 
be that the pressure imposed by schools is often reflected in the 
authoritative control of organizational rules, such as the teacher eval­
uation system linked to students’ achievement, and teachers must adjust 
their surface state to comply with the organization’s emotional rules 
(Xin & Wei, 2022). Second, teacher stress significantly and positively 
predicted deep acting and the expression of naturally felt emotions, 
which is inconsistent with previous research findings. Most studies show 
that stress is negatively correlated with the expression of naturally felt 
emotions (Zhang et al., 2025), while the relationship between stress and 
teachers’ deep acting is relatively complex. There are three types of 
research results: no significant relationship between the two (Zhang 
et al., 2025), stress positively predicts deep acting (Zhang et al., 2013), 
and stress negatively predicts deep acting (Li et al., 2022). Stress posi­
tively predicted teachers’ expression of naturally felt emotions in this 
study, which might be due to the large sample size, making the corre­
lations between variables more sensitive. Additionally, the complex 
correlations between stress and deep acting may stem from the fact that 
most studies categorize emotional labor into two types: surface and deep 
role-playing. Deep role-playing encompasses a broad range of meanings; 
therefore, research findings may vary depending on the characteristics 
and nature of participants’ work. Furthermore, we must acknowledge 
the complex relationship between stress and emotional labor, particu­
larly in deep role-playing. According to Goh et al. (2022), the 
stress-buffering model is not applicable to Chinese samples. In the 
context of China’s collectivist school culture, professional teachers who 
are supported may experience an increase in their psychological burden 
because of concerns about disrupting team harmony or revealing per­
sonal shortcomings, which, in turn, reinforce burnout.

(burnout) mediates the relationship between stress and outcome (work 
engagement). More importantly, this study extends the SSO model by 
incorporating emotional labor as a mediating variable. The impact of

4.4. School support has a weak reverse adjustment effect
Teacher stress significantly and positively predicted surface acting, 
deep acting, and burnout across all school support levels. However, at 
low levels of school support, the paths of teacher stress as a predictor of 
surface acting and burnout were much less significant, whereas the 
paths of teacher stress as a predictor of deep acting were more signifi­
cant. This is inconsistent with the observations of Koeske and Koeske 
(1993), who posited that emotional exhaustion reduces its effect as a 
predictor of turnover intention at higher levels of social support. The 
“reverse” moderating effect can be explained as follows: first, the sample 
size of the current study was large, resulting high sensitivity, such that 
small effects can be determined as having significant p-values. Second, 
the school support in this study was primarily spiritual, involving 
attention, care, and recognition from schools. Although such support 
may enhance teachers’ emotional labor strategies and teaching out­
comes, alleviating the negative effects of stress on predicting emotional 
labor strategies and burnout is challenging. In some cases, spiritual 
support and recognition from the schools are likely additional sources of 
stress. When schools provide high levels of support (such as additional 
psychological counseling), teachers may feel guilty about not being able 
to repay the school’s support, and feelings of guilt are an important 
factor that trigger teacher burnout (Chang, 2009).
5. Suggestions
5.1. Clarify the sources of teachers’ pressure and establish reasonable 
teacher evaluation mechanisms
According to the results of this study, student academic stress and 
social and school evaluation stress were the most important sources of 
teacher stress. Because of the influence of the exam-oriented education 
environment and the need for students’ entrance examinations, stu­
dents’ academic performance, such as grades and rankings, is an 
important indicator for schools to evaluate the effectiveness of teachers’ 
work. It is also the focus of social and parental attention, which also 
creates a great deal of stress on teachers. Education departments could 
consider incorporating indicators of students’ overall development, such 
as ideology and morality, into the evaluation of teachers’ work, establish 
a diversified teacher evaluation system, and appropriately reduce the 
proportion of final grades that are determined by students’ test scores. 
At the same time, “value-added evaluation” can be considered for 
teachers, which focuses more on the overall progress of the class and 
students as a result of the teacher’s work, rather than on a single 
outcome of test scores. Additionally, in the context of educational 
digitization reformation, technological stress is an evolving and pro­
gressively important stressor. Schools should pay close attention to 
teachers’ attitudes and psychological reactions to technology, set up 
technology service departments, provide timely training and guidance 
for teachers who have difficulties using educational technology, and 
maintain and update technological equipment and software to alleviate 
the work stress of teachers caused by the use and development of 
technology.
5.2. Consider the status of teachers’ emotional labor and provide 
emotional support services
When teachers face pressure, they will more often use surface acting 
to cope with the work content, but excessive surface acting will lead to a 
decline in their enthusiasm for work, resulting in teacher burnout. 
Therefore, schools and relevant departments need to realize that 
teaching is a profession that requires considerable emotional labor and 
should provide opportunities and channels for teachers to express their 
expression of naturally felt emotions. First, schools should provide 
teachers with comprehensive mental-health services. Schools should 
invite certified mental health professionals to conduct 2–3 themed 
workshops per semester. The topics included stress management, 
emotion regulation, and mindfulness exercises. These sessions should 
include interactive elements such as role-playing, group discussions, and 
scenario exercises to enhance practical applications. Schools can also 
establish full-time or part-time counseling positions to provide teachers 
with regular counseling. Simultaneously, in line with digital trends, they 
could consider developing mental health apps exclusively for teachers, 
providing online counseling, stress relief courses, and other services. 
Second, teachers’ own initiatives should be developed by providing 
activities and opportunities for teachers to communicate with each 
other, encouraging experience sharing and emotional support among 
teachers, and letting them face emotional challenges at work together. 
Third, home-school cooperation should be strengthened to enhance 
understanding and trust between home and school, jointly support 
teachers’ work, and reduce the cost of teachers’ emotional labor through 
communication with parents and schools.
5.3. Increase the pertinence and strength of school support and build a 
comprehensive teacher development environment
The results of this study show that sometimes excessive moral sup­
port can act as a form of stress and play a “reverse” regulatory role; 
therefore, schools should fully consider the needs of teachers and their 
professional development when providing support. First, schools should 
provide teachers with the support they need, by understanding their true 
needs through regular research or symposiums, and establishing effec­
tive feedback mechanisms and channels. Second, material support and 
investment should be enhanced for teachers, to provide a material basis 
for their professional development and to motivate them to improve 
their work engagement. Third, building a good organizational culture 
enhances teachers’ sense of organizational identity. An excellent orga­
nizational culture can improve teachers’ identification with the school, 
and organizational identification can significantly predict teachers’ 
work commitment (Gao & Yang, 2022) and enhance their sense of 
belonging and happiness.
6. Limitations and future work
This study has some limitations and possibilities for further explo­
ration in terms of research content and methodology. In terms of 
research content, the moderating role of school support in the process of 
emotional labor predicting burnout and work engagement, and burnout 
predicting work engagement, has not been fully explored; attention

research methodology, although this study examined common meth­

longitudinal designs to explore the mechanisms by which teacher stress 
affects emotional labor, burnout, and work engagement; simulta­

equation modeling, and future research needs to be conducted to test the
