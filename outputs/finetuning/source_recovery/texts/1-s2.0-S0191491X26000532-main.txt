This study examines cognitive-activation teaching profiles among science teachers using TIMSS 2019 data from
14 countries, adopting a person-centred approach to instructional diversity. Through latent class analysis, four
distinct teacher profiles are identified, All-Around Teachers, Balanced-Interactive Teachers, Discussion-Oriented
Traditionalists, and Low-Engagement Experimentalists, each reflecting unique combinations of general and
inquiry-related cognitive-activation practices. These profiles vary across countries, highlighting the role of national contexts in shaping instruction. Multilevel analyses show context-dependent associations with science
achievement: Low-Engagement Experimentalists are often linked to lower outcomes, All-Around Teachers linked
to higher achievement in Middle-Eastern countries, and Discussion-Oriented Traditionalists showing advantages
in Anglo countries. While mapping TIMSS items onto teaching frameworks involves conceptual and methodological constraints, the findings offer insight into the diverse ways cognitive-activation strategies are enacted in
classrooms. The study contributes to discussions on teaching quality by offering context-sensitive implications for
teacher professional development and policy aimed at enhancing science education globally.

1. Introduction
Teacher instruction plays a crucial role in shaping students' learning
experiences and academic outcomes, particularly in science education,
where complex cognitive skills such as critical thinking, problemsolving, and hypothesis generation are required (Teig et al., 2022).
Research has consistently emphasized the importance of effective
teaching practices in fostering student engagement and achievement.
Given the unique cognitive demands of science learning, instruction
should extend beyond the delivery of isolated concepts to incorporate
inquiry-based strategies that encourage exploration, active knowledge
construction, and scientific reasoning (Pedaste et al., 2015). Among
these practices, cognitive-activation teaching strategies (CATS), characterized by tasks that challenge students to apply knowledge, reflect,
and reason, has gained growing attention as a promising approach to
deepen understanding and promote higher-order thinking (Klieme et al.,
2009). It is important to note that CATS is not an isolated instructional
approach but is embedded within the broader landscape of teaching
quality, often positioned alongside classroom management and supportive climate as one of the three core dimensions of instructional
quality (Klieme et al., 2009; Schlesinger & Jentsch, 2016b). Within

science education specifically, CATS encompasses both general strategies (e.g., encouraging students to justify their reasoning) and
inquiry-based strategies more directly tied to scientific practices (e.g.,
designing and interpreting experiments). Understanding how these two
sub-dimensions operate together in real classrooms is central to this
study. However, rather than attempting to capture inquiry-based
teaching as a holistic pedagogical model, this study focuses on
inquiry-related
cognitive-activation
practices.

teacher-reported frequencies of discrete instructional activities within
the TIMSS framework, these activities serve as empirical proxies for
cognitively activating instruction rather than a full operationalization of
inquiry-based pedagogy.

(ILSAs), such as the IEA Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) and the OECD Programme for International Student
Assessment (PISA), have played a prominent role in educational

2019; Teig et al., 2022). These assessments provide comprehensive data
on student achievement and educational contexts, offering valuable
insights into instructional effectiveness. In science education, researchers have increasingly relied on ILSA data to examine teacher

Traditional research on teacher effectiveness in ILSAs has predominantly employed variable-centred approaches, which focus on general
trends rather than individual differences in instructional practices.
These methods often rely on Likert-scale responses to quantify teaching
effectiveness, reducing diverse instructional styles to a single composite
score. Such an approach can obscure important distinctions between
teachers. For instance, two teachers with similar overall scores on a
teaching effectiveness scale may differ significantly in their specific
strengths—one may excel in classroom management while another is
more adept at fostering student engagement and cognitive stimulation.
By averaging across these dimensions, variable-centred analyses may
fail to capture meaningful variations in instructional quality.
To address these limitations, this study adopts a person-centred
approach, using latent class analysis (LCA) to identify distinct profiles
of science teachers based on their instructional practices. Unlike
variable-centred methods, LCA accounts for heterogeneity within the
teacher population, recognizing that teachers employ different combinations of instructional strategies rather than adhering to a single, uniform approach (Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018). While person-centred
approaches have been applied to teaching, these studies have varied
considerably in scope and focus. For example, Teig and Nilsen (2022)
used latent profile analysis to identify instructional quality profiles
across primary and secondary science classrooms, and Holmes and
Schumacker (2020) applied latent class analysis to teacher characteristics to investigate teaching effectiveness. However, studies using ILSA
data specifically to derive teacher typologies based on
cognitive-activation or inquiry-related instructional practices (and to
link these profiles systematically to student science achievement across
diverse national contexts) remain limited. In particular, prior work has
tended either to rely on variable-centred analyses of individual practices
or to examine profiles in single-country contexts, leaving comparative,
multi-country evidence relatively sparse.
Thus, this study aims to fill this gap by using TIMSS data to identify
distinct teacher profiles and explore how they relate to student science
outcomes. By adopting a person-centred analytical approach, this
research seeks to offer a more nuanced understanding of instructional
effectiveness and contribute to the ongoing discourse on how teaching
practices influence science learning across diverse educational systems.

literature review defines cognitive-activation teaching, explores its

and reviews empirical findings on its relationship with student science
achievement. The methodology describes the data sources, measures, and
analytical strategy used in the study. The results present the identified
teacher profiles and their relationship with student science outcomes.

2.1. Inquiry-based and general cognitive-activation teaching strategies
(CATS)
In science education, cognitive-activation teaching strategies (CATS)
include approaches that prompt students to engage in higher-order
thinking and active knowledge construction. One such approach is
inquiry-based teaching (IBT), a pedagogical model grounded in
constructivist learning theory (Jiang & McComas, 2015). IBT emphasizes student-centred instruction, where the teacher acts as a facilitator
to support students in engaging with core elements of scientific inquiry—such as formulating questions, generating hypotheses, designing
investigations, and interpreting results (Prince & Felder, 2006).
In this study, we examine teacher-reported frequencies of specific
instructional activities (e.g., asking students to explain their reasoning
or design experiments) as indicators of inquiry-related cognitive-activation practices within the TIMSS framework. We do not claim to capture inquiry-based teaching as a fully realized pedagogical orientation.
Rather, these discrete activities serve as observable proxies that partially
reflect the kinds of cognitively demanding, inquiry-aligned instruction
described in the literature. This distinction is important: our analyses
characterize patterns of instructional behaviour as reported by teachers,
not the broader constructivist or student-centred pedagogical stance
that defines IBT as a model.
While the terms “inquiry” and “inquiry-based teaching” are often
used interchangeably, it is essential to distinguish between them (Teig,
2022). Inquiry, as a process, refers to students' engagement in scientific
reasoning tasks, such as experimentation and explanation. In contrast,
inquiry-based teaching refers to the broader pedagogical framework in
which teachers intentionally create and support these inquiry opportunities, guided by student-centred and constructivist principles.
Previous research has shown that IBT fosters deeper learning and
long-term knowledge retention, particularly when it moves beyond
isolated tasks to create cohesive, exploratory learning environments (de
Jong et al., 2023). Constantinou et al. (2018) argue IBT supports the
development of advanced cognitive skills by helping students grapple
with complex concepts and apply their understanding in authentic
contexts. Given the variation in how IBT is defined and implemented
across studies, it is crucial to specify how the present study conceptualizes and operationalizes it.
Scientific inquiry processes are often structured pedagogically
through the inquiry cycle, which breaks down complex learning tasks
into manageable, interrelated phases.(Constantinou et al., 2018; van
Joolingen & Zacharia, 2009). Pedaste et al. (2015) conducted a rigorous
systematic review, identifying commonalities and differences in the
terminologies used to define inquiry phases. As shown in Fig. 1, their
analysis establishes a framework reflecting the most widely accepted
understandings of inquiry phrases in teaching. This framework captures
the key aspects of the approach and simplifies inquiry into five phases:
orientation, conceptualization, investigation, conclusion, and
discussion.
The orientation phase is the initial step where students are introduced to a theory or topic, either through teacher guidance, environmental cues, or independent exploration (Scanlon et al., 2011). This
phase is set to stimulate curiosity and set the stage for further investigation by identifying key concepts and leading to the formulation of a
problem statement (Jong, 2006).

Recognizing this complexity, the present study does not aim to

The conceptualization phase looks deeper into the problem identified during orientation. This phase contains two sub-phrases: questioning seeks to generate open-ended questions about the stated problem,
while hypothesis generation results in developing testable statements,
both drawing on existing knowledge(Mäeots et al., 2008).
In the investigation phase, students conduct experiments or explorations to answer the research questions or test the hypotheses generated in the previous phase (Kuhn, 2005, 2010). This phrase contains
three sub-phrases: exploration sub-phrase allows for systematic observations or investigations and does not require a hypothesis; experimentation involves a more strategic approach and the realization of such
approach towards the investigation of a specific hypothesis; and data
interpretation focuses on analyzing the collected data to draw results,
ultimately leading to an interpretation of the research question.(Scanlon
et al., 2011)
The discussion phase refers to the process of disseminating findings
in two sub-phases (communication and reflection). Communication involves students sharing their findings from previous phrases with
other students through documents, presentations, discussions, or other
forms of interactions and fostering feedback and new insights (Scanlon
et al., 2011). Meanwhile, reflection is an internal process where students
contemplate the inquiry cycle itself, including evaluating the design of
the study, proposing new problems, and identifying ways to improve
future investigations (White & Frederiksen, 1998).
In the conclusion phase, students revisit their initial hypotheses or
research questions and determine if the results provide substantial evidence to support them (Scanlon et al., 2011). This reflection can
generate a final conclusion and possibly draw to new insights.
As shown in Fig. 1, these phases are not necessarily sequential and
can occur iteratively. Importantly, while many of the classroom activities measured in our study map onto individual inquiry phases, we
recognize that IBT as a pedagogical approach is more than the sum of
these activities. It is defined by the teacher’s intent to cultivate a
student-centered, constructivist environment that supports inquiry
holistically.
However, CATS is not exclusive to science education; it is widely
applied across various disciplines (Schlesinger & Jentsch, 2016b; Teig
et al., 2019a). Although often embedded in scientific contexts, these
strategies are considered generalizable because they reflect broader
instructional principles applicable to different subject areas (Baumert
et al., 2010). Key features of general CATS include prompting students to
connect new content with prior knowledge, engage with complex and
non-routine problems, and participate in discussions that promote critical thinking (Klieme et al., 2009; Schlesinger & Jentsch, 2016a; Tomáš
& Seidel, 2013).
While research has examined how teachers implement both general
and inquiry-based CATS, a persistent challenge lies in their practical
overlap. The boundary between these two strands is often blurred in
classroom practice, as effective teaching frequently involves integrating
both types of strategies to stimulate student engagement and support

2.2. Inquiry-based and general cognitive-activation teaching strategies in
TIMSS
Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) is a
large-scale international assessment that compares students' math and
science skills in fourth and eighth grades (I. V. S. Mullis et al., 2009).
Since its launch in 1995 with 45 participating educational systems,
TIMSS has grown significantly, with 64 education systems involved in
the most recent cycle (2019). This section examines how CATS,
including both general and inquiry-oriented practices, are represented
in the TIMSS teacher questionnaires across recent assessment cycles.
Although the TIMSS context questionnaire framework does not offer
an operational definition of CATS, and does not explicitly conceptualize
inquiry-based teaching as a guiding instructional model, it consistently
includes items that reflect cognitively demanding and student-engaging
practices, including elements commonly associated with inquiry (Martin
et al., 2015; I. V. Mullis & Martin, 2017). Since the 2011 cycle, the
teacher questionnaire items capturing such practices have remained
largely stable, enabling longitudinal comparison across TIMSS rounds.
In more recent cycles, the student questionnaires have expanded to
include constructs such as teaching clarity and classroom climate, with
the goal of capturing a broader set of instructional features beyond those
commonly linked to CATS. In contrast, the teacher questionnaires provide two sets of items: one targeting general instructional activities, and
the other focusing on science-specific practices. Both sets include practices that can be interpreted as cognitively activating, though TIMSS
itself does not explicitly label them as CATS.
In this study, we apply a theoretically grounded framework (Fig. 1)
to organize selected TIMSS teacher questionnaire items into two categories: general CATS and inquiry-based CATS. This classification is
researcher-defined and guided by prior literature (e.g. Teig et al.,
2019b), not by TIMSS’s original design. General CATS refer to practices
that promote critical thinking and knowledge construction across subjects, such as linking new content to prior knowledge or encouraging
student discussion. Inquiry-based CATS, in contrast, are defined here as
instructional strategies that encourage students to engage in core elements of scientific inquiry, such as planning investigations, interpreting
data, and using evidence to draw conclusions, under the guidance of the
teacher.

Recently, a person-centred approach has gained popularity in social
sciences and is beginning to be applied in the field of educational
effectiveness, aiming to address some limitations of traditional methods
(Escribano Alisio, 2020; Holmes & Schumacker, 2020). Variable-centred
analyses often focus on isolated instructional practices, potentially
overlooking how different combinations of practices operate in
real-world settings. Person-centred approaches, on the other hand,
cluster individuals based on their overall patterns of responses, allowing
for richer insights while preserving much of the item-level information
(Laursen & Hoff, 2006). This methodological innovation has facilitated
the identification of new relationships between inquiry-based teaching
and science achievement, offering nuanced understandings that go
beyond linear associations.
In summary, the relationship between inquiry-based teaching and
science achievement is multifaceted and context-dependent. While
general inquiry practices often exhibit positive relationships with
achievement, science-specific inquiry practices tend to show more
negative or complex associations, such as curvilinear patterns (Teig
et al., 2018).The variation in findings underscores the importance of
considering the instructional context, the specific components of inquiry
being measured, and the analytical approaches employed. As research
continues to evolve, integrating person-centred approaches may help
address existing gaps and offer deeper insights into the nuanced interplay between teaching practices and student learning outcomes
(Carrasco et al., 2023; López-Hornickel & Sandoval-Hernández, 2023).

2.3. Inquiry-based teaching and its relationship with student science
outcomes
A substantial body of research has been conducted to explore the
relationship between inquiry-based teaching and science achievement,
employing various methodologies, including longitudinal studies,
experimental designs, and qualitative and quantitative analyses (Minner
et al., 2010; Querevalú-Pazos, 2023; Schroeder et al., 2007). However,
the findings have been inconsistent, largely due to variations in how
inquiry-based teaching is defined and measured across studies.
Several meta-analyses and systematic reviews have synthesized the
findings on inquiry-based teaching and its impact on science achievement (Furtak et al., 2012; Lazonder & Harmsen, 2016; Querevalú-Pazos,
2023). For instance, Minner et al. (2010) highlighted that guided inquiry
approaches were generally more effective than unguided or minimally
guided methods. de Jong et al. (2023) provided evidence that
inquiry-oriented instruction could improve science achievement,
particularly when combined with explicit instruction.
As mentioned before, large-scale assessments, such as TIMSS and
PISA offer a degree of consistency by providing standardized measures
for researchers using their data (Teig et al., 2022). These assessments
have been pivotal in examining the relationship between inquiry-based
instructional practices and student outcomes in science. However, even
within this framework, findings exhibit variability. For instance, AlSalouli et al. (2024) analyzed TIMSS 2019 grade 4 data from seven

3.1. Data
TIMSS (Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study) is a
global assessment conducted every four years since 1995 by the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement
(IEA) (Martin et al., 2020). It measures trends in mathematics and science achievement at the fourth and eighth-grade levels while gathering
data on educational contexts, including home and school environments,
curriculum, and teaching practices.
It uses a two-stage sampling design, where schools are randomly
selected first, followed by students within those schools. To improve
accuracy, TIMSS employs plausible values and multiple imputed scores
that account for uncertainty in student performance. This method allows
for more reliable achievement comparisons across countries and
educational settings.
For this research, both teacher and student data in grade eight are
used to address the research question. Given the complexity of the
teacher-classroom-student relationship—where a single classroom may
have multiple teachers and a single teacher may teach multiple classrooms—the data required careful filtering. Specifically, the sample size
of classrooms in each country was calculated, and cases where classrooms were associated with multiple teachers were removed. Only
countries retaining more than 90% of their classrooms after this filtering
process were included in the analysis. Additionally, student weights
were applied to ensure that the sample accurately represents the target
population.
As a result, this study focuses explicitly on three country groups that
met the criteria for sufficient data integrity. These include MiddleEastern countries (Jordan, Iran, Kuwait, Egypt, and Oman), Asian
countries (Hong Kong SAR, Chinese Taipei, Japan, Singapore, and
Malaysia), and Anglo countries (Ontario [Canada], England, New Zealand, and Gauteng [South Africa]). The selection ensures that the dataset
is robust and representative, allowing for meaningful insights into the
interactions between teachers, classrooms, and students across diverse
educational contexts.

3.2.3. Control variables
Socioeconomic status (SES) was controlled using the Home Educational Resources scale, based on the number of books at home, access to
study supports (internet and own room), and parental education. To
account for contextual effects, a classroom-level SES measure was
aggregated from individual SES scores and included in the analysis.
3.3. Analytical strategy
To address the first and second research questions, we employed a
three-step approach that integrates latent class analysis (LCA) with
subsequent analyses of class-related outcomes (Vermunt, 2010). In the
first step, LCA is used to estimate the latent classes based on response
patterns, identifying distinct teaching profiles. In the second step, the
class membership probabilities derived from the LCA are assigned to
individuals using modal assignment or proportional assignment techniques. In the final step, these class memberships are used to analyze the
relationships between the latent classes and external variables, such as
science achievement, while accounting for classification uncertainty.
This sequential approach ensures that the estimation of latent classes is
independent of the subsequent analyses, minimizing potential biases
and producing robust results. An alternative approach would have been
to model general and inquiry-oriented practices as latent factors using
confirmatory factor analysis prior to profile estimation. However, given
the conceptual overlap between these practices and the study’s aim of
capturing naturally occurring instructional patterns, we prioritised an
item-level person-centred approach. This allows the identification of
profiles that reflect combinations of practices without imposing a priori
dimensional constraints.
For the first research question, we conducted a structurally homogeneous multigroup LCA (Kankaraš & Vermunt, 2014) using Mplus 8.11
(Muthén & Muthén, 2017) to examine teaching practice profiles across
countries. This approach ensures consistent class patterns across nations
while allowing the proportion of individuals in each class to vary by
country. Additionally, this method supports meaningful country comparisons and aids in the interpretation of teaching profiles across diverse
educational contexts.
Models with 1–6 latent classes were tested, and the optimal model
was selected based on several criteria. First, we evaluated statistical fit
indices, such as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-size adjusted BIC (SABIC), as
shown in Table 2. Lower values of these indices indicate better model fit.
Second, we examined entropy values, which reflect the clarity and
precision of class membership assignments, with higher values indicating better separation between classes. Third, we considered the
substantive interpretability of the resulting profiles, ensuring that the
identified classes were meaningful and aligned with theoretical expectations and prior research.

classes by interpreting their profiles based on the item-response probabilities for each indicator. These probabilities represent the likelihood of

3.2. Measures
3.2.1. Outcome variable
This study utilizes science achievement as an outcome variable.
Science achievement in the TIMSS assessment for grade eight is
evaluated across four content areas: biology, chemistry, physics, and
Earth science. Achievement scores are estimated using item response
theory (IRT) scaling procedures, and five plausible values are provided
to account for the matrix-sampling booklet design. All five plausible
values are included in the analysis, and the results are combined using
Rubin’s rules (Cairns & Areepattamannil, 2019). Further details about
the construction and reliability of the TIMSS achievement scales can be
found in the TIMSS 2019 technical report (Martin et al., 2020).

subjects. These practices include items such as relating the lesson to
students' daily lives, linking new content to students’ prior knowledge,
and encouraging them to complete challenging exercises that go beyond

4. Results
4.1. Model selection
We tested latent class solutions from 1 to 6 classes using a structurally homogeneous multigroup LCA. Model fit indices (AIC, BIC, aBIC,
log-likelihood, and entropy) are presented in Table 2 and Fig. 2. Fit
indices declined steeply up to the four-class solution, after which improvements became marginal (L2 change < 3.33% from 4 to 5 classes,
and 1.16% from 5 to 6 classes). Entropy was highest for the two-class
(0.882) and four-class (0.881) solutions, indicating clear class separation for both. The four-class model was selected as it offered the best
balance of statistical fit, parsimony, and substantive interpretability.
Compared to the three-class solution, the four-class model provided
greater differentiation between levels of implementation of general
versus inquiry-based CATS (Items 1–7 versus Items 8–12), yielding more
theoretically meaningful profiles. A partially homogeneous model was
also estimated to assess robustness; comparative fit indices supported
the adequacy of the structurally homogeneous solution for crossnational comparison.

classifications for each teacher.

5. Discussion
The findings of this study contribute to the expanding body of
literature investigating teaching practices and their associations with
student achievement in science education. By employing latent class
analysis (LCA) to identify distinct teacher profiles, this study provides a
more nuanced understanding of the implementation of inquiry-based
teaching across diverse educational contexts. The distribution of these
profiles across countries highlights that instructional practices may
reflect not only individual teacher preferences but also by broader systemic, cultural, and policy-related factors. These findings are consistent
with prior research (e.g., AlSalouli et al., 2024; Perera et al., 2022; Teig
et al., 2018), underscoring the contextual variability in the effectiveness
of inquiry-based teaching. Specifically, the results reveal that teacher
profiles are characterized by varying levels of frequency in implementing both general inquiry-based teaching and science-specific inquiry-based teaching. These profiles, in turn, show different associations
with student science achievement across countries, suggesting that the
relationship between inquiry-based approaches and student achievement may be context-dependent.

5.1. Teacher profiles

The distribution of teaching profiles across Middle-Eastern, Asian,
and Anglo countries may reflect the interplay of cultural, systemic, and
educational factors, each region exhibiting unique trends that may be
related to its historical and policy-driven educational priorities. By
exploring why these patterns emerge, we can better understand the
systemic challenges and opportunities that relate to teaching practices
globally.

5.2. Country patterns

Our analysis identified four distinct latent classes of teachers, each
demonstrating unique engagement patterns with inquiry-based teaching
practices:
All-Around Teachers exhibit high engagement across all inquirybased practices, embodying the pedagogical ideals of constructivist
learning theory. This theory emphasizes inductive learning experiences,
where students actively construct knowledge through inquiry phases
such as orientation, conceptualization, investigation, discussion, and
conclusion (Pedaste et al., 2015). These teachers integrate elements of
both general and subject-specific inquiry-based teaching, potentially
fostering cognitive engagement and higher-order thinking skills, as
highlighted by Constantinou et al. (2018)
Balanced Interactive Teachers primarily focus on interactive
teaching practices, incorporating experimental methods at moderate
levels. Their engagement mirrors general inquiry-based teaching strategies, such as linking content to prior knowledge, encouraging critical
discussions, and fostering problem-solving (Pedaste et al., 2015). While
their cautious application of experimental methods suggests room for
growth, these teachers demonstrate versatility by blending interactive
approaches with inquiry-based elements.
Discussion-Oriented Traditionalists prioritize traditional and
interactive methods, excelling in fostering discussions and linking lessons to students’ daily lives while showing minimal engagement with
experimental or inquiry-based practices. This teaching style aligns with
studies suggesting that traditional methods prioritize content comprehension and interaction without incorporating the complex phases of
inquiry needed to foster advanced cognitive abilities (Furtak et al.,
2012). Their limited engagement with experimental methods reflects
challenges identified in the literature, where teachers often require
structured guidance and support to adopt scientific inquiry strategies
effectively (Schroeder et al., 2007).
Low-Engagement Experimentalists demonstrate minimal engagement across all teaching practices, reflecting limited implementation of
inquiry-based instruction. Their teaching style highlights the challenges
of adopting inquiry-based methods, especially in contexts where
teachers may lack the necessary resources or training.
Taken together, these profiles not only represent distinct patterns of
instructional practice but also appear to reflect a broader gradient in
teachers’ engagement with inquiry-based teaching. Specifically, the
profiles range from teachers who report frequent and varied use of
inquiry-oriented practices (All-Around Teachers), to those who combine
interactive strategies with more limited inquiry elements (Balanced
Interactive Teachers), to teachers whose practices remain primarily
discussion-based and traditional (Discussion-Oriented Traditionalists),
and finally to teachers who report relatively low engagement across
most instructional practices (Low-Engagement Experimentalists).
Similar gradients in teaching practice have been observed in previous
research (e.g. Teig & Nilsen, 2022), suggesting that differences in
instructional patterns may reflect broader variation in teachers’
instructional repertoires and their opportunities to implement
inquiry-oriented approaches.
These profiles reveal distinct strengths and gaps, underscoring the
need for tailored professional development. All-Around Teachers
demonstrate comparatively broader engagement with inquiry-based
instruction, while Balanced Interactive Teachers and DiscussionOriented Traditionalists could benefit from strategies to enhance

5.2.1. Middle-eastern countries
The Middle-Eastern group displayed a relatively strong representation of Balanced Interactive Teachers and teaching profiles, although
traditional approaches remain significant in some countries. This variation is likely rooted in ongoing educational reforms across the region
(Hijazi & Zoubeidi, 2017). Countries with higher adoption of All-Around
Teachers could have prioritized modernizing their education systems,
with initiatives emphasizing professional development, student
engagement, and inquiry-based methods. These reforms may be driven
by economic imperatives, such as aligning education with global standards to enhance workforce competitiveness (Kamel, 2014). Additionally, it might be that cultural norms that emphasize teacher authority
and respect could provide a conducive environment for implementing
structured inquiry-based teaching, where teachers guide students
through interactive and experimental activities.
On the other hand, countries where Discussion-Oriented Traditionalists dominate may face systemic barriers such as resource constraints,
higher student-teacher ratios, underdeveloped teacher training programs, or a curriculum heavily focused on content delivery (Kamel,
2014). In these contexts, traditional methods emphasizing discussions
and rote learning remain the norm, as they align with longstanding
teaching practices that prioritize exam preparation over inquiry-driven
exploration. The low prevalence of Low-Engagement Experimentalists
may suggest that most teachers in the region, even in
resource-constrained settings, strive to maintain at least some level of
student engagement, reflecting cultural values that prioritize active
teacher-student interactions.
5.2.2. Asian countries
Asian countries displayed a strong dominance of Low-Engagement
Experimentalists, indicating significant challenges in adopting inquirybased teaching methods. This trend may be associated with systemic
and cultural barriers (Pham & Pham, 2020). High-stakes examinations
are a defining feature of education in this region, particularly in places
like Hong Kong, Taiwan, and Japan. These exams drive a focus on rote
learning and teacher-centred instruction, leaving little room for the
integration of inquiry-based approaches. The rigid structure of curricula
in these systems often prioritizes coverage of extensive content over
deep, inquiry-driven exploration(Sasiwuttiwat & Tangkitvanich, 2019).
Teachers may feel pressured to adhere to traditional methods that are
seen as the most efficient way to prepare students for standardized tests.
Cultural factors could also play a critical role. In many Asian societies, education systems emphasize respect for authority and structured
learning environments, which might discourage the adoption of studentcentred, inquiry-based practices that require a more flexible classroom
dynamic (Zhao, 2015). However, countries like Malaysia, with higher
proportions of Balanced Interactive Teachers, have begun to implement
reforms aimed at fostering student engagement and critical thinking
(Shan et al., 2016). These efforts may reflect recognition by policymakers of the need to shift away from exam-driven systems toward more
holistic education models. Despite these efforts, progress is uneven, with

learning, reflective dialogue, and the development of analytical skills
(Howe & Abedin, 2013). However, the limited representation of
All-Around Teachers suggests that these systems might not fully
embraced comprehensive inquiry-based teaching, particularly in terms
of experimental methods. This may be due to systemic inertia, where
established teaching norms and assessment practices prioritize discussions and theoretical exploration over hands-on, experimental approaches (Oliver et al., 2021).
The presence of Balanced Interactive Teachers reflects growing efforts to integrate interactive and inquiry-based strategies into these
systems, driven by educational reforms aimed at improving student
engagement. However, barriers such as limited teacher training in
experimental methods, time constraints, and curriculum rigidity may
prevent the widespread adoption of comprehensive inquiry-based
practices. Additionally, resource disparities may limit teachers’ ability
to implement experimental methods that require specialized materials
or infrastructure (Lingard & Lewis, 2016; Oliver et al., 2021). The
presence of Low-Engagement Experimentalists in some countries indicates persistent challenges in providing adequate support for all
teachers, particularly in underfunded schools or systems with significant
teacher workload pressures.

5.3.2. Asian countries
Across the Asian education systems examined, no significant differences in student science achievement were observed between teacher
profiles. This suggests that the relationship between instructional approaches and student outcomes may be more complex or less pronounced in these contexts. One possible explanation lies in the region’s
strong emphasis on exam-oriented education, where structured,
content-focused teaching is often prioritized over exploratory, inquirybased approaches. In such systems, teachers who employ inquirydriven methods may face challenges aligning their practice with highstakes testing expectations, potentially limiting the impact of these
strategies on student achievement.
These findings align with previous research (Aditomo & Klieme,
2020; K. Lau & Lam, 2017), which found negative or inconsistent associations between inquiry-based science instruction and student performance in several Asian countries. These findings highlight the
importance of considering systemic and cultural contexts when evaluating the effectiveness of instructional practices, as approaches that
foster deep learning in one environment may not yield the same benefits
in another.
5.3.3. Anglo speaking countries
In Anglo countries, the effectiveness of Discussion-Oriented Traditionalists was particularly pronounced. In England and New Zealand,
students taught by this profile consistently achieved higher science
scores compared to those taught by Low-Engagement Experimentalists,
and in New Zealand, they also outperformed students taught by AllAround Teachers. These results highlight the compatibility of
discussion-focused teaching with the Anglo education systems’
emphasis on fostering critical thinking, argumentation, and analytical
skills. Discussion-Oriented Traditionalists may excel in contexts where
student engagement through dialogue and debate is prioritized, aligning
with broader cultural expectations for education.
While previous study (Oliver et al., 2021) using PISA 2018 data in
the UK and New Zealand found a negative association between
inquiry-based teaching and science achievement, this study shows that
certain

inquiry-based methods may hinder science learning, potentially due to
ineffective application or lack of necessary scaffolding. In contrast, the
strong performance of Discussion-Oriented Traditionalists in this study
indicates that structured, teacher-led discussions, when combined with
elements of inquiry, may enhance student outcomes. This suggests that
the effectiveness of inquiry-based teaching depends on how it is integrated into instruction, with teacher guidance and structured dialogue
playing a crucial role in improving science achievement.

5.3. Relationship between teacher profiles and student science
achievement
The analysis highlights significant differences in student science
achievement across teacher profiles in Middle-Eastern, Asian, and Anglo
countries, while also identifying contexts where these differences are not
significant. These variations reflect how systemic, cultural, and educational factors interact with teaching practices and may shape patterns in
student outcomes. Below, the significant results for each region are
discussed in detail, followed by an explanation of non-significant
findings.
5.3.1. Middle-eastern countries
In Middle-Eastern countries, the effectiveness of All-Around Teachers was particularly evident in Jordan and Saudi Arabia. In Jordan,
students taught by All-Around Teachers significantly outperformed
those taught by Low-Engagement Experimentalists, emphasizing the
importance of combining interactive and experimental methods to foster
deeper understanding and engagement. Similarly, Discussion-Oriented
Traditionalists in Jordan also achieved significantly better outcomes
than Low-Engagement Experimentalists, suggesting that even traditional methods emphasizing discussions are preferable to disengaged
teaching styles. These patterns reflect the region's increasing investment
in educational reform, particularly in countries like Jordan and Saudi
Arabia, which have made strides in professional development and curriculum modernization. In Saudi Arabia, All-Around Teachers were the
most effective across all comparisons, likely due to a systemic focus on
comprehensive teaching practices that balance content delivery with
interactive and inquiry-driven strategies.

5.4. Limitations

interpreting the findings. First, the mapping of TIMSS questionnaire
items onto general and inquiry-based CATS reflects a researcher-derived
framework and does not represent how the TIMSS instrument itself
defines or categorizes instructional models. Second, because the TIMSS
sampling design selects classrooms—not individual teachers—the findings may not fully capture the diversity of teaching practices within
schools or countries. Third, the use of cross-sectional data limits causal
inference and does not account for how teaching profiles may shift over
time or how they may relate to long-term student outcomes. Fourth,

For educators, the findings highlight the need for pedagogical flexibility when implementing inquiry-based instruction. Rather than
relying solely on either general or science-specific inquiry strategies,
teachers may need to adapt their practices to suit diverse classroom
contexts, curriculum goals, and systemic pressures. Professional development initiatives could emphasize this balance, supporting teachers in

Additionally, school leaders and policymakers may consider encouraging collaborative teaching cultures where educators can co-develop
and refine inquiry-oriented strategies suited to their local contexts.

person-centred approach can enhance our understanding of instructional diversity and its contextual manifestations. While interpretations
of inquiry-based teaching must remain cautious—particularly when

emphasizing the interplay between systemic conditions and classroom
practices, this research offers a foundation for further efforts to promote
effective and equitable science education.

6. Conclusion
This study employed multi-group latent class analysis (LCA) to
identify four distinct teacher profiles based on their reported use of
cognitive-activation teaching strategies (CATS) in science, and examined how these profiles are associated with student achievement across
14 countries. By testing both structurally and partially homogeneous
models, the analysis revealed that while the structurally homogeneous
model demonstrated better overall fit, the partially homogeneous model
offered valuable insights into cross-national variation in instructional
practices. A key contribution of this study lies in its person-centred
approach, which moves beyond traditional variable-centred models to
capture how combinations of general and inquiry-oriented teaching
strategies co-occur in real-world classrooms.
These findings underscore the role of systemic and cultural factors in
shaping both the distribution and potential effectiveness of teaching
profiles. For example, Middle Eastern countries, characterized by a high
proportion of All-Around Teachers, might benefit from more targeted
support for hands-on experimental teaching. In Asian contexts where
high-stakes testing dominates, reforms in assessment and increased
curricular flexibility may create space for inquiry-based practices.
