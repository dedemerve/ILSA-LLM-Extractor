The study aimed to determine factors affecting teachers’ instructional practices (teaching practices) based on the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018 Turkish data.
Despite the hierarchical data structure, the data did not fit the multi-level model, so it was
decided to use multiple regression analysis. The analysis results showed that teacher self-efficacy,
self-related efficacy in multicultural classrooms, teacher cooperation, diversity practices to the
third model, teachers’ perceived disciplinary climate, and professional development are needed
for teaching diversity, social utility value, student behavior stress, and participation among
stakeholders, school location, personal utility value, need professional, development in subject
matter and pedagogy, gender, and lack of resources explained 36 % variance in teacher practices.
Teacher self-efficacy was the most significant predictor, explaining 29 % of the variance in
teaching practices.

1. Introduction
TALIS 2018, implemented in 48 countries, is a large-scale and ongoing survey of teachers, school administrators, and their teaching
environments; the first was conducted in 2008, focusing on themes such as pedagogical practices and professional characteristics at the
institutional and individual levels. The last cycle, TALIS 2018, was administered online to teachers and school principals. It focuses on
the pedagogical and institutional conditions that support learning and reveals the differences between these conditions within and
between countries. TALIS 2018 includes themes of teachers’ instructional practices, school leadership, professional practices, teacher
education, initial preparation, teacher feedback and development, school climate, job satisfaction (including motivation), teacher
human resource measures and stakeholder relations, teacher self-efficacy, innovation, equity, and diversity. Innovation, equity, and
diversity are two new themes in the TALIS 2018 (OECD, 2019a).
TALIS 2018 defines teaching practices through three sub-dimensions: clarity of instruction, cognitive activation, and classroom
management (OECD, 2019a). Clarity of instruction focuses on how content is presented, cognitive activation assesses
knowledge-building processes, and classroom management ensures the conditions needed for both. Enhancing teaching effectiveness
requires understanding the factors influencing these practices, which involve not only theoretical knowledge but also practical skills,
professional identity, and shared values (Lampert, 2009). In the literature, there were various studies about teaching practices. Studies
conducted using TALIS data (Alanoğlu, 2022; Büyükgöze et al., 2022; Calkins et al., 2023; Cooc et al., 2023; Hoque et al., 2023; Lim,

The first theme, self-efficacy, a key element of social cognitive theory, refers to beliefs in one’s ability to execute actions to achieve
goals (Bandura, 1997). It interacts with personal factors, behavior, and environment (Dellinger et al., 2008) and includes classroom
management, instruction, and student engagement dimensions in TALIS 2018 data. Reeves and Hamilton (2022) found a positive
correlation between teacher cooperation, self-efficacy, and job satisfaction, but no link between collaboration and teacher practices.
Self-efficacy impacts professional development, mobility, and school climate (Schwarzenthal et al., 2023) and is enhanced by professional development (Ökten, 2023) and principal support (Alanoğlu, 2022).
Second, equity and diversity significantly influence teaching practices (OECD, 2012). Equity involves eliminating inequalities to
help each learner reach their potential, closely linked to education quality (Wahlström, 2022). Diversity reflects the dynamic interplay
of students’ and teachers’ socio-cultural and economic identities, such as gender, language, religion, race, immigration status, and
ability (Abdulrahim, 2024). TALIS 2018 evaluates these concepts through two sub-dimensions: diversity practices and self-efficacy in
multicultural classrooms (OECD, 2019a). Research shows that refugee students have fewer resources and less inclusion than
non-refugee peers, though teachers are often well-prepared for multicultural settings (Cooc & Kim, 2023). Teachers’ perception of class
heterogeneity influences assessment practices, with more frequent assessments in diverse classrooms (Lim, 2024). Additionally,
motivation to address inequality is shaped by socio-political factors and varies by country (Wiksten & Green, 2021). Finnish teachers
engaged in professional learning communities show stronger self-efficacy in multicultural education (Mo et al., 2021).
Third, professional practices, defined as teacher cooperation through exchange, coordination, and collaboration, play a significant
role in teaching (OECD, 2019a). Teacher professionalism involves agency—the ability to make choices, act on principles, and create
change—enhanced through social recognition and collaboration (Molla and Nolan, 2020). Methlalgl (2022) found that teachers who
collaborate less tend to use more explicit instruction and less cognitive activation. Feedback and classroom characteristics are modestly
linked to collaboration, with teachers in challenging classrooms relying more on cognitive activation but participating less in professional development. Schools that invest in feedback, facilitation, and collaborative frameworks foster a culture of learning through
collective action, critical analysis, and participation (De Jong et al., 2021).
School climate encompasses teachers’ perceptions of disciplinary climate, teacher-student relations, and stakeholder participation
(OECD, 2019a). It reflects the "quality and character of school life," including norms, values, interpersonal relationships, and organizational structures (Cohen et al., 2009, as cited in Grazia & Molinari, 2020). TALIS 2018 shows that disciplinary climate and
stakeholder engagement significantly impact job satisfaction and well-being, with teacher-student relationships having similar effects
across societies (Nalipay, 2023). Turkish data highlight that classroom management self-efficacy improves perceived disciplinary
climate, which, in turn, influences time spent on discipline. A negative climate requires more time for discipline, while a positive one
shifts focus toward teaching (Yüner & Pekmezci, 2021).
This theme focuses on meeting teachers’ needs through professional development. Feedback helps teachers identify areas for
improvement, with input from principals, administrators, and external sources positively impacting practices (Angeli, 2022; Reeves &
Hamilton, 2022). Feedback influences self-efficacy, job satisfaction, and school climate (Ainley & Carstens, 2018), with most teachers
reporting its benefits (OECD, 2010). Student feedback also enhances reflection, helps teachers meet individual needs, and fosters
dialogue between teaching and learning, ultimately improving teaching practices (Mandouit, 2018).
Teacher motivation and perceptions are explained with the sub-dimensions of personal utility motivation to teach, social utility
motivation to teach, and perceptions of value and policy influence (OECD, 2019a). In the literature, teacher motivation is explained as
the effort expended on teaching, influenced by various contextual factors and reasons based on the intrinsic values of individuals to
choose and pursue the teaching profession (Han &Yin, 2016). Motivations for becoming a teacher, primarily intrinsic and altruistic, are
positively associated with teacher self-efficacy in classroom management, instruction, student engagement, and working in multicultural classrooms. (Calkins et al., 2024). It is also associated with the experience. Early career teachers often face burnout, but
motivation stabilizes with experience. More experienced teachers tend to be more controlled in their teaching style and are less likely
to leave the profession toward the end of their careers (Hellebaut et al., 2023).
Work stress and well-being include workplace well-being, workload stress, and student behavior stress (OECD, 2019a). Workplace
well-being covers the impact of the job on mental, physical health, and personal life, while workload stress stems from excessive
preparation, teaching, marking, and administrative tasks. Student behavior stress involves student-related responsibilities. Well-being,
defined as life satisfaction and quality, consists of autonomy, purpose, relationships, self-acceptance, personal growth, and mastery
(Greenier et al., 2021). In contrast, unmanaged work stress can lead to burnout (Yu et al., 2015). School climate and stakeholder
engagement improve well-being, reducing turnover (Nalipay, 2023; Zhang et al., 2021). Therefore, well-being and stress indirectly
affect the teaching and learning process.
Job satisfaction refers to the positive emotional state that a person experiences due to satisfaction with the work he/she does. The
job satisfaction theme has three sub-dimensions: Job satisfaction with the work environment, job satisfaction with the profession, and
workload stress. It influences teacher-student relationships and enthusiasm (Madigan & Kim, 2021). Teachers with high job satisfaction, regardless of country, pursue better outcomes by focusing on student success (Hoque et al., 2023). TALIS 2018 data showed
that gender, social good motivation, self-efficacy, team innovation, teaching experience, teacher-student relationships, and professional development at the teacher and school levels increased teacher job satisfaction in South Korea and Japan, defined as East Asian
culture. At the same time, the school sector, lack of special needs personnel, school delinquency, and violence were the distinguishing

teachers’ job satisfaction include “conducive working conditions, promotion opportunities, fair remuneration, support from headteachers, colleagues, the community, teacher empowerment, and friendships” (Sahito & Vaisanen, 2020, p. 3). In addition, job

Team innovativeness concerns teachers being open to change, finding new solutions to problems as a team, and applying new ideas
in the TALIS 2018 data (OECD, 2019a). It is strongly linked to the ability of team members to share a common vision (Doronin et al.,
2020). Teachers’ perceptions of classroom autonomy and the collaborative culture in their schools were related to teachers’ perceptions of team innovativeness and the indirect effect of collaborative culture through integrated professional learning (Nguyen et al.,
2021). In addition to the direct effects of distributed leadership on teachers’ collective innovation, there are indirect effects through job
satisfaction and professional collaboration. It is also suggested that distributed leadership can increase innovation by affecting
teachers’ teaching practices and emotions (Büyükgöze et al., 2022).
Last, the school leadership theme covers subdimensions of school leadership and participation among stakeholders (OECD, 2019a).
Definitions vary by country and culture; the EU describes a school leader as someone managing a school with organizational, pedagogical, and educational duties (Pont, 2020). An analysis of school leadership across 42 education systems using TALIS 2018 data
found that leadership is shaped more by cultural and contextual values than by geographic or political factors (Veletić & Olsen, 2021).
Furthermore, the indirect impact of leadership on teaching practices through collective teacher efficacy is influenced by teachers’
uncertainty avoidance, highlighting how national cultural dimensions shape leadership effects (Özdemir et al., 2024).
Investigating the factors influencing teacher practices is essential for developing strategies to improve education. Analyzing
teaching practices, pedagogical approaches and school climate can enhance educational policies and teacher training programs. In
addition, these studies can help identify the challenges and support needs that teachers face and provide baseline data to make school
environments more inclusive and supportive. Consequently, a detailed examination of the different dimensions of teaching practices
can expand scientific knowledge in education by providing vital information to improve the overall quality of the education system and
allowing concrete steps to be taken towards the development of teaching practices. Teaching practices that affect learning, especially
in Türkiye, constitute the main subject of the study. In Türkiye, where education ranks low on the OECD’s Better Life Index (2024),
analyzing teaching practices is crucial for enhancing education quality. In addition, the factors affecting teaching practices have
generally been addressed in a more limited manner in studies, and considering the data holistically has been deemed important to
produce a summative study. For this reason, this study aims to determine how the predictors affect teaching practices based on TALIS
2018 Turkish data.

2.3. Data analyses
The framework of the TALIS data set was hierarchical at all national levels, as in Turkish data sets. Two questionnaires were carried
out for the participants: a teacher and a school principal questionnaire. Therefore, there are data on teacher and school levels. Both are
nested in schools. Multilevel modeling is suitable when the data are nested in a group (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). For this reason, the
hierarchical linear model was preferred in the study. Teacher-level variables are defined as level 1, and school-level variables are
defined as level 2. Two-level HLM was conducted separately for the Turkish data set.
2.4. Preliminary analysis of the data
Missing data were imputed before the HLM analysis using the predictive mean matching method. Assumptions were checked, and
finally, analysis was conducted using the “lm4” package in R. First, random effect analysis of the variance model (unconditional or
intercept-only model) was conducted using dependent and grouping variables to determine the model’s suitability. The results were
significant and supported the HLM. There are alternative ways to use multilevel modeling, such as ICC (Kleiman, 2017). The model’s
suitability was also evaluated by intraclass correlations (ICC). Stapleton (2013) defined ICC as “for a population as a ratio of the

4. Discussion
This study aimed to determine critical factors affecting teaching practices among Turkish teachers using TALIS 2018 data. Initially,
multilevel modeling was considered; however, low ICC values indicated that most of the variance occurred within groups rather than
between them. Therefore, multiple regression analysis examined the relationships between factors related to teacher characteristics,
school, and teaching practices.
The regression analysis identified teacher self-efficacy as the most significant predictor, accounting for 29 % of the variance in
teaching practices. This aligns with research showing that teachers who believe in their abilities are more likely to implement effective
practices (Bandura, 1997). High self-efficacy is crucial for successful educational reforms (Gordon et al., 2023) and positively influences behavior, classroom quality, personal success, and well-being, while reducing burnout (Zee et al., 2016). Gibbs (2003)
emphasized that teachers’ belief in managing challenges boosts self-efficacy and reduces stress, enhancing performance. Goal-oriented
teachers with high self-efficacy display greater commitment and motivation, further shaping their teaching practices. Therefore,
teaching practice can be explained mainly by the teachers’ self-efficacy beliefs.
Equity and diversity accounted for 3.5 % of the variance in teaching practices, making them the second most significant predictors.
Adapting teaching to cultural diversity and teachers’ belief in managing diverse classrooms positively influence practices. Consistent
with these findings, Björnsson (2020) found that teachers’ general self-efficacy strongly correlates with their self-efficacy in multicultural classrooms. Ryan et al. (2020) emphasized that teaching for diversity requires a systematic, collective effort to address inequalities. Additionally, teachers are better prepared to teach refugee students despite limited resources (Cooc and Kim, 2023), as their
commitment to managing diversity shapes their teaching methods and enhances practices.

The disciplinary climate in the school and the participation among stakeholders have significant effects on teaching practices. A
positive school climate, a safe school environment, and teachers’ ability to meet students’ well-being and academic, emotional, and
social needs were essential and firmly related (Kutsyuruba et al., 2015). In addition, analyzing the multidimensional structure of school
climate and interventions to reduce problem behaviors within this structure improved student and teacher well-being (Grazia &
Molinari, 2021). Classroom management self-efficacy influenced disciplinary climate, with negative climates increasing time spent on
discipline and positive ones allowing more time for teaching (Yüner & Pekmezci, 2021). Positive school climates foster cooperation,
respect, trust, and teachers’ confidence in influencing student learning (Thapa et al., 2013). Thus, improving climate and stakeholder
participation can enhance teaching practices and create supportive learning environments.
Feedback and development were other significant predictors of teaching practices. Teachers’ needs for professional development in
subject matter and pedagogy and for teaching for diversity affected teaching practices and explained 0,03 % of the variance.
Addressing these needs through targeted professional development can lead to gradual but valuable improvements in teaching
practices. Similar to these findings, Research by Reeves and Hamilton (2022), Angeli (2022), and Ainley and Carstens (2018) found
that feedback positively shapes teaching behaviors. Feedback from stakeholders helps teachers see their shortcomings and correct
them, which positively affects their teaching practices. Scheeler and her colleagues (2004) reviewed the literature and confirmed that
using specific, corrective or positive feedback is helpful as a supervisory practice. It reinforces effective teacher behavior and reduces
undesirable behaviors.

In terms of teacher motivation, only personal utility motivation to teach affected teaching practices. Like the findings of this study,
Pelletier and Rocchi (2016) have indicated that teacher motivation is essential in continuing their profession, achieving success, and
creating a healthy school environment. Teachers with high intrinsic motivation engaged their students by interacting more with them,
strengthening their motivation to be effective in the workplace and improving their interpersonal teaching style (Abós et al., 2018).
This may cause teachers to feel practical, more motivated, creative, and effective, positively affecting their teaching practices. Besides,
personal utility motivation as a reason for being a teacher changes from one culture to another. A study found that Chinese and Turkish
people did not give more importance to intrinsic value and ability motivations, while German and US people did. It seemed related to
China and Türkiye’s being a collectivist culture. Even Turkish teachers preferred being teachers because of job security (Watt &
Richardson, 2012). For this reason, teaching practice may be affected only by personal utility motivation based on Turkish data.
Work stress and well-being sub-dimensions, which include workplace well-being and stress and workload stress, were not significant predictors. However, student behavior stress was a significant predictor of the teaching practices. Since student behavior stress
is related to teachers’ responsibilities for student achievement, classroom management, and being intimated by students in the TALIS
2018 data, it partially matches with the sub-dimension of teaching practice. When students are not interested in learning and do not
attend classes eagerly, teachers feel that they try to teach in vain. It causes teacher stress and, in the end, burnout (Pines, 2002). A state
of well-being allows one to continue the teaching profession, thus providing more opportunities for professional development (Acton &
Glasgow, 2015). Friedman (1995) also found that student behavior stress had various effects on teachers working in different school
cultures, and teachers’ ideology affected their self-rated stress levels related to students’ behaviors. This means that personal and
cultural factors are significant in this issue. High-stress levels indicate that teachers are more likely to adopt test-oriented teaching
practices than the general curriculum (Putwain & Roberts, 2009, as cited in Ryan et al., 2019). This may indirectly have an impact on
teaching practices, affecting their quality.
Gender was a significant predictor of teaching practices, with female teachers scoring higher than males. Wiens et al. (2022) found
that gender influenced teaching practices and classroom management, with females spending more time on explicit instruction.
Similarly, Büyüköztürk et al. (2014) noted that female Turkish teachers favored constructivist methods and student-oriented activities
over direct instruction. In terms of classroom management, Oktan and Kıvanç Çağanağa (2015) revealed that gender was not a factor
shaping classroom management practices in Cyprus. Erden et al. (2016) approved this finding in their meta-analysis study conducted
in the Turkish context.
School location and lack of material resources were significant predictors of the study, while personal resources and teacherstudent ratio were not. Teachers working in rural areas had lower teaching practice scores than in towns and cities. However,
teachers in schools lacking material resources had higher scores. This may be explained by teachers having to use their teaching skills
more when they lack material resources. White and Reid (2008) asserted that teachers should be place-conscious to be effective. To
achieve that, they suggested that teacher training programs focused on the relationship between different places and social practices.
Job satisfaction was not a significant predictor of teaching practices. The literature results showed that job satisfaction influenced
school climate (Nalipay, 2023; Sahito & Vaisanen, 2020), student-teacher relations, and the enthusiasm reflected in the work (Madigan
& Kim, 2021; Niu et al., 2023). Job satisfaction mostly affected gender, motivation, self-efficacy, team innovation, and teaching

experience. At the same time, the school sector, lack of special needs personnel, school delinquency, and violence were the distinguishing factors at the school level regarding teacher job satisfaction (Niu et al., 2023). It may be said that job satisfaction is more
related to organizational issues and may affect teaching practices with different mediators. Similarly, team innovativeness was not a
significant predictor of the teaching practices. Nguyen et al. (2021) stated that teachers’ perceptions of classroom autonomy and their
schools’ collaborative culture were related to their perceptions of team innovativeness. As job satisfaction, it is an organizational
factor. Atasoy and Yalçın (2023)) found that team innovativeness supported teachers’ professional practices, and professional practices affected instructional practices. However, team innovativeness did not directly support instructional practices. As in job satisfaction, it requires a mediator. Although distributed leadership had a significant direct effect on teacher innovativeness (Hsieh et al.,
2024), school leadership was not a significant predictor of the teaching practices in the study. Bellibaş and his colleagues (2020)
researched the effect of two different school leadership types on the quality of instruction based on TALIS 2013 data. They found that
instructional leadership practices had a modest but direct impact on classroom practices and improved teachers’ instructional quality,
while distributed leadership in schools did not directly affect classroom practices. They used job satisfaction and collaboration as
variables with instructional quality. In sum, organizational factors do not directly affect teaching practices, and they have some
mediating roles.
5. Conclusion
The study investigated the factors affecting teaching practices using TALIS 2018 Turkish data. The study highlights the need to
prioritize teacher self-efficacy, promote collaboration, improve school climate, equity, and diversity, feedback and development,
manage work stress, and ensure the well-being and teacher motivation to strengthen teaching practices.
The findings show that self-efficacy is the most critical part of effective instruction. It also reveals that Turkish teachers emphasize
self-efficacy for effective teaching. Therefore, improving teacher self-efficacy may affect improving the quality of teaching and
learning. Improving teachers’ classroom management facilities and new instructional activities that motivate students and improve
student-teacher communication may affect teacher self-efficacy. For this reason, to increase pre-service teachers’ self-efficacy levels,
they can be exposed to different learning environments and experiences to see their potential. Determining which factors affect Turkish
people’s self-efficacy will be the subject of another study.
As the second predictor, equity, and diversity are becoming more prominent because of migration. Türkiye has students from
different cultures, such as Syria, Afghanistan, Iraq, etc. It leads teachers to put more effort into instructional practices to provide equity.
Apart from that, inclusive education is another dimension of the classroom. Teachers must adjust the teaching and learning context to
address all student profiles. For this reason, both teacher training programs and national curricula should be designed with equity and
diversity issues in mind.
The school climate and feedback were found to be associated with teaching practice. When the school climate is positive, it creates
an environment that fosters improvement. Feedback has the same effect. When feedback is given correctly, it enables teachers to
realize their needs. For this reason, need analysis is required before designing teacher training programs and professional activities in
schools. Moreover, a positive school climate fosters teacher cooperation and motivation. Professional learning communities among
teachers can be supported in schools to reinforce cooperation. It indirectly helps teachers manage stress and enhance their well-being.
All of them are predictors of teaching practices in the study. However, teachers attributed extrinsic values to teaching. In Türkiye,
being a teacher is regarded as a secure and steady job. To motivate teachers intrinsically, Türkiye should change its policies to increase
the value of being a teacher. Besides, teachers’ work stress was found to be related to student behaviors. Since teachers must manage
the classroom and teach the content in a limited time to provide students with success, they can feel a burden on them. It leads to stress
and burnout at the end. To deal with this issue, awareness should be raised that student success or failure is not only the teacher’s
responsibility. In addition, psychological guidance should be provided for teachers; different workshops can be held on stress management. Teachers can work together to solve classroom management problems or misbehavior among students. Policymakers can
arrange the class sizes in small numbers to decrease classroom management problems.
Although job satisfaction and school leadership were not significant predictors in the study, further research is needed to explore
the contextual factors affecting their influence. Limited teacher autonomy, centralized decision-making, and top-down curricula
hinder teachers’ influence over content and methods, impacting satisfaction. It may affect job satisfaction. Policymakers can encourage
teacher autonomy. They can be empowered by giving them space to make their own decisions, and school leaders can support teachers
for their improvement. In addition, the work environment can be arranged by considering teachers’ needs. In sum, it can be said that
teaching practices in Türkiye are affected by various dimensions, and to enable teachers to be effective in teaching, all dimensions
should be handled together.
This study attempted to provide a comprehensive perspective on teacher practices by including a wide range of variables related to
teaching practices, an essential strength of the study. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The analysis was based
solely on the Turkish dataset from the TALIS 2018 study, and due to differences in cultural, economic, and other contextual factors,
conducting similar analyses with data from other countries may yield different results. Therefore, conducting future studies with a
larger dataset and conducting comparative analyses considering contexts in different countries may increase the generalizability of the
findings. The data used in the study was gathered from one TALIS cycle and only includes the Turkish data set. Therefore, the results
should not be generalized to all TALIS participants. Conducting a longitudinal analysis utilizing TALIS datasets would enhance the
ability to make broader generalizations regarding teacher practices over time.
The study included the data from the primary schools (ISCED 1), lower secondary schools (ISCED 2), and upper secondary schools
(ISCED 3). Considering these three levels together allowed for the evaluation of teacher practices within a general framework;
9

International Journal of Educational Research 130 (2025) 102557

A. Altun et al.

however, since detailed comparisons between levels were not made, pedagogical approaches and practices specific to each level of
education could not be examined in depth. In future studies, it is recommended that comparative analyses be conducted between levels
of education and that the specific teaching practices of each level be revealed in more detail.
Thanks to its large-scale and survey-based structure, TALIS 2018 data is suitable for quantitatively analyzing teacher practices and
school environments in a general framework. Closed-ended questions and large sample groups allow comparative statistical analyses
across countries and education levels. Although this situation reveals general trends in teacher practices, it limits the in-depth examination of pedagogical processes, teacher experiences, and classroom practices. Therefore, supporting TALIS data with qualitative
research methods in future studies will contribute to revealing teachers’ pedagogical approaches and classroom experiences in more
detail and contextually.
