Abstract

This paper explores the state of teenage career development in Malta. It sets out findings
from the OECD Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), a comparative
international survey of young people in secondary education. PISA 2022 notably provides
considerable data on young people’s engagement in, and perceptions of, career
development, including information on occupational and educational intentions and
participation in career development activities. This paper compares student responses in
Malta to averages across the OECD, disaggregated by a range of student characteristics,
including gender, socio-economic background, academic proficiency and school type
attended. Students in Malta who express an occupational expectation are very strongly
focused in one field of employment: the professions. They also increasingly expect to
complete tertiary education. The occupational expectations of young people are poorly
aligned with actual patterns of labour market demand. Students participate in career
development activities and express attitudes about their career development in ways that
are largely comparable to OECD averages. However, teenagers in Malta are less likely to
engage directly with employers and people in work than their OECD peers, losing out on
important opportunities for career-related exploration. In comparison however to selected
countries, students in Malta are more likely to engage in core guidance activities than peers
in seven of nine jurisdictions.

Teenage career development in context

Concerns over the poor transitions of young people into the labour market are high in many
countries. In spite of young people leaving education more highly qualified and with more
years of schooling to their name than any previous generation, on average across the OECD
young adults are much more likely to be unemployed than people over the age of 25.
Societies turn to career guidance systems to help young people visualise and plan their
futures, enabling them to position themselves well to make appropriate investment
decisions in their education and training and to convert their accumulated human capital
into successful employment. Growing international longitudinal evidence demonstrates
strong relationships between forms of teenage career development (typically assessed at
age 15) and employment outcomes ten years later (OECD, 2021[1]), illustrating the
preventative role that effective guidance can play in reducing the risk of poor outcomes in
the early labour market. Analysis of longitudinal data provides a framework for assessing
the relative strengths and weaknesses of guidance systems.
PISA 2022 provides the most comprehensive available international dataset on the state of
student career development at ages 15-16. Students from all OECD countries and the great
majority of participating non-OECD countries responded to questions related to their
participation in career development activities, including activities routinely associated in
longitudinal data with better employment outcomes. Data are also available on teenage
career thinking (for example, in relation to clarity of career vision and the alignment of
occupational and education plans) and attitudes towards the effectiveness of schools in
enabling career development (OECD, 2024[2]; [3]). Breaking down data by demographic
subgroups provides considerable insight into the extent to which the career development of
students can be seen as equitable.

Methodology

PISA 2022 collected data from nearly 700 000 students in 81 OECD member and partner
economies. In Malta, data was collected from 3 127 students in 46 schools. Additionally,
principals from each school provided questionnaire responses to complement the data. To
minimise the time burden on students, not all questionnaire items were presented to every
participant. Specifically, for the section on career development activities, students were
typically asked about four or five of the eleven available questions.
Data from Malta are compared with OECD averages and disaggregated by the following
student characteristics:
•

All students: Includes the full sample of students.

•

Gender (Female vs. Male)

•

Economic Social and Cultural Status (Disadvantaged vs. Advantaged):
o Socio-economic status is measured using the PISA Index of Economic, Social,
and Cultural Status (ESCS).
o Advantaged: Students in the top quartile of ESCS within their country or
economy.
o Disadvantaged: Students in the bottom quartile of ESCS.

•

Performance Level (High vs. Low Performers):
o Performance is assessed using PISA scores in reading, mathematics, and
science.
o High Performers: Students achieving at least Level 4 proficiency in one core
subject while maintaining at least Level 2 proficiency in the other two.
o Low Performers: Students scoring below Level 2 proficiency in any subject.

•

Immigrant background (Native-Born vs. Foreign-Born):
o Native-Born: Students completing the PISA test in their country of birth.
o Foreign-Born: Students born outside the country where they completed the
test.

•

School Type (Public vs. Private): Results are categorised based on whether
students are enrolled in a state, church or independent school. Note: This
characteristic is specific to Malta and does not have corresponding OECD-wide
date for comparison.

Insights from the analysis

Career plans. How clear are the occupational expectations of students in Malta?
In PISA, students are asked ‘What kind of job do you expect to have when you are about
30 years old?’. Students are invited to write in responses which are then codified using the
2008 edition of International Standardised Classification of Occupations (ISCO). In PISA
2022, 30% of students can be classified as uncertain about their future career plans
(compared to 38% of students on average across the OECD) (Table 1) as they failed to
write in an answer, say they do not know or their response was too vague for classification.1
Teenage career uncertainty, defined as the inability or unwillingness to articulate an
occupational expectation at 15, is associated in many national longitudinal studies with
poorer employment outcomes than would otherwise be expected. As students approach
upper secondary education, the absence of career plans can undermine informed and
coherent investment in education and training provision (OECD, 2024[3]). In Malta, as
across many OECD countries, levels of teenage uncertainty have risen since 2015 when it
was 11% and 2018 when it was 16%. Career uncertainty is particularly high at 40% among
low performers on the PISA academic assessments (Table 2). Studies of OECD data show
that levels of uncertainty are commonly lower where students engage more strongly in
career development activities (Covacevich et al., 2021[4]; OECD, 2024[3]).

Occupational expectations. What are the jobs that students expect to have?
Where students do express an occupational expectation, their expectations tend to be
concentrated among a small number of possible jobs (Table 3). In PISA, students are asked
to write in the kind of job that they expect to have around the age of 30. Responses are then
coded using ISCO which is based around ten major groups that are in turn subdivided into
increasingly granular two-, three- and four-digit occupational codes. Coding is done on the
best match for the written in occupation. Coding at three digits provides consistency in
comparisons. In Malta, 58% of all students (Table 3) expect to work in one of the most ten
popular career choices among their peers, higher than the OECD average (46%). Broken
down by gender, levels of concentration are notably higher. In Malta, 69% of girls expect
to work in one of the ten most popular jobs among their female peers as do 57% of boys
(Table 4). Levels of concentration are particularly high among high performing students
(72%), the most socially advantaged students (66%) and students attending independent
schools (73%). Higher levels of concentration provide insight into the strength of labour
market signalling within a jurisdiction (Table 12). At the age of 15, when data are collected
by PISA, students have progressed significantly within their secondary education. In many
systems, this is around the age when students select specialist programmes of study related
in part of their career plans.

Labour market relevance. To what extent do career plans align with actual patterns
of labour market demand?
ISCO groups occupations into ten major categories. In most OECD countries, most students
expect to work in one field: the professions. Across the OECD, an average of 58% of
students expect to work as a doctor, engineer, teacher, lawyer, or other professional
occupation. In Malta, these occupational expectations are shared by 68% students
expressing a career plan. Combined with the 5% of students who plan on working as senior
managers, in all 73% of respondents in Malta expect to work in high status occupations
(Table 13). Such ambition is notable among girls (80%), the most socially advantaged
students (83%), high academic performers (88%) and students attending independent
schools (89%) (Table 16). Through PISA it is possible to explore the ways in which socioeconomic background shapes the career plans of students. In Malta, a 12-percentage point
gap is observable between the percentage of high performing students from the most
socially advantaged quartile (94%) and high performing peers from the most socially
disadvantaged quartile of respondents (82%). This compares to an OECD aspiration gap of
15 percentage points (Table 17). It is notable too that high proportions of low performing
students on the PISA assessments also expect to work in high status occupations as senior
managers or, most commonly, as professionals. Two-third of low performing students from
the most advantaged social backgrounds expect to work in such occupations, as do 44% of
their low performing, socially disadvantaged peers (Table 17). Such high levels of interest
in fields that commonly require tertiary qualifications for entry raise questions, discussed
below, about student understanding of the relationships between educational achievement
and access to employment opportunities. Due to the high level of interests in professional
and managerial careers, the proportion of students in Malta planning on working in
occupations at the highest risk of automation is lower than the OECD average (Table 30).
Figure 1 compares the occupational expectations stated by students in Malta against the
actual distribution of employment across the country (Table 13) and finds that the two
representations of labour market demand and potential supply have little in common. As
the table illustrates, whereas 68% of students expect to work as a professional, this
employment represents only 20% of jobs in the labour market. Across eight of the ten other
major groups within Malta, demand for employment outstrips potential supply as
articulated through student career plans. The table suggests weaknesses in the strength of
labour market signalling in Malta. ISCO major group 7 relates to the skilled trades. Here,
3.9% of students expect to work in such a profession, compared to an OECD average of
7.6% and actual labour market demand of 10% (Table 13). As across OECD countries,
interest in the skilled trades is heavily found among boys, socially disadvantaged students,
low academic performers, the foreign-born and students educated in the public sector
(Table 14).

With regard to four occupational areas of high strategic importance within Malta, in three
levels of general interest (as expressed by students in their stated occupational expectations)
increased between 2018 and 2022. Interest in careers in health rose from 12.6% to 13.7%,
in engineering from 5.5% to 6.6% and in IT from 6.2% to 7.9%. In the fourth field,
education, interest fell from 9.5% to 7.1% over the period, a phenomenon which also
occurred across OECD countries. However, within that data, it is possible to see movement
towards more balanced interests by gender with the interest of boys in teaching increasing
from 4.2% to 4.8% between 2018 and 2022. A more modest increase is visible too among
boys in careers in health. Equally, the period saw increasing proportions of girls expressing
interest in engineering (5.5% to 6.8%) and IT (2.7% to 3.2%) (Table 29).

Educational ambitions. What are the educational plans of students?
As well as expressing strong interest in high status occupations, PISA shows that students
in Malta also state strongly that they will complete tertiary education (defined as levels 5
to 8 in the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED): 72% express such
educational ambition, compared to an OECD average of 69% (Table 18). 2 Among
subgroups, more than 80% of socially advantaged, high performing and independentlyeducated students expect to complete tertiary education. Majorities of all subgroups
anticipate completing tertiary education, including 57% of low performing students on the
PISA academic assessments (Table 19). Looking at low performers by social background,
73% of the most socially advantaged students expect to complete tertiary education as do
44% of the most socially disadvantaged. Among high performers, a higher aspiration gap
is apparent than in the case of occupational expectations. Whereas 95% of socially
advantaged high performers expect to complete tertiary education, this applies to 78% of
their high performing, social disadvantaged peers (Table 20), an aspiration gap of 18
percentage points which compares to an OECD average aspiration gap of 21 percentage
points. In this context, it should be noted that longitudinal studies suggest that students with

Career alignment. How aligned are the educational and occupational plans of
students?
Where students expect to work in an occupation which is typically entered through tertiary
education (notably, senior management, professional and associate professional
occupations categorised within ISCO major groups 1, 2 and 3), but do not plan on
completing tertiary education, they can be classified as being misaligned in their future
planning. A range of longitudinal studies in different countries have followed aligned and
misaligned students into the labour market and routinely find misalignment to be associated
with worse employment outcomes than would be expected given student characteristics
(OECD, 2024[2]). In Malta, levels of misalignment are slightly lower than the OECD
average 20% (Table 21). In Malta, as across the OECD, misalignment in concentrated
among low performers and social disadvantaged students: around one in three of both
groups exhibit misalignment (Table 22). Data from across the OECD indicate that students
engaging more deeply in forms of career development are less likely to be misaligned
(Mann, Diaz and Zapata Posada, 2024[6]; OECD, 2024[2])

Career Development. How do students engage in career development activities?
PISA 2022 shows that students in Malta tend to engage in career development activities at
a comparable or higher level than is typical across OECD countries (Table 25). However,
in three important areas engagement levels are notably lower: internships, job
shadowing/work-site visits and organised tours of ISCED 3-7 institutions. In such
experiential development activities, students have considerable opportunity for learning
through first-hand engagements in workplaces and educational institutions. These activities
provide important opportunities for students to explore and investigate potential futures in
employment, potentially developing social contacts and skills of value within ultimate
transitions into work (OECD, 2021[7]). A similar fall in engagement levels is evident with
regard to participation in job fairs. In each of these forms of career development,
participation levels fell between the PISA studies of 2018 and 2022. While it is possible
that such activities which take students into direct contact with educational institutions,
workplaces and people in work may be have compromised by the COVID-19 pandemic,
on average across OECD countries participation levels remained at a similar level
(Table 25). Importantly, over this period Malta maintained a very high level of student
engagement with career advisors, one of the highest levels of engagement across all
participant jurisdictions in PISA 2022 (Table 25). It is also notable within the data that
boys are consistently more likely to engage in experiential career development activities,
while girls are more likely to engage in classroom or home-based activities (Figure 2).
This is a pattern that is also witnessed across OECD countries (Table 26).

With regard to the economic, social and cultural background of students (Figure 3), PISA
2022 shows that students from the most socially disadvantaged quartile of respondents
engage more or comparably than their most socially advantaged peers in most career
development activities with the major exceptions relating to post-secondary education
programmes (Table 26). More equitable guidance systems will ensure that students from
more disadvantaged backgrounds consistently engage more strongly in guidance through
their schools, responding to what is commonly more limited access to information,
resources and experiences through their family networks (OECD, 2024[8]; Payne and
Gollings, 2024[9]). Across OECD countries, it is students from more advantaged social
backgrounds who tend to engage more strongly in guidance activities. In Malta, with the
exception primarily of activities focused on progression to post-secondary education, it
tends to be more disadvantaged students who engage in greater proportions or engagement
rates are comparable.

Career development is a process of cumulative engagement in activities and experiences
which build over time to support student visualisation, planning and preparation for
ultimate transitions into work. PISA 2022 provides opportunity to compare the engagement
of students in important guidance activities related to career exploration (Table 32).
Figure 4 shows the proportion of students who report having taken part in two exploration
activities enabled by educational institutions (participation job shadowing/work-site visits
and job fairs) as well as speaking with a Careers Advisor in school, providing opportunity
to plan and reflect on such activities to optimise benefit. In Malta, 23% of students report
having engaged in all three activities, a higher percentage than seven other comparative
jurisdictions and the OECD average. Only Greece and Sweden report higher levels of
student engagement.

Career attitudes. What do students think about their career preparation?
In spite of their stronger than average engagement in Career Development Activities, many
young people in Malta express scepticism about the extent to which their schooling is
preparing them well for their lives in work. Belief in the extrinsic value of education, or
instrumental motivation, represents a further form of career development that has been
associated with better ultimate employment outcomes, indicating that students are
confidently engaging in education (Covacevich et al., 2021[4]). Compared to OECD
averages, students in Malta are 14.2 ppt more likely to agree that ‘school has done little to
prepare me for adult life when I leave school’, 7 ppt more likely to agree that ‘school has
been a waste of time’ and 6.7 ppt more likely to disagree that ‘school has taught me things
which will be useful in a job’ (Table 23). Such sceptical attitudes are most consistently
apparent among students from the most socially disadvantaged quartile and lower
performers (Table 24). Looking at other attitudes towards career development however,
students in Malta are as likely than their OECD peers to agree that ‘school has given me
confidence to make decisions’ and that they ‘feel well-prepared for my future path after the
final year of compulsory education’ (Table 28).
