A B S T R A C T  
This paper examines the role of after-class learning time in educational performance. In the Program for In­
ternational Student Assessment (PISA) 2015, Chinese students spent the longest time on study out of school 
compared with OECD countries but ranked only 10th in mathematics, reading and science. It is doubtful that the 
longer students study after school, the better their academic performance will be. Based on a representative 
survey of middle school students in China, we draw on generalized propensity score matching to explore the 
causal effect between after-class learning time and academic performance, find that the two have a relatively 
stable “inverted U” relation, and determine that the best academic performance can be achieved when after-class 
learning time is kept at 3 h per day on weekdays and 8 h per day on weekends. More than these optimal times 
will hinder academic performance. We further find that the effect of after-class study time on academic per­
formance varies among different groups. There is evidence that after-class learning time influences academic 
performance through two channels: students’ perceived learning difficulty and self-efficacy. This paper concludes 
that in addition to the appropriate homework assigned by teachers, parents should keep extracurricular tutoring 
for their children within a reasonable amount of time to maximize students’ academic performance.   
1. Introduction 
Since reform and opening up, China has seen a gradually widening 
gap between the rich and the poor, and the rate of return on higher 
education is gradually increasing. This makes parents more willing to 
invest all their resources into their children’s education, and the prev­
alent parenting style is changing from permissive to intensive (Doepke 
et al., 2019). Parents are increasingly enrolling their children in tutoring 
in a variety of subjects. In China, after-class tutoring is a popular method 
of extracurricular learning. According to the 2015 PISA, 74.0 % of 
Chinese students participated in math tutoring, 3.6 percentage points 
higher than the average of OECD countries (Chen and Zhi, 2018). On the 
other hand, China only has nine-year compulsory education at present; 
junior high school students must take examinations to progress to senior 
high schools. For junior high school students in some provinces, if their 
grades are not high, they will not be able to attend academic high 
schools and can only attend vocational high schools, with some even 
dropping out of school altogether (Shi et al., 2015). In China, this means 
that they will have difficulty obtaining a place at a university, and they 
will find it impossible to find a satisfactory job without a university 
diploma. That is, if junior high school students do not earn good grades, 
they are very likely to be discarded by society. In this relatively cruel 
education system, every student bears a very heavy academic burden 
and must complete long hours of study every day. It is safe to say that 
Chinese students are overrun from childhood with test papers, home­
work and cram classes. 
In 2015, the Chinese provinces of Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu and 
Guangzhou participated in the Program for International Student 
Assessment (PISA).1 As shown in Fig. 1, compared with OECD countries, 
Chinese students have the longest out-of-school study time, with an 
average of 33.1 h per week. For additional instruction time (related to 
academic instruction), Chinese students ranked fourth, with an average 
of 13.81 h per week. However, as shown in Fig. 2, the overall score of 
Chinese students on the 2015 PISA was ranked only 10th, behind those 
from the Hong Kong, Macao and Taiwan regions of China, who also 
participated in the test. In 2009, Shanghai represented China to partic­
ipate in PISA for the first time, ranking first in mathematics, reading and 
science. In 2012, Shanghai was also ranked first. However, when the

would happen if we measured study time and academic performance of 
junior high school students in all provinces and cities? 
This cannot help but raise the question whether long periods of study 
can truly produce good results. After-class learning is often cited as a 
potential predictor of academic success, generally including homework, 
cram school and tutoring. Studies have suggested that after-class 
learning is an important supplement to school academic activities 
(Cooper et al., 2006). Supplementary reading materials and selected 
teaching practices after school may boost mean achievement and help 
explain variation among students (Fuller et al., 1994). It seems that the 
more time they spend on learning after class, the better grades they earn 
(Plant et al., 2005). However, many students say homework after school 
is a major source of stress (Kouzma and Kennedy, 2002) and occupies 
what would otherwise be leisure time (Coutts, 2004). Flunger et al. 
(2015) proposed that a large amount of time spent on learning after class 
could reflect either a serious learning style (related to good grades) or an 
inefficient learning style (related to poor grades). Therefore, many 
scholars dispute the relation between after-class learning time and ac­
ademic achievement. Influenced by the traditional Chinese cultural 
belief that “practice makes perfect”, most students work very hard 
beginning in primary school. However, does studying longer after class 
improve their grades? If not, how long should they continue studying 
after class to earn the best grades? What is the mechanism? Our research 
focuses on these questions in the following section. 
This study obtains data from the 2014 China Education Panel Survey 
to examine the impact of after-class learning time on academic perfor­
mance. Specifically, we use the total time spent doing homework 
assigned by schoolteachers, doing homework assigned by cram school 
and parents, and taking cram school courses to measure total after-class 
learning and examine its influence on the total scores of middle school 
students. Moreover, we investigate the heterogeneous effects of after- 
class learning times among different groups. To avoid the endogeneity 
problem caused by ordinary least squares regression, this paper follows 
the design of Imbens (2000) and Hirano and Imbens (2005) and uses the 
generalized propensity score matching method to explore the causal 
relation between learning time and academic performance. 
The contributions of this paper mainly include the following points. 
First, the data of the China Education Panel Survey were sampled from 
2870 county-level administrative units in 31 provinces, autonomous 
regions or municipalities, making our research results more nationally 
representative. Second, generalized propensity score matching is used to 
verify the causal relation between continuous after-class learning time 
and academic performance, reducing the influence of bias and con­
founding variables. To our knowledge, no previous study has used this 
method to observe the causal effect of after-class learning time on stu­
dents’ performance per unit of time. Third, further applying the dose-

2. Literature review 
Previous studies on the relation between after-class learning time 
and academic performance have been inconsistent (Cooper et al., 2006). 
Some scholars believe that the longer students study after class, the 
better their grades will be. Zhu and Leung (2012), based on data from 
the Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS), 
find that time spent on math homework by Hong Kong students daily has 
a significant positive impact on their scores. Keith et al. (2004), using 
data from the National Education Longitudinal Study in the United 
States, also find that time spent finishing homework in school has a 
significant positive impact on students’ performance but that time spent 
finishing homework outside school has no significant impact. Masui 
et al. (2014) study the relation between learning time investment and 
academic performance of college students and find that for most courses, 
the effect of learning time on academic performance even exceeds that 
of the students’ personal characteristics. Andrietti and Velasco (2015) 
apply survey data from a public university in Spain and find that study 
time of college students has a significant impact on their academic 
performance and that it is more important than their attendance rate. 
Kitsantas and Zimmerman (2009) find that students’ homework indi­
rectly affects their academic performance through self-efficacy and re­
sponsibility beliefs as mediating variables. 
Other scholars believe there is a negative correlation between after- 
class learning time and grades. The main reason for this correlation is 
that large amounts of homework will reduce students’ learning moti­
vation (Trautwein et al., 2002) and increase learning anxiety (Warton, 
2001). Excessive tutoring will also have negative effects on students’ 
mental health and increase their anxiety. Specifically, Trautwein et al. 
(2002), based on the data of 125 seventh-grade classes in Germany, find 
that long amounts of time spent on homework have a negative effect on 
improvement of math scores. Another study of Kitsantas et al. (2011) 
based on PISA data also shows that increasing time spent on math 
homework does not lead to higher math scores. In a study of Dutch 
students, Westerhof et al. (2000) find a weak negative correlation be­
tween homework time and academic performance. They explain that the 
negative correlation is due to students being given the same amount of 
homework but students with better grades taking less time to complete 
it. 
Other researchers believe that there is a weak or unreliable relation 
between the weekly study time reported by college students and their 
grade point average (GPA) (Lahmers and Zulauf, 2000). Flunger et al. 
(2015) find little correlation between homework time and grades but 
that homework effort has a positive predictive effect on grades. The 
results also confirm that the length of homework time alone is not suf­
ficient to explain differences in students’ performance. Maltese et al. 
(2012) also find that there is no significant relation between homework 
time and academic performance by using data from two representative 
high school students’ samples. Plant et al. (2005) explain why learning 
time cannot predict grades, believing that students with higher grades 
are more likely to have higher levels of related learning skills and pro­
fessional knowledge acquired previously and can achieve better grades 
with less study time. That is, learning time is an important predictor of 
academic performance only when learning quality and previous 
achievement are taken into account. 
In addition, Beaton et al. (1996), based on a study of 13-year-old 
students in 41 countries, find that in most countries, there is a curvi­
linear correlation between students’ homework time and performance 
in science. When the homework time reaches a certain inflection point, 
students can achieve the best performance, but once they pass this in­
flection point, their academic performance will decline. Liu (2012) uses 
data from the Taiwan Education Panel Survey and finds that extracur­
ricular tutoring can improve academic performance but that this posi­
tive effect decreases with the extension of tutoring time. Many Chinese 
scholars have reached similar conclusions based on PISA data. For 
example, Shen (2014) finds that homework time has a significant posi­
tive impact on math, reading and science scores but that the positive 
correlation is weakened after homework time exceeds 11 h per week. He 
and Wen (2017) believe that homework time exceeding 24.78 h per 
week will reduce students’ math scores. Ma and Zheng (2017) find that 
there is a threshold effect between extracurricular learning time and 
academic performance. When extracurricular learning time reaches a 
certain critical point, academic performance can be improved qualita­
tively. Fang et al. (2018), based on data evaluating basic education from 
the National Collaboration and Innovation Center, find that students 
who complete their homework within one hour a day or less have higher 
scores. There is no significant positive effect of school tutoring time on 
academic performance, and maintaining 6−8 h of after-class tutoring 
time per week is most conducive to improving academic performance. 
There are four common limitations in the studies of the above 
scholars. First, most Chinese studies only examine the situation of stu­
dents in Shanghai based on 2012 PISA data (Shen, 2014; He and Wen, 
2017; Ma and Zheng, 2017), which are not representative of the whole 
country. Second, many scholars only verify the correlation between 
learning time and academic performance, but the potential endogeneity 
problems and reverse causality cannot be solved. Although Fang et al. 
(2018) apply the instrumental variable method to solve the potential 
endogeneity and reverse causality, the data they use only included 
samples from five provinces of China, which are also not representative. 
Third, most scholars limit their research to the direct influence of 
after-class learning time on academic performance (Zhu and Leung, 
2012; Keith et al., 2004; Masui et al., 2014; Andrietti and Velasco, 2015) 
and rarely discuss the indirect influence. Since after-class learning time 
may affect academic performance by affecting noncognitive abilities, it 
is very important to investigate possible mechanisms through which 
after-class learning time affects student outcomes. Fourth, even if some 
scholars find that too much time spent learning is not conducive to the 
development of academic performance (Westerhof et al., 2000; Traut­
wein et al., 2002; Kitsantas et al., 2011), there is no clear solution that 
tells students what they should do, such as how long they should keep

3. Empirical approach 
3.1. Data 
We obtain data from the China Education Panel Survey (CEPS), a 
large nationally representative survey with the 2013–2014 school year 
as the baseline and two cohorts of middle school students in the 7th and 
9th grades as the starting point, which randomly selected 28 counties 
and city districts, 112 schools and 438 classes throughout China. A total 
of 19,487 samples are collected from the baseline data, including stu­
dents’ gender, age, household registration, parents’ years of education, 
teachers’ work experience and other personal or school characteristics 
that can meet the empirical needs of our paper. 
Our academic performance scores are standardized scores in the core 
subjects—Chinese, mathematics, and English—provided by schools’ 
midterm exams. In China, all students in the same school and grade are 
assigned the same syllabi and midterm papers, making the sample re­
sults comparable. The standardized CEPS score is calculated according 
to school and grade, and all subjects are adjusted to a score with a mean 
value of 70 and a standard deviation of 10. Since the questionnaire does 
not specifically distinguish after-class study time on each core subject, in 
the following part we use the total of each core subject’s standardized 
score to measure academic performance. 
To measure after-class learning time, we use student responses to a 
time utilization questionnaire, including three aspects, “average time for 
doing homework assigned by schoolteachers per day”, “average time for 
doing homework assigned by cram school and parents per day”, and 
“average time for taking cram school courses per day (related to 
schoolwork)”. The questionnaire asks for both weekday and weekend 
times for all three aspects. The sum of these three aspects will be used to 
measure total after-class learning time per day. 
Student characteristics include gender (1 if a student is a boy and 
0 otherwise), household registration (1 if a student is registered as rural 
and 0 otherwise), grade (1 if a student is in 9th grade and 0 otherwise), 
siblings (1 if a student is the only child in the family and 0 otherwise), 
and cognitive ability scores. Family background characteristics are 
mainly measured by years of parents’ education and parents’ occupa­
tion. The reason family economic situation is not included is that there is 
no information related to family income or expenditure in CEPS. In 
addition, we measure the characteristics of the head teacher with the 
question “what is your professional title in teaching?” and measure class 
quality with the question “how is the academic performance of your 
class compared with other classes of the same grade?” on the head 
teachers’ questionnaire. Table 1 reports the summary statistics of the 
main variables.

4. Main results 
4.1. Effect of after-class learning time on academic performance 
Based on Eq. (1), Table 2 shows the OLS regression results of after- 
class learning time on academic achievement. Columns (1) and (3) are 
the results of bringing in the first-order term of after-class learning time, 
and the regression results of columns (2) and (4) bring in the second- 
order term of after-class learning time according to the past literature 
(Ma and Zheng, 2017; He and Wen, 2017). Table 2 shows that there is no 
significant effect of average after-class learning time every weekday on 
academic performance. When the second-order term of learning time is 
included, the coefficient of the first-order term is significantly positive, 
and the second-order term is significantly negative. In other words, the 
positive effect of average after-class learning time every weekday on 
academic performance first increases and then decreases. Although 
there is a significant positive effect of average after-class learning time 
every weekend on academic performance, it also shows a trend of first 
increasing and then decreasing when the second-order term of learning 
time is included. We preliminarily verify the nonlinear relation between 
after-class learning time and academic performance. He and Wen (2017) 
point out that there may be a threshold for students’ after-class learning 
time, and when the learning time is close to or equal to this threshold, 
students exhibit their best academic performance. However, beyond this 
threshold, after-class learning time does not improve students’ academic 
performance. Next, we will discuss this in more detail using the gener­
alized propensity score matching method. 
4.2. Causal effect of after-class learning time on academic performance 
Fig. 3 shows the causal effect of after-class learning time on academic 
performance obtained by generalized propensity score matching, 
namely, the dose-response function based on Eq. (7). Fig. 3 only captures 
the values of after-class learning time every weekday between [0, 8] 
hours and the values of after-class learning time every weekend between 
[0, 16] hours. Mainly referring to the Appendix, Fig. 1 shows that the 
dose-response function’s upper and lower bounds of the 95 % confi­
dence interval will be expanded when the average learning time on 
weekdays is more than 8 h per day and on weekends more than 16 h per 
day. It cannot be guaranteed that the effect of after-class learning time 
on academic performance is still statistically significant. 
According to Fig. 3, there is an “inverted U” relation between after- 
class learning time and academic performance. That is, the gradual 
increasing of after-class learning time will first promote academic per­
formance. However, reaching a certain inflection point will hinder its 
improvement. Specifically, keeping after-class learning time at 3 h per 
day on weekdays can lead to higher scores between 210 and 215 points. 
Similarly, keeping learning time at 8 h per day on weekends can lead to 
better grades. This is similar to the conclusion drawn by Beaton et al. 
(1996) in a comparative study of 41 countries, where the relationship 
between time spent doing homework and students’ average achieve­
ment was curvilinear in many countries, with the highest achievement 
being associated with a moderate amount of homework per day (up to 
one hour). Students doing up to one hour a day of homework had higher 
average achievement than students spending no time on homework. 
Students reporting more than one hour of homework had lower average 
achievement in most countries. Overall, longer after-class learning time 
is not better. For any student, there should be an optimal learning time, 
and there is no additional positive effect of prolonged learning time on 
academic performance. The reason may be that after-class learning time 
can be divided into elapsed time and time-on-task (He and Wen, 2017). 
Long learning time after class causes students to feel tired, which may 
seem to lengthen the time and create the illusion of serious learning, but 
in fact, it has the opposite effect, and students’ time on task is greatly 
reduced. To discuss the reason in more detail, we will investigate 
possible mechanisms through different learning times that affect stu­
dents’ academic performance differently in Section 4.4. 
4.3. Heterogeneous effects 
We have already verified the causal effect of after-class learning time 
on academic performance and obtained the optimal point of learning 
time per day. To test whether the effects vary according to students’ 
characteristics, we draw the dose-response function of after-class 
learning time on academic performance from four dimensions 
including gender, grade, household registration and only child status. 
Here, we only include the effect of average after-class learning time 
every weekday on academic performance. As shown in Fig. 4, the shapes 
of dose-response curves are very similar to those shown in Fig. 3, indi­
cating that the “inverted U” relation is quite stable. Second, for both 
boys and girls, the optimal learning time point is 3 h. Girls’ scores at the 
inflection point reach approximately 219 points and boys’ scores close 
to 210 points. With other learning times, although boys’ academic 
performance is generally lower than that of girls, maintaining the 
optimal learning time brings more benefits to boys. Specifically, keeping 
after-class learning time at the optimal point (3 h) compared with not 
learning (0 h) improves boys’ scores by more than 10 points and girls’ 
scores by less than 10 points. Additionally, keeping after-class learning 
time at the optimal point (3 h) compared with excessively long learning 
(8 h) results in a similar decrease in the scores of both boys and girls. 
Husain and Millimet (2009) stated that the popular press, educators, and 
policymakers have put forth the idea that developed countries are in the 
midst of a ‘boy crisis’, where boys are losing ground to girls across all 
stages of education, for example, with average test score gaps in reading 
and writing, prevalence of learning disabilities, grade repetition, and 
college enrollment rates. The optimal learning time simulated in our 
article implies that the gender gap in academic performance will be 
narrowed when boys’ after-class learning time is maintained at an 
optimal point. This important finding can also be used to provide solu­
tions to ‘boy crisis’. 
From the point of view of different grades, students in 7th grade can 
achieve the highest scores if they keep their after-class learning time at 
2 h, while 9th graders can earn the highest scores if they keep it at 
approximately 5 h. For students in 9th grade, keeping after-class 
learning time at the optimal point (5 h), compared with excessively 
long learning (8 h), entails a small increase in the scores. This is mainly 
because Chinese students in 9th grade face the high school entrance 
examination, and they need to allocate much time to study to fully 
prepare for the entrance examination (He and Wen, 2017). In other 
words, students facing the pressure to graduate middle school have to 
study longer to achieve high scores. 
Regarding household registrations and only child status, the optimal 
learning times for rural students and those with siblings are higher than 
those for urban students and those without siblings. For the former 
students, studying for 4 h after class will produce the same academic 
performance as 3 h for the latter. That is, students having disadvantaged 
backgrounds or sharing educational resources with siblings need to 
study longer than other students to obtain high scores. The main reason 
is that students with higher socioeconomic status may have more 
educational resources. Their parents can not only spend more money 
buying books or educational services but also invest more time to 
accompany and guide their children’s study to improve their academic 
performance (Fang et al., 2018). This is consistent with the premise that 
disadvantaged students receive less family education investment, so 
after-class learning time plays a more important role in disadvantaged 
students’ academic development. Specifically, for disadvantaged stu­
dents, our results suggest that they can make up for this academic gap 
due to differences in family background by appropriately extending

4.4. Mechanism of after-class learning time affecting academic 
performance 
Our main findings in Table 1 and Fig. 3 suggest that appropriate 
after-class learning time can improve academic achievement, while 
excessive learning time will hinder it. In this section, we investigate 
possible mechanisms of the effects of different learning times on student 
outcomes. Based on a series of studies on the mechanisms of homework 
quantity affecting academic performance (Kitsantas and Zimmerman, 
2009; Kitsantas et al., 2011), we speculate that continuous after-class 
learning time will change students’ perceived learning difficulty and 
self-efficacy, thus indirectly affecting their academic performance. With 
the appropriate amount of time, the extension of after-class learning 
time gives students more control over their academic performance so 
that they can perceive overcoming academic difficulty and have strong 
academic confidence (Mischo and Hagg, 2002). However, after reaching 
the inflection point, too much learning time may cause students to have 
negative emotions, such as anxiety (Trautwein et al., 2002; Warton, 
2001), doubts about their own ability, doubts that they can overcome 
their academic difficulties, and easy loss of confidence in their future 
academic development. As the research of Flunger et al. (2015) shows, a 
large amount of time spent on learning after class reflects a serious 
learning style to a certain extent, which will help students improve their 
performance. However, exceeding a certain amount reflects an ineffi­
cient learning style that leads to poor academic performance. In the 
following analysis, our rich data allow us to directly test these two 
mediating variables. On questionnaires, students were asked to describe 
whether core subjects were difficult for them at present2, with learning 
difficulty measured on a scale from 1 to 4 (1=Not difficult at all; 2=Not 
very difficult; 3 = A bit difficult; 4=Very difficult). Additionally, we 
measure self-efficacy with the question “Are you confident in your 
future?” (1=Not confident at all; 2=Not so confident; 3=Somewhat 
confident; 4=Very confident). Table 3 only presents the mechanism for 
weekdays, and the mechanism for weekends is shown in Table A1 in 
Appendix A. 
Regarding the mediating variable learning difficulty, for example, 
Table 3 reports that with the appropriate amount of time, the total effect 
of after-class learning time on academic performance is positive (the 
regression coefficient is 2.254, significant at the 1% level). Additionally, 
the appropriate learning time significantly decreases learning difficulty 
(the coefficient is 0.057, significant at the 1% level), through reducing 
learning difficulty to promote academic performance (the coefficient is 
2.024, significant at the 1 % level). The mediating effect is 0.230, ac­
counting for 10.21 % of the total effect. In excessive amounts, the total 
effect of after-class learning time on academic performance is negative 
(the regression coefficient is 0.418, significant at the 1 % level). 
Furthermore, excessive learning time significantly increases learning 
difficulty (the coefficient is 0.020, significant at the 1% level) through 
increasing the sense of learning difficulty to hinder academic perfor­
mance (the coefficient is 0.338, significant at the 1 % level). The 
mediating effect is 0.080, accounting for 19.17 % of the total effect. 
Self-efficacy is another mediating variable. In appropriate amounts, 
after-class learning time promotes academic performance through 
increasing self-efficacy. The mediating effect is 0.156, accounting for 
6.99 % of the total effect. In excess, after-class learning time hinders 
academic performance through decreasing self-efficacy. The mediating 
effect is 0.023, accounting for 5.38 % of the total effect. Similar to the 
conclusion of Kitsantas and Zimmerman (2009), learning time indirectly 
influences academic performance through self-efficacy. Appropriate 
learning time (before the inflection point) can improve academic per­
formance by decreasing the sense of learning difficulty and increasing 
the sense of self-efficacy, while excessive learning time (after the in­
flection point) can hinder academic performance by increasing the sense 
of learning difficulty and decreasing the sense of self-efficacy. This gives 
a reasonable explanation for the “inverted U” curve shown by the 
dose-response function.

amounts of time studying to achieve the best scores.

5. Conclusions and implications 
Chinese students face great learning pressure from childhood. Under 
the cruelly competitive system, students’ extracurricular study time is 
only increasing. On the one hand, there is still controversy about 
whether after-class learning time can promote academic performance. 
On the other hand, little literature has explored the causal effect of 
continuous after-class learning time on academic performance. Based on 
data of the China Education Panel Survey and the design principle of 
generalized propensity score matching, this paper analyzes the causal 
relation between after-class learning time and academic performance of 
middle school students and seeks to separate out confounding factors in 
the samples to obtain a more accurate estimation result. 
The specific research findings are as follows. First, the regression 
results of the ordinary least squares method show that for both weekdays 
or weekends, there is a nonlinear relation between after-class learning 
time and academic performance, which is similar to the conclusions of 
most studies (Beaton et al., 1996; Liu, 2012; Shen, 2014; He and Wen, 
2017; Ma and Zheng, 2017; Fang et al., 2018). Second, the results of 
generalized propensity score matching show a significant “inverse U” 
causal effect between after-class learning time and academic perfor­
mance. Test scores first improve and then decline with the increase of 
learning time. In other words, after-class learning time can promote 
academic development to some extent. There is an inflection point (3 h 
per day on weekdays and 8 h per day on weekends) at which students 
achieve the best grades. This inflection point can be called the “optimal 
after-class learning time”. However, when after-class learning time ex­
ceeds the inflection point, academic development will be hindered. 
Third, further research finds that the causal effect of after-class learning 
time on academic performance varies among different groups. Students 
who come from disadvantaged backgrounds or share educational re­
sources with siblings must study longer than others to earn the best 
grades. Fourth, after-class learning time influences academic perfor­
mance through students’ perceived learning difficulty, and self-efficacy 
promotes academic development by decreasing the sense of learning 
difficulty and increasing the sense of self-efficacy before the inflection 
point. After the inflection point, academic development is hindered by 
increasing the sense of learning difficulty and decreasing the sense of 
self-efficacy. Fifth, through a series of robustness checks, such as 
replacing variables that measure academic performance, examining 
different types of learning time, and using a third-order equation to fit 
the dose-response function, the conclusion is found to be basically 
consistent with the original text, and the stability of the result is verified. 
As China is the largest developing country in the world, it is valuable 
to explore the influence of after-class learning time on Chinese students’ 
academic performance. Too much after-class learning time places a 
heavy academic burden on Chinese students. The academic burden 
problem has long plagued the development of basic education in China, 
which is not only unfavorable to the improvement of students’ academic 
performance but also has a negative impact on their physical health and 
mental health, such as significantly reducing sleep time and sports time 
and increasing the incidence of myopia (Fang et al., 2018). However, 
Chinese schools attach great importance to the graduation rate, teachers 
value teaching performance, and parents are afraid that their children 
will lose at the starting line in life. Chinese students not only need to 
obey the school and their parents but also face fierce peer competition, 
which leads to a lack of autonomy in controlling their after-class 
learning time. Our results can be used to broaden the understanding 
of the relationship between after-class learning time and academic 
achievement. Although it is commonly observed and recognized that a 
long study time is not conducive to improvement in academic perfor­
mance (Westerhof et al., 2000; Warton, 2001; Trautwein et al., 2002; 
Kitsantas et al., 2011), our study can help educators further understand 
what optimal learning time can contribute to the best academic 
performance. 
Our findings also provide a number of useful implications for poli­
cymakers implementing interventions related to after-class learning 
time. It has been proven that after-class learning for too long will have a 
negative impact on students’ academic achievement, so the benefits of 
learning time should not be overstated (He and Wen, 2017), and it is 
crucial to develop a scientifically based learning strategy. More broadly, 
the results of this paper also provide a solution to the "boy crisis" 
problem. The past two decades have witnessed the reversal of a 
remarkable gender gap in education. In the US and many other devel­
oped countries, girls outperform boys in reading scores, top GPA dis­
tribution in high school, and college attendance (Fortin et al., 2015). The 
results of our heterogeneity analysis suggest that optimal learning time 
plays a greater role for boys, suggesting that interventions targeting boys 
with poor academic performance are likely to have high returns. In other 
words, maintaining an optimal learning time can narrow the gender gap 
in academic performance. 
In addition, our study extends after-class learning time from tradi­
tionally focused homework time to homework time and cram school 
time to attempt to fill this gap in the literature (Kitsantas and Zimmer­
man, 2009; Kitsantas et al., 2011; Zhu and Leung, 2012; Maltese et al., 
2012; Flunger et al., 2015). As early as the 1960s, Chinese government 
leaders proposed a policy to reduce the academic burden of primary and 
middle school students, but the effect has not been obvious. At the end of 
2018, China’s Ministry of Education again issued measures to reduce the 
burden on primary and secondary school students, which include strict

controls on the total amount of homework. For example, the total 
amount of homework in the fifth and sixth grades should not exceed one 
hour per day, and the total amount of homework in junior high school 
should not exceed 1.5 h per day. However, the above policies mainly 
focus on reducing the burden of school courses, which ignores the 
increasing phenomenon of cram school. The next step should focus on 
the growing academic burden outside school and seek to improve the 
quality and efficiency of school education. As our study has found, 
whether after-class learning time required by school (doing homework 
assigned by school teachers) or after-class learning time required by 
cram school (taking cram school courses and doing homework assigned 
by cram school), prolonged learning time does not always have a posi­
tive impact on students. Therefore, teachers should try to avoid simple, 
repetitive and punitive tasks when assigning homework. Students also 
need to choose extracurricular tutoring within a reasonable learning 
time. 
It is worth noting that the conclusions drawn in this paper need to be 
treated with caution. The benefits of learning time for students are well 
proven (Keith et al., 2004; Kitsantas and Zimmerman, 2009; Zhu and 
Leung, 2012; Masui et al., 2014; Andrietti and Velasco, 2015), and our 
findings do not imply a rejection of after-class learning. The point is that 
students who study too long will experience reduced perceived 
self-efficacy and increased perceived learning difficulty. The negative 
effects of these noncognitive outcomes will further hinder the 
improvement in academic performance. Finally, there are two primary 
limitations to this study. First, we cannot investigate the optimal 
after-class learning time for each subject, as the China Education Panel 
Survey only includes overall learning time. Second, we only use stu­
dents’ self-report indicators as after-class learning time variables. The 
selective bias caused by recall may lead to overestimation or underes­
timation of the results.
