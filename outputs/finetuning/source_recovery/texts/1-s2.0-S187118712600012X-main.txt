This study investigates Vietnamese undergraduates' complex problem-solving (CPS) abilities and
strategies by analysing both performance and process data. A sample of 589 participants
completed computer-based CPS assessments, with problem-solving proficiency evaluated using
frameworks adapted from the PISA 2012 cycle. The findings revealed students tended to perform
better in the exploration phase but faced challenges when applying that knowledge to derive
effective solutions. Male students demonstrated an advantage in exploring, gathering, and processing information essential by engaging in more frequent interactions or operations during the
task. The link between student persistence and performance varies depending on the model’s
structure. The number of interactions is a stronger predictor of performance than time-on-task. A
majority of students (70%) lacked effective strategy, “Vary-One-Thing-At-A-Time” (VOTAT)
strategy, for model-based tasks, though males were more active in exploration. The study also
identified habitual visual scanning and interaction patterns during the exploration stage, which
aligned with conventional left-to-right, top-to-bottom reading habits. These patterns suggest a
systematic interaction style, potentially influenced by visual salience, perceived importance, or
established scanning heuristics. These findings provide valuable insights into students' CPS
abilities, strategic approaches, and behavioural patterns. The study concludes by discussing
instructional and practical frameworks to enhance and assess problem-solving competence within
educational contexts.

1. Introduction
Problem-solving is one of the most in-demand skills in today’s job market, valued for its ability to overcome challenges and drive
success in a rapidly changing work environment (James & Margaret, 2012; OECD, 2014). Analytical thinking and problem-solving are
the two highest-priority skills for the global workforce over the next five years (World Economic Forum, 2023). Many countries have
recognised this and made problem-solving a key focus of their national education programs (van Merriënboer, 2013). The demand for
individuals with high problem-solving ability in the workplace is rising because these competencies are now considered a basic

requirement for meaningful participation in a digital and information-rich society (Zhou et al., 2012). Aligning educational outcomes
with global labour trends, particularly the cognitive demands of problem-solving emphasized by the World Economic Forum (2023),
may enhance students’ preparedness for future personal and professional challenges.
Most researchers agree that assessment models, which mainly measure isolated knowledge, are inadequate for evaluating 21st-century skills such as problem solving (Pepper, 2011; Voogt & Roblin, 2012). They call for new assessment approaches that incorporate
authentic and diverse tasks. These upgraded models should employ dynamic strategies to gather comprehensive information on how
problem-solvers apply their skills, including their outcomes, processes, and reasoning in technology-rich environments (Child & Shaw,
2016; Maxwell, 2021; Pepper, 2011; Rolim & Isaias, 2019; Van Vo & Csapó, 2023).
Interactive simulations, in particular, are highly effective for assessing cognitive and social skills relevant to the 21st century,
including problem-solving through sequences of actions (Almond et al., 2014; Scoular, 2019). Interactive simulations or
computer-based problem-solving tasks or simulation problem-solving tasks (hereafter referred to as simulation tasks) can mirror
real-life challenges, providing a more ecologically valid assessment of students’ complex problem-solving abilities (Xu et al., 2018).
Beyond producing traditional outcome scores - often referred to as performance data (Greiff et al., 2016; Krieger et al., 2021; OECD,
2013) or product data (Goldhammer et al., 2017; Teig et al., 2020) - simulation-based tasks also generate rich streams of information
known as log file data. These log files record the sequence of students’ actions, decisions, and strategies as they work through a
problem, thereby providing insights not only into whether students arrived at a correct solution but also into how they approached the
task. Such information sheds light on learners’ behavioural patterns (e.g., trial-and-error attempts, use of available tools, or navigation
paths) as well as their underlying cognitive processes (e.g., planning, monitoring, and strategy selection) during problem solving.
Because of this, log file data are commonly classified as process data, offering researchers and educators a powerful means to examine
the dynamics of problem solving in real time and to better understand the development of higher-order thinking skills (He et al., 2021;
Maddox, 2023). Empirical studies (e.g., Goldhammer et al., 2017; Teig et al., 2020) suggested that analysing process data not only
yields new insights into problem-solving behaviour but also serves to validate the assumptions underlying task design. The combined
use of product data and process data offers to deepen understanding of student performance and to strengthen measures of test
engagement (Ercikan et al., 2020; Maddox, 2023).
In this regard, the present study aims to explore complex problem-solving of Vietnamese university students, using performance
data and process data through simulation tasks. It seeks to provide a preliminary understanding of students’ problem-solving behaviours and to lay the foundation for future research. In addition, as in many East and Southeast Asian cultures, Vietnamese students
are frequently trained in linear and hierarchical thinking, characterized by habitual top-to-bottom and left-to-right processing (Li,
2012). These habits may influence the ways in which Vietnamese learners approach problem-solving tasks. This study explores such
behaviours, which may help educators and designers develop more culturally responsive and cognitively aligned learning environments. Taken together, the study seeks to contribute to the existing literature by drawing on both product and process data to enhance
understanding of students’ CPS. It is expected to provide not only deeper insights into students’ abilities and behaviours but also into
their habitual thinking patterns, thereby identifying areas where further support (Zhou et al., 2016) or intervention (Chen & Wang,
2020) may be needed.
2. Theoretical background
2.1. Complex problem-solving
A problem is a situation when having a goal, but not knowing how to achieve it. Problem-solving competency involves psychological processes and essential activities to tackle unknown situations in dynamic environments, where routine actions are insufficient
to attain success (OECD, 2013). This study refers to the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2012 framework for
the domain of Creative Problem Solving. Although the official PISA title for the domain was “Creative Problem Solving”, the tasks
involved were characterized by high levels of complexity, connectivity, and dynamism, aligning closely with the well-established
construct of Complex Problem Solving (CPS) in psychological and educational research (Dörner & Funke, 2017).
CPS is defined by an individual’s ability to interact with dynamic task environments, systems that change over time or in response
to user actions. Such tasks are often intransparent, meaning key patterns and causal rules are hidden and must be uncovered through
active exploration (Dörner & Funke, 2017; Greiff & Funke, 2017; OECD, 2014). CPS is not an isolated skill but rather a critical factor in
broader academic and cognitive performance. It demonstrates strong connections to both general cognitive abilities and
domain-specific problem-solving competencies, including knowledge application and transfer within specialised fields (Molnár et al.,
2013). CPS has been linked to higher-order cognitive traits, such as intelligence and inductive reasoning, highlighting its role as a key
component of cognitive flexibility and real-world decision-making (Greiff et al., 2013; Greiff & Neubert, 2014; Molnár et al., 2013;
Schweizer et al., 2013). Previous studies revealed a positive correlation between CPS proficiency and academic achievement, suggesting that students with strong CPS abilities tend to perform better in school performance (Greiff & Neubert, 2014; Schweizer et al.,
2013).
2.2. Assessing complex problem-solving
Several empirical studies (e.g., Greiff et al., 2013; Kretzschmar et al., 2016; Krieger et al., 2021; Lotz et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2022)
employed simulation tasks to assess students’ CPS abilities. These studies emphasized integrations of cognitive, contextual and
technological aspects into simulation tasks, which play a crucial role in understanding how a participant navigates CPS scenarios in

daily life. According to the literature, the MicroFIN and MicroDYN models (Funke & Greiff, 2017; OECD, 2014) are among the most
commonly used foundational assessment frameworks.
MicroFIN structure ໿based on finite-state automata to design problem-solving simulation (Fischer et al., 2015; Funke, 2001). A
MicroFIN task qualitatively illustrates the interconnections among elements in a problem simulation. It consists of a set of states, the
transitions between them, and input signals that initiate these transitions (Funke, 2001; Greiff, Fischer et al., 2015). It is referred to in
this study as a realistic simulation because they mirror the logic of everyday devices (e.g., vending machines, robotic cleaners, and coffee
makers), where the system’s transparency is low until the user interacts with the interface.
MicroDYN model involves multiple complex systems, based on linear structural equations as an assessment model to measure the
performance in multiple microworlds (Greiff, et el., 2015). A problem simulation task based on this approach is embedded into a
fictitious scenario. The dynamism and connectivity in a MicroDYN system is modelled by linear equations (Greiff, Fischer et al., 2015;
Krieger et al., 2021). It is referred to as a model-based simulation, as it requires the solver to identify the quantitative weight of relations between input and output variables.
The MicroFIN and MicroDYN frameworks have been employed and validated in various previous studies (e.g., Greiff et al., 2013;
Kretzschmar et al., 2016; Krieger et al., 2021; Lotz et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2022) and in Programme for International Student
Assessment (PISA) (OCED, 2013, 2014). Both types of simulation CPS assessment typically measure CPS in two dimensions (stages):
knowledge acquisition and knowledge application (Dindar, 2018; Kretzschmar et al., 2016; Schweizer et al., 2013; Wirth & Klieme, 2003).
In knowledge acquisition stage, the participant engages in exploratory interaction to reduce intransparency. This stage aims to
understand the underlying structure, rules, and causal relationships that govern how the system operates. The participant manipulates
available variables or elements to observe how these changes affect system states or output variables. Through systematic experimentation, observation, and inference, the participant begins to form mental models of the system’s dynamics. This stage emphasizes
information gathering, hypothesis formation, and pattern recognition, allowing the participant to build a conceptual understanding of
how different components interrelate and influence outcomes.
Once a mental model is established, the participants transition to the knowledge application phase, where they regulate their actions
to reach specific goal states. This stage tests the ability to manage the system's dynamism and maintain control over interrelated
variables to achieve desired outcomes. Together, these stages illustrate how problem solvers move from learning about the system to
using that knowledge to manage complex and dynamic situations.
2.3. Gender difference in complex problem-solving
There is a variation in CPS abilities regarding gender difference, observed across contexts, influenced by national and cultural
factors. Some previous studies found no significant performance gap between males and females. For example, Dindar (2018) found no
significant effect of gender on CPS ability in high school students in Turkey. Additionally, Wu and Molnár (2021) found no significant
difference among Chinese undergraduate students in CPS tasks.
In contrast, in a cross-national study by Wüstenberg et al. (2014), involving 890 Hungarian and German students (grades 8–11),
found that males outperformed females. Similarly, Hungarian males significantly outperformed Hungarian females (Wu & Molnár,
2021). In line the trend, OECD's PISA 2012 survey on CPS reported that male students outperformed females in most participating
countries (OECD, 2014). To explain the patterns, Borgonovi and Greiff (2020) suggested that these findings are linked to societal
factors, where differing social paths and expectations for males and females may contribute to observed gender differences. In the
Vietnamese context within Confucian Heritage Cultures, as noted by Nguyen et al. (2006), students are socialized to value accuracy
and compliance over trial-and-error. These expectations are often more pronounced for females, leading to a cautious exploration
style. This differs from male students, who tend toward active manipulation, suggesting a gender gap in problem-solving behaviour.
Several studies, particularly those utilizing the MicroDYN approach, suggest that males are more likely to employ the highly
effective “vary one thing at a time” (VOTAT) strategy than females (Greiff et al., 2015; Wüstenberg et al., 2014). VOTAT involves
systematically manipulating a single variable while holding all others constant to infer causal relationships. This strategic difference is
a primary explanatory factor for the small, yet consistent, male advantage in the knowledge acquisition component of CPS. Wittmann
and Hattrup (2004) explained that male relies on more efficient strategies for completing problem-solving tasks, which allows them to
detect variables and system structure clearly.
Conversely, female participants are sometimes observed to employ more cautious or exploratory strategies, which, while thorough,
can be less efficient or systematic for rapid rule detection (Goldhammer et al., 2014). However, females often demonstrate an
advantage, utilizing more collaborative language and consensus-building strategies (Griffin et al., 2012). These patterns suggest that
gender differences in strategy may be context-dependent and linked to general problem-solving styles.
2.4. Research on problem-solver’s behaviour based on process data
Research into problem solvers’ behaviour through process data has gained increasing attention in recent years. Some studies have
examined the total number of response actions, while others have focused on the amount of time participants spend on tasks.
Meanwhile, several researchers have investigated the strategies that problem solvers employ to achieve their goals.
2.4.1. Number of interactions (actions) and problem-solvers’ achievements
Number of actions is increasingly considered important information about problem-solver behaviour (Goldhammer et al., 2017).
Number of actions is the count of discrete interaction (e.g., clicks, drag-and-drop) taken during task engagement. Araneda et al. (2022)

revealed a nonlinear link between proficiency and action frequency, with middle-proficiency students performing more actions than
their low- or high-proficiency peers in mathematics tasks. Furthermore, Goldhammer et al. (2017) examined the relationship between
the number of actions performed during task completion and task success. Their results showed an inverted U-shaped relationship
between the number of actions and performance for low-complexity tasks, whereas for high-complexity tasks, the relationship followed a monotonically increasing S-shape, indicating that a greater number of actions was associated with a higher probability of
correctly completing the tasks. Eichmann et al. (2020) corroborated the pattern, showing a positive correlation between CPS performance and interaction count (more clicks often correspond to richer exploration and higher success). However, a recent study by
Molnár and Greiff (2023), who studied 1828 first-year university students to analyse transitions across successive problem-solving
processes, found that the number of interactions proved a stronger predictor of performance than time-on-task. Furthermore, a
study by Chen et al. (2019), analysing PISA 2012 CPS highlighted how both time and the number of clicks collectively influence
outcome prediction models. The association between number of actions and task success is not simple, or linear. The number of actions
appears to correspond to different psychological processes, conditional on the range of values, and task features (Goldhammer et al.,
2017).
2.4.2. Time-on-task and problem-solvers’ achievements
Time-on-task is defined as the duration during which an individual is actively and meaningfully engaged in a learning or performance activity (Kovanovic et al., 2016). It encompasses the duration of perceptual, cognitive, and psychomotor activities involved
in the solution process, from task initiation to completion (Goldhammer et al., 2014).
Time-on-task is one of the most widely studied behavioural indicators in problem-solving tasks. It plays a key role in understanding
task completion, as it reflects and differentiates underlying cognitive behaviours (Goldhammer et al., 2014). Time-on-task can be
understood in two contrasting ways (Goldhammer et al., 2014; Van Vo & Csapó, 2023): longer engagement may enhance accuracy by
allowing thorough processing (Eichmann et al., 2020), but shorter times may also signal high proficiency and fluency (Greiff et al.,
2016).
The empirical study by Goldhammer et al. (2014), which applied computer-based assessment data to analyse the interaction between time, task difficulty, and individual skill level, found a positive relationship between time-on-task and problem-solving
achievement. On average, individuals who spent more time on a problem were more likely to achieve a correct solution. This positive effect was found to increase with task difficulty, suggesting that for more challenging problems, a longer, more thorough approach
is a necessary component of success. However, this effect was found to decrease as a person's skill level increased. A highly skilled
problem-solver could achieve success in less time than a low-skilled peer. For a proficient problem-solver on an easy task (a
low-demand situation), the positive time-on-task effect was dramatically reduced and even became negative. This indicates that the
meaning of time-on-task is highly contextual and serves as a diagnostic indicator of the cognitive strategy employed (Goldhammer
et al., 2014).
In the context of CPS tasks developed on the basis of the microDYN model, former studies indicate that the time invested in CPS
tasks may influence performance outcomes. Scherer et al. (2015) found that increased time-on-task positively predicted school
achievement in CPS contexts, suggesting that extended engagement allows for deeper processing and strategy application. However,
an empirical study by Molnár and Greiff (2023) indicated that spending time on a task did not necessarily lead to better outcomes.
Proficient solvers tend to allocate greater time to early-stage exploration to deliberate efficient problem-solving trajectory rather than
relying on random trial-and-error (Molnár & Csapó, 2018).
2.4.3. Strategies applied and complex problem-solving
In complex problem solving, strategies are systematic patterns of actions or thought processes that problem-solvers employ to
explore a problem space, test hypotheses, and achieve task goals efficiently (Funke & Greiff, 2017; Fischer et al., 2012). Effective
problem solvers in MicroDYN tasks tend to use systematic exploration strategies - VOTAT - to engage in structured hypothesis testing
(Greiff et al., 2015; Wüstenberg et al., 2012). The VOTAT strategy is the gold standard for systematically identifying causal relationships in opaque, dynamic systems (Greiff et al., 2015). A VOTAT involves manipulating a single input variable while keeping all
other variables constant to clearly determine that variable's effect on the outputs.
The conscious and correct application of the VOTAT strategy is consistently shown to lead to significantly higher achievement in
the knowledge acquisition component of CPS, which, in turn, facilitates successful application in the goal attainment phase
(Wüstenberg et al., 2014). Students who effectively apply VOTAT show the highest overall performance (Molnár et al., 2022). VOTAT
is a core approach within the control of variables strategy (CVS), an essential scientific reasoning skill that enables individuals to
design effective experiments and draw valid conclusions from their results (Van Vo & Csapó, 2021).
Conversely, strategies characterized by non-systematic exploration, such as trial-and-error, rigid repetition of unsuccessful actions,
or rapid, unexamined manipulation of multiple variables, are associated with lower achievement (Fischer et al., 2015; Wu & Molnár,
2018).
2.4.4. Habitual thinking and problem-solving behaviour
Habitual thinking refers to the reliance on automatic, cognitive processes, well-established strategies when confronting novel,
dynamic, and opaque problems (Kahneman, 2011). Arieli and Sagiv (2018) found that individualistic mindsets enhance performance
on rule-based tasks, whereas collectivist mindsets boost success on context-based problems. In cross-cultural contexts, these habits are
often products of long-term socialization within specific educational and cultural frameworks.
A cross-cultural study by Nisbett et al. (2001), focusing cognitive styles (holistic versus analytic), revealed that holistic thinkers

(typically from East Asian cultures), prioritise contextual relations, attending to background cues and dialectical reasoning, while
“analytic thinkers” (common in Western cultures) focus on objects, categories, and formal logic. This distinction may influence
strategy selection in dynamic problem-solving tasks. For example, while successful model-based simulation tasks often require the
VOTAT strategy to isolate causal links (Greiff et al., 2015), holistic thinkers may struggle with this isolation.
In addition, as in many East and Southeast Asian cultures, Vietnamese students are frequently trained in linear and hierarchical
thinking, characterized by habitual top-to-bottom and left-to-right processing (Li, 2012). In such contexts, teachers are typically cast as
knowledge transmitters reproducing established concepts, while students, socialized to respect hierarchical order, rarely challenge this
dynamic (Wei et al., 2024). These habits may influence the ways in which Vietnamese learners approach problem-solving tasks.
Habitual thinking, in the negative sense, manifests as cognitive tendencies or biases that lead problem-solvers to favour mental
shortcuts (heuristics) over systematic analysis. In CPS environments, this generally impairs the critical initial phase of knowledge
acquisition. Problem-solvers often engage in confirmation bias, habitually seeking information that confirms their initial, often superficial, hypothesis about the system's function while neglecting contradictory evidence (Wason, 1960). In CPS tasks, this limits the
systematic exploration required to identify true causal structures (Greiff et al., 2015). Understanding the aspects could be important for
aligning assessment design and instructions with participants' cultural perspectives.
3. The present study
Viet Nam has participated in the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), conducted by the Organisation for
Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), since the 2012 cycle (OECD, 2014). Vietnamese students demonstrated moderate
academic performance in PISA. According to the 2022 PISA results, Vietnam scored 469 in mathematics, just three points below the
OECD average (472). Meanwhile, its science (462) and reading (472) performances were marginally lower than the OECD averages of
476 and 485 (OECD, 2023). However, the country has not yet taken part in the problem-solving strand, and little is known about how
Vietnamese schools foster the development of this ability.
In Vietnam, there is a growing demand to strengthen problem-solving skills in the national education curriculum (MOET, 2017).
Problem-solving is considered a core competency at the higher education level. However, research has indicated a disconnect between
universities and industry needs (Mainga et al., 2022; Tran, 2012), resulting in curricula that often fail to keep pace with labour market
demands (Tran, 2015). Addressing this gap requires fostering creativity, innovation, and problem-solving abilities to better align
higher education with real-world challenges (Mainga et al., 2022).
The present study aims to explore students’ problem-solving abilities and strategies, utilising both performance and process data
based on the CPS PISA 2012 framework tasks. The investigation focuses on performance scores, number of interactions, strategies
applied and time-on-task. Gender differences are also considered in the regards. In particular, this study seeks to characterize participants’ habitual thinking patterns derived from process data. Accordingly, we focus on the following three research questions (RQs)
RQ1. What are complex problem-solving abilities and strategies among Vietnamese students?
RQ2. Are there significant differences between male and female students in complex problem-solving?
The first two research questions build up to each other during our investigation. We tend to explore student’s complex problemsolving performance in general and in individual tasks along with main aspects of log-file analysis such as number of interactions,
strategies applied and time-on-task.
RQ3. Are there habitual thinking patterns (e.g., top-left scanning bias) that shape how students engage with object positions during
problem solving?
We hypothesise that Vietnamese students may display a top-left scanning bias due to habitual linear and hierarchical thinking
patterns (Li, 2012). This is not merely a visual habit but a strategic constraint: by prioritizing objects in the top-left quadrant regardless
of their functional relevance, students may fail to engage in the systematic, non-linear exploration required to decode complex causal
structures. Thus, RQ3 serves as a bridge to explain the process-level reasons behind the performance patterns observed in RQ1 and
RQ2.
4. Methods
4.1. Participants
The main study involved 589 undergraduate students from a university in southern Vietnam, consisting of 332 females (56.4 %)
and 257 males (43.6 %). The study took place in the first semester of the 2023–2024 academic year. Students were informed about the
research project's purpose and their role in it, allowing them to decide whether to participate. The participating students had no prior
experience with the microworld model-based simulation tasks. Each participant was given a unique code to access the online survey
and test, which took about 30–40 min to complete. Participation was voluntary, with no impact on their academic performance.
Students could withdraw at any time before submitting the survey without needing to provide a reason. Data collection occurred in the
university computer rooms. This study received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the author’s university
(Approval No: AGU-C2023–16–06) and all ethical guidelines were followed.

Additionally, there were three model-based simulation tasks, named as Air Conditioner Control, Social Activity, and Basketball

The CPS tasks were adapted and translated from the original English version into Vietnamese. Two education experts and two
lecturers reviewed the adapted items, and an independent researcher conducted a backtranslation into English to ensure accuracy.
After addressing translation, content validity, and technical issues, the revised version was piloted with five students prior to the main
study (see Vietnamese CPS tasks version at https://www.digistem.ie/cps/vi.
Each stage of the simulation tasks was presented on a single computer screen. Students can move forward items by clicking on the
“Next” button, depicted as an arrow icon at the bottom of the screen. The items were presented in a fixed-order delivery format.
Students were not able to revisit previous items once they had moved on. The online CPS tests were developed and administered via a
web-based platform.
The current study demonstrated acceptable reliability in the unidimensional model, with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.76 (ω
= 0.80) and a final deviance of 6632.588 with 13 parameter estimates in (AIC= 6658.588, BIC = 6668.599). For the two-dimensional
model, Cronbach's alpha values were 0.65 (ω = 0.70) for knowledge acquisition and 0.66 (ω = 0.79) for knowledge application,
respectively, with a final deviance of 6513.636, with 15 parameter estimates (AIC= 6543.6361, BIC= 6555.188). The two-dimensional
model had a lower deviance, suggesting it is a better fit to the current data. A moderate positive correlation was observed between
knowledge acquisition and knowledge application in the two-dimensional model (r = 0.459). The finding aligns with existing studies
(Kretzschmar et al., 2016; Schweizer et al., 2013), which confirmed that a two-dimensional model is a more accurate construct for
assessing CPS abilities with the framework.
4.3. Scoring and data analysis
The scoring of the CPS tasks were conducted according to the official grading guidelines of PISA (OECD, 2014) and previous studies
(e.g., Greiff et al., 2013; Neubert et al., 2014). Dichotomous scores were assigned to each item across both dimensions. Particularly for
knowledge acquisition, in the model-based simulation tasks, credit was granted if the causal model was drawn correctly (1 point);
otherwise, no credit was assigned (0 points). In the realistic simulation task, correct responses were assigned 1 point, while incorrect
responses received 0 points. Similarly, for knowledge application in both two models, credit was given if all goals were achieved;
otherwise, no credit was assigned. Then, raw CPS scores were subsequently transformed onto a standardized scale using maximum
likelihood estimation via the Rasch model, employed in ACER ConQuest software (Adams & Wu, 2010). The Rasch analysis also served
to verify the psychometric quality and internal consistency of the CPS instrument.
Process data are automatically generated as participants interact with the elements of the CPS tasks on a web-based platform. These
interactions are formatted as a sequence, S = s1 , s2 , s3 , …, sn , where each action si is part of a predefined action set A, meaning si ϵ A =
{a1 , a2 , …, am }. Actions within this sequence may be repeated. Since the actions are ordered, each action is linked to the one that
follows it. Building on these principles, the study generated a sequential array of participants' actions. These interactions were coded
based on the specific simulations and task objectives. Each element in this array included a unique participant identifier, the time spent
on the action, and the specific activity performed. This raw data was subsequently converted into a data frame for further statistical
analysis.
Pearson correlation and t-tests were employed for research questions 1 and 2 (RQ1 & RQ2), with analyses conducted in R and
Python. Graphs and plots were primarily generated using the ggplot2 package in R (Wickham, 2016).
For research question 3 (RQ3), dispersion analysis was utilised to investigate distinct spatial distribution patterns of student interactions within the CPS tasks. This analysis was performed using the Matplotlib package in Python (Hunter, 2007).

5.1.2. Gender difference in complex problem-solving
Prior to comparing scores across groups, a measurement invariance test was performed to ensure that the construct was measured
equivalently across genders. To assess potential gender-related bias in individual test items, Differential Item Functioning (DIF)
analysis was conducted, with males serving as the focal group. Using Angoff’s delta method and item purification parameters (a =
0.542, b = 0.905) in the final iterations, along with a detection threshold of 1.13 (significance level: 5 %), the analysis revealed that 12
items showed no DIF as illustrated in Fig. 3 (See more Appendix B).
Fig. 4 depicts the distribution of CPS abilities among students, highlighting differences between males and females. Additionally,
the distribution patterns differed noticeably between the two groups. The trend of male students outperforming female students was
observed in both the knowledge acquisition and knowledge application phases. Notably, the gender gap was more pronounced in the
exploration or knowledge acquisition stage.
Furthermore, results from the t-test indicated a significant overall gender difference on the CPS test. Male students scored higher
(Mean= − 0.90, SD=1.65) than female students (Mean=− 1.33, SD=1.83), t(543.67)=3.569, p<.001, Cohen's d = 0.30. Notably, males
outperformed females in the knowledge acquisition phase, with male students scoring significantly higher (Mean=− 0.49, SD=1.40) than
female students (Mean=− 1.20, SD=1.30), t(516.32)=4.95, p<.001, Cohen's d = 0.42. In other words, male students appeared to have
an advantage over female students in exploring, gathering, and processing information necessary for problem-solving. However, no
significant gender difference was found in the knowledge application phase [female (Mean= − 1.56, SD=1.57) , male (Mean= − 1.49,
SD=1.68), p=.596, Cohen's d = 0.05], suggesting that performance at this stage is not influenced by gender.

The number of interactions varied across the CPS tasks (Fig.5). Students took more actions in the realistic simulation tasks than in

activity within these tasks did not necessarily translate to better performance and may even reflect inefficient or trial-and-error
problem-solving strategies. In contrast, the realistic simulation task demonstrated a positive correlation between the number of actions and achievement (r = 0.198, p < 0.001), indicating that more active engagement was associated with higher performance

5.2.3. Strategies applied and CPS achievements
5.2.3.1. Student strategies in the realistic simulation task. In the exploratory stage of the Robot Cleaner simulation, we analysed 10,569
records of interactions. Participants were able to manipulate five different objects in each observation. In theory, this allows for 32
distinct types of strategies, depending on the number and combination of variables that were modified and controlled. The results
indicated that the correlation between students’ achievement and the specific strategies employed was inconclusive.
However, a notable finding emerged: the number of observations was significantly and positively associated with students’
achievement at this stage (r = 0.110, p = 0.024). This positive trend was observed consistently across both male and female groups.
5.2.3.2. Student strategies in the model-based simulation tasks. Student strategies in model-based simulation tasks can be categorised
based on the number of variables manipulated for each observation. For this study, an observation is defined as an instance in which
participants configured the objects and then clicked the “Apply” button to run a simulation. Referring to previous studies (e.g., Greiff,
Wüstenberg, et al., 2015; Molnár & Csapó, 2018), we classified strategies into four types: Change 3 (varying three variables), Change 2
(varying two variables), Change 1 (varying one variable), and Change 0 (no variables varied). It is noted that Change 0, in which no
variables are varied, is considered a valid strategy in this study because, within the model-based simulation tasks, the output variable
can influence itself (the so-called Eigendynamic), representing growth or decay processes that occur independently of other variables
(Krieger et al., 2021). In non-eigendynamic systems, although the “Change 0” strategy does not yield new information regarding the
system’s autonomous behaviour, it is interpreted as a passive or redundant interaction.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, the most common approach among students was to vary all three variables simultaneously, accounting for

of student observations during the exploratory phase reflected the use of this strategy. These findings suggest that 70 % of student
actions employed suboptimal or inappropriate strategies, potentially hindering effective model-based reasoning.
Gender differences were observed regarding usage of strategies in the exploratory phase. Female students were more likely to
employ suboptimal strategies compared to their male counterparts. Female students significantly more often varied two variables
simultaneously for a single observation than male students, t(1544) = 2.402, p = 0.016, Cohenʹs d = 0.12. Additionally, female

In contrast, male students were significantly more likely to apply the appropriate strategy of varying only one variable at a time
(Change 1) than female students, t(1090) = 2.591, p = 0.009, Cohenʹs d = 0.14. These results suggest that male students, on average,
adopted more effective strategies in model-based tasks.
5.3. Habitual thinking and complex problem-solvers’ behaviour
Among the five objects available for interaction, students exhibited a pronounced preference for the Yellow box, which was selected
and relocated 2129 times, considerably more often than any other object. The Red box followed with 1683 interactions, and the Robot
with 1299. In contrast, the Green and Blue boxes were manipulated significantly less frequently, with 1083 and 944 interactions,
respectively.
Fig. 8 illustrates the data distribution patterns for the positions of the five movable objects: Robot, Red, Yellow, Green, and Blue
boxes. These distributions allow us to identify high-density regions (hotspots) where students frequently selected and placed objects
during exploration.
Dispersion analysis revealed distinct spatial distribution patterns. The Robot exhibited the widest dispersion along the x-axis (σ =
3.61, IQR = 6.3), yet maintained relatively uniform point density (CV = 0.67), suggesting broad coverage of the simulation space. In
contrast, the Green box showed pronounced clustering (CV = 1.12), with localised high-density areas and sparse regions, despite its
more limited x-axis spread (σ = 2.81, IQR = 4.0). The Red, Yellow, and Blue boxes displayed moderate dispersion (σ ≈ 2.8 − 3.1) with
varying density heterogeneity (CV = 0.74 − 0.83). The Robot was placed in more exploratory, widely dispersed locations, while the
coloured boxes were handled in a more constrained, positionally dependent manner.
A notable behavioural distinction emerged in how these objects were spatially relocated. The Robot was typically moved across a
broader range of the interface, indicating exploratory spatial behaviour. In contrast, the colour-coded boxes (Yellow, Red, Green, and
Blue) were generally repositioned closer to their original locations, suggesting a more localised and perhaps habitual manipulation
strategy. This indicates different cognitive approaches: exploratory for the Robot and conservative or efficiency-driven for the colorcoded boxes.
Moreover, the frequency of interactions exhibited a clear spatial trend. Objects located toward the top-left quadrant of the screen
received more attention and manipulation than those situated further to the right or lower on the screen. This top-left bias suggests a
habitual visual-scanning pattern aligned with common reading and interaction habits in left-to-right, top-to-bottom cultures.
Interestingly, this top-down interaction preference became even more pronounced in the model-based simulation environment. As
illustrated in Fig. 9, students interacted significantly more with variables located at the top of the interface, with decreasing
engagement observed for variables positioned lower down. This pattern underscores a systematic approach in students’ interaction
habits, potentially guided by visual salience, perceived importance, or conventional scanning heuristics.

achievement (Molnár & Greiff, 2023).

indicating that more active engagement was associated with higher performance outcomes. These findings align, in part, with the
study by Ventura and Shute (2013), which found that in game-based assessments, persistence and active involvement were strong
predictors of learning performance. In such tasks, success depends on extensive exploration to identify hidden states and transitions;
accordingly, a greater number of interactions reflects a more comprehensive mapping of the system’s possibilities (Sonnleitner et al.,

Regarding time-on-task, students tended to spend more time on the realistic simulation compared to the model-based simulation
tasks. The relationship between time spent and performance was generally unclear and depended on the model structures of simulation
tasks. It is negative in the model-based simulation tasks, but positive in the realistic simulation task. These findings partly align with
those of Goldhammer et al. (2017), suggesting that the time-on-task effect is largely shaped by differences in task characteristics as well
as individual ability levels. This is also supported by the findings of Molnár and Greiff (2023), who used model-based simulation tasks
and found that increased time-on-task did not necessarily lead to better outcomes. Overall, it appears that students are more engaged in
the realistic simulation task, as response times can serve as an important indicator of test-taking effort and motivation (Wise & Kong,
2005). Students who interacted more and spent more time on these tasks tended to perform better. However, this pattern was not
observed with model-based simulation tasks. They may spend significant time due to distraction or cognitive stalling, while an expert
problem-solver may achieve a high score quickly through high-efficiency heuristics.
The further analyses revealed the clearer reasons behind that. In the model-based simulation tasks, the effective problem-solvers
need to apply appropriate strategies - Vary-One-Thing-at-a-Time or VOTAT. The results showed that students lacked essential strategic
approaches when engaging with model-based simulation tasks. In particular, they did not know how to apply the VOTAT strategy to
test and identify cause-effect relationships between variables. They clicked on elements without a hypothesis, thereby increasing
cognitive noise and hindering the construction of an accurate mental model (Greiff et al., 2015). This strategy plays not only an
important role in the general domain of CPS (Wüstenberg et al., 2012) but also serves as a key approach in specific scientific subjects
(Van Vo et al., 2023).
6.2. Gender differences in CPS
Regarding gender difference, male students significantly outperformed female students, particularly in the knowledge acquisition
stage. The data revealed persistent gender differences on the CPS test, with male participants consistently achieving higher scores than
females across the entire spectrum of complex problem-solving simulations. This result aligns with several previous studies indicating a
male advantage (Greiff et al., 2018; ; Wüstenberg et al., 2014).
Males exhibited significantly more interactions than females overall. In contrast, female students spent significantly more time than
their male counterparts across all simulation tasks. Male students performed a notably higher average number of actions per unit of
time compared to female students. Despite spending less time on these tasks, male students outperformed female students.
Male students demonstrated significantly greater likelihood of employing the optimal strategy of isolating single variables
(VOTAT) compared to female students. Surprisingly, female students showed substantially more instances of observational passivity
(taking no action). Notably, males achieved higher performance across tasks, and more efficient strategies where fewer interactions
yielded better results.
These patterns may be explained by societal factors, particularly differing social pathways and expectations for males and females
(Borgonovi & Greiff, 2020). Specifically, Confucian Heritage Culture exerts a considerable influence on learning behaviours in
Vietnam. In the Vietnamese educational context, traditional gender roles often emphasize orderliness and compliance for female
students, while encouraging experimentation and technical mastery among male students (Nguyen et al., 2006). This may appear in
CPS as greater risk aversion among females, reflected in cautious observational passivity, whereas males may be socialized toward
more assertive trial-and-error engagement.
6.3. Habitual thinking patterns and spatial bias
An analysis of interaction frequency revealed a distinct spatial bias among participants. Students demonstrated a higher frequency
of interactions with interface elements positioned in the top-left quadrant of the screen, while elements located toward the bottom or
right received less attention. This top-left attentional bias is consistent with established visual-scanning patterns shaped by cultural
reading conventions, particularly in left-to-right, top-to-bottom reading systems (Li, 2012). In Vietnam, as in many East and Southeast
Asian education systems, linear and hierarchical thinking is strongly emphasized. This is reflected in standardized reading practices
and the structure of instructional materials (Li, 2012; Nhat et al., 2018).
Such patterns suggest that habitual cognitive frameworks may influence problem-solving behaviour. Specifically, students may be
prone to confirmation bias, habitually seeking information that aligns with pre-existing expectations while overlooking contradictory
evidence (Wason, 1960). This highlights the role of culturally reinforced thinking habits as potential barriers to adaptive reasoning in
complex task environments.
6.4. Educational and methodological implications
The findings suggest several potential implications for educational practices. The study contributes to the literature by demonstrating how process data can be utilized both to strengthen measures of test engagement and to enrich interpretations of student
performance (Maddox, 2023).
The findings underscore the importance and potential of intentionally enhancing exploration strategies, in which focus on critical
reasoning, scientific inquiry (Zhou et al., 2016) and support learning in novel technological environments (Vainikainen et al., 2015;
Wu & Molnár, 2021). It needs more advocating for problem-oriented and discovery-based learning activities in curricula. Although
most curricula currently focus on domain-specific problem-solving within particular subjects or themes, both domain-specific and
domain-general problem-solving skills are closely related and can transfer across contexts (Molnár et al., 2013).

The finding that 70 % of Vietnamese students lack effective VOTAT strategies necessitates a move beyond generic problem-solving
instruction toward an integration into university curricula. It is vital to teach students both the concept and application of the controlof-variables strategy, particularly the VOTAT method, as part of scientific inquiry approach (Van Vo et al., 2023). Rather than isolated
logic courses, VOTAT should be introduced in introductory science or research methodology modules. Integrating more
problem-solving tasks into subject-specific curricula can help students develop a habit of using this strategy, reinforcing strategic
thinking and improving their scientific reasoning skills in general domain. For example, in an economics simulation, students could be
tasked with isolating the impact of interest rates on inflation while keeping other factors constant, thereby transitioning VOTAT from a
general skill to a subject-specific heuristic.
Stakeholders need to prioritise the effective implementation of problem-based instruction, with explicit emphasis on problemsolving strategies during classroom activities and foster transferable cognitive skills essential for academic and real-world success
(Alibali et al., 2009; Herrmann et al., 2023; Tomic, 1995). Key instructional details, such as knowledge acquisition techniques and
application in novel situations, should be systematically reinforced. This needs to provide actionable insights for curricula.
Vietnamese students, similar to others in East and Southeast Asian cultures, are accustomed to top-to-bottom, left-to-right information processing, shaped by both traditional script formats and educational layout conventions. Moreover, the hierarchical thinking,
deference to order, and structured progression, all of which may subconsciously guide students toward top-positioned elements as
more authoritative (Li, 2012). These findings suggest that problem-solving behaviours are not merely products of cognitive ability or
instructional design but are deeply shaped by habitual thinking patterns, visual-spatial heuristics, and cultural learning experiences.
A notable concern is the lack of critical thinking and initiative when students, particularly females, encounter unfamiliar problems.
Many tend to remain passive rather than actively seeking possible solutions. Recognising these cultural and cognitive influences can
help educators and system designers create more culturally responsive and cognitively aligned learning environments.
This study makes theoretical and methodological contributions to international educational research. The study offers practical
value by demonstrating the application of the MicroDYN and MicroFIN frameworks in the design of CPS tasks. Model-based simulation
(MicroDYN) tasks may encourage the use of scientific reasoning strategies, while realistic simulation (MicroFIN) tasks tend to engage
participants more actively. Future research could explore how to combine these frameworks in CPS tasks to enhance performance and
assess accuracy more effectively. This involves designing instructional activities that reduce extraneous cognitive load by providing
clear guidance and structuring complex problems into manageable components.
Its contributions are valuable for educators and researchers looking to advance the design of interactive simulations and improve
the evaluation of complex cognitive aspects of problem-solving in educational contexts. Especially, the rise of Artificial Intelligence
applications compels us to rethink and reimagine approaches to learning and assessment (Farazouli et al., 2024; Overono & Ditta,
2023). This shift emphasises the need to focus more on the process rather than solely on the outcomes. This approach could inspire
teachers and researchers to adapt these methods, creating their own interactive simulation tasks to enhance learning and research
outcomes.
6.5. Limitations and future research
The study is subject to certain limitations. This study, being the first to assess CPS among students in Vietnam, has several limitations. Technical issues with the online tasks may have influenced the results. Additionally, the empirical data was drawn from a
limited sample of undergraduate students. Future research should aim to recruit more diverse samples to thoroughly assess the relationships found. Time-on-task can serve as a predictor of task success, depending on task characteristics and individual abilities
(Goldhammer et al., 2017). In the present study, strong evidence was found for the influence of task properties (model constructs) on
time on task in predicting success. However, participant characteristics were not incorporated into the analysis. Future studies should
account for these individual differences to provide a more comprehensive understanding. The current investigation used only the
number of actions to examine the problem-solvers’ behaviours. Future studies could explore more advanced approaches into action
sequences (He et al., 2021), such as network analysis (Zhu et al., 2016), to delve deeper into behavioural patterns. Additionally, spatial
interaction patterns were not examined using formal statistical significance tests (e.g., across screen regions or by gender). Future
studies should incorporate inferential methods to assess the robustness and generalizability of these patterns. Finally, factors like the
order of task delivery and instructional styles might impact a problem-solver’s performance, but these were not examined in this study.
