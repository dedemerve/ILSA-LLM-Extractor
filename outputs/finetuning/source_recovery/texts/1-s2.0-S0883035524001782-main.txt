The efficiency of schools can be measured as the ratio between the outputs produced (achievement) and the inputs utilised (human and technical resources). Once efficiency levels are obtained for each school through Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) techniques, one can assess how
an education system as a whole is either far or close to a maximum efficient educational frontier
given available resources. Further, one can rely on this estimated frontier to gauge the importance
of students, family and school factors pushing efficiency in different directions, and whether the
efficiency-equality trade holds. This exercise is relevant to the realities of education systems of the
global south where resources are limited and inequalities are vast. In this paper, we present novel
evidence for the Latin America region (9 countries) by using the latest two waves of data from
PISA (years: 2018 and 2022), a pooled sample of 4969 schools. Importantly, given the surveys’
timing, we are able to compare efficiency before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, contrasting
leading determinants. We find that, at regional level, efficiency slightly increased from 0.75 to
0.78, and only being constant in three (Brazil, Chile and Colombia) out of the nine countries.
Barriers of efficiency hold across the two waves overall, with systemic factors such as ICT access
and school type and location being more empirical relevant post-pandemic. Also, the link between low access to minimum knowledge and efficiency, as well as inclusion, worsened post
pandemic in half of the countries. During the pandemic, school efficiency was found to be boosted
− among others− by higher school support, better stock of ICT at home and communication with
teachers, whereas lack of coordination with education authorities was found to be a barrier.

1. Introduction
The notion of school efficiency (namely: achieving the maximum educational output for a given level of resources) is a key policy
concept for the long term financial sustainability of education systems’ which are underfunded and resource-constrained (De Witte and
López-Torres, 2017; Dufrechou, 2016). These education systems, in turn, have also experienced a severe educational disruption due to
the COVID-19 pandemic (Dias et al., 2022; Reshi, 2023), needing additional resources for the delivery of remote learning, keeping up
the level of students’ engagement. Hence, empirically contribute to the discussion of efficiency for relative poorer education systems is
vital because their limited resources are required to be efficiently administered, as well as due to the expansion of educational access
these systems have experienced yielding new challenges linked to quality and equity (Filmer et al., 2018).
In the case of poorer countries with deep educational inequalities, the pandemic impacts on learning have been more acute since

Earlier estimates by Azevedo (2020) suggested that the LAC region would have lost a Learning-Adjusted Years of Schooling (LAYS)

proportion of students below minimum proficiency as measured by PISA, from 53% to 68%). This has been further confirmed by
Azevedo et al. (2021), whose estimates indicate a loss for the region of around 0.9–1.1 years (from 7.8 years to 6.9-6.7 years in gloomy
scenarios). This is not surprising as the region experienced the longest period of school shutdown (on average 62 weeks) in the world
(UNESCO, 2024a).
In this paper, relying on OECD PISA data for the 2018 and 2022 waves for the region (comprising nine countries), we estimate the
region school efficiency levels for this period relying on the Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) technique (Thanassoulis, 2001; Thanassoulis et al., 2016) which mimics an education production function where school inputs are transformed into outputs (Hanushek
and Woessmann, 2011). We estimate the efficiency scores for 2552 (2018 wave) and 2417 (2022 wave) schools as well as their determinants in a second stage regression relying on recent bootstrap methods yielding bias-correct estimates (Badunenko and Tauchmann, 2019; Simar and Wilson, 2007). We also investigate the role of school policies during the pandemic as leverage mechanisms for
achievement and thereby efficiency.
Further, given the large inequality in the LAC region, we assess whether raising efficiency is at odds with school equality by relying
on an array of inequality indicators as determinants of school efficiency. The linkage of efficiency with equity in the education sector −
and whether they are substitutes or complements− has been studied in different settings (see, for instance, Chakraborty and Blackburn, 2013; Choi et al., 2021; Grosskopf et al., 2014). The main argument is that a more intense use of resources and range of
educational inputs are required to increase learning levels of relatively more disadvantaged students while this, in turn, may hinder
efficiency if their growth on learning levels does not raise to the same extent of the higher resources invested in these worse-off
students’ group. Long term educational segregation, and the vast amount of resources needed to address it, may render efficiency
and equity to be substitutes (i.e., higher levels of efficiency being associated with more inequality at schools). Yet, research on school
efficiency for education systems of richer countries (Agasisti and Zoido, 2018; Cherchye et al., 2010) and of poorer countries (Delprato
and Antequera, 2021a; Delprato and Antequera, 2021b) shows that this is not the case, as efficiency and equity are found to be
complements, particularly in public schools. This is promising as inclusion driven efficiency can help towards both the sustainability
and equity of LAC education systems. Here, we re-assess this for the whole group of LAC countries and at two points in time, pre and
post pandemic.
Our paper adds to the scarce the literature on efficiency for the LAC region, offering a new update based on the most recent data for
the region, with its leading contribution is, as far as we are aware, to be the first regional study which compares school efficiency before
and after the huge educational disruption caused by COVID-19 pandemic and schools’ closure. It should also be highlighted, however,
that we look into efficiency differences pre and post pandemic for the region, without claiming time causal effects. Our contribution to
the body of literature for the region is due to current studies mostly focusing on the persisting ex-post effects of the COVID-19 pandemic
on educational outcomes such as dropout and learning achievement (e.g., Betthäuser et al., 2023; Moscoviz et al., 2022; Patrinos et al.,
2022), rather than efficiency, as we do in the current paper.
Specifically, we explore the following research questions (RQ):
• RQ1. Is there a shift on the level of school efficiency for the whole LAC region before and after the pandemic based on the 2018 and
2022 PISA working sample? How does this shift on school efficiency − mediated by the COVID-19 pandemic− vary between the
nine countries included in the sample?
• RQ2. Which generic students and schools characteristics are associated with school efficiency and does the impact of these
characteristics differ before and after the pandemic?
• RQ3. Have the relationship of inequality and exclusion with school efficiency for the LAC region and between countries changed
before and after the pandemic?
• RQ4. Have policy factors at the student and school levels (to counteract the pandemic) influenced the degree of school efficiency on
the region as a whole and per country?

2. Background
One of the consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic was the prolonged closure of educational establishments and the shift from inperson education to technology-mediated teaching. Suddenly, students and teachers had to adapt to a new teaching modality and to
the social isolation that interfered with their education and wellbeing (Dias et al., 2022). In the LAC region, this transition was mostly
carried out with the lack of necessary Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) preparation and training of teachers and
without investment in schools’ infrastructure and households’ internet connectivity (Álvarez et al., 2020). Thus, the pandemic may
have worsened the consequences of socioeconomic inequality behind educational outcomes. Indeed, the dimension of the learning
crisis in the region is still huge: PISA 2022 data shows 75% of 15-years-old students in the region are unable to master foundational
skills in maths and 50% are not able to do so for reading (Ortiz et al., 2024).
This background section includes two sub-sections. In Section 2.1 we review some literature for the LAC region about school efficiency and the pandemic. Then, we outline some key elements and patterns to frame our DEA analysis through leading hypotheses
which we present in Section 2.2.
2.1. The pandemic and school efficiency in the LAC region
Approximately 1.7 billion students were impacted by the pandemic, which caused the largest disruption of educational systems in
human history (Reshi et al., 2023). In the case of Latin American education systems, it is key to highlight that some countries faced the
pandemic without additional resources. Total education related investment in the region, which was around 4.5% of GDP prior to the
pandemic, it now stands at 3.9%.3 There is some country heterogeneity on education expenditure patterns at secondary level, however.
Government expenditure on secondary education as a percentage of GDP (for seven out of nine countries with recent available data4 of
our working sample; see Section 3 for information on the paper’s data used) diminished in five countries (in the range of 17% for
Uruguay to 9% for the Dominican Rep.) and rose in two countries (5% in Mexico and 6% in Peru) for the 2019–2021 period. Yet, in
countries where there was an increment in the financial effort directed towards education (2019–2021 period), corresponded to
countries with relatively low total spending on education in 2021 (CEPAL, 2023).
On the whole, governments of the LAC region prioritised investment in social protection policies and health (with increments of
22%-13%) but, in contrast, investment in education5 went down (UNESCO, 2024b). Besides, it is important to emphasise that the LAC
region has leading challenges on educational access, low learning levels and deepened structural inequalities. For instance, according
to Berlanga et al. (2020) less than 50% of secondary schools students in the region achieve basic competences based on PISA 2018.
Weak learning achievement is compounded by poor ICT access. A report by CEPAL (2020) shows that only 70% of people in the region
have internet connection, and 46% of children (aged 5–12) live in households which do not have an internet connection. There are also
significant gaps on ICT access by socioeconomic status; students in the top wealth quartile are at least four times as likely (in comparison to the students from the bottom quartile) to have a laptop at home (Berlanga et al., 2020).
The impact of the pandemic in education has generated particular interest among researchers in the field. There are various studies
examining countries’ educational policies implemented during the pandemic. Chavez et al. (2022) conduct a systematic literature
review to analyse educational inequality, finding that this process is magnified when analysing access to the necessary devices to
maintain the teacher-student relationship virtually during the pandemic. Dias et al. (2022) examine the selected practices by public
and private schools in Brazil (Rio de Janeiro) during the pandemic and their impact on learning, finding clear differences based on
equality and quality by school type.
Mateus et al. (2022) also analyse the strategies implemented in this context in four LAC countries (i.e., Argentina, Chile, Ecuador
and Peru) and through focus groups they look into the impact of the pandemic on teaching practices at primary level. They find that
there is a need for relevant ICT training and strategies to address connectivity gaps, lack of adequate environments and work overload.
Avanesian et al. (2021) study the effectiveness of remote education policies implemented by the ministries of education in different
countries following schools’ closures. Their analysis reveals that, globally, the lack of availability of required technological elements
for remote education leaves around 30% of students without access. This estimated rate is lower for the LAC region, where it reaches
almost 10% on average.
Despite the global nature of school shutdowns brought by the pandemic, the impact on education has not been the same across
different regions, due to existing differences in school infrastructure and regional length of school closures. During school closures,

LAC is the region where schools remained closed for the longest period, after South and West Asia. LAC educational establishments

duration of closures in Central and Eastern Europe (North America and Western Europe) (UNESCO, 2024b). This prolonged school

and teachers, with younger children and students from more vulnerable groups experiencing the greatest losses (Asadullah et al., 2023;

From a similar perspective, Krüger et al. (2023), analysing the case of an Argentine city (Bahía Blanca), assert that the institutional
response to crises like the pandemic deteriorates when educational systems do not offer the same schooling conditions for everyone.
The impact of the educational system due to the COVID-19 pandemic varied depending on each educational institution. Moreover, as
teaching was transferred to students’ homes, inequalities in economic, educational, and cultural capital became more visible. Lichand
et al. (2022), when analyzing the case of the Brazilian city of São Paulo find that remote learning imposed by the pandemic not only
generated severe learning losses for students who continued their education when in-person classes resumed, but also increased the
risk of dropout. This study shows that worsening educational outcomes occurred during the transition period, therefore suggesting that
the greatest cost was a result of moving from one teaching system to another. Bracco et al. (2024) provide new evidence on the effects
of the pandemic on human capital formation in Latin America, establishing the significant long-term consequences in terms of income
levels and poverty.
Following up from the studies above, one would expect that learning surveys conducted after the pandemic would show worse
learning levels and deepening gaps. In most countries that participated in the latest PISA editions (2018 and 2022) a decrease in
average performance in mathematics and reading was observed, whilst performance in science remained relatively stable. For LAC
countries, to some extent, a similar pattern is observed, with a decline in mathematics achievement between 2018 and 2022 (UNESCO,
2024b). In a recent report, Ortiz et al. (2024) examine performance trends between 2018 and 2022 for the LAC region based on PISA
data, and the potential role that the COVID-19 crisis could have had on observed learning trends, and which countries and students
appeared to be most affected by the pandemic disruption. The authors find that: ”Between 2018 and 2022, performance either
continued to deteriorate from 2015 or lost momentum from an improving trajectory, especially in math. Six out of nine LAC countries
that participated in PISA in 2015, 2018, and 2022 showed a decline in math performance” (p. 31). Also, their analysis for the
2018–2022 period suggests that learning declined more in mathematics than in reading and science, with four countries experiencing a
deterioration in their average performance in mathematics, while just one country’s performance in reading declined.6 The report
argues that educational expansion in secondary education access for the last decade may be one of the reasons behind these negative
drops in achievement.
What is concerning, however, is the increasing population of students falling into the low performing group. As stated by Ortiz et al.
(2024) on the magnitude of the learning crisis experiencing the region: ”Of the 12 LAC countries that participated in the 2018 and 2022
assessments, a significantly greater number of students in Argentina, Brazil, Colombia, Costa Rica, Mexico, Peru, and Uruguay scored
below basic proficiency Level 2 in math, with the increase in the percentage of low performers between the two most recent rounds of
PISA ranging from 4 to 12 percentage points, or 6–20%” (p. 34). This could be explained by pervasive inequality of the region’s
education systems with a stable gap between poorer and richer students, with seven out of nine LAC countries showing rather constant
differences in math performance between richer and poorer students between 2018 and 2022 (Ortiz et al., 2024).
It is also pivotal to analyse whether there is a significant difference in the efficiency levels of educational establishments caused by
the pandemic and related schools’ closures given the lack of current empirical evidence. Studies on school efficiency after the
pandemic are practically nonexistent, due to the lack of availability of databases for large groups of countries with post-pandemic data.
Most school efficiency studies rely on data prior to the pandemic.
Focusing on LAC, we can mention the work done for Uruguay by Santín and Sicilia (2015), who, using PISA 2009 and 2012 data,
found a decrease in efficiency between this period in public schools. In the same country, Sicilia (2014) evaluates the efficiency of
public schools with PISA 2012 data, finding inefficiency levels close to 10%. Efficiency was also estimated for Uruguay with 2017 data
by Azar and Sicilia (2023) using a meta-frontier approach, and they find that efficiency could increase by around 20% in mathematics
and reading. Vera et al. (2011) estimates efficiency using DEA and Stochastic Frontiers Analysis (SFA) in primary schools for
Argentina, finding higher efficiency levels in private schools compared to public ones and in urban schools compared to rural ones.
Similarly for Chile, Mizala et al. (2002) uses DEA and SFA methodologies, finding higher efficiency levels in private schools compared
to public schools. Henriques et al. (2022) use DEA to study efficiency in 159 secondary schools in Ecuador. A notable result of this study
is that inefficient schools could improve their students’ results by around 12% without changing educational resources but by
strengthening school infrastructure.

for 10 countries in the region and relates this efficiency to the use and availability of ICT, finding a level of inefficiency around 25% for

3.1. DEA Specification
PISA datasets contain standard variables used in school efficiency studies; that is, outputs (i.e., learning scores) and inputs (i.e.,
family SES, school infrastructure).9 In the DEA specification, we consider variables which mirror an education production function
(Hanushek, 2020) where school inputs are transformed into outputs and, next, we select a set of environmental variables to explain
school efficiency levels (Worthington, 2001). The selection of inputs (I) and outputs (O) for the DEA analysis − average values at the
school level− is guided by previous studies on school efficiency (e.g., Agasisti and Zoido, 2018; Cordero et al., 2017; De Jorge and
Santín, 2010; De Witte and López-Torres, 2017; Delprato and Antequera, 2021a; Sutherland et al., 2009). Outputs are school average
values for students’ scores on math, reading and science, and following the standard practice we use the first (out of ten) plausible
values.10 We employ four types of educational inputs measuring various dimensions of school resources: school (physical and
educational) infrastructure,11 the student-teacher ratio (STR) and the school average of household wealth of students.
Summary statistics for outputs and inputs are shown in Panel A of Table 1, highlighting the challenges facing LAC education
systems. For instance, for the 2018 PISA wave, the average values for learning scores are between 383 and 401, around 20% lower in
comparison to the OECD’s average (which ranges between 487 and 489) (OECD, 2019a), and this gap has persisted because for the
2022 PISA wave the LAC average performance is 19% lower than the OECD’s average (OECD, 2023a). Also, the average performance
for the 5-years period of the two waves remained stable (only with minor increments for reading and science of around 7 points) and
the region has a lower stock of other school inputs such as a higher student-teacher ratio (STR) and larger students’ poverty in
comparison to OECD richer countries. Overall, when contrasting DEA’s analysis output and inputs across the two waves, the direction
of efficiency changes (RQ1) remain illusive since the slightly increment in performance is matched with an increment on the values of
input too (e.g., SES by 0.17, physical school infrastructure by 0.016, while educational school infrastructure and STR differences are
close to zero).

3.3. Inequality indicators
Given the rise of inequality of Latin American educational systems as a result of the pandemic (Benza and Kessler, 2022; Bracco
et al., 2024), it is relevant to assess whether the trade-off between equality and school efficiency holds (see, for instance: Agasisti and
Zoido, 2018) and, if so, whether the degree of this trade-off has worsened after the pandemic. With this aim, we consider various
inequality indicators as school determinants of efficiency which are shown in Table 2 (Panel A).
Specifically, we include the Gini coefficient of learning math and reading scores (measuring the extent of heterogeneity on the
distribution of learning scores within a school), the degree of inclusivity (i.e., proportion of students who obtain a score at level 1 or
below), the association of family SES with achievement (correlation coefficient and proportion of disadvantaged students in terms of
SES and IT stock levels who are high performers), as well as ratios of learning with SES and IT stock at home. A comparison of school
inequality for the 2018–2022 period (columns 1–5 and columns 3–7, Panel A of Table 2) shows that inclusion deteriorated post
pandemic as proportion of low achievers increased (= 3.4% for math), as well as the correlation of SES with learning (Δρ = 1.9 for
math and Δρ = 4.1 for reading).
3.4. COVID-19 Pandemic variables
A further aim of the paper is to check the role of students’ households settings and ICT resources (used for remote learning) on
efficiency using PISA 2022 data. It has been highlighted by recent literature the criticality of home resources on achievement and
educational engagement during the pandemic (Betthäuser et al., 2023; Moscoviz et al., 2022; Reimers, 2022), but less is know on its
impact on school efficiency. Therefore, this is the research gap we attempt to fill here. Also, we investigate the leverage of school
policies (in terms of students’ material provided, teachers communication and support, tracking learning) and the duration of schools’
closures on efficiency. We include this set of covariates in Panel B of Table 2.
Educational institutions of LAC launched various distance learning programs and provided content and learning materials to
maintain students pedagogically engaged during the pandemic (Álvarez et al., 2020; Ortiz et al., 2024). The efficacy of emergency
remote teaching implemented through online learning platforms and the positive effect it may have had on efficiency through
increasing achievement via fostering pedagogic engagement, however, requires students’ access to ICT and also a reliable internet
connection for a region where the digital divide in the use of educational technologies is quite vast (Abizanda et al., 2022). Also,
tracking students’ learning and teacher support suffered from significant barriers in the region because of the need of teacher ICT

4. Methodology
4.1. Estimation of school efficiency − DEA
We use DEA to compute efficiency scores for each school (for details, see: Johnes, 2004; Thanassoulis, 2001; Thanassoulis et al.,
2016). The underlying assumption of DEA is that each decision making unit (i.e., school) transforms inputs into outputs through a
production process yielding a measure of technical efficiency. This measure of technical efficiency is computed relative to the estimated frontier which represents the maximum output that each school can achieve given the available resources. We rely on the
concept of technical efficiency (Farrell, 1957), that is, we assess whether units are reaching the highest possible level of product with
the inputs used.

for the given data wave under different technologies. For PISA data waves, each school k (k = 1, …, K) is defined by a combination of

5.3. Impact of inequality on school efficiency
A core issue for Latin American resources-constrained education systems is to examine whether school technical efficiency can be
increased without hindering equality and, additionally, if there is change in this trade-off considering the educational disruption set off
by the COVID-19 pandemic. Estimates of Table 5 (columns 1 and 5) indicate that, overall, the trade-off of school efficiency and equality
does not hold.
In the case when inequality is measured by math scores (Panel A of Table 5), it can be clearly seen that the negative relationship of
the proportion of low performers (achieving level 1 or below) and the Gini coefficient (with larger values indicating more inequality on
achievement scores within a school) suggest that the efficiency of a school can be augmented by reducing within-school inequality.
Policies leading to a better and fair distribution of math achievement within a school can lead to higher efficiency particularly for the
2022 wave (as the estimate is of − 0.1617 compared to − 0.1296 for the 2018 wave). On the same vein, when inequalities are
measured by ratios of SES and ICT and correlation of family SES with achievement at the school level, they are only significant for the
post pandemic PISA wave. Interestingly, the higher the gap on math achievement between poorer and better off students (SES-low over
SES-high ratio), the lower the efficiency degree achieved by a given school as its coefficient estimated for the 2022 PISA wave is equal
to − 0.0289. In other words, narrowing the learning gap by SES origin of students can lead to higher efficiency.
What’s more, a finding which holds for both waves, is that efficiency is positively related to the school prevalence of high achievers
(level 2 or above) coming from deprived family environments (those living in households of lower wealth or with low IT resources at

5.3.1. Countries’ results for inequality on school efficiency

lack of educational materials and internet at schools, shortage of teachers for remote learning, lack of experience in remote education
delivery and weak coordination and support from educational stakeholders, are all barriers having negative impacts on efficiency.
6.2. Policy implications
Policymakers and education stakeholders of the region aiming to boost school efficiency, and as a consequence the sustainability of
the secondary education sector by enhancing a better linkage of resources-achievement, should focus on array of policies at the micro
and macro levels of education systems. We select three dimensions where policymakers can redirect resources to increase efficiency.
First, students’ PC availability at home − strongly influenced by households’ wealth (Berlanga et al., 2020)− is an obstacle for
efficiency (without considering the additional hurdle of the lack of connectivity in poor households; Lezama et al., 2023; Santos et al.,
2021). But this can be offset by governments’ higher investment of ICT across schools (given its positive linkage with efficiency),
particularly prioritising disadvantaged schools and closing gaps in access to digital devices and resources. Digital technologies can
bridge gaps in access to quality education regardless of a student’s geographical location or economic background (Ortiz et al., 2024).
Second, more investment in the public education sector is key to narrow the higher gap observed on the private-public school efficiency gap post-pandemic because financial constraints caused by the pandemic led to a shift of students moving private schools

moving into public schools creating a tension between existing public education offer and students’ demand (Álvarez et al., 2020).
Third, to reassign funds to boost pedagogic engagement of existing students in the system so that to prevent dropout with money
transfers and establishing a system to keep track on students on the base of cohorts dropout-risk demographic profiles to alleviate the

financial stress on families (Álvarez et al., 2020; Salas-Pilco et al., 2022).
Results presented in this paper open the way for future research. One the one hand, given the raise of private supply in the region
(Abizanda et al., 2022; Verger et al., 2018) and its links with segregation, the same analysis of our study can be carried by school type
(private and public schools) so that to pin down sub-system equilibriums across time in the LAC region from the point of view of
efficiency and its determinants and role of equity in either type of schools. On the other hand, the role of super efficient schools for the
2022 wave (where the output-input rationale is essentially distinct due to remote learning) should be carefully examined per country,
looking at the reasons behind this by location and school types.
6.3. Limitations
The paper’s reported estimates for the second stage regression on efficiency’s determinants represents associations rather than
causal impacts due to the standard limitation of PISA cross-section data on which the analysis is based. This implies that the estimates
reported should be considered with caution and cannot be interpreted in a causal sense per se. Having a panel data structure instead
would allow us to inspect learning growth yielding a more robust analysis of school efficiency based on flows and cumulative effects of
schools. Besides, it would be important to bring into the empirical analysis the concept of cost efficiency − alongside the technical
efficiency we address in the paper− though this would require some key information on school expenditure patterns so that to
determine whether pre and post-pandemic efficiency levels are achieved in a cost-effective and affordable manner. Another limitation
of the analysis is that schools’ compositional changes across time on their students populations driven by differential patterns on
dropout rates before and after the pandemic, may introduce some selection bias23 in the analysis weakening the validity of associations
found between students’ background as determinants of school efficiency.

Appendix F. Robustness analysis − outliers
The presence of outliers is a concern for the performance of DEA’s estimates. Khezrimotlagh (2015) describes different sources of
outliers within DEA analysis that can be grouped into the four categories: (i) inputs and outputs can be suffer from measurement error;
(ii) wrong selection of inputs and outputs; (iii) non-homogenous school units (or decision making units, DMUs) among observations;
and (iv) the existence of Near and Far Data, i.e., a DMU which in comparison to other DMU uses a much greater (bit less) value of input
and only produces a bit greater (much less) value of output.
Leading techniques to account for this issue is the order-m method proposed by Cazals et al. (2002) which draws on earlier research
by Wilson (1995)’s procedure to detect outliers and Simar (2003) which demonstrated how order m-estimations can identify outliers
by accounting for sensitivity of supper-efficient DMUs. A related technique to detect outliers is the α-order efficiency method of Aragon
et al. (2005) which has the advantage of being less time consuming because it does not need resampling as in the case of the Cazals’
approach. Essentially, the α-order approach chooses a percentile on the efficiency distribution based on conditional quantiles of the
distribution, rather than a maximum efficiency benchmark.
Here, we undertake some robustness checks to evaluate our results’ validity once the likelihood of outliers is taken into account. We
rely on Tauchmann (2012)’s implementation work in partial frontier efficiency analysis, following two steps. Firstly, we use the
α-order method for outliers detection which relies on the idea that the share of super-efficient DMUs (schools) should be decreasing
(Daraio and Simar, 2007); this technique looks into a discontinuity above which super-efficient DMUs are more likely to occur.
Secondly − and after finding the level of α where the discontinuity happens− we exclude those schools and re-estimate the DEA model
to contrast it to our previous estimates. Results for the first step are shown in Figure F1 and for the second step in Table F1.
Fig. F1 shows discontinuities happening for α values of 0.97 for PISA 2018 (outliers = 18%) and of 0.76 for PISA 2022 (outliers =
59%). When we re-estimate Eq. (4) for the DEA analysis using the new sample of non-outliers schools (2,085 and 969 for the 2018 and
2022 waves, correspondingly), leads to consistent findings as shown in Table F1. Results are aligned with those presented in Table 3
which are based on the full samples of schools. For the 2018 PISA wave, excluding outliers tends to over-estimate the country level of
efficiency with estimates being higher for all nine countries (ranging between 1.00% for Chile and 5.9% for Panama), whilst for the
2022 PISA wave over-estimation of efficiency happens in six out of the nine countries (and ranging from − 6.7% for Uruguay and 3.1%
for Mexico). The inclusion of outliers (namely: super-efficient schools) does not modify the direction and patterns of countries’ efficiency when compared to the earlier estimates (those shown in Table 3). This even holds for the 2022 wave with more than half of
schools classified as outliers with only two countries significantly changing their ranking when outliers are excluded (i.e., Mexico and
Uruguay). Still, due to the rate of outliers for the 2022 wave, estimates for this wave may be considered with some degree of caution.
