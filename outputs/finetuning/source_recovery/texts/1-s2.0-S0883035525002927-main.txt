Opportunity to learn (OTL), commonly conceptualised as content coverage, plays a pivotal role in
shaping student achievement and educational equity. However, the literature lacks comparative
analyses of different OTL modelling approaches using the same dataset, which is essential to
isolate the effects of model structure from variations caused by context, population, or assessment
design. This study addresses that gap using structural equation modeling to compare four widely
used analytical approaches of OTL—composite, domain-general, domain-specific, and latent variable
models—using Grade 4 mathematics and science data from the Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) 2015 and 2019 in Norway and Sweden.
Results show that model performance varied significantly across countries, subjects, and
assessment cycles. The composite approach provided stable but limited insights. The domaingeneral and domain-specific models offered more targeted but inconsistent findings. The
domain-specific model showed strong alignment between content coverage and achievement in
specific subdomains, especially in Norwegian mathematics and Swedish science. The latent
variable model yielded the most robust and consistent results across countries and subjects,
demonstrating its utility in capturing shared variance across domains and reducing measurement
error.
Mathematics showed more stable OTL–achievement relationships than science, and Norway
exhibited stronger model performance than Sweden, particularly in 2015. The findings highlight
how OTL is operationalised significantly affects explanatory power and interpretability. This
study offers empirical and methodological insights into OTL research, guiding researchers on
model selection. It also informs policymakers how different models may serve distinct purposes,
such as monitoring trends or designing targeted curriculum and instructional interventions.

1. Introduction
Opportunity to learn (OTL) is a cornerstone of educational effectiveness. It significantly influences student learning and academic
achievement (e.g., Schmidt et al., 2015). OTL encompasses the extent of students’ exposure to specific subject matter, ensuring they
have the necessary time and resources to master the curriculum content. OTL is also closely tied to educational equity, as disparities in
OTL can exacerbate achievement gaps among different student groups (Blömeke, 2024). This connection between OTL and equity

highlights the importance of understanding how OTL impacts student performance.
Despite widespread acknowledgment of its importance, the conceptualisation and measurement of OTL remain subjects of ongoing
debate and research. Previous studies have employed diverse approaches to measure OTL. Some have relied on simple metrics, such as
instructional time, while others have focused on more complex measures, such as content coverage, including what topics are taught
and how thoroughly they are addressed in the classrooms (Floden, 2002). Various models incorporating content coverage have been
developed, each contributing valuable insights. However, this diversity poses a critical challenge: Which OTL model is most appropriate for specific educational contexts and research objectives?
This question is particularly important given the significant gap in the current literature. Although several OTL models have been
extensively used in research (see, e.g., Scheerens, 2017), comprehensive comparative analyses that evaluate the relative strengths and
weaknesses of different conceptualisations are lacking. Even fewer studies have systematically compared them within the same context
and dataset. This gap is critical because different OTL models may capture distinct aspects of educational equity. For instance, a model
using a single OTL score to represent science as a whole might provide a broad overview of students’ learning opportunities but fail to
reveal specific achievement gaps within subdomains, such as biology, chemistry, or physics. Without such comparisons, stakeholders
lack clear guidance on which models are most effective in explaining variations in student performance across diverse educational
contexts.
To address this issue, our study aims to illustrate various OTL models and their relationships to student achievement. By examining
different OTL models side by side, we seek to identify which approaches are most effective in explaining student achievement. This
comparison will provide valuable insights for stakeholders in making informed decisions about which OTL models are best suited to
their needs.
Our research focuses on mathematics and science achievement in Sweden and Norway, using data from the Trends in International
Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) for Grade 4 students in 2015 and 2019. The selection of these two Nordic countries is
strategic, as they share similar economic and cultural backgrounds, have comparable population sizes, and implement comprehensive
school systems without academic selection at the compulsory level (see Blömeke, 2024). This similarity in educational contexts allows
for a more controlled comparison of OTL models while minimising the influence of confounding variables that might arise from
significant systemic differences.
Furthermore, the choice to analyse data from Grade 4 students serves an important methodological purpose. By focusing on
younger learners, we aim to mitigate potential self-selection bias in student grouping, which can become more pronounced in later
grades (Hanushek & Wößmann, 2006; van de Werfhorst, 2019). This approach can enhance the validity of our findings and provide a
clearer picture of how different OTL conceptualisations relate to student achievement in the early stages of formal education.
By identifying the strengths and weaknesses of these models, this study may contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the
relationship between OTL and student achievement. These insights could play a crucial role in shaping curriculum design, improving
instructional practices, and guiding educational policy decisions.
2. Background and theory
2.1. The conceptualisation of OTL as content coverage
The fundamental understanding of the OTL concept was articulated by Carroll (1963): “the learner will succeed in learning a given
task to the extent that [the learner] spends the amount of time that [the learner] needs to learn the task” (p. 725). Carroll’s model
emphasized time spent on mastering curriculum aspects as the core measure of OTL, presenting the idea that learning outcomes
depend on both the time allocated for instruction and the time students require to master the material (1963). This foundational view
of OTL has since evolved to encompass a wider range of factors, such as curriculum design, school environment, and peer influences
(see, e.g., Martinez Fernandez, 2005).
In the context of curriculum theory and the evaluation of educational outcomes, OTL is considered a measure of the enacted
curriculum—what actually happens in schools and classrooms—as opposed to the intended curriculum, which includes the educational
goals and content outlined in national or regional curriculum documents or by teachers’ planning goals (see e.g., Schmidt et al., 1997).
This distinction is fundamental for designing OTL measures used in large-scale assessments, including TIMSS and PISA (Programme for
International Student Assessment).
In this study, we focus on the conceptualisation of OTL as content coverage, a key concept integral in the development of international large-scale assessments (see e.g., McDonnell, 1995). Content coverage is an essential component of OTL, as it determines what
students are exposed to during their time in the classroom. This approach is based on the supposition that “students who are confronted
with relevant subject matter in the classroom should–ceteris paribus–perform better than students who were not given the opportunity
to learn the subject matter” (Eggen et al., 1987, p. 134). This principle underscores the importance of equitable access to curriculum
content as a determinant of student performance and highlights the need to systematically investigate how variations in content
coverage shape student outcomes across educational contexts.
Empirical studies have shown that OTL, particularly when conceptualised as content coverage, is a strong predictor of academic
achievement (see, e.g., Marzano, 2003; Scheerens, 2017; Scheerens et al., 2007). A recent study by Kristensen and Rolfe (2024)
confirms high correlations between enacted curriculum and student achievement in mathematics and science across Nordic countries.
Given the significant impact of OTL on student achievement, evaluating different approaches to modelling content coverage is
essential for accurately measuring educational opportunities.

2.2. Approaches in modelling the relations between OTL and student achievement
In exploring how OTL relates to student achievement, we identified four distinct approaches to modelling OTL and its relation to
student achievement in the literature:
1. Composite approach: Creating an average score from multiple OTL indices to predict overall achievement (e.g., Luyten, 2017;
Rolfe et al., 2021);
2. Domain-general approach: Using separate OTL domain scores to predict overall achievement (e.g., Aguirre-Muñoz & Boscardin,
2008; Claessens et al., 2012; Engel et al., 2013);
3. Domain-specific approach: Relating domain-specific OTL scores to corresponding scores for the achievement domains, such as
the Biology domain for science and Data for mathematics (e.g., Kristensen & Rolfe, 2024); and
4. Latent variable approach: Using a latent OTL factor based on domain-specific OTL scores to predict overall achievement (e.g.,
Rolfe et al., 2022; Yang Hansen & Strietholt, 2018)
The composite approach (Fig. 1) involves creating an average of multiple OTL indices and using this combined score to predict
overall achievement (e.g., Luyten, 2017; Rolfe et al., 2021). Luyten’s (2017) study provides a comprehensive analysis using data from
TIMSS 2011 (Grades 4 and 8) and PISA 2012, examining 22 countries that participated in both assessments. For TIMSS data, Luyten
averaged the OTL indices from the TIMSS database across various domains within mathematics and science. The PISA analysis utilised
a single index from the PISA database to measure student experience with formal mathematics. When modelling the OTL-achievement
relationship, student socioeconomic background and content coverage in mathematics and science were regressed on each subject’s
achievement scores separately for each data cycle.
The domain-general approach (Fig. 2) involves creating separate OTL domain scores and relating these scores to a single overall
achievement score (e.g., Aguirre-Muñoz & Boscardin, 2008; Claessens et al., 2012; Engel et al., 2013). This approach is most commonly
used for analysing data from outside the international large-scale assessments, such as those from school district level studies (e.g.,
Aguirre-Muñoz & Boscardin, 2008) and nationally representative longitudinal research (e.g., Claessens et al., 2012; Engel et al., 2013).
Aguirre-Muñoz & Boscardin (2008) conducted research using data from English language proficiency with 1038 grade 6 students
and 27 teachers across seven Southern California schools. Their study measured OTL through teacher surveys covering multiple aspects: teaching experience, content expertise, content coverage, classroom processes, assessment practices, and classroom resources.
The researchers divided content coverage into writing and literacy domains, based on “content coverage questions [which] addressed
the degree to which key assessment content was covered throughout the course of the school year” (Aguirre-Muñoz & Boscardin, 2008,
p.191). This study showed that both literary analysis coverage and writing coverage had significant positive effects on student performance in curriculum-embedded assessments.
Furthermore, two significant studies from Claessens et al. (2012) and Engel et al. (2013) examined nationally representative
longitudinal data from the U.S. Early Childhood Longitudinal Studies – Kindergarten (ECLS-K) to evaluate student progress in
kindergarten. Claessens et al. (2012) explored whether the academic advantages gained from preschool attendance continued to
benefit students during their kindergarten year in both reading and mathematics. They constructed four content coverage measures
from teacher questionnaires: basic mathematics (e.g., counting out loud, recognizing and naming generic shapes, ordering objects),
advanced mathematics (e.g., place value, reading two-digit numbers, adding single-digit numbers), basic reading (e.g., alphabet and
letter recognition, working on names of letters, practicing writing the letters, and writing one’s own name), and advanced reading (e.
g., matching letters to sounds, common prepositions, and using context cues for comprehension) (Claessens et al., 2012, pp. 411-412).
Using entry tests in reading and mathematics to control for prior student knowledge, they found that higher levels of exposure to
advanced content could both maintain preschool gains and compensate for lack of preschool in both reading and mathematics as
opposed to exposure to basic content.
Engel et al. (2013) focused on potential misalignment between instructional content and student achievement in kindergarten
mathematics. They modelled the content areas within the kindergarten curriculum by creating four content coverage measures with
increasing complexity: basic counting and shapes, patterns and measurement, place value and currency, and addition and subtraction
(p.160). The study confirmed that increased teaching time devoted to more advanced content correlated positively with overall
mathematics performance.
The domain-specific approach (Fig. 3) relates OTL domain scores directly to corresponding achievement domain scores. Kristensen
and Rolfe (2024) exemplify this approach in their analysis of three waves of TIMSS Grade 4 data from Nordic nations (Denmark,
Finland, Norway, and Sweden). They divided mathematics content coverage into the domains of Number, Geometry, and Data, and
science content coverage into the domains of Life, Physical, and Earth Sciences. These content coverage scales were derived from
teacher questionnaire data. They regressed the domain-specific content coverage scales, along with the number of books at home (used
as a proxy for student SES), on the corresponding domain-specific achievement scores for mathematics and science. Their analysis
identified several significant positive relationships between individual content coverage scores and achievement scores. However, no

Finally, the latent variable approach (Fig. 4) models the OTL-achievement relationship by constructing a single latent factor representing content coverage, which is then regressed on overall student achievement. This conceptualisation has previously been
applied to both PISA (Yang Hansen & Strietholt, 2018) and TIMSS data (Rolfe et al., 2022). In Yang Hansen and Strietholt’s (2018)
work, content coverage was conceptualised as a latent factor indicated by three components: two scales representing content coverage
in Algebra and Geometry, respectively, and a third component representing the frequency with which students encountered formal
problems in their mathematics lessons (p. 647). This latent factor positively predicted achievement across PISA participating countries,
and in some countries (i.e., Singapore, Australia, Liechtenstein, Austria, the Netherlands and Germany), it exhibited high indirect
effects by mediating the relationship between students’ SES and achievement.
Using TIMSS mathematics data Grade 8 in Sweden, Rolfe et al. (2022) created a latent variable of content coverage based on the
scales of Number, Algebra, Geometry and Data domains. They then modelled the relationship between the latent variable and
achievement in conjunction with a number of teacher behaviour factors. The latent variable of content coverage was not a significant
predictor of achievement. However, when the students were divided into groups taught by specialist and non-specialist mathematics
teachers, the latent factor became a significant predictor of outcomes for students taught by non-specialist teachers (Rolfe et al., 2022).
In addition to the above outlined variations in conceptualising the OTL construct for inclusion in statistical analyses, we found two
broad modelling approaches used in the highlighted studies. The majority of studies included in our review used structural equation
modelling techniques such as two-level modelling or confirmatory factor analysis (see Luyten, 2017; Kristensen & Rolfe, 2024; Rolfe
et al., 2021; Rolfe et al., 2022; Yang Hansen & Strietholt, 2018). A smaller number, notably those utilising the domain-general
approach to predict overall achievement from separate OTL domain scores, employed regression techniques in their analyses (see
Aguirre-Muñoz & Boscardin, 2008; Claessens et al., 2012; Engel et al., 2013).
Taken together, the various approaches to modelling the relationship between OTL and student achievement underscore the
complexity and multifaceted nature of this relationship. Each approach provides unique insights; however, their comparability is
limited due to differences in datasets and contexts across the studies. This highlights the need to evaluate these approaches using the
same dataset to gain a more consistent and comprehensive understanding of how content coverage relates to student outcomes. Such a
comparison would allow researchers to identify the strengths and limitations of each approach and determine their relative effectiveness in relation to student achievement.

3. The present study
While prior research has used a variety of approaches to OTL as content coverage, few studies have directly compared these approaches using the same dataset and educational context. This limits our understanding of how different operationalisations of OTL
relate to student achievement and which modelling strategies are best suited for educational research and policy.
Consequently, this study addresses that gap by comparing four widely used modelling approaches to OTL—(1) composite, (2)
domain-general, (3) domain-specific, and (4) latent factor models—using data from the TIMSS 2015 and 2019 cycles. We focus on
Grade 4 students in Sweden and Norway and analyse data from both mathematics and science.
The following research questions (RQs) are used to guide the study:
1. How do different approaches to modelling OTL as content coverage compare in explaining student achievement in mathematics and science?
2. To what extent does the explanatory power of these OTL models vary across subjects (mathematics and science), countries (Norway and
Sweden), and assessment cycles (TIMSS 2015 and 2019)?
In the next sections, we describe the methods used to operationalise and compare the four OTL models across subjects, countries,
and TIMSS cycles. We then present the results of each modelling approach and discuss the findings in light of their strengths (e.g.,
precision, generalisability), limitations (e.g., oversimplification, complexity), and suitability for different research and policy contexts.
This discussion provides practical guidance on when and how each model may be most appropriately applied in future studies or
educational monitoring efforts.
4. Methods
4.1. Data
This study draws on data from the 2015 and 2019 cycles of TIMSS for Norway and Sweden, with an emphasis on Grade 4
mathematics. In 2015, Norway administered the TIMSS Grade 4 survey to both 4th and 5th graders. However, in TIMSS 2019, the
survey was only administered to 5th graders. The shift in the Norwegian sample was implemented to enhance the comparability
between Norway and other Nordic countries (Bergem et al., 2016; Kavli, 2018).
4.2. Variables
The variables used in this study build on the work of Kristensen and Rolfe (2024), who analysed responses from curriculum and
teacher questionnaire in TIMSS 2015 and 2019. Their study examined the variation between the prescribed and implemented curriculum in the Nordic region, including Norway and Sweden. Based on their findings, the data used in this study are judged to broadly
reflect the expected curriculum in both countries. The source variables used in this paper are presented in Appendix A.
4.2.1. Achievement
Student achievement in mathematics and science, including sub-domains—Number, Geometry, and Data in mathematics; Earth
Science, Physical Science, and Life Science in science—is measured through five plausible values per achievement score in the TIMSS
data sets (see e.g., Martin et al., 2016). Descriptive statistics for all achievement variables are presented in Appendix B.
4.2.2. Content coverage
Content coverage is measured through the teacher questionnaire. Teachers were asked when a number of topics within each
subdomain of mathematics and science were taught to their class (see Table 1 for the number of topics per subdomain in each cycle).
Response options were: “Mostly taught before this year”, “Mostly taught this year”, and “Not yet taught or just introduced”. The TIMSS
datasets include derived variables indicating the percentage of students taught topics within each of the sub-domains in mathematics
and science. These were estimated by identifying students whose teachers responded either “Mostly taught before this year” or “Mostly
taught this year” for each topic. The percentages were then averaged across all topics within each subdomain. If a teacher had three or

4.2.3. Socioeconomic status (SES)
The indicator of SES used in this study is the number of books in the home. This measure is commonly used as a proxy of SES in
educational research (e.g., Akiba et al., 2007; Ammermueller & Pischke, 2009) and is particularly prevalent in studies involving
children and young people (Strietholt et al., 2019). In the present study, the number of books is measured using a five-point scale,
where 0 = “None to 10 books at home”, 1 = “Enough to fill one shelf (11-25 books)”, 2 = “Enough to fill one bookcase (26-100 books)”, 3 =
“Enough to fill two bookcases (101-200 books)”, and 4 = “More than 200 books at home”. Descriptive statistics for SES are presented in
Appendix B.
4.3. Analysis strategy
In line with many previous works in the literature (e.g., Luyten, 2017; Kristensen & Rolfe, 2024; Rolfe et al., 2021; Rolfe et al.,
2022; Yang Hansen & Strietholt, 2018), the analyses presented in this study utilise Structural equation modelling (SEM). The SEM
framework allows for the investigation of relationships between latent and manifest variables using regression analysis by specifying
paths between dependent and independent variables (Hox & Bechger, 2007). It accounts for measurement error, improving the accuracy of parameter estimates, and is well suited for modelling relationships across multiple levels of data (Hoyle, 2012). These
features make SEM an appropriate framework for comparing alternative model specifications in this study.
The data were prepared in SPSS v29 and analysed with Mplus v8.1 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998-2017). TIMSS achievement scores are
provided as sets of five plausible values, which enable estimation of student ability within the multiple test booklet framework used in
the assessment. Accordingly, we used the TYPE=IMPUTATION syntax within Mplus to incorporate all five plausible values for each of
the achievement scores tested in these analyses.
Given the hierarchical structure of the data (students nested within classrooms), we applied two-level modelling. At the student
level, we used student-level weight, calculated as the product of student response adjustment and the student weight factor. At the
classroom level, we used school and classroom-level weights based on the combinations of school and classroom response adjustments
and weight factors.
Separate models were run for each country, subject, and assessment cycle, resulting in a total of 32 models across the four
modelling approaches. All models were specified as a two-level model estimated using the maximum likelihood estimator with robust
standard errors (MLR). SES was included as a control variable at the student level to account for the well-established effect of socioeconomic background on academic achievement (e.g., Sirin, 2005; Kjeldsen et al., 2024). Achievement was measured at the student
level but subsequently aggregated to the classroom level for analysis. Since content coverage was derived from the teacher questionnaire, it was specified only at the classroom (between) level.
4.4. Four models tested
In line with the four approaches identified from the literature, we run four series of models in which student SES predicts
achievement at student and classroom levels:
1. Composite approach: A computed OTL score (mean of subdomain scales) predicts overall achievement (Fig. 5a);
2. Domain-general approach: Domain-specific content coverage scores predict overall achievement (Fig. 5b);
3. Domain-specific approach: Domain-specific content coverage scores predict achievement in the corresponding domain (Fig. 5c);
and
4. Latent variable approach: Domain-specific content coverage scores load onto a latent OTL factor, which in turn predicts overall
achievement (Fig. 5d).
To assess model performance, we considered (1) the presence and strength of statistically significant OTL–achievement coefficients, (2) the consistency of findings across countries, subjects, and TIMSS cycles, and (3) the proportion of variance in
achievement explained at the classroom level or R². This multifaceted assessment allows us to compare not only predictive power but
also the stability and interpretability of each model across contexts. In doing so, we tried to capture both the immediate strength of the
statistical relationships and their broader relevance. The approach also highlights important trade-offs: some models may provide
stronger predictions in specific contexts but lack generalisability, while others may offer more stable patterns across subjects and
countries at the expense of domain-level detail.
5. Results
5.1. RQ1: How do different approaches to modelling OTL as content coverage compare in explaining student achievement in mathematics
and science?
To evaluate strategies for operationalising OTL expressed as content coverage, we analysed four model structures with both
mathematics and science data from the Norwegian and Swedish Grade 4 samples of TIMSS 2015 and 2019 (32 models in total). In all
models, SES predicted achievement at the individual level with a standardised coefficient of 0.2-0.3 (see Appendix C, tables C1, C2, C3,
C4). At the classroom level, SES also significantly predicted achievement, with stronger associations observed in Sweden than in
Norway.

In the following section, findings from each of the four modelling approaches are presented, focusing on the classroom-level
relationship between OTL and student achievement. Significant findings are summarised in Table 2.
5.1.1. Model 1. Composite approach
In this first approach, the mean of the three content coverage subdomain scores was used to predict overall achievement in
mathematics and science, separately. As shown in Table C1, significant relationships were observed only for mathematics in Norway,
in both 2015 and 2019. The predictive strength of content coverage was slightly weaker in 2019 (0.218) than in 2015 (0.321). No
significant associations were found for science in Norway or for either subject in Sweden.
5.1.2. Model 2. Domain-general approach
This approach used individual subdomain coverage scores to predict overall achievement in mathematics and science, respectively.
The results indicated poor predictive power of single content coverage scales (Table C2). Significant findings were observed in two
cases: in the 2015 Norwegian sample, content coverage in Data topics predicted mathematics achievement (0.309), and in the 2019
Swedish sample, content coverage in Geometry and Physical Science weakly predicted overall achievement in mathematics (0.175)
and science (0.225).
5.1.3. Model 3. Domain-specific approach
This model tested whether each content coverage subdomain predicted achievement in the corresponding domain (Table C3). For
Norway 2015, content coverage in Data (0.306), Geometry (0.274), and Number (0.328) significantly predicted achievement in their
respective domains. In 2019, only Number coverage significantly predicted Number achievement (0.264). No significant relationships
were found in science for Norway. In Sweden, the 2015 sample showed no significant results. However, in 2019, Geometry coverage
predicted achievement in Geometry (0.185), and Physical Science coverage predicted achievement in both Physical Science (0.188)
and Earth Science (0.198).
5.1.4. Model 4. Latent variable approach
The final modelling approach involved specifying a latent OTL factor, indicated by the subdomain-specific content coverage scores,
to predict an overall achievement score. As summarised in Table C4, this approach yielded the highest number of significant models. In
four of the eight subject-by-country-by-TIMSS cycle combinations, OTL significantly predicted achievement at the classroom level.
Norway 2015 was the only mathematics dataset in which OTL was not a significant predictor of achievement, while Sweden 2019 was
the only case where OTL significantly predicted science achievement.

6. Discussion and implications
This study investigates how OTL relates to student achievement by comparing various OTL models and examining their relationships to mathematics and science achievement across different countries and TIMSS cycles. It identifies which approaches are
most effective in explaining student outcomes in order to provide valuable insights for researchers, policymakers, and practitioners in
making informed decisions about which modelling strategies are best suited to their needs. By identifying the strengths and weaknesses
of these models, this study may contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between OTL and student achievement. The intention is not to provide definitive or prescriptive guidance on a universally “best” model, but rather to present a
methodological illustration of how different operationalisations of OTL can yield varying results, even in a relatively controlled
context. Focusing on two similar Nordic countries was a deliberate choice to limit systemic confounds and isolate the influence of
model choice on findings.
The goal of this study was therefore to show the methodological consequences of selecting different OTL modelling approaches
under similar conditions. The findings reveal that the relationship between OTL and student achievement is highly context-dependent,
varying across models, subjects, countries, and assessment cycles. Some modelling approaches, particularly when applied to mathematics in Norway, demonstrated strong and stable links between OTL and achievement (Table 2). In contrast, other contexts, such as
science achievement or the Swedish data, yielded weaker or more fragmented relationships depending on the model used (see e.g.,
Table 2). Overall, no single modelling approach consistently outperformed the others across all contexts, highlighting that model
effectiveness depends on the specific subject area, national setting, and assessment cycle.
Across countries, Norway consistently showed stronger and more stable associations between content coverage and mathematics
achievement, especially in 2015 (Table 2). Sweden, by contrast, displayed more significant findings in science achievement in 2019.
These differences likely stem from variations in curriculum structure, national educational priorities, and reforms over time. Mathematics curricula are typically more structured, cumulative, and standardised across countries (Schmidt et al., 2002; Schmidt et al.,
1997), which may explain the stronger and more consistent predictive relationships for mathematics. In comparison, primary science
curricula are often broader, interdisciplinary, and more responsive to local contexts (Schmidt et al., 1997), making consistent links
between OTL and achievement more difficult to capture.
In addition to the effects of OTL on student achievement, SES was consistently significant at both the student and classroom levels
across all models, subjects, countries, and cycles (Tables C1-C4). SES emerged as a stable and powerful predictor of achievement,
reinforcing longstanding evidence that social background remains a key driver of educational outcomes (e.g. Sirin, 2005). Including

Second, broader structural factors such as SES continue to exert a strong and pervasive influence on achievement, which must be

6.1. Comparison of different OTL models in predicting student achievement
The comparison of different OTL models highlights that model selection must be driven by the specific goals of the research or
policy needs. Each model offers distinct advantages and trade-offs depending on the level of generality, specificity, and stability
required (Table 4).
The Composite Approach (Model 1) averages OTL indices across all domains to predict overall achievement. Its main strength lies
in its simplicity and intuitive appeal, offering a broad overview of the general effect of OTL on achievement. By summarising multiple
indicators of OTL into a single measure, it captures overall variability and provides a relatively stable predictor by reducing noise from
fluctuations across specific domains. This stability is particularly evident in mathematics achievement in Norway in 2015 and 2019
(Table C1). However, its simplicity comes at a cost. Averaging OTL across domains risks oversimplifying variation and masking the
differential effects of specific content areas (McNeish & Wolf, 2020). All OTL indices are treated as equally important, which might not
reflect their actual impact on achievement.
Given these strengths and limitations, the composite approach is best suited for identifying general trends in OTL and achievement
across subjects, cycles, and educational systems or countries. It is also valuable for informing broad education policy decisions where
detailed domain-specific diagnosis is not required. For instance, Rolfe et al. (2021) used the composite approach to examine whether
differences in content coverage contributed to the widening socio-economic gaps in mathematics outcomes across four TIMSS cycles in
78 countries.
However, for studies focused on identifying which particular content areas most strongly influence achievement, or for informing
targeted instructional interventions and curriculum development, the composite approach may lack the precision needed. By averaging across domains, it reduces the ability to detect specific effects and may overlook specific areas like Algebra or Physics, where
focused improvements in content coverage could yield the greatest educational gains.
The Domain-General Approach (Model 2) uses separate OTL domain scores to predict overall achievement. This model captures
variability by examining the individual effects of different content areas on overall student achievement.
A key strength of this approach is its diagnostic insight: it allows researchers and policymakers to identify which content areas, such
as Geometry or Biology, have the strongest effect on student overall achievement. Additionally, the domain-general model offers the
potential to explore cross-domain interactions that may shape broader patterns of achievement.
However, this approach introduces additional complexity. Domain scores may overlap or interact in unpredictable ways, increasing
the risk of multicollinearity, which complicates both model estimation and interpretation. There is also a potential mismatch between
some OTL domains and achievement measures, which can weaken the model’s predictive strength. While the domain-general model
provides valuable diagnostic insights, its results may be less generalisable across educational systems or assessment cycles. For
example, in this study, the Data domain was related to mathematics achievement only in Norway and only in 2015. This finding differs
from previous studies using this approach, which have primarily relied on local or nationally representative longitudinal data (e.g.,
Aguirre-Muñoz & Boscardin, 2008; Claessens et al., 2012; Engel et al., 2013) rather than international cross-sectional datasets like
TIMSS.

The Domain-Specific Approach (Model 3) links OTL scores directly to their corresponding achievement domains (e.g., content
coverage in Data to student achievement in Data domain). This model offers high specificity and strong theoretical alignment,
reflecting the intuitive connection between the content taught and the content assessed. It provides clear and precise insights into
domain-level strengths and weaknesses and allows for the exploration of potential cross-domain interactions that could influence
overall achievement.
Despite its strengths, the domain-specific approach also has notable limitations. Its findings tend to be highly context-specific,
which limits their generalisability across countries or educational systems. Furthermore, comprehensive data across all domains
may not always be available, and lower within-level variance explained can reduce the model’s overall predictive power. For example,
teachers’ estimates of content coverage within a domain may reflect uniform inclusion or exclusion of certain topics across a country,
thereby limiting variability across classrooms (see Kristensen & Rolfe, 2024).
This approach is particularly well-suited for diagnosing specific content gaps and for informing targeted instructional or curriculum
interventions. For instance, Kristensen and Rolfe (2024) found no consistent pattern of predictive relationships between domain
coverage and domain-specific outcomes across TIMSS cycles in the Nordic region (Denmark, Finland, Norway, and Sweden), despite
the implementation of curriculum reforms in all countries except Norway. Domain-specific findings such as these can help policymakers evaluate subject-specific reforms—such as those targeting mathematics and science education—and assist teachers in aligning
instruction more closely with student needs in particular topics or content areas.
Finally, the Latent Variable Approach (Model 4) creates a latent OTL factor from domain scores to predict overall achievement.
This model captures the underlying construct of OTL by accounting for shared variance across domains, substantially reducing
measurement error associated with individual OTL indicators (Brown, 2015). It addresses a key limitation of simpler models, such as
the Composite Approach, by recognising that domains do not contribute equally to achievement and by highlighting which areas exert
stronger effects.
This study shows that, compared to other models, the latent variable model was the most robust and consistent across different
countries, subjects, and assessment cycles (see Table C4). However, it relies on high-quality, multidimensional OTL data for reliable
estimation and carries a risk of overfitting, particularly when sample sizes are small or when domains are highly specific.
This approach is best suited for researchers aiming to explore both general and domain-specific effects of OTL using multilevel or
complex study designs. For instance, Rolfe et al. (2022) used this approach to examine how OTL, teacher qualifications, and
instructional behaviour were related to between-class equity gaps in Swedish schools. Policymakers may also find latent variable
models useful for understanding the broader impacts of OTL while still accounting for the unique contributions of specific content
areas, thereby informing more nuanced and balanced policy decisions.
7. Limitations and further research
While this study provides meaningful contributions to understanding how different models of OTL relate to student achievement, it
also has several limitations that point to important areas for future research.
First, the measurement of OTL in this study is limited to teacher-reported or curriculum-based indicators of content coverage. While
this approach is common in large-scale assessments, it does not capture how content is taught, the depth of instruction, or the extent to
which students engage with the material. Future research should incorporate more comprehensive OTL measures, such as classroom
observation, teaching quality, or student-reported learning experiences. Mixed-methods approaches that combine quantitative data
with qualitative insights could help validate and enrich the interpretation of OTL constructs.
Second, model-specific constraints must be considered. Although the latent variable model offered the most consistent and robust
results across countries and cycles, it requires large sample sizes and high-quality multidimensional data. Future studies could replicate
these modelling strategies using other ILSAs that allow within-student and between-subject comparisons. It would also be valuable to
apply these models to older students to examine whether the relationships between OTL and achievement differ by age or educational
stage. Such directions are particularly important for testing measurement stability, addressing multicollinearity issues, and capturing
more nuanced relationships between OTL and student outcomes.
Third, the alignment between the OTL indicators and the achievement outcomes may be inconsistent, particularly in science. TIMSS
science assessments may not always reflect what is emphasised in national curricula, especially in systems where science is taught in an
integrated or thematic fashion. The resulting mismatch could influence the observed relationship between OTL and achievement. To
improve construct alignment, future studies should undertake curriculum mapping and textbook analysis to determine how closely test
frameworks mirror national instructional goals. Moreover, expanding the analysis to include countries with more decentralised systems or diverse curricular traditions—particularly those with alternative conceptions of science content (see Schmidt et al., 1997)—
could shed light on how curricular divergence shapes the functioning of OTL models.
Fourth, the use of cross-sectional data from TIMSS limits the scope of this investigation in two respects: it prevents the inclusion of
prior-achievement measures that are consistently comparable across countries, and it precludes the identification of causal relationships between OTL and achievement. Further research applying the modelling strategies tested here to a longitudinal dataset
might yield a deeper insight into the mechanisms through which OTL affects student achievement. A key challenge to extending this
work in a multi-country context is the scarcity of internationally comparable longitudinal datasets that would make such analyses

opportunity. Together, these directions suggest a broader research agenda: one that not only tests OTL models across subjects, age
groups, and countries but also enriches the measurement and theoretical framing of OTL itself. Doing so will support more contextsensitive and actionable insights into how education systems can ensure that all students are equipped to succeed and reach their
