Young people’s civic engagement 
in Italy: evidence on grade 8 students 
from the international civic and citizenship 
education study (ICCS) 2016
Valeria Damiani1*   , Elisa Caponera2, Bruno Losito3, Laura Palmerio2 and Gabriella Agrusti1 
Abstract 
Civic engagement represents a key aspect of a democratic society and is itself a multi-
dimensional construct. Research has shown that the promotion of civic engagement 
is an important component of civic and citizenship education intended in a broad 
sense, pertaining to formal, non-formal and informal learning situations. Within 
a national scenario characterised by increasing levels of general institutional disaffec-
tion and a lack of electoral and political involvement, the recent reform in the area 
of civic and citizenship education (Law 92/2019) has led to lively debates on the sta-
tus of its implementation in Italian schools and recent research at the national level 
has highlighted the lack of activities to promote students’ engagement. This study 
further investigates the ICCS 2016 findings on civic engagement of Italian students 
with two objectives: to identify possible groups of Italian eighth-grade students 
on the basis of their level of civic engagement and to examine the associations 
among these groups and some key factors reflecting school contexts and students’ 
attitudes and behaviours. To reach these goals, the data analyses encompassed a latent 
profile analysis and a logistic regression model. The latent profile analysis allowed 
the identification of three groups of Italian students: disengaged, moderately engaged 
and engaged students. The results of the regression model showed that engaged 
students were characterised by a higher level of citizenship self-efficacy. These findings 
confirm those of previous research on the key role of internal self-efficacy in promoting 
students’ civic engagement.
Keywords:  Civic and citizenship education, ICCS 2016, Italy, Self-efficacy
Introduction
Students’ civic engagement, and the role of civic and citizenship education (CCE) con-
cerning its development in schools, is an area that remains mostly unexplored in Italy, 
given that most studies on Italian youth political and civic engagement are mainly 
focused on informal and non-formal learning contexts. Despite educational guidelines 
and recommendations having recognised the relevance of students’ active participa-
tion in school life and in extracurricular activities to CCE (Eurydice, 2017), efforts to

promote it in schools appear extremely fragmented: while some schools foster different 
forms of student engagement (comprising within-school and out-of-school activities), 
others do not consider this a priority. School actions related to students’ engagement 
are generally considered inadequate in Italy, where only a few students are involved in 
promoting initiatives and taking part in decision-making processes across all school 
levels (Bombardelli & Codato, 2017). It has to be noted that schools’ initiatives may 
vary in relation to their autonomy and to the curricular choices made at an individual 
school level (Council of Europe, 2004; Damiani, 2016). In spite of support by educational 
authorities, the promotion of students’ civic engagement and CCE in general lacks con-
crete policies for its implementation (in terms of curriculum development, assessment, 
teachers’ training), also lacking efficient monitoring and data from standardised assess-
ments or information about its implementation in general (Losito, 2009; Palmerio et al., 
2021). The Italian context shares a condition with other educational systems, where dec-
larations of principle on CCE are often misaligned with the educational practice and its 
varied approaches for delivery make it difficult to monitor and gather data about this 
learning area (Hamilton & Parsi, 2022).
In light of the above, the IEA International Civic and Citizenship Study (ICCS) rep-
resents a unique source of national-level data on CCE, its main components including, 
among others, the measurement of Italian students’ civic engagement inside and outside 
school and expected participation in the future.
In this study, we analysed the ICCS 2016 findings with the aim of investigating the 
extent of civic engagement among Italian eighth-grade students, as well as school- and 
individual-level factors that are associated with it. Given the huge diversity across the 
different educational systems that took part in the ICCS, it is relevant to deeply analyse 
and further review findings within a specific national context.
Most academic research so far has focused on specific aspects of students’ civic 
engagement and their associations with a wide range of variables, but in this study we 
explore civic engagement as a multidimensional construct whose components are 
related to students’ engagement within and outside of school and their expectations of 
becoming involved in electoral and political participation in the future. Moreover, we 
analyse those elements in relation to their attitudes and behaviours that may influence 
students’ civic engagement in Italy.
The first sections of this paper focus on a review of the literature on students’ civic 
engagement, its multidimensional characteristics, the extensive array of research con-
cerning individual aspects of civic engagement, and their relations with selected factors. 
Furthermore, we review the relevance of those factors in the field of CCE with specific 
consideration of the Italian context. We then describe the methodology, the analysis and 
the results. In the last section, we discuss some implications for CCE in the Italian edu-
cational system and suggest possible directions for future research.
Background
Civic engagement represents a key aspect of a democratic society and has been the focus 
of extensive research in various fields, including political socialisation, psychology (espe-
cially in the area of social psychology and developmental psychology) and CCE (Amnå, 
2012).

There is still no consensus about the definition of the term ‘civic engagement’. Several 
studies on the topic have highlighted the multidimensionality of a construct that is char-
acterised by unclear differences between social, civic and political dimensions (Berger, 
2009). According to Barrett and Brunton-Smith (2014), the term ‘engagement’ must be 
considered as conceptually distinct from the term ‘participation’. While the former is 
related to psychological features (i.e. having interest, attitudes, opinions and knowledge 
on civic and political issues), the latter is mainly related to behaviours.
Adler and Goggin (2005) distinguished between narrower definitions of the term ‘civic 
engagement’ that are mostly related to activities carried out in a specific context (e.g. 
community service, collective action, political involvement, social change) and broader 
definitions that link civic engagement to the concept of social capital (Putnam, 2000). 
In highlighting the different dimensions and elements that constitute civic engagement, 
Adler and Goggin identified a wide range of activities that can be related to the term as 
falling along a continuum that encompasses formal and collective actions as well as indi-
vidual and informal activities that can be carried out in the community or in the political 
sphere (ranging from engaging in political discussion with friends to running for public 
office).
Ekman and Amnå (2012) suggested that civic engagement can be expressed through 
different types of behaviour in varying contexts, this linking engagement with partici-
pation. They identified a typology of different forms of engagement, including non-
participation, civil participation and political participation. This typology considers the 
dimensions of disengagement and latent and manifest forms of political participation, as 
well as individual and collective forms of engagement.
Research has also identified several factors that can be associated with diverse patterns 
of engagement and participation, from the ones pertaining to the macro level (i.e. the 
electoral system, population features such as the size of the electorate, the structure of 
political institutions, and the characteristics of a country from a historical, cultural and 
economic perspective), to demographic and social factors (for a review, see Barrett & 
Brunton-Smith, 2014; Barrett & Pachi, 2019).
In this study, we consider civic engagement as a multidimensional construct that 
encompasses several dimensions, namely eighth-grade students’ current engagement 
both in school and out of school, their disposition towards future engagement, their atti-
tude towards civic participation, and the home and peer contexts that are connected to 
engagement.
As reviews of cross-national contexts for CCE have shown (Annette, 2008; Eurydice, 
2017), schools play a pivotal role in promoting civic engagement (Youniss, 2011) and 
in developing students’ political interest and self-efficacy through their involvement in 
school activities and the promotion of a democratic learning environment (Kennedy 
et al., 2011, 2014; Pasek et al., 2008). Research evidence suggests that participation in 
school-based political activities tends to have a positive influence on students’ future 
civic engagement as adults (Keating & Janmaat, 2015; Schulz et  al., 2013). Moreover, 
studies have repeatedly pointed out that students’ civic engagement can be developed in 
formal, non-formal and informal learning situations (Council of Europe, 2004; Eurydice, 
2017). The literature on the so called whole-school approach to CCE has highlighted 
the positive impacts of a good school and classroom climate and of civic participation

both inside and outside school (in terms of, for example, school governance and local 
community involvement) on students’ civic learning (Council of Europe, 2018; Thapa 
et al., 2013; Tzankova et al., 2021). Concerning this, results from IEA studies (CIVED 
and ICCS) have repeatedly highlighted the association between an open classroom cli-
mate for discussion and students’ interest in political issues and levels of civic knowledge 
(Campbell, 2008; Schulz et al., 2010, 2018a, 2018b; Torney-Purta et al., 2001).
The conceptualisation of civic engagement adopted in this paper is strongly connected 
to the models used in the IEA–CIVED framework (Torney-Purta et al., 2001) that con-
siders CCE in relation to the ecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner, 1988) and to 
the model of situated cognition (Lave & Wenger, 1991). According to this model, which 
is also adopted in ICCS, students develop their civic identities through experiences 
that are nested within different contexts—that is, home, peers and school. Youth civic 
engagement is thus understood to include behavioural and attitudinal components that 
are related to diverse situations both inside and outside school. This view is consistent 
with studies of youth political socialisation that have focused on political participation 
as the main feature of civic engagement (Putnam, 1993). It is also in line with other stud-
ies that have analysed the influence of students’ social background and family context 
in shaping their political dispositions, different forms of young people’s participation 
in society, and levels of students’ achievement in civic knowledge (Dassonneville et al., 
2012; Torney-Purta & Wilkenfeld, 2009).
Several secondary analyses have used ICCS data to analyse young adolescents’ civic 
engagement, their future political participation, and their associations with a wide range 
of factors inside and outside school. These factors pertain to school, peer and neigh-
bourhood contexts (Wilkenfeld & Torney-Purta, 2012), an open classroom climate 
(Reichert et al., 2018); parents’ participation in school life, students’ civic-related beliefs 
(Cosgrove & Gilleece, 2012), past or current school participation, and citizenship self-
efficacy (Schulz, 2018). Other studies have examined students’ future civic and politi-
cal participation (Hooghe & Dassonneville, 2013; Mirazchiyski et al., 2014; Quintelier & 
Hooghe, 2013).
Research has also identified civic knowledge as a positive predictor of students’ 
expected future civic engagement (Schulz et al., 2010, 2018a, 2018b; Torney-Purta et al., 
2001), and there is evidence of associations between students’ level of civic engagement 
and their level of academic achievement and self-esteem (Dàvila & Mora, 2007; Gerber, 
1996; Marsh, 1992).
In Italy, a study conducted by Manganelli et. al. (2014) reviewed the role of civic 
knowledge and efficacy beliefs in promoting adolescents’ expectation of participating in 
civic activities, considered as a construct to include their expectation of participating in 
electoral, political and legal protest activities. Using ICCS 2009 data, the authors found 
positive significant associations between Italian students’ self-efficacy and their expected 
political and civic participation and stressed the negligible effect of civic knowledge on 
their civic participation. Another study based on Italian ICCS 2009 data analysed the 
effects of different factors at the individual and school levels on a single feature of civic 
engagement: students’ future political participation. The results showed that high soci-
oeconomic status (SES), selected student-level factors (civic knowledge and self-effi-
cacy), and variables at the school level (e.g. perception of an open classroom climate for

discussion and the value of participation at school) were positive predictors of students’ 
political participation once they become adults (Manganelli et al., 2012).
Civic and citizenship education in Italy: what is the role of civic engagement?
In the Italian education system, CCE has for decades been relegated to the status of a 
minor subject, with less relevance in the curriculum compared with other subjects 
(Dei, 2002). For a long time, it was integrated with the subject of history, with neither a 
defined instruction time allocation nor a dedicated assessment/mark. Complaints about 
the limits of its curricular implementation are recurrent in the educational debate; in 
recent decades, different educational policies have been implemented to address these 
criticisms but these have not so far yielded measurable results (Palmerio et al., 2021).
In 2008, the subject Citizenship and Constitution was introduced in Italian school cur-
ricula (though without specifying instruction time), both as an integrated topic in a wide 
range of subjects, depending on the school level (most commonly social sciences and 
law), and as a cross-curricular approach (involving all disciplines of the course of study). 
The trial period of the new subject Citizenship and Constitution, promoted by the Min-
istry of Education in 2010, turned out to be a substantial failure because of poor imple-
mentation guidelines and a lack of funding. This lack of implementation measures has 
frequently been registered in Italian educational policies (Palmerio et al., 2021).
Citizenship and Constitution was included in the national guidelines (Indicazioni 
Nazionali) for the curriculum of the first cycle of education (students ages 3–14). Its 
main objectives were related to knowledge of the Italian constitution, fostering an ethic 
of responsibility and legality, and the improvement of students’ social contexts, start-
ing with the school ((Ministero dell’Istruzione, dell’Università e della Ricerca [MIUR], 
2012). The updated document about the guidelines, Curricular Guidelines and New Sce-
narios (Indicazioni Nazionali e Nuovi Scenari; (Ministero dell’Istruzione, dell’Università 
e della Ricerca [MIUR], 2018) stressed the relevance of CCE to all subjects across the 
curriculum in the first cycle of education, placing specific emphasis on global citizenship 
and sustainability. These documents did not address the issue of students’ engagement at 
school for neither the primary nor the lower secondary school level.
Both documents mentioned above provide general guiding principles and leave cur-
riculum planning and choices of teaching and assessment practices or activities to 
encourage student involvement to individual schools. No national monitoring actions 
have been implemented to examine how schools implement and organise Citizenship 
and Constitution, and research at the national level lacks systematic and reliable data to 
inform educational policies with regard to the multiple dimensions of CCE in schools 
(including student engagement). Although education authorities have often expressed 
support in recent years and carried out CCE actions, a systematic design for implement-
ing CCE continues to be missing at the national level, and declarations of principles 
appear not to be reflected in school practice (Palmerio et al., 2021).
With the passing of Law 92/2019, the most recent educational reform in the field, 
Citizenship and Constitution disappeared and a new subject was introduced. Civic 
Education (Educazione Civica) is intended as a separate subject to be implemented 
across all subjects with a dedicated time allocation. No specialist teachers have been 
identified to teach it and selected groups of teachers in each classroom are supposed

to deliver it. According to this legislation, students’ civic engagement is only consid-
ered in terms of promoting active citizenship through extracurricular activities that 
should be carried out through partnerships between schools and the local commu-
nity. This last reform in the area of CCE has led to lively debate on inconsistencies in 
the main assumptions of the law itself (Losito, 2021) and on the status of its imple-
mentation in Italian schools. Recent research at the national level has highlighted 
the lack of activities to promote students’ engagement and the need to deliver CCE 
through a competence-based approach that provides students with learning oppor-
tunities to actively deploy their civic knowledge, attitudes and skills in formal, non-
formal and informal contexts (Ambel, 2020; Losito et al., 2021; Tzankova et al., 2021).
Within this scenario in the field of CCE in Italy, Italian students’ civic engagement 
has received limited attention in empirical research. In view of this particular national 
context, we sought with this study to extend national research on the topic, consider-
ing multiple dimensions of civic engagement and analysing its association with factors 
related to school contexts and students’ attitudes and behaviours. This study is an exam-
ple of how research can link ICCS findings to specific national contexts.
Research questions
Data from ICCS 2016 show, on average, high levels of civic engagement among Ital-
ian students both inside and outside school, namely in the discussion of political and 
social issues outside school and in their preparedness to participate in school activi-
ties (Schulz et al., 2018a, 2018b).
We used ICCS 2016 data to analyse civic engagement and to explore the following 
research questions:
•	 Is it possible to identify distinct groups of Italian eighth-grade students based on 
their level of civic engagement?
•	 Which variables (related to students’ attitudes and behaviours within and outside 
school) are associated with the identified groups?
•	 Are there any differences in the average level of civic knowledge across these groups?
The goals of the study were (a) to identify diverse profiles of participation that 
encompass the multiple dimensions of Italian students’ civic engagement; and (b) to 
verify whether and to what extent these profiles are associated with students’ civic 
knowledge and other variables that reflect school contexts and individual attitudes 
and behaviours. To reach these goals, the data analysis encompassed two steps: (a) a 
latent profile analysis (LPA) and (b) a logistic regression model.
ICCS used a two-stage sampling design (for a detailed description, see Schulz et al., 
2018a, 2018b). Cases with missing values for one or more explanatory variables were 
excluded from the analysis. The overall sample consisted of 3329 eighth-grade Italian 
students aged 12.3 to 17.1 years (M = 13.9, SD = 0.56) attending 170 schools, 52 per 
cent of whom were boys. All schools that participated in ICCS 2016 were included in 
the analysis; the overall exclusion rate was 3 per cent. The exclusion rate did not affect 
the sample composition.

Data analysis
We conducted the analyses using the student weights: the variable total student weight 
was normalised so that the sum of the weights was equal to the student sample size in 
the data. Descriptive and correlational analyses were conducted with the software IDB 
Analyzer 5.0. The LPA, carried out using Mplus 8.2 (Muthén & Muthén, 2009), was per-
formed to identify groups of students who had similar profiles for multiple dimensions 
of civic engagement. The LPA allowed us to create homogeneous groups of engaged stu-
dents, defined by multiple indicators of civic participation.
LPA is a type of latent variable mixture model in which the term ‘latent variable’ refers 
to the latent categorical variable of cluster membership. There are K categories or clus-
ters in this latent categorical variable. The term ‘mixture’ refers to the fact that the data 
are drawn from a population that cannot be described by a single probability distribu-
tion, but from a population made up of a variety of distributions, one for each cluster, 
each with its own set of parameters (Oberski, 2016).
We performed the LPA analysis using robust maximum-likelihood estimation. Models 
were estimated using 1000 random sets of start values with 50 iterations each; the 50 
best solutions were retained for final stage optimisation to avoid converging on a local 
solution.
Variances and covariances were not constrained and were allowed to be different 
across groups.
Number of classes was determined using the Bayesian information criterion, Akaike 
information criterion, and adjusted Bayesian information criterion in order to identify 
the number of classes (see, for example, Tein et al., 2013). We also report the entropy 
values for informational purposes.
In the second step of the analysis, we conducted two separate logistic regression analy-
ses by means of IDB Analyzer 5.0 to better analyse the relationship between students’ 
profiles of civic engagement and the selected variables related to students’ engagement.
Measures
The variables used to identify students’ latent profiles for civic participation refer to dif-
ferent dimensions of students’ civic engagement, namely behavioural intentions towards 
future actions, participation in activities inside and outside of school, attitudes towards 
civic participation, and home and peer context.
The LPA included the following variables.
•	 Students’ expected active political participation (POLPART) was derived from stu-
dents’ reports on different forms of political participation, including the intention 
to join a political party, a trade union or an organisation committed to a political 
or social cause, as well as preparedness to run as a candidate (five items). The four 
response options ranged from “I would certainly do this” to “I would certainly not do 
this” (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81).
•	 Students’ expected electoral participation (ELECPART) was derived from students’ 
answers regarding the likelihood that they would vote in local and national elections 
and get information about candidates before voting (three items). The four response

options ranged from “I would certainly do this” to “I would certainly not do this” 
(Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82).
•	 Students’ expected participation in legal activities (LEGACT) was derived from stu-
dents’ rating of their intention to act in different situations, such as “take part in a 
peaceful march or rally” or “contact an elected representative” (seven items). The 
four response options ranged from “I would certainly do this” to “I would certainly 
not do this” (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82).
•	 Students’ willingness to participate in school activities (SCACT) was measured 
through students’ rating of their preparedness to take part in different school-level 
activities such as “join a group of students campaigning for an issue you agree with” 
(five items). The response options were on a four-point Likert-type scale ranging 
from “very likely” to “not at all likely” (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.78).
•	 Students’ participation at school (SCHPART) was measured through students’ 
reports on having undertaken different types of civic engagement at school, such as 
“taking part in decision-making about how the school is run” (six items). The three 
response options ranged from “within the last 12 months” to “never” (Cronbach’s 
alpha = 0.54).
•	 Students’ participation in the wider community (COMPART) was based on students’ 
answers regarding their participation in activities such as “a voluntary group doing 
something to help the community” (seven items). The three response options ranged 
from “within the last 12 months” to “never” (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.67).
•	 Students’ perceptions of the value of participation at school (VALPARTS) was meas-
ured through students’ rating of their agreement or disagreement with statements 
such as “organising groups of students to express their opinions could help solve 
problems in schools” (five items). Students were asked to indicate their degree 
of agreement on a four-point Likert-type scale ranging from “strongly agree” to 
“strongly disagree” (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.70).
•	 Students’ discussions of political and social issues outside school (POLDISC) was 
measured though students’ rating of their frequency of participation in discus-
sions on political and social issues outside school with either parents/guardians or 
peers—for example, “talking with friends about political or social issues” (four items). 
Students reported their frequency of undertaking the activities using four response 
categories from “Never or hardly never” to “Daily or almost daily” (Cronbach’s 
alpha = 0.66).
For these variables, students’ responses were placed on a scale with a mean score of 50 
across all countries and a standard deviation of 10, using item response theory partial 
credit scaling to derive the scale scores (Schulz et al., 2018a, 2018b).
The variables used in the regression model are related to the aspects that research has 
indicated as associated with students’ engagement in relation to school contexts, their 
attitudes and their behaviours.
With regard to the factors related to students’ attitudes and behaviours, on the basis of 
the literature review we identified the following variables: students’ sense of citizenship 
self-efficacy (CITEFF), students’ engagement with social media (SOCMED), and trust in 
institutions (INTRUST).

The question related to students’ sense of citizenship self-efficacy asked students how 
well they thought they would perform seven different engagement-related activities, 
such as “discuss a newspaper article about a conflict between countries” (Cronbach’s 
alpha = 0.80). The question related to engagement with social media asked students to 
indicate how often they engaged in three different activities, such as “sharing or com-
menting on another person’s online post regarding a political or social issue” (Cron-
bach’s alpha = 0.59).
In response to the question on trust in a wider range of groups or organisations, stu-
dents rated the degree to which they trusted different civic institutions, such as political 
parties.
Factors included in the model related to students’ perceptions of the school and class-
room contexts encompassed the possibility of civic learning in school (CIVLRN) and 
students’ perceptions of an open classroom climate (OPDISC). The question related to 
civic learning in school asked students to report the extent to which they had learned 
about seven different topics at school—for example, “Political issues and events in other 
countries” (seven items) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.76). The question about students’ per-
ception of openness in classroom discussions (OPDISC) asked students how frequently 
(never, rarely, sometimes, often) specific situations happen during regular lessons—for 
example, “Teachers encourage students to discuss issues with people with different opin-
ions” (six items) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.70).
The regression model also includes the civic knowledge scale (CIVKNOW). The civic 
knowledge scale, which is based on 87 items, reflects students’ knowledge about and 
understanding of civic issues. The ICCS, using item response theory estimates, calcu-
lates a civic knowledge score for each student that is later converted into a standardised 
score of the overall distribution (relative to all students in the participating countries) 
with an international average of 500 and a standard deviation of 100 (fixed in ICCS 
2009). The scale was considered as a dependent variable and all five plausible values were 
used (see also Schulz et al., 2018a, 2018b).
Results
Correlation analyses
The correlation analysis (Table 1) showed a positive association between all the variables 
considered in the LPA. The association was strongest between students’ expected partic-
ipation in legal activities (LEGACT) and students’ expected active political participation 
(POLPART), and between LEGACT and students’ willingness to participate in school 
activities (SCACT).
The associations between students’ participation in the wider community (COM-
PART) and students’ expected electoral participation (ELECPART), and between COM-
PART and students’ perception of the value of participation at school (VALPARTS), 
although statistically significant, could be considered weak (< 0.10; see Cohen, 1988).

Discussion
In this study, we investigated Italian students’ civic engagement using ICCS 2016 data 
with two objectives: (a) to identify possible groups of Italian eighth-grade students on 
the basis of their level of civic engagement; and (b) to examine the associations among 
these groups and some key factors reflecting school contexts and students’ attitudes and 
behaviours.
The results of the LPA identified three subgroups of Italian students with different civic 
engagement profiles: disengaged, moderately engaged and engaged students. The analy-
sis allowed us to discriminate among the three groups. Italian students in the engaged 
group were characterised by a widespread openness towards all the dimensions iden-
tified for the construct of civic engagement (i.e. civic and political engagement inside 
and outside the school context). In contrast, students in the disengagement group were 
characterised by less disposition towards engagement in relation to all the dimensions 
considered in our engagement construct. It is important to note, however, that the dis-
engagement group comprised a relatively small proportion of students (7 per cent). Most 
grade 8 students appeared to be moderately or fully engaged in school life and in activi-
ties with external organisations.
Results from the logistic regression model suggested that students in the high-engage-
ment group were characterised by higher levels of students’ sense of citizenship self-
efficacy. The other variables included in the model showed mostly significant relatively 
small relationships, with differences between engagement groups. For civic knowledge, 
we recorded no statistically significant associations.
These findings confirm those from previous research (Ainley & Schulz, 2011; Manga-
nelli et al., 2014) that have highlighted the key role of internal self-efficacy in promoting 
students’ engagement. In order to act and participate actively, perceived ability to under-
stand politics and to take part in collective decision-making processes is considered cru-
cial (Hahn, 1998; Pasek et al., 2008; Sohl & Arensmeier, 2015). Moreover, these results 
expand existing research on the relevance of students’ self-efficacy for their engagement, 
exactly because in this study civic engagement was considered as a multidimensional 
construct that encompasses not only different forms of expected civic engagement but 
also students’ engagement inside and outside of school.

The lack of association with civic knowledge (Schulz et al., 2010, 2018a, 2018b; Tor-
ney-Purta et al., 2001) is consistent with previous findings that highlighted the absence 
or inconsistency of relationships between knowledge of civic issues and different types 
of expected engagement (with the exception of expected electoral participation, where 
stronger positive associations were recorded).
In the Italian national context CCE at school has undergone continual reforms with 
implications for young peoples’ engagement that are not yet fully understood. This study 
provides findings to inform on how to promote students’ civic engagement beyond 
school instruction (that is, civic knowledge), through the fostering of self-efficacy and 
the provision of opportunities to make students confident in their capacity to act effec-
tively in political and civic-related issues. These aspects are crucial to strengthen stu-
dents’ active involvement inside and outside school and in the future.
Such results have implications for the way CCE is conceived and delivered, and they 
emphasise the importance for schools to develop learning situations and contexts that 
enable students to deploy knowledge and skills to deal with tasks and problems in dif-
ferent forms and of varying complexity. In this regard, research on the whole-school 
approach has amply highlighted the benefits of CCE delivered throughout the school 
context, which is considered a democratic learning environment across its dimensions 
(e.g. in relation to school management and organisation, opportunities for participa-
tion offered to students, teaching approaches and assessment criteria, cooperation with 
external organisations and networks with other schools; Council of Europe, 2018). The 
school context itself thus becomes an example of and a field for practising democratic 
active citizenship for students and for all those involved in school life, including families 
and external bodies.
Conclusions
Providing students with opportunities to engage actively in civic-related activities is a 
necessary condition to promote the students’ sense of self-efficacy, but it is not suffi-
cient if it is not supported by initiatives focused on metacognition—i.e. reflection on the 
knowledge and the processes triggered through learning and the implemented actions 
to accomplish a task (Bandura, 1977; Flavell, 1979). Several studies have emphasised the 
association between metacognition and constructs related to self-efficacy (Aydin et al., 
2011; Coutinho, 2008).
The promotion of metacognition and opportunities to foster students’ self-efficacy 
in civic-related issues strongly pertain to the area of citizenship competence (Council 
of the European Union, 2018). Envisaging CCE delivery in schools with a focus on the 
development of students’ citizenship competence means providing context-based, chal-
lenging and demanding tasks wherein students can mobilise and deploy specific values, 
attitudes, skills and knowledge to accomplish them. The opportunity to reflect on the 
efficacy of the processes and the resources activated in responding appropriately to rel-
evant tasks is thus essential for students so they can understand the effectiveness of their 
behaviours and the strategies adopted in a given situation and so monitor the level of 
their developing citizenship competence (Brett et  al., 2009; Council of Europe, 2018; 
Rychen & Salganik, 2003).

It is important to acknowledge some limitations of this study. Firstly, we analysed civic 
engagement only from the perspective of students, considering different dimensions of 
the construct that refer only to dispositions and attitudes among students in their eighth 
year of schooling. Future analyses could investigate in more depth how whole-school 
approaches and, more generally, the characteristics of school contexts effectively pro-
mote youth civic engagement across and beyond the curriculum, using the ICCS results 
as a starting point for follow-up research studies (including small-scale and qualitative 
approaches). Study results should also be considered in relation to other ICCS 2016 
participating countries, as this study is based on Italian data only. Finally, we must note 
that the cross-sectional nature of the ICCS sampling design does not allow inferences of 
causal relationships.
Although conclusions derived from our findings refer only to the Italian national con-
text, they nevertheless confirm previous research conducted comparing different edu-
cational systems. Results showed that fostering students’ sense of civic self-efficacy is 
crucial for the promotion of their civic engagement. Self-efficacy can be developed by 
providing opportunities to solve problems in different contexts and by strengthening 
students’ metacognitive skills. All these factors imply a competence-oriented approach 
to CCE and require the implementation of an open and democratic learning environ-
ment. A democratic learning environment is crucial for students to deploy their knowl-
edge and skills in specific scenarios, to reflect on the strategies adopted, and to regulate 
the development of their citizenship competence. According to this perspective, the 
whole-school approach to CCE, focusing on the school as a democratic learning envi-
ronment across all its different aspects, not only promotes greater engagement and a 
sense of belonging to the school among students and school staff, but also has the poten-
tial to improve teacher-student relationships and the teaching–learning process, as well 
as fostering a competence-oriented approach to CCE (Cohen et al., 2009; Ranson et al., 
2005). This is particularly relevant for the Italian education system, in which students’ 
engagement is mainly considered in relation to extracurricular activities (Law 92/2019). 
Despite its strong recognition in ministerial recommendations and guidelines in the 
Italian context, competence-oriented curricula and delivery for CCE are still widely 
neglected aspects at the school level (Damiani & Losito, 2019).
