How perceived teaching autonomy predicts job satisfaction in China: The
mediating role of self-efficacy and the moderating role of school climate
Zhiyong Xie * , Xueqiang Zou *
South China Normal University, Department of Teacher Education, China

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
School climate
Teaching autonomy
Teacher self-efficacy
Teacher job satisfaction

This study investigates the mechanisms by which teaching autonomy potentially influences job satisfaction,
focusing on the mediating role of teacher self-efficacy and the moderating effect of school climate. Utilizing
structural equation modeling (SEM) and moderated mediation analysis on data from the TALIS 2018 survey of
teachers in Shanghai, the results reveal that: (1) teaching autonomy directly and positively predicts job satisfaction, with a direct effect accounting for 76.84 % of the total effect; (2) teacher self-efficacy serves as a crucial
mediator in the relationship between teaching autonomy and job satisfaction, contributing to 23.16 % of the total
effect; and (3) school climate significantly moderates the mediating role of self-efficacy, with a more positive
school climate amplifying the positive impact of self-efficacy on job satisfaction. The study concludes that
improving teacher self-efficacy and job satisfaction largely hinges on cultivating a supportive school climate, as
well as reinforcing teaching autonomy.

1. Introduction
Teachers are central to the educational process, playing a pivotal role
in students’ academic and personal development. Their job satisfaction
is crucial not only for their professional growth and well-being but also
for the overall effectiveness of educational institutions and student
outcomes. The Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS)
2018 indicates that job satisfaction among Chinese teachers is generally
lower than the international average, highlighting the need for urgent
attention in China’s educational context (Tang et al., 2020). Key factors
influencing teacher job satisfaction include school climate, teaching
autonomy, and teacher self-efficacy (Klassen & Tze, 2014; Malinen &
Savolainen, 2016; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2017).
In China, teaching autonomy is particularly complex due to the
traditionally centralized education system, which is deeply rooted in
Confucian values of hierarchy, authority, and collective responsibility.
While centralization has ensured consistent educational standards
nationwide, it has also restricted teachers’ autonomy, potentially
undermining their job satisfaction (Hallinger & Lu, 2013). Although
centralization offers benefits like uniformity and equitable access to
education, it poses challenges in balancing administrative control with
the need for teacher empowerment and autonomy. Empowerment theory provides a valuable framework for understanding these dynamics,

suggesting that delegating power and responsibility to individuals, and
allowing them to make autonomous decisions, enhances job satisfaction
and performance (Conger & Kanungo, 1988). Moreover, empowerment
boosts self-efficacy by increasing individuals’ confidence in their ability
to achieve goals. In the educational context, granting teachers sufficient
autonomy is essential for fostering a sense of empowerment and professional fulfillment. However, the autonomy of teachers has long been
significantly restricted, which directly affects their job satisfaction
(Strong & Yoshida, 2014). Based on the empowerment theory, this study
proposes that teacher self-efficacy mediates the relationship between
teaching autonomy and job satisfaction, while school climate has a
moderating effect on this mediation process.
Despite the recognized importance of teaching autonomy, its relationship with job satisfaction has not been extensively explored,
particularly in the Chinese educational context. This study aims to fill
this gap by examining how teaching autonomy influences job satisfaction, focusing on the mediating role of teacher self-efficacy and the
moderating role of school climate.
The research questions of this study are:
(1) How does teachers’ perceived teaching autonomy directly affect
job satisfaction?

2. literature review
2.1. Theoretical basis and key constructs definition
2.1.1. Empowerment Theory
Empowerment theory is a vital framework for understanding how
teaching autonomy influences teacher job satisfaction. This theory emphasizes the importance of granting individuals greater control and
decision-making power in areas that significantly impact their lives,
thereby fostering a sense of ownership and competence (Spreitzer, 1995;
Thomas & Velthouse, 1990). In the educational context, teaching autonomy exemplifies this empowerment by allowing teachers more
freedom and authority over key aspects of their work, such as curriculum design, classroom management, and instructional strategies. This
decentralization of decision-making not only enhances the quality of
education but also supports teachers’ professional growth and development (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2014). Teaching autonomy, within this
framework, refers to the extent of control teachers have over their
professional activities. This autonomy is crucial for fostering innovative
and effective teaching practices, as it enables teachers to tailor their
methods to the specific needs of their students and the unique challenges
of their teaching environments (Ingersoll, 2003). When teachers have
the autonomy to make decisions that directly impact their classrooms,
they are more likely to feel empowered, motivated, and satisfied in their
roles (Pearson & Moomaw, 2005).
Empowerment in education requires school leaders and policymakers to create environments that support and encourage teaching
autonomy. This involves adopting democratic management practices,
ensuring that teachers have access to adequate resources, and cultivating a school climate that nurtures creativity and initiative
(Tschannen-Moran, 2009). Teaching autonomy enhances job satisfaction by fostering teachers’ self-efficacy, a mechanism supported by Peng
et al., 2022, who found that autonomy-driven efficacy gains reduce
stress and improve engagement. (Bandura, 1997). In conclusion,
empowerment theory highlights the critical role of teaching autonomy
in enhancing teacher self-efficacy and job satisfaction. By giving
teachers greater control over their professional environments, educational institutions can foster a more dynamic and fulfilled workforce,
which is essential for achieving the broader goals of educational reform
and modernization.

2.1.3. Teacher Job Satisfaction
Teacher job satisfaction refers to the overall feelings and attitudes
that teachers hold toward their work, encompassing their evaluations of
the work environment, job content, interactions with colleagues, and
compensation (Toropova et al., 2020). It is often described as a general,
emotionally colored perception of their profession and working conditions, reflecting their emotional and cognitive responses to various aspects of their job (Ferlianto, 2015). According to Maslow’s hierarchy of
needs theory, human behavior is driven by the fulfillment of needs, and
teacher job satisfaction aligns with the need for self-actualization, which
is crucial for professional growth and sustained commitment to teaching
(Wang, 2016).
Teacher job satisfaction is influenced by both individual and environmental factors. Individual factors include teaching experience, with
mid-career teachers reporting lower satisfaction than early- or latecareer peers (Tang et al., 2020; Klassen & Chiu, 2010). While gender
and education level show limited predictive power (Wu, 2016), intrapersonal factors like self-efficacy play a critical role, as evidenced by
TALIS 2018 studies in East Asia (Niu et al., 2023). Environmental factors
significantly influence teacher satisfaction, with research demonstrating
that urban teachers have access to superior resources compared to their
rural counterparts (Mu et al., 2016). Furthermore, school climate,
particularly in terms of disciplinary norms and teacher-student relationships, has been identified as a robust predictor of job satisfaction
(Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2011).
Campus climate, which encompasses organizational climate,
teacher-student relationships, disciplinary atmosphere, and the physical
environment, also plays a critical role. A positive organizational climate
and supportive teacher-student relationships are strongly correlated
with higher job satisfaction (Jiang, 2005). Diagne’s (2023) study based
on TALIS 2018 data also confirmed this finding. Furthermore, teacher
self-efficacy, which tends to increase with teaching experience, is linked
to school climate, with more supportive climates fostering higher
self-efficacy and, consequently, greater job satisfaction (Hosford &
O’Sullivan, 2016; Klassen & Chiu, 2010).
In summary, teacher job satisfaction is influenced by a complex
interplay of individual and environmental factors. These factors are not
isolated but interact dynamically to shape teachers’ overall job satisfaction, with significant implications for educational policy and school
management (Baroudi et al., 2020). Understanding these influences is
essential for creating supportive work environments that promote both
teacher well-being and educational effectiveness.

2.1.2. Teaching Autonomy
Teaching autonomy refers to the freedom and control that teachers
have within legal and ethical boundaries over various aspects of their
teaching, including presenting content, establishing student relationships, participating in curriculum creation, and setting goals (Liu et al.,
2021; Smylie, 1992). This autonomy is crucial for achieving creative
teaching and reflects the teacher’s sense of professional agency
(Anderson, 2002; Schwarz-Franco & Hadar, 2024). Scholars typically
categorize teaching autonomy into two main components: curriculum
autonomy, which includes the rights to "select teaching materials" and
"develop instructional plans and procedures," and general teaching autonomy, encompassing rights related to "classroom behavior standards"
and "decision-making in teaching" (Pearson, 1998). Additionally, the
concept of teaching autonomy has been expanded to include teaching
control, influence, participation in teaching affairs, and the overall degree of teaching authority (Kreis & Brockopp, 1986).

2.2. Relationship between teaching autonomy and job satisfaction
In contemporary educational practice, teaching autonomy is
increasingly recognized as a critical factor influencing teachers’ job
satisfaction (Alkan et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2021). According to
self-determination theory, autonomy is one of the fundamental psychological needs that fosters intrinsic motivation and engagement
among teachers, which in turn enhances their job satisfaction(Reeve

et al., 2004). Teaching autonomy allows teachers to exercise control
over various aspects of their teaching activities, such as curriculum
development, instructional methods, and classroom management. This
control provides opportunities for teachers to actualize their educational
ideals and fulfill their personal and professional development needs
(Erss et al., 2016).
Empirical studies have consistently found that teaching autonomy
positively and significantly predicts teachers’ job satisfaction, thereby
reducing the likelihood of burnout (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2014; Simbula
et al., 2010). When teachers have the autonomy to make decisions
regarding their teaching practices, they are more likely to experience a
sense of ownership and empowerment, leading to increased job satisfaction and reduced stress levels(Nizam & Adil, 2014). Moreover,
research has shown that teaching autonomy is not only a predictor of job
satisfaction but also plays a crucial role in teachers’ intentions to remain
in the profession. This correlation is particularly evident in environments where teachers perceive their autonomy as being supported by
school leadership and educational policies (Worth & Van den Brande,
2020).
In addition, teaching autonomy has been linked to various positive
outcomes, including higher levels of professional commitment and a
stronger sense of professional identity. Teachers who perceive higher
levels of autonomy tend to report greater job satisfaction, which in turn
positively impacts their overall well-being and reduces turnover intentions (Pearson & Moomaw, 2005). As teaching autonomy gradually
expands, teachers’ sense of empowerment and professionalism significantly increases, effectively reducing their work-related stress. The
expansion of teaching autonomy is also associated with an increased
sense of professionalism, which enhances teachers’ motivation to
engage in continuous professional development and improves their
teaching effectiveness (Wermke & Salokangas, 2015).
Given the significant impact of teaching autonomy on job satisfaction, it is essential to explore the underlying mechanisms through which
this autonomy influences various aspects of teachers’ professional lives.
Understanding these mechanisms can provide a theoretical foundation
for developing strategies to enhance teacher retention, reduce burnout,
and improve overall job satisfaction. Therefore, based on the above
literature review, this study proposes Hypothesis 1: Teaching autonomy
positively predicts job satisfaction.

Teacher self-efficacy is also a key factor in generating intrinsic
motivation and sustaining mental health, acting as a buffer against the
stresses of the profession. Studies have shown that high levels of teacher
self-efficacy are associated with greater job satisfaction and lower rates
of burnout, underscoring its importance in the educational context
(Donohoo, 2018; Dou et al., 2017; Manasia et al., 2020). Analysis of
TALIS 2008, 2013, 2018 data revealed a significant positive correlation
between teacher self-efficacy and job satisfaction, with higher
self-efficacy associated with higher teaching quality (Diagne, 2023;
Kasalak & Dağyar, 2020). Other studies have confirmed that high levels
of collective teacher efficacy are linked to greater job satisfaction and
negatively predict teacher burnout (Eyüp, 2022).
Given the strong link between self-efficacy and job satisfaction, it is
evident that self-efficacy not only enhances the positive effects of
teaching autonomy but also mediates the relationship between autonomy and overall job satisfaction. Therefore, based on the above literature, this study proposes Hypothesis 2: Teacher self-efficacy mediates
the relationship between teaching autonomy and job satisfaction.
2.4. The moderating role of school climate
School climate refers to the collective perception of school members
regarding the behaviors, interpersonal relationships, organizational
structures, and values within the school. It characterizes the school
environment and significantly influences the behavior and attitudes of
its members (Cohen, 2009). According to ecological systems theory,
individual development is shaped by ongoing interactions between individuals and their environments, with school climate serving as a
critical external factor within the teacher’s ecological system
(Bronfenbrenner, 2000; Hosford & O’Sullivan, 2016). This climate plays
a crucial role in shaping teachers’ professional development and overall
job satisfaction.
A systematic review has categorized school climate into four key
dimensions: academic climate, campus safety, community, and institutional environment. These dimensions help build a robust educational
framework that supports learning and development by fostering positive
relationships between teachers and students, as well as enhancing the
organizational and structural characteristics of the school (Wang &
Degol, 2016). The TALIS 2018 data further divides teachers’ perceptions
of school climate into three main components: disciplinary climate,
teacher-student relationships, and democratic participation. Empirical
studies have shown that positive perceptions of school climate are
strongly correlated with higher levels of teacher self-efficacy and job
satisfaction (Aldridge & Fraser, 2016).
Research involving high school teachers has demonstrated that
various dimensions of school climate are linked to teacher self-efficacy
and job satisfaction. For instance, improving the overall school
climate can significantly enhance teacher self-efficacy, thereby boosting
job satisfaction and aiding in teacher retention (Aldridge & Fraser,
2016). Furthermore, a China-based TALIS 2018 study revealed that
teachers’ perceived disciplinary climate also significantly impacts their
job satisfaction (Liu et al., 2020). Positive changes in school climate,
especially in service-oriented environments, can motivate teachers to
perceive their work as meaningful and fulfilling (Veletić & Olsen, 2021),
leading to greater alignment with and commitment to the school’s
values. Thus, the impact of teacher self-efficacy on job satisfaction is
moderated by the quality of the school climate, underscoring the
importance of a supportive and positive school environment in maximizing the benefits of teaching autonomy. Based on the above literature
review, this study proposes Hypothesis 3: The mediating effect of
teacher self-efficacy on the relationship between teaching autonomy and
job satisfaction is moderated by school climate.

2.3. The mediating role of teacher self-efficacy
Teacher self-efficacy refers to teachers’ beliefs and judgments about
their own abilities to effectively educate students and manage the
classroom environment (Friedman & Kass, 2002). It is a crucial
component of teacher quality, significantly impacting both professional
growth and student outcomes (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2014; Lili & Man,
2020). Rooted in social learning theory and self-determination theory,
self-efficacy reflects the belief in one’s ability to influence events and
outcomes, thereby forming the foundation of autonomy (Bandura,1997;
Sugarman & Sokol, 2012). Teachers with high self-efficacy are more
likely to feel empowered and successful in their work, and they view the
outcomes of their autonomous actions as direct reflections of their capabilities (Lu et al., 2014; Sokmen and Kilic, 2019).
Recent empirical studies have reinforced the positive relationship
between teacher self-efficacy and teaching autonomy, demonstrating
that self-efficacy mediates the relationship between autonomy and job
satisfaction, as teachers with greater confidence in their abilities translate autonomy into more effective practices, thereby achieving professional fulfillment (Zee & Koomen, 2016; Aldridge & Fraser, 2016).
Moreover, the impact of teaching autonomy on student outcomes, such
as self-awareness, extroversion, and academic performance, is often
mediated by the teacher’s self-efficacy (Piza et al., 2020). Autonomous
teaching methods are positively associated with job satisfaction because
teachers’ belief in their ability (self-efficacy) mediates this relationship,
as demonstrated in cross-cultural TALIS analyses (Burić & Kim, 2021).

3.2. Measure
3.2.1. Teaching autonomy
The teaching autonomy scale in TALIS 2018 consists of five items,
including "Determining course content," "Choosing teaching methods,"
"Assessing student learning," "Disciplining students," and "Determining
the amount of homework." Each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale
(1–4, where 1= Strongly Disagree, 2= Disagree, 3= Agree, 4= Strongly
Agree), with higher scores indicating greater perceived autonomy in
classroom teaching. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale is
0.923, and each index is within an acceptable range.
3.2.2. Teacher self-efficacy
The teacher self-efficacy scale in TALIS 2018 comprises three dimensions: instructional self-efficacy, student engagement self-efficacy,
and classroom management self-efficacy, each with four items,
totaling 12 items. For example, instructional self-efficacy includes items
like "Using a variety of instructional strategies in my classroom," student
engagement self-efficacy includes "Motivating students who show low
interest in schoolwork," and classroom management self-efficacy includes "Clearly explaining my expectations concerning student
behavior." Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1–4, where 1=
Strongly Disagree, 2= Disagree, 3= Agree, 4= Strongly Agree), with
higher scores indicating greater perceived self-efficacy. The overall
Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale is 0.956 (including 0.919 for selfefficacy in classroom management, 0.897 for self-efficacy in teaching,
and 0.898 for self-efficacy in student engagement), and all indices are
within an acceptable range.

3. Method
3.1. Participants
Data for this study were obtained from the TALIS 2018 survey, which
includes a representative sample of teachers from Shanghai, the only
Chinese city participating in this cycle. This dataset provides a unique
opportunity to analyze teacher job satisfaction within the context of
China’s urban educational system. In 2018, a stratified sample of 3976
lower secondary school teachers was drawn from 198 schools in
Shanghai, China. The survey focused on professional characteristics and
teaching practices at both the school and teacher levels. Among the
participants, 2941 were female (74 %) and 1035 were male (26 %).
Regarding educational background, 35 teachers (0.9 %) held qualifications below a bachelor’s degree, 3413 (85.8 %) had a bachelor’s degree,
and 515 (12.9 %) held a master’s degree, with 13 cases (0.3 %) having

3.2.3. School Climate
The school climate scale in TALIS 2018 is assessed through both the
teacher and principal questionnaires; however, this study primarily used
the teacher questionnaire. The scale comprises three dimensions: disciplinary climate (4 items), teacher-student relationships (4 items), and
participatory decision-making (5 items), totaling 13 items. For instance,

3.2.5. Teacher experience (control variable)
Given that prior research indicates teaching experience can influence
teachers’ self-efficacy and job satisfaction (Klassen & Chiu, 2010), we

was measured using the item on years of service from the TALIS 2018
questionnaire, to account for potential effects of teaching tenure on the
primary variables.

4.3. Mediating effect of self-efficacy

3.2.6. Data analysis procedure
Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0 and Mplus 8.3. We first
assessed common-method bias via Harman’s single-factor test in SPSS
27.0 and computed descriptive statistics and Pearson correlations
among teaching autonomy, teacher self‑efficacy, school climate, job
satisfaction, and control variables in SPSS 27.0. We then employed a
triple moderated mediation model to examine how teaching autonomy
(X) influences job satisfaction (Y) through teacher self-efficacy (M), with
school climate (W) moderating three pathways: (1) X→M, (2) M→Y, and
(3) X→Y direct path. Analyses were conducted in Mplus 8.3 (Muthén &
Muthén, 1998-2019) following OECD TALIS technical guidelines
(OECD, 2019). Teacher final weights (TCHWGT) and 100 BRR replicate
weights (TRWGT1-TRWGT100) were incorporated to handle complex
sampling design, with standard errors adjusted via Balanced Repeated
Replication (BRR) (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2006). Parameters were
estimated by Maximum Likelihood (ML), showing satisfactory fit:
Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) =0.049 (<0.08 cutoff) (Hu & Bentler, 1999).Conditional effect analyses revealed that
school climate (W) significantly enhanced the mediation pathway (β IMM
= 0.054, p = .002). At high W levels (+1SD), the indirect effect (0.108)
was 108 times stronger than at low levels (-1SD) (0.001). W also
significantly moderated the direct path (β XW→Y = 0.140, p = .005), with
direct effects rising from 0.001 (low W) to 0.271 (high W). The model
demonstrated good explanatory power, accounting for 24.4 % variance
in job satisfaction (Y) (p < .001). All results reported standardized estimates (STDYX) at α = .05. Conditional effects were computed via
model constraints at W = Mean (0) ±1SD.

To further explore the mediating effect of self-efficacy within the
model, this study employed complex sampling design analysis using
BRR-weighted maximum likelihood estimation in Mplus 8.3. The results
are presented in Table 2. Teaching autonomy was found to have a significant total effect on job satisfaction (β = 0.272, t = 5.192,
p < 0.001). When the mediating variable, self-efficacy, was examined,
teaching autonomy significantly predicted self-efficacy (β = 0.313,
t = 6.344, p < 0.001), and self-efficacy, in turn, positively predicted job
satisfaction (β = 0.201, t = 2.893, p = 0.004). The direct effect of
teaching autonomy on job satisfaction remained significant after accounting for the mediator (β = 0.209, t = 3.669, p < 0.001). The significant indirect effect (β = 0.063, t = 2.736, p = 0.006) indicates
partial mediation by self-efficacy.
Additionally, the 95 % confidence intervals for both the direct effect
of teaching autonomy on job satisfaction and the mediating effect of selfefficacy do not include zero (see Table 3). These effects account for
76.84 % and 23.16 % of the total effect, respectively. Therefore, Hypothesis 2 is supported.

school climate functions as a triple amplifier—it simultaneously intensifies autonomy’s direct effect on satisfaction, augments autonomy’s

be counterproductive. Some studies suggest that over-empowerment can
lead to a lack of direction and increased occupational stress (Esfandiari

maintain a supportive school climate, which plays a crucial role in
ensuring teachers’ overall well-being and job satisfaction.

Teachers Law, which guarantee teaching autonomy, practical implementation in classroom reforms remains insufficient (Jia, 2014).

instructional organization, teacher-student interactions, curriculum
design, and teaching evaluation. This approach would not only help
teachers navigate their professional responsibilities more effectively but
also support their rapid growth in professional expertise.
To achieve these goals, a transition from traditional centralized
leadership models to more distributed leadership approaches is needed,
as these have been shown to significantly enhance teacher job satisfaction (Xin & Tahir, 2024). However, it is crucial to strike a balance—excessive delegation of authority could lead to other challenges.
Therefore, finding a balanced threshold where the positive impacts of
autonomy occur within a stable and supportive environment is essential
for maximizing teacher job satisfaction.

5.2. The mediating role of teacher self-efficacy in the link between
teaching autonomy and job satisfaction

The study found that school climate plays a crucial moderating role
in the relationship between teaching autonomy and job satisfaction.
When school climate is supportive, it enhances the positive effects of
autonomy on job satisfaction, as seen in the studies by Otrębski (2022)
and Akgöz, Şahin, and Erdoğan (2024). On the other hand, when the
school climate is less favorable, increased autonomy may not yield the
same positive outcomes, or may even exacerbate dissatisfaction, as
demonstrated in Oduro et al., 2024 and Özdemir & Çakalcı (2022).
These findings suggest that a strong, positive school climate can
amplify the benefits of autonomy, making it an essential factor for
improving job satisfaction among teachers. However, in environments
where school climate is weaker, even increased autonomy may not be
sufficient to enhance teacher morale and satisfaction. These findings
underscore that school leaders must prioritize both autonomy and
climate improvements, as isolated autonomy policies may fail without a
supportive environment.
This study extends previous research by uncovering that the quality
of the school climate can alter the impact of teacher self-efficacy on job
satisfaction. In a supportive and positive school environment, teachers
are more likely to feel that their efforts and achievements are recognized, which strengthens the link between self-efficacy and job satisfaction. Conversely, in an average or negative climate, even high levels
of self-efficacy may not lead to greater job satisfaction due to the lack of
external support. This finding is corroborated by Christina Gray et al.,
who suggest that a positive school climate significantly contributes to
teachers’ mental health, enabling them to better support students’
learning and growth (Gray et al., 2017).
Additionally, the study reveals teaching autonomy’s contextdependent compensatory role: in average school climates, it sustains
self-efficacy development despite limited organizational support, offering schools a practical interim strategy during climate improvement.
This allows teachers to exercise professional agency through instructional decisions, reinforcing identity and motivation (Dou et al., 2016;
Akgöz et al., 2024).
Moreover, autonomy maintains a resilience foundation for selfefficacy, empowering teachers to innovate under constraints
(Dunn-Wisner, 2004). However, in truly unfavorable climates (M-1SD),
autonomy loses efficacy benefits, confirming climate’s threshold effect.
Thus, while autonomy provides near-term efficacy protection in moderate environments, school leaders must prioritize climate development
(Choi & Mao, 2021) to fully activate autonomy’s satisfaction potential.

The mediation analysis in this study reveals that teacher self-efficacy
significantly mediates the relationship between teaching autonomy and
job satisfaction.This finding is consistent with Katsantonis’ (2020) results derived from the TALIS 2018, which identified teacher self-efficacy
as a mediating mechanism while demonstrating its cross-culturally
invariant pattern. Specifically, when teaching autonomy is limited,
teachers are more likely to experience lower self-efficacy, which can
subsequently lead to reduced job satisfaction. This finding aligns with
self-efficacy theory (Bandura, 1997; Henson, 2001) and Maslow’s hierarchy of needs theory (Khan et al., 2021; Taormina & Gao, 2013),
illustrating the theoretical mechanism by which self-efficacy influences
job satisfaction. Self-efficacy, defined as an individual’s belief in their
ability to perform tasks and achieve goals, is crucial in shaping teachers’
confidence in their professional abilities (Bandura,1997). When teachers
are granted autonomy, they are more likely to feel competent and
empowered, which enhances both their job satisfaction and confidence
in their teaching capabilities. Conversely, reduced autonomy can limit
teachers’ decision-making power regarding teaching methods and
classroom management, potentially undermining their self-efficacy.
This diminished self-efficacy can lead to lower job satisfaction, as
teachers may feel less effective and unable to positively impact student
learning outcomes (Fradkin-Hayslip, 2021).
Practically, these findings underscore the importance of recognizing
and supporting the central role of teacher self-efficacy in the educational
process. For schools and policymakers, this means that enhancing
teacher autonomy is not just about improving job satisfaction directly,
but also about bolstering the self-efficacy that underpins effective
teaching (Tschannen-Moran & Hoy, 2001). For teachers with low levels
of autonomy, it is crucial to provide more opportunities for
decision-making and self-directed professional development to prevent
the erosion of self-efficacy and job satisfaction (Skaalvik & Skaalvik,
2014; Collie et al., 2012).
Additionally, schools should prioritize efforts to enhance teachers’
self-efficacy by addressing factors that influence it, such as emotional
exhaustion, peer relationships, and classroom management (Fernandez
et al., 2016). Other important factors include teachers’ personal values,
teaching practices, professional development opportunities, and the
overall team atmosphere (An et al., 2021). To foster a supportive and
motivating work environment, schools should offer emotional support in
both professional and personal contexts, while cultivating a positive
organizational culture. By doing so, schools can create a harmonious and
motivated teaching team, ultimately leading to improved job satisfaction and educational outcomes.

6. Conclusion and limitations
This study offers significant insights into the complex relationships
between teaching autonomy, teacher self-efficacy, school climate, and
job satisfaction within the educational context. The findings underscore
that teaching autonomy plays a pivotal role in enhancing job satisfaction
among teachers. Specifically, when teachers are granted greater autonomy in their instructional practices, they tend to experience higher
levels of job satisfaction. This positive relationship is further reinforced
by the mediating role of teacher self-efficacy. Teachers who perceive
greater autonomy are likely to develop a stronger sense of self-efficacy,
which in turn enhances their job satisfaction. This highlights the
importance of fostering environments where teachers feel empowered to
make decisions about their teaching methods and classroom management, as this empowerment significantly contributes to their overall job
satisfaction.
Moreover, the study reveals that school climate serves as a critical
moderating factor in this relationship. A positive and supportive school
climate not only directly enhances job satisfaction but also amplifies the
beneficial effects of teaching autonomy on self-efficacy and, consequently, on job satisfaction. Conversely, in less favorable school climates, even high levels of teaching autonomy may not translate into
increased job satisfaction due to insufficient external support. These
findings suggest that while autonomy is crucial, it must be

While offering meaningful contributions, this study is subject to
several limitations. First, the exclusive focus on lower-secondary (middle school) teachers may limit the generalizability of findings to primary, upper-secondary, or non-traditional educational settings. Second,
the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences and longitudinal
assessment of how teaching autonomy, self-efficacy, school climate, and
job satisfaction evolve temporally. Third, reliance on self-reported
measures for all key variables raises concerns about social-desirability
bias and common-method variance. Finally, while the BRR-weighted
SEM approach rigorously tested the triple moderated mediation
model, future studies could employ multilevel modeling to account for
school-level clustering and Bayesian estimation to enhance precision in
interaction effect estimation.
To address these limitations and extend current findings, subsequent
investigations should: (1) expand sampling diversity by including primary, upper-secondary, and vocational educators across institutional
contexts; (2) adopt longitudinal designs to disentangle temporal relationships between autonomy, self-efficacy, climate dimensions, and
job satisfaction; (3) implement mixed-methods approaches (e.g., integrating observational data with quantitative surveys) to triangulate selfreports and elucidate contextual mechanisms; (4) operationalize school
climate multidimensionally by examining distinct subdomains (e.g.,
instructional leadership, teacher collaboration, resource allocation) as
differential moderators; and (5) apply sampling-aware analytic techniques such as multilevel weighted least squares or Bayesian SEM to
account for nested data structures and survey design effects. Such
methodological advancements would enhance ecological validity and
inform precision policymaking for educator well-being. By addressing
these limitations, future research can build on the findings of this study
to develop more comprehensive strategies for enhancing teacher satisfaction and retention in various educational contexts.
