Diagnostic Modeling Exploration

Cognitive Diagnostic Models (CDMs) provide ﬁne-grained diagnostic feedback,
but their central component—the Q-matrix—remains costly and labor-intensive
to construct. This study explores the automated generation of Q-matrices using
general-purpose AI, including ChatGPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-pro, and Claude-sonnet-4.
We evaluated two prompting strategies (all-at-once and one-by-one) across TIMSS
2007, TIMSS 2011, and PISA 2012 mathematics assessments. Results show that
AI-generated Q-matrices approximate human baselines with competitive model ﬁt-
ting performance (AIC, BIC, log-likelihood, and SRMSR) and acceptable classiﬁ-
cation discrepancies. While AI predictions for larger and more complicated assess-
ments (TIMSS 07 and 11) were generally sparser than human-generated Q-matrices,
they still achieved equal or better ﬁt statistics under most CDMs. In contrast, for
the smaller and less complicated PISA 2012 assessment, AI-generated Q-matrices
matched human density and ﬁtting quality. Importantly, chatbot-human matching
accuracy remained high across models, with Gemini beneﬁting from all-at-once
prompting, ChatGPT-4o maintaining stable performance under both strategies, and
Claude showing sensitivity to prompt structure. These ﬁndings highlight both the
promise and current limitations of automated Q-matrix generation, underscoring
opportunities for integrating LLMs into scalable diagnostic assessment practices.
Introduction
Diagnostic measurement enables classiﬁcations on multiple latent variables rep-
resenting important (and often actionable) attributes (Rupp et al., 2010). Diagnostic
Classiﬁcation Models (DCM) or Cognitive Diagnostic Models (CDM) represent a
framework that has been relatively recently developed and is valued for its ability to
provide discrete, multivariate, and ﬁne-grained information toward the development
of targeted instructional support for examinees (Xin et al., 2022). The Q-matrix,
which delineates the relationships between latent attributes and items, plays a pivotal
role in mapping the intricate cognitive skills underlying assessment. The generation
of a Q-matrix is a complex task that has traditionally relied upon the cooperation be-
tween human experts in different measurement areas (e.g., content, psychometrics).
A challenge to the broader use of CDM is the time and cost intensiveness of
developing an integral component for model estimation, the Q-matrix (Zhao and
Huang, 2019), which supports understanding the relationship between skill/attribute
mastery and item responses. Buck et al. (1998) describe a typical procedure to build
a Q-matrix: (1) develop an initial list of skills, (2) construct an initial Q-matrix based

on what skills are required for each item, (3) analyze data using appropriate Diag-

Q-matrix based on statistics for each skill along with the theoretical impor-
tance of the skill. Steps 3 and 4 are repeated until a well-deﬁned Q-matrix
is achieved. In this procedure, step 2 is very time consuming if the item
pool is large. Steps 3 and 4 require large student response data to apply
statistical analysis.
To increase the efﬁciency and accuracy of building Q-matrices, some researchers
focused on applying different statistical analysis for Q-matrix validation (de la
Torre and Chiu, 2016; Ma and de la Torre, 2020a; Nájera et al., 2021), other re-
searchers have begun to direct their efforts to increasing the efﬁciency with which
Q-matrices can be generated (Chung, 2019; Li et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020; Zhao
and Huang, 2019). The time and resource intensiveness of developing Q-matrices
is the impetus for several lines of current research (see Zhao and Huang, 2019).
In demonstrating the potential efﬁciency of supervised learning text classiﬁcation
models, these researchers both reduce the time required by expert review and un-
derscore the need for examinee time and data as the outcome for their models. Ad-
ditionally, Zhao and Huang, with their reliance upon a content-expert-determined
Q-matrix as their outcome, concede their uncertainty on whether their effective
models approached a close attribute-to-item relationship or something less perfect
(see p. 277). Consistent with Xue and Bradshaw’s (2021) point, expert-designed Q-
matrices may lack precision in the attribute-to-item relationship and beneﬁt from
empirical input.
Yet, these efforts, to date, required examinee results and/or substantial amounts
of expert development and review. With the emergence of general large language
models (LLMs), the rise of artiﬁcial general intelligence (AGI) represents an un-
precedented opportunity for addressing challenges in the educational ﬁeld today
(Baidoo-Anu and Ansah, 2023). OpenAI’s ChatGPT, for instance, has proven valu-
able in educational assessment through item and response generation (Cheung et al.,
2023) as well as adaptive personalized learning and feedback (Xu et al., 2023).
Similar advances are evident in Anthropic’s Claude and Google DeepMind’s Gem-
ini, which demonstrate strong capabilities in producing coherent, context-sensitive
educational content and supporting interactive assessment design. Recent multi-
modal extensions of these models, such as ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Claude
Sonnet-4, further extend their utility by enabling interpretation of diverse inputs be-
yond text, including diagrams, graphs, and ﬁgure plots (Comanici et al., 2025; Ra-
machandran et al., 2025). This multimodal functionality allows AI agents to engage
with mathematical assessments in richer ways, such as interpreting plotted func-
tions, geometric ﬁgures, and statistical graphs, alongside traditional text-based prob-
lem solving. By bridging visual and textual modalities, these systems open new
opportunities for designing assessments that more closely mirror authentic disci-
plinary practices in mathematics and science, where reasoning often requires co-
ordinated interpretation of symbols, equations, and graphical representations (see
also Ray, 2023, pp. 140-142, for more on the challenges and shortcomings of
these systems).

Methods
In this exploratory research, we aimed to develop an Attribute-based Automatic Q-
Matrix Generator (AQMG) by leveraging state-of-the-art AI agents. Speciﬁcally, we
employed OpenAI’s ChatGPT-4o, Anthropic’s Claude-sonnet-4, and Google Deep-
Mind’s Gemini-2.5-pro as the core generative engines for Q-matrix prediction. The
overall framework of the AQMG is shown in Figure 1. To guide the generation pro-
cess, we implemented two distinct prompting strategies, each offering a different
pathway for constructing Q-matrices:
• One-by-one method: The AI agent receives the content of a single assessment
item along with the full list of attributes, and returns the corresponding attribute
vector (q-vector) for that item. This process is repeated until all items are coded,
producing the full Q-matrix iteratively.
• All-at-once method: The AI agent receives the content of all assessment items
and the complete list of attributes simultaneously, and returns the full Q-matrix
in a single step.
For each AI agent, we specify the same prompt under the same prompt method.
Generally, these prompts were structured to follow four steps:
1. Specify role and task: Clearly articulate the AI agent’s role and explicitly
deﬁne the objective of the chatbot (i.e., to generate a Q-matrix mapping as-
sessment items to attributes).
2. Understand content: Supply the chatbot with the full item content (provided
as PDF ﬁles) together with the corresponding attribute deﬁnitions to ensure
contextual understanding.
3. Set instructions and constraints: Establish key parameters for Q-matrix gen-
eration, including dimensionality, binary structure, and completeness con-

Step 1. Specify Role and Task
When articulating the desired task for a chatbot, it is essential to provide a clear
and speciﬁc deﬁnition of its intended purpose and functionality. In this ﬁrst step, we
designated the AI agent as an expert performing Q-vector prediction for a cognitive
diagnostic modeling (CDM) task in mathematics and deﬁned its role as generating
a Q-matrix that maps assessment items to the attributes they measure. To establish
a shared understanding of the expected output, we also supplied the chatbot with a
concise deﬁnition of the Q-matrix.
Step 2. Understand Content
To establish a shared understanding of the expected output, we provided the chat-
bot with a concise deﬁnition of the Q-matrix together with detailed deﬁnitions of
all attributes. In addition, we attached a PDF ﬁle containing the complete item con-
tent, including text, ﬁgures, item identiﬁers, response options, and answer keys. This
combination of resources ensured that the chatbot had all the necessary context to
generate Q-matrices in alignment with the intended task.
Step 3. Set Instructions and Constraints
We explicitly speciﬁed the rules and parameters that the AI agents were required
to follow when generating the Q-matrix. This step was critical for ensuring that the
outputs adhered to the structure and completeness expected in cognitive diagnostic
modeling. The prompt directed the AI to determine which attributes were required to
solve each item and to list only the attribute IDs rather than full attribute names. The
AI was also instructed to include the item ID shown on each PDF page to clearly
link each prediction to the corresponding assessment item. To prevent omissions,
we emphasized that no item should be left unassigned and that each attribute must
appear in at least one item within the ﬁnal dataset. By setting these constraints, we
aimed to produce a complete, binary-structured Q-matrix that accurately mapped all

Step 4. Prediction Method
In the prediction stage, we implemented two prompting strategies to generate the
Q-matrix. In the all-at-once method, the AI agent received all assessment items and
attributes simultaneously and produced the Q-matrix in a single batch. To safeguard
against omissions, the agent was instructed to conduct a self-check: if any attribute
was not assigned to at least one item, or if any item lacked an assigned attribute, the
system revisited the predictions and updated them accordingly. In contrast, the one-
by-one method required the AI agent to process each item individually, producing a
q-vector for that item before moving to the next. After all items were coded, the agent
again performed a self-check to ensure that every attribute was represented in the
dataset. Through these procedures, both methods embedded an internal veriﬁcation
step that enforced the structural completeness of the Q-matrix, thereby aligning the
AI-generated outputs with the requirements of cognitive diagnostic modeling.
Our experimental results demonstrated that the chatbots consistently maintained
the binary structure of the Q-matrix. On rare occasions, the output text contained
only a partial Q-matrix, but such dimensionality issues were easily resolved by re-
prompting the chatbot to generate a matrix with the correct dimensions. Importantly,
with our well-designed prompt method and embedded self-checking strategy, we ob-
served only three instances of incompleteness or error across a total of 180 runs.
Further examination indicated that these cases were not attributable to the prompt
design itself but to transient server-side issues, as the identical prompts successfully
produced complete Q-matrices on subsequent attempts. Thus, while earlier exper-
iments highlighted challenges with completeness, our revised approach effectively
ensured that the AI agents generated Q-matrices fully meeting the structural require-
ments of cognitive diagnostic modeling.
Voting Classiﬁcation
To enhance the robustness and consistency of the AI-generated Q-matrices, we
introduced a voting classiﬁcation procedure. Each prompt method (one-by-one and
all-at-once) was run multiple times (10 iterations in this study), and the ﬁnal Q-
matrix estimate was obtained by averaging the predictions across runs and applying a
majority-vote rule (over 50%) for each cell. Given the time cost and lower efﬁciency
of the one-by-one approach, voting classiﬁcation was applied primarily to the all-at-
once method. This procedure led to more stable and higher-quality Q-matrix outputs,
improved handling of ambiguity, and reduced bias in the AI agents’ predictions in a
single output.
To evaluate the effectiveness of these methods, we compared the Q-matrices gen-
erated by the AI agents with those produced by human experts, examining both
model ﬁtting statistics under different cognitive diagnostic models and the degree
of similarity between them.

Experimental Results
The Trends in Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) is an international as-
sessment of the trends in mathematics and science achievements, which is run by
the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement. Many
studies have applied the CDMs to the TIMSS datasets (Foy et al., 2013; Lee et al.,
2011; Martin, 2008). In the experiment, we chose the TIMSS 07 and 11 data. The
dataset comprised 25 items for TIMSS 07 and 26 items for TIMSS 11, each item
contained both the item stem and response options. The entire item content was
sourced from TIMSS technical reports (Foy et al., 2013; Williams et al., 2007). The
Q-matrices were speciﬁed in the previous research (Evran, 2019; Lee et al., 2011).
The response data was from the “CDM” R package (v8.2-6, Robitzsch et al., 2020),
which included a total of 698 students for TIMSS 07 and 1,010 students for TIMSS
11. In TIMSS 07 (Lee et al., 2011), the matrix was utilized as the true value, while
for TIMSS 11, we considered 4 Q-matrices (Evran, 2019) as the true candidates. The
15 attributes measured by items are listed in Table 1.
In addition to TIMSS 07 and 11 data, we also introduce the Programme for In-
ternational Student Assessment (PISA), a large-scale international assessment of 15-
year-old students’ competencies in reading, mathematics, and science, coordinated
by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). In this
study, we focused on the PISA 2012 mathematics dataset. Following prior research,
we used a set of 12 publicly released mathematics items administered across re-
gions including Shanghai, Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan. Each item included a
full problem statement, response options, and in some cases accompanying ﬁgures.
The Q-matrix for these items was generated through expert calibration as described
in Zhang et al. (2025), which deﬁned six key mathematical competencies as the at-
tributes to be measured. The six attributes are listed in Table 2. The expert-based
Q-matrix was developed through a calibration process by a panel including middle
school teachers, mathematics teaching and research staff, mathematics education ex-
perts and doctoral students. The attribute with more than 50% proportion in experts’
calibration was determined as selected and was calibrated. For validation, we em-
ployed the student response data comprising 6,561 students across the four regions
(Shanghai: 1,763; Hong Kong: 1,376; Macau: 1,602; Taiwan: 1,820).
The expert-generated Q-matrices in both TIMSS and PISA served as the bench-
mark standard against which we compared the Q-matrices predicted by the AI agents.
In our revised method, we employed state-of-the-art chatbots—OpenAI’s Chat-
GPT, Anthropic’s Claude, and Google DeepMind’s Gemini—rather than relying
solely on Python API calls. This change reﬂects the rapid development and acces-
sibility of these applications in recent months. A key advantage of using chatbots
is their user-friendly interface: ﬁles containing assessment items can be attached di-
rectly, allowing educators and researchers to interact with the models without requir-
ing knowledge of Python or other programming languages. This makes the approach
more practical for adoption in educational contexts. In addition, modern chatbots
provide several technical advantages. They support multimodal inputs, enabling the
integration of text, images, and even audio in the assessment process; they offer im-
mediate interaction and iterative reﬁnement of outputs; and they beneﬁt from contin-

Chatbot-Human Matching Accuracy
In this experiment, we approached the AQMGs as an unsupervised clustering
task in machine learning. Given that the Q-matrix was sparse, the classiﬁcation task
was inherently imbalanced, characterized by a higher prevalence of 0s compared
to 1s in the Q-matrix. AUC is one good metric to evaluate the imbalanced cluster-
ing method, but in the research, the chatbot cannot provide the predicted probabil-
ity. Instead, we introduced precision, F1-score, and geometric mean (G-mean) to
evaluate the classiﬁcation accuracy. In diagnostic classiﬁcation, because high false-
positive rates result in the oversight of instances where true nonmastery exists, poten-
tially leading to the administration of unnecessary treatments, precision (true posi-
tive/(true positive + false positive)) is a good metric to indicate the false-positive rate
(i.e., a high precision indicates low false positive). The F1 score is a metric com-
monly used in binary classiﬁcation tasks, combining both precision and recall into
a single value. It is particularly useful in situations where there is an imbalance be-
tween the number of instances in each class. The G-mean is the root of the product
of class-wise sensitivity. This measure tries to maximize the accuracy on each of the

We used the exploratory analysis performance to compare different Q-matrices
and validate the most appropriate Q-matrix for the TIMSS 07 and TIMSS 11 data

diagnostic classiﬁcation models (DCMs; i.e., DINA, DINO, RRUM, GDINA, LLM).
The model ﬁt results for all models were evaluated by AIC, BIC, loglikelihood and
Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMSR) statistics. All models were ﬁtted
using the “GDINA” R package (Ma and de la Torre, 2020b). The results are shown
in Tables 7-9.
The model ﬁtting results for TIMSS 07 and TIMSS 11 show nuanced differences
between human- and AI-generated Q-matrices (Tables 7 and 8). In most cases, the
AI-generated Q-matrices achieved better model ﬁtting statistics than their human-

ception arises with ChatGPT-4o: due to its smaller token size limit, the generated
Q-matrices are generally sparser than those produced by humans. This sparsity oc-
casionally leads to higher AIC and BIC values and lower log-likelihood estimates
than the human-generated Q-matrices, suggesting that while structurally valid, the
AI-produced matrices may capture less information about item-attribute relation-
ships in larger assessments. These results highlight how input constraints can shape
the efﬁciency of model ﬁt indices, even when the outputs are otherwise coherent.

data alignment across all AI agents. Both human- and AI-generated Q-matrices
achieved SRMSR values within acceptable thresholds, conﬁrming that residual-
based ﬁt remained adequate. This demonstrates that, although AIC and BIC values
reﬂect efﬁciency limitations in some AI-generated matrices, the underlying ability
of these Q-matrices to reproduce observed student response patterns is not compro-
mised. In other words, the AI-generated Q-matrices may not always be the most
parsimonious representations, but they nonetheless provide satisfactory data ﬁtting
for cognitive diagnostic modeling.

Grouping Discrepancy Analysis
To conduct this comparison in the TIMSS 11 dataset, which includes 4 differ-
ent human experts generated Q-matrices, we selected one of the human-generated
Q-matrices as a reference point. We then compared the classiﬁcation results of the
other three human-generated Q-matrices to this reference, as well as the classiﬁcation
results of the chatbot-generated Q-matrices, all within the same Diagnostic Classi-
ﬁcation Models (DCMs). For instance, under the DINA model, we ﬁrst chose one
human-generated Q-matrix as the reference classiﬁcation result. Next, we calculated
the classiﬁcation matching rates of the remaining human-generated Q-matrices (Hu-
man 2-4) and all AI-generated Q-matrices relative to this reference. This process was
repeated four times under the DINA model, with each of the four human-generated
Q-matrices (Human 1-4) being used as the reference in turn. This approach allowed
us to evaluate the classiﬁcation discrepancies both among the human-generated Q-
matrices and between the human-generated and AI-generated Q-matrices.

one-by-one prompting strategies.

Discussion and Future Directions
This study examined the capacity of state-of-the-art multimodal large language
models (LLMs)—ChatGPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-pro, and Claude-sonnet-4—to generate
Q-matrices for cognitive diagnostic modeling across three international assessments
(TIMSS 07, TIMSS 11, and PISA 2012). Several important patterns emerged.

First, AI-generated Q-matrices consistently produced strong data-ﬁtting perfor-
mance across diagnostic classiﬁcation models (DCMs). In TIMSS 07 and TIMSS
11, AI outputs tended to be sparser than human expert Q-matrices, reﬂecting both to-
ken input constraints (notably in ChatGPT-4o) and the challenges of modeling larger
item-attribute sets. Despite this sparsity, AI-generated Q-matrices frequently yielded
comparable or superior AIC, BIC, and log-likelihood statistics relative to human
matrices, suggesting that LLMs can generate structurally lean yet effective represen-
tations. In PISA 2012, where the item and attribute set was smaller, AI-generated
Q-matrices matched human density more closely, demonstrating that dataset size
moderates the alignment between human and AI outputs.
Second, chatbot-human matching analyses revealed that AI agents achieve accept-
able ﬁdelity, though human-human consistency remains higher. Discrepancy patterns
varied across DCMs: more ﬂexible models such as GDINA and LLM attenuated
differences between human-human and human-AI outputs, while restrictive models
such as DINA and DINO magniﬁed them. This ﬁnding suggests that the choice of
DCM plays a crucial role in determining the practical viability of AI-generated Q-
matrices.
Third, the prompting strategy inﬂuenced performance in model-speciﬁc ways.
ChatGPT-4o maintained stable accuracy across both all-at-once and one-by-one
prompts, indicating robust contextual reasoning. Gemini-2.5-pro performed best
under the all-at-once strategy, consistent with its optimization for batch reason-
ing and large token processing. Claude-sonnet-4, however, showed instability un-
der the one-by-one approach, particularly in TIMSS 11, producing Q-matrices with
higher discrepancies and reduced reliability. This underscores the sensitivity of some
LLMs to prompt structure and highlights the importance of tailoring strategies to
model architecture.
Building on these ﬁndings, several directions warrant further exploration. One pri-
ority is improving the completeness and density control of AI-generated Q-matrices.
Although our self-checking strategies reduced errors, occasional sparsity suggests
the need for adaptive prompts or ﬁne-tuned models that can dynamically balance
parsimony with full coverage of attributes. Another avenue is the development of
hybrid AI-human workﬂows in which LLMs generate initial drafts and experts re-
ﬁne them. Such an approach would preserve the efﬁciency gains of automation while
ensuring accuracy for high-stakes uses.
Future work should also investigate model-speciﬁc optimization. Given Gem-
ini’s strength in batch reasoning, ChatGPT-4o’s robustness to prompt variation,
and Claude’s sensitivity to item-level prompts, tailoring strategies to individual
model architectures could further enhance reliability. Expanding beyond mathe-
matics to domains such as reading and science, and testing LLM-generated Q-
matrices on larger and more diverse assessments, will be essential for evaluating
generalizability. Finally, the integration of multimodal capabilities—especially
through models like ChatGPT-4o—offers opportunities to process diagrams, ﬁgures,
and non-textual item content, which are increasingly common in modern assess-
ments. Longer-term, interactive diagnostic systems where LLMs iteratively reﬁne
Q-matrices with expert or learner feedback could democratize the use of cognitive
diagnostic modeling, lowering barriers for educators and scaling its adoption.
