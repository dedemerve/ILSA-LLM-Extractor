A B S T R A C T
As artificial intelligence (AI) and generative AI (GenAI) technologies become increasingly inte­
grated into everyday life, the need for validated tools that measure people's knowledge about AI 
grows. Here, we present the development and validation of a theoretically driven, performance- 
based scale for assessing AI and GenAI knowledge. The scale is grounded in a two-axial frame­
work. One axis captures three knowledge types: content knowledge (what AI is and where it is 
encountered), procedural knowledge (how AI systems operate and are used), and epistemic 
knowledge (what features and construction processes characterize AI outputs). The other axis 
encompasses three knowledge domains: technology-related knowledge (AI systems), user-related 
knowledge (users' interaction with AI), and society-related knowledge (the social and ethical 
implications of AI). Based on an online survey of 800 internet-using adults from Israel, the 26- 
item scale was evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis, which demonstrated an acceptable 
model fit. It was further validated through two-stage structural equation modeling and group 
comparisons. Overall, the scale was found to be both valid and practically insightful: while it 
reproduces the expected relationships with additional constructs (e.g., trust in GenAI, attitudes 
toward AI) and expected differences between demographic groups, it also provides nuanced in­
sights on the intricacies of AI knowledge. For example, the scale indicates that the relationship 
between trust in GenAI and knowledge about AI is grounded in both epistemic and societal 
knowledge. Thus, this novel tool affords more precise investigations into how different types and 
domains of AI knowledge relate to perceptions, behaviors, and decision-making in an AI-mediated 
world.

on different AI-infused technologies. Younger and educated individuals are reported to possess greater knowledge about the

2024). Such disparities in AI literacy can lead to a digital divide, where access to the benefits of using AI becomes inequitable (Bentley 
et al., 2024; Boˇzi´c, 2023; C. Wang et al., 2024). The emergence of generative AI (GenAI) has accelerated public engagement with these 
technologies, further highlighting concerns about literacy gaps (Bozkurt, 2024; Capraro et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). Studies show that 
GenAI users, males, and people with higher education have greater knowledge about AI than GenAI non-users, females, and people 
with lower education levels (Bitton et al., 2024; Gesser-Edelsburg et al., 2024; Gillespie et al., 2023; Goldenthal et al., 2021; Greussing 
et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024; Selwyn & Gallo Cordoba, 2022). The increasing prevalence of AI technologies, coupled with widening social 
disparities in their understanding and use, underscores the importance of assessing AI literacy across diverse populations. AI literacy is 
defined as “an individual's ability to clearly explain how AI technologies work and impact society, as well as to use them in an ethical 
and responsible manner and to effectively communicate and collaborate with them” (Chiu et al., 2024, p. 4). It encompasses 
knowledge, skills, and attitudes (Knoth et al., 2024), all of which require reliable tools for assessment and monitoring.
Knowledge about AI and GenAI plays a key role in the acceptance and use of the technology (Handoko et al., 2024; Kelly et al., 
2023; Kühl et al., 2023; Y. J. Lee et al., 2024). More AI knowledge is generally associated with positive attitudes and greater trust 
(Basch et al., 2025; Gillespie et al., 2023; Kaya et al., 2024; Obenza et al., 2024); however, the relationships between knowledge, 
attitudes, trust, and behavior are not entirely clear-cut. Knowledge of AI risks and biases can lead to lower trust (Gillespie et al., 2023; 
Liehner et al., 2023; Scantamburlo et al., 2024), and knowledge is not necessarily a predictor of attitudes (Kovacevic & Demic, 2024). 
Some studies suggest that experience, rather than knowledge, has the greater impact on attitudes and trust (Heezen, 2023). Such 
findings suggest complex relationships between AI knowledge and people's behavior and choices regarding AI, indicating that different 
dimensions of knowledge may play distinct roles in these relationships.
This article introduces a new AI literacy measurement tool, adding to existing scales by addressing several key needs. First, the tool 
offers a performance-based and scalable measurement approach, contrasting with the predominantly self-report formats used in 
existing scales (Lintner, 2024). By performance-based, we mean assessing objective knowledge using questions scored as correct or 
incorrect. Second, the tool focuses, while elaborating, on the knowledge component of AI literacy, whereas several other tools do not 
distinguish between knowledge, skills, and attitudes (Almatrafi et al., 2024; Lintner, 2024). Third, it assesses knowledge of both AI and 
GenAI. Despite the growing influence of GenAI technologies, few current scales explicitly address these (see Gokcearslan et al., 2024; 
Jin et al., 2025; S. Lee & Park, 2024 for those that do).
Notably, the new tool addresses different types and domains of AI knowledge. These distinctions are grounded in cognitive theory, 
which suggests that different types of knowledge, such as conceptual (facts, principles) and procedural (actions, processes), involve 
distinct cognitive processes and qualities (De Jong & Ferguson-Hessler, 1996; Krathwohl, 2002). Types and domains of AI knowledge 
refer to thematic areas or topics within the broader sphere of AI. This conceptual framework draws on both established AI literacy 
frameworks and the internationally validated PISA 2025 framework for assessing scientific knowledge (OECD, 2023). These dis­
tinctions enable a nuanced assessment of individuals' understanding of AI, uncovering underlying relationships between different 
knowledge components and their associated constructs, such as attitudes and trust.
This article describes the development and validation process of the measurement tool, presenting findings from a representative 
survey of adult internet users. Thus, the article contributes to the conceptualization and empirical research of AI knowledge and 
literacy, confirming some previously established findings in this research field and revealing new insights into the associations be­
tween types of knowledge, attitudes, and trust.
2. Theoretical background
2.1. Conceptualizing AI literacy
AI and GenAI follow other information and communication technologies whose emergence has shaped our understanding of 21st- 
century competencies (OECD, 2019, pp. 1–146). Digital-age literacy encompasses multiple dimensions, including cognitive di­
mensions of problem-solving and creativity, sociocultural and contextual dimensions related to living in technologically enhanced 
societies, emotional dimensions that regulate attitudes and behaviors, and epistemological dimensions concerning the evaluation of 
information produced by these technologies (Aguilera et al., 2020; Eshet, 2004; Martínez-Bravo et al., 2022). Accordingly, AI literacy 
is recently conceptualized as individuals’ competency “to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively 
with AI; and use AI as a tool online” in different contexts (Long & Magerko, 2020, p. 2).
That said, existing conceptualizations of AI literacy lack a unified framework or included components. Long and Magerko (2020, 
pp. 1–16) propose a framework that defines AI competencies across five themes: what AI is, what AI can do, how AI works, how AI 
should be used, and how people perceive AI. Touretzky and colleagues (2023) recognize five knowledge domains: perception, rep­
resentation and reasoning, learning, natural interaction, and societal impact. Both frameworks emphasize technological knowledge, e. 
g., how AI works and what it does, while addressing human-AI interactions and the social implications of such technologies. Thus, they 
effectively cover three prominent dimensions: the technology itself, its use in various scenarios, and its societal aspects. This triptych 
approach is common among existing frameworks, which also highlight competencies for evaluating and appraising AI systems and 
their products, as well as ethical considerations (Almatrafi et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2024).

Recent years have seen various AI literacy scales with diverse frameworks, assessment tools, and measured constructs (e.g., Carolus

While multiple AI literacy scales are available, most rely on self-reports, and only a few exist for the standard assessment of GenAI

Zhou's (2023) scale focuses only on certain literacy dimensions, combining several knowledge-related items with measurements of 
perceived values and risks, and intentions to use GenAI. Lee and Park's (2024) scale focuses on ChatGPT, measuring technical pro­
ficiency, critical evaluation, communication proficiency, creative application, and ethical competence. These and similar efforts, 
however, often focus on AI literacy as a holistic construct, rely on self-reports of subjective knowledge, or focus on particular aspects of 
AI literacy or GenAI technologies.
These and additional conceptual and empirical gaps, which are further detailed below, highlight the need for a performance-based, 
multifaceted instrument that distinctly measures AI and GenAI knowledge. This paper presents, therefore, the development and 
validation process of a performance-based two-axial scale. Inspired by MacKenzie and colleagues (2011), the process consisted of four 
stages: (I) conceptualizing the theoretical construct, (II) developing the measurement and scale, (III) evaluating and refining the scale, 
and (IV) assessing the scale's validity. The following describes these stages, detailing the procedures and results that led to the vali­
dation of the proposed instrument.
3. The AI-GenAI concept inventory
3.1. Conceptualizing a two-axial framework for AI knowledge (stage I)
AI literacy encompasses three interconnected components: a cognitive component involving knowledge and understanding of 
concepts and theories, a behavioral component pertaining to skills and practices, and an affective component reflected in attitudes 
(Knoth et al., 2024). Focusing specifically on the cognitive aspects of AI literacy and addressing different dimensions of AI knowledge 
provides higher-resolution insights into strengths and weaknesses at both individual and societal levels.
Given the growing ubiquity of GenAI, assessments of AI knowledge should address both AI and GenAI (Jin et al., 2025) for two 
reasons. First, while GenAI has unique characteristics, it is an instantiation of AI (Chan & Hu, 2023; Chen & Feng, 2024, pp. 325–329) 
and thus shares core foundational knowledge, e.g., bias, model training, etc. Second, although the public has nuanced views about 
different applications of AI, it still lacks the technical vocabulary to describe those differences (Field Reid et al., 2023; Schumann 
Florance, 2024). That is, the public largely conflates AI and GenAI in everyday discourse and use. Hence, conceptualizing AI 
knowledge nowadays should include general aspects of AI and particularities characterizing GenAI.
The theoretical framework draws on existing conceptions of AI literacy and the various forms of knowledge it involves, such as 
technical, social, or normative knowledge. It depicts two axes of knowledge dimensions. The first identifies three knowledge domains, 
inspired by the literature: (1) AI technology addresses the technology itself, (2) AI and its users addresses application of the technology, 
and (3) AI and society considers the technology's social aspects. We refer to this dimension as the TUS axis (Technology-Users-Society). 
For the second axis we drew on the PISA 2025 Science framework (OECD, 2023), which identifies three types of scientific knowledge: 
(1) content knowledge, referring to facts, concepts, ideas, and theories established by science, (2) procedural knowledge, referring to 
scientific procedures and practices used to construct content knowledge, and (3) epistemic knowledge, referring to the defining 
features of scientific knowledge and its construction processes. We find this division suitable for considering AI knowledge. Accord­
ingly, we consider content AI knowledge to focus on basic concepts related to what AI and GenAI are and where they can be found in 
everyday life. Procedural AI knowledge focuses on the workings and use of AI and GenAI technologies. Epistemic AI knowledge is 
relevant too, as AI and GenAI are information-generating technologies. In particular, large language models (LLMs) create and mediate 
knowledge, while their underlying mechanisms shape the nature, reliability, and limitations of this knowledge. Hence, we consider

4. Method
An online survey was deployed via Qualtrics in October–November 2024 among participants who represent the adult internet user 
population in Israel, by gender, age, and education level (see Appendix A.1). The focus on an internet user population reflects the 
current requirement of GenAI for internet access and accommodates data collection via an online survey. To recruit participants, we 
used a polling service and employed quota sampling. Participants were compensated in accordance with their agreement with the 
polling service. To increase the quality of the gathered data, we excluded from the initial 1143 participants 55 (4.8 %) who did not 
complete the survey, 55 (4.8 %) who completed the questionnaire in under 5 min, and 233 (20 %) participants who answered 
incorrectly to at least one of the three attention checks – resulting with a final count of 800 participants. In terms of socio-technological 
context, the population of Israel is relatively highly educated, with a 50 % tertiary education rate, and a 92 % internet penetration rate 
(Adult Education Level, 2022; World Bank Open Data, 2022). Israel is ranked ninth in The Global AI Index (n.d.), and the public's 
experience with ChatGPT increased from 16 % in 2023 to 46 % in 2024 (Greussing et al., 2025). The study was ethically approved by 
the institution's Behavioral Sciences Research Ethics Committee (approval number 2024-084).

● Attitudes toward AI were based on Calice and colleagues (2022), using a six-statement Likert scale (Appendix A.3), ranging between

● Trust in GenAI was measured on an 11-item Likert scale (Appendix A.4), drawn from Greussing et al. (2025) and ranging from 1 to 5

● Accumulating experience with GenAI. Participants reported whether they had heard of or used five current GenAI tools: ChatGPT, 
Microsoft's Copilot, Google's Gemini, Perplexity AI, and Claude (for descriptive statistics, see Appendix A.5). Drawn from 
(Greussing et al., 2025), the scale ranged between 1 (I am hearing about it here for the first time) and 6 (I use it daily), as 3 indicated 
‘I used it once or twice,’ and 4–6 indicated varying degrees of regular use (I use it several times a month, a week, or daily). 
Interested in regular use regardless of degree vs. minimal experience or none at all, we recoded the values so reported regular use (i. 
e., values 4–6) was recoded 2, reported use of GenAI only once or twice (i.e., value 3) was recoded 1, and no experience with the 
technology (values 1–2) was recoded 0. Summing up the recoded experience with the five GenAI tools, the accumulating expe­
rience was then divided into four categories, in line with the ordinal nature of the value: no or almost no experience with GenAI 
tools (scores 0–1, n = 338, 42.3 %), limited experience with GenAI tools (scores 2–4, n = 287, 35.9 %), varied experience with 
GenAI tools (scores 5–7, n = 104, 13 %), and extensive experience with GenAI tools (scores 8+, n = 71, 8.9 %).
● Analytic AI knowledge was measured using a performance-based test assessing participants' understanding of how training data 
patterns affect machine learning (i.e., a type of AI) outputs. This task targeted higher-order cognitive processing—specifically, 
analysis (Anderson et al., 2001)—by requiring participants to break down patterns in a toy-model dataset, analyze its biases, and 
infer the influence of these biases on AI outputs. For example, participants were shown a toy-model training set containing images 
of either orange cats or small black ants on green leaves. Participants were then asked about the AI's chances of successfully 
recognizing a cat in an image of a small black cat against a green background and to explain why. The test included 10 questions, 
five for each of two distinct toy-model datasets. Each dataset consisted of 6–8 images, half depicting one object (an orange cat or a 
beetle car) and half another object (a black ant on a green leaf or a white sailboat against blue sky and sea). The five follow-up 
questions per dataset increased in difficulty, requiring participants to predict and explain the AI's behavior. The analytic AI 
knowledge score was calculated as the total number of correct responses (range: 0–10, n = 800, M = 4.71, SD = 1.82). For the full 
wording and images, see Appendix A.6.

4.2. Data analysis
Statistical analyses were performed on R version 4.5.0 and IBM SPSS Statistics version 29. Utilizing R, we assessed whether our 
suggested items are consistent with the theoretical two-axial construct by conducting confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using a 
diagonally weighted least squares (DWLS) estimator and 3-factor models. To validate the tool's construct, we employed structural 
equation modeling (SEM) in R with a diagonally weighted least squares (DWLS) estimator. We used IBM SPSS to assess the internal 
consistency of the factors using Cronbach's alpha. Additionally, to compare group differences in the AI knowledge scores, we con­
ducted independent-sample t-tests for dichotomous variables (i.e., gender) and one-way ANOVA with Tukey HSD post hoc tests for 
ordinal variables (accumulating experience with GenAI tools, age groups, and education levels). For variables in which the assumption 
of homogeneity of variance was violated according to Levene's test, we employed Welch ANOVA and Games-Howell post hoc tests, as 
detailed below.
5. Scale evaluation and refinement (stage III)
To assess the relevance and reliability of the suggested theoretical construct, we conducted two CFAs, before and after refining the

scale. The two-axial framework served as the foundational rationale guiding the scale's development from initial design to item 
creation. The nine measured knowledge categories in the framework define and form the latent constructs rather than merely 
reflecting them. In contrast to CFA, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) is recommended primarily for reflective constructs, and its use in 
this case may undermine construct validity and introduce biases to structural parameter estimates (MacKenzie et al., 2011, pp. 295-6). 
Hence, to maintain consistency with our theoretical model and adhere to established guidelines for formative constructs, we conducted 
two successive CFAs, each using a 3-factor model to assess goodness-of-fit for both theoretical axes—the CPE and TUS axes.
The first CFA was performed on a random subsample of 400 responses. After confirming the absence of outliers, we used a DWLS 
estimator in R, which is appropriate for ordinal and categorical data (Mîndril˘a, 2010). Model fit was assessed using multiple indices 
and accepted thresholds (Dilbeck, 2017): a significant chi-square test (p ≤.05), the root mean square error of approximations 
(RMSEA) ≤.08, the standardized root mean square of the residuals (SRMSR) ≤.10, and the comparative fit index (CFI) ≥.90. For the 
CPE model, the results were: χ2(321) = 1301.927, p < .001, RMSEA = .088, SRMSR = .142, CFI = .838. For the TUS model: χ2(321) =
1425.164, p < .001, RMSEA = .093, SRMSR = .144, CFI = .817. Factor loadings indicated that 26 out of 27 items had significant 
loadings above the .30 threshold (Hair et al., 2019, pp. 151–152). Item 14 failed to meet this criterion in both models (CPE: .298; TUS: 
.279) and was subsequently removed from the scale (see Appendix A.7).
Following the refinement, a second CFA was conducted on the remaining 400 responses using the same DWLS estimation method. 
The revised CPE model demonstrated improved fit: χ2(296) = 821.888, p < .001, RMSEA = .067, SRMSR = .120, CFI = .868. Similarly, 
the TUS model yielded: χ2(296) = 927.178, p < .001, RMSEA = .073, SRMSR = .124, CFI = .841. Although SRMSR and CFI values did 
not meet the ideal thresholds, all 26 items demonstrated significant loadings (>.30) across both models (see Appendix A.8), indicating 
structural consistency. The refined scale, therefore, shows improved psychometric properties. Although some limitations in model fit 
indices are apparent, the results for both theoretical axes near the cutoff values, thus “can be interpreted as evidence in favor of the 
validity of the hypothesized model” (MacKenzie et al., 2011, p. 313). Internal consistency, assessed via Cronbach's alpha, is reported in 
Table 3. Although some of these values are modest, they fall within acceptable ranges for multidimensional formative scales (see 
Stadler et al., 2021; Taber, 2018) and are supported by the consistent item loadings across the two CFAs. Thus, the Cronbach's alpha's 
results indicate the scale is useful, while leaving room for future improvement.
5.1. Putting the construct into use
Having a valid construct, we utilized the two-axial scale and the gathered data to demonstrate the insights that the scale can 
provide, among others.
Overall, the majority of participants (64.9 %) answered correctly on more than half of the questions. Comparing the proportioned 
scores across knowledge types (CPE) reveals that while the local adult internet using population is most knowledgeable about the 
workings and use of AI technologies (i.e., procedural knowledge, M = .667, SD = .238), and least knowledgeable about the features 
and construction processes that characterize AI outputs (i.e., epistemic knowledge, M = .545, SD = .262), there are no dramatic 
differences between knowledge type (for content knowledge: M = .575, SD = .224). However, comparing knowledge scores across 
dimensions (TUS), depicts a slightly different image: while the local population is most knowledgeable about the use of the technology 
(i.e., AI and its user, M = .643, SD = .221) and the technology itself (i.e., AI technology, M = .610, SD = .236), its knowledge about the 
social aspects of the technology (i.e., AI and society, M = .465, SD = .260) is relatively limited. These results highlight the gap between 
understanding how to utilize the technology and the potential implications of such utilization.

6. Assessing scale validity (stage IV)
In the fourth and final stage of scale validation, we evaluated whether the responses to the scale behave as expected (MacKenzie 
et al., 2011), inter alia, within a network of theoretically related constructs, also known as a nomological network or nomological 
validity (Kock et al., 2024). Specifically, we (1) assessed interrelations among the three factors within each of the two latent constructs 
(CPE and TUS), (2) examined the associations between AI knowledge scores and theoretically relevant external constructs—analytic AI 
knowledge, trust in GenAI, and attitudes toward AI—and (3) tested for known-group differences in AI knowledge based on partici­
pants' demographics and experience with GenAI.
For the first and second steps, we employed structural equation modeling (SEM). We tested three models: a model using the overall 
AI knowledge score (CFI = .819, TLI = .811, RMSEA = .06, SRMSR = .067), thus assessing the scale behavior as a whole; the CPE 
construct (CFI = .883, TLI = .877, RMSEA = .06, SRMSR = .11); and the TUS construct (CFI = .824, TLI = .814, RMSEA = .059, 
SRMSR = .067). Although not all indices met ideal thresholds for goodness of fit (i.e., CFI≥.95, TLI> .9, RMSEA≤.06, and SRMR≤
.08), the values are sufficiently close, thus indicating relatively good fit (Rosenthal, 2017). Diagrams of SEM results are presented in 
Figs. 1–3 (see also Appendices Tables A.9-A.11).
The results of the three models show consistent patterns. For the first step, the intercorrelations among the latent factors were 
positive and high in both models. For the CPE construct, factor association ranged from r = .621 to r = .825 (p < .001), and for the TUS 
construct, from r = .847 to r = .995 (p < .001). These results suggest that while both models reflect cohesive constructs, the CPE model 
better captures differentiation among its subcomponents due to its comparatively lower inter-factor associations.
For the second step, evaluating the scale's nomological validity, the SEMs also examined the factors' associations with three 
additional constructs: while analytic AI knowledge functioned as a parallel and slightly different construct, trust in GenAI and attitudes 
toward AI are constructs frequently linked to AI knowledge and AI literacy. As expected, small to moderate positive associations 
emerged between analytic AI knowledge and the two-axial knowledge scores, ranging from r = .244 (content knowledge) to r = .447 
(society-related knowledge), all p < .001. These findings confirm conceptual overlap with analytic AI knowledge while also supporting 
the distinct focus of each. Trust in GenAI and attitudes toward AI demonstrated significant relationships with AI knowledge, although 
these relationships were inconsistent in nature. Specifically, associations between AI knowledge and trust in GenAI were negative and 
limited to overall AI knowledge (r = −.144, p = .016), epistemic knowledge (r = −.287, p < .001), and society-related knowledge (r =
−.245, p < .001). No significant associations were found with content, procedural, technology-related, or user-related knowledge. A 
significant negative association was also observed between trust and analytic AI knowledge (CPE: r = −.325, p < .001; TUS: r = −.368, 
p < .001), reinforcing the associations found with the overall AI knowledge score. Regarding attitudes toward AI, no association was 
found between attitudes and overall AI knowledge. The strong relationship between attitudes and trust in GenAI suggests that the 
relationship between overall AI knowledge and attitudes toward AI was mediated by the negative relationship with trust. Simulta­

7. Discussion
This study presents a systematic four-stage process for developing and validating a two-dimensional framework and corresponding 
performance-based measurement instrument for assessing AI and GenAI knowledge. The two-axial theoretical framework is inspired 
by the PISA 2025 Science framework (OECD, 2023), which includes content, procedural, and epistemic knowledge (CPE), and by 
existing conceptions of AI literacy (e.g., Yuan et al., 2024), which encompass three dimensions: AI technology, AI and its user, and AI 
and society (TUS). Following initial scale development and content validity assessment, we conducted two sequential CFAs using 
responses from 800 internet-using adults, which resulted in a refined 26-item scale with good model fit and significant factor loadings. 
Subsequent validation through SEM and known-group analyses confirmed the scale's construct validity. The scale as a whole and both 
scale dimensions performed as expected through the SEMs, demonstrating strong positive associations between knowledge types and 
domains, alongside small to moderate associations with analytical AI knowledge. The scale also exhibited significant associations with 
trust in GenAI and attitudes toward AI, consistent with existing literature (Bitton et al., 2024; Carolus et al., 2023; Pinski & Benlian, 
2024; Tully et al., 2025); however, these associations varied across knowledge types and dimensions (detailed below). Thus, the paper 
offers theoretical, methodological, and empirical contributions.
The theoretical contribution lies in the conceptualization of AI knowledge from two orthogonal perspectives (Table 1), juxtaposing 
two AI dimensions. The first TUS dimension focuses on three knowledge domains: technology-related, addressing the AI system; user- 
related, addressing users’ interaction with AI; and society-related, addressing the social and ethical implications of AI. The second CPE 
dimension focuses on three knowledge types: content knowledge, addressing what AI and GenAI are and where they are encountered; 
procedural knowledge, addressing how AI and GenAI work; and epistemic knowledge, considering how underlying mechanisms of AI 
and GenAI shape their outputs and what characterizes them. By distinguishing between forms of AI knowledge, thus proposing a more 
comprehensive conceptual map of AI knowledge, the framework enriches previous conceptualizations of AI literacy (see, e.g., 
Almatrafi et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2021). Specifically, the two-axial framework draws on the globally acknowledged conceptualization of 
science literacy (see OECD, 2023) and adapts it to the AI domain, while building on existing separations between technological, user, 
and societal aspects (Almatrafi et al., 2024; Lintner, 2024) and systematically integrating the two perspectives. Furthermore, by 
conceptualizing epistemic knowledge as a distinct component of AI knowledge, the framework attends to the unique challenges and 
related growing concerns of evaluating AI-generated information (e.g., Kidd & Birhane, 2023; Spitale et al., 2023).
The proposed measurement tool offers a methodological contribution to the assessment of AI knowledge and broader AI literacy by 
addressing key gaps in current assessment approaches. The performance-based tool overcomes biases inherent in self-assessment (e.g., 
Snibsøer et al., 2018), which burden most available tools as they assess self-reported knowledge (Lintner, 2024). The tool responds to 
the growing prominence of GenAI and the unique challenges and opportunities it entails (e.g., Gesser-Edelsburg et al., 2024; Spitale 
et al., 2023), and it enables a more precise empirical examination of the knowledge component of AI literacy. Furthermore, the 
research tool opens new avenues for assessing AI knowledge and calibrating educational or training programs more efficiently. For 
example, in the hands of education policymakers, the scale can be used to evaluate baseline AI knowledge across different de­
mographic groups. By identifying specific knowledge gaps (such as a deficit in society-related knowledge, as observed in this study), 
they could design differentiated training programs that target these gaps rather than a generic AI awareness campaign. For another 
example, healthcare organizations implementing AI diagnostic tools could use the scale to identify staff members with low epistemic 
knowledge. This would enable targeted training on AI limitations and on output verification procedures to cultivate reflective, cautious 
reliance on such systems.
Lastly, the two-axial scale provides empirical contributions by measuring distinct types and domains of AI knowledge and their 
relationships with trust and attitudes toward AI, thereby offering insights into the nature of AI knowledge itself. Survey results revealed 
that participants were least knowledgeable about the underlying mechanisms and characteristics of AI outputs (i.e., epistemic 
knowledge), as well as the social and ethical implications of AI (i.e., society-related knowledge). Contrastingly, participants were most 
knowledgeable about AI's workings and use (i.e., procedural knowledge and user-related knowledge). These findings highlight a 
problematic asymmetry, whereby individuals demonstrate proficiency in the use of AI technology while lacking adequate under­
standing of its output credibility and its societal ramifications. Furthermore, the results concerning demographic groups portray 
different knowledge profiles rather than simply describing groups possessing “more” or “less” AI knowledge (e.g., Bitton et al., 2024; 
Gillespie et al., 2023). For example, the results suggest that 55+ adults maintain operational understanding of AI (procedural 
knowledge) relatively well but struggle more with understanding AI's limitations and output characteristics (epistemic knowledge).
Meaningful insights also concern the relationships between AI knowledge, trust, and attitudes toward AI. Our findings revealed a 
negative association between trust and AI knowledge, aligning with previous studies (e.g., Tully et al., 2025) but not all (e.g., Greussing 
et al., 2025; Gillespie et al., 2023). As such, the systematic, performance-based assessment of distinct knowledge types and domains 
offers a more nuanced understanding of this relationship. Results suggest that the negative association between trust and knowledge 
about AI stems from heightened awareness of AI's epistemic limitations and societal risks. This explanation not only aligns with Long 
and Magerco's (2020) proposal that increased AI knowledge enables more calibrated trust in AI systems, but also with Ferrario and 
colleagues' (2020) distinction between simple and reflective trust. Simple trust is non-cognitive and based on reliance, referring to 
I. Klein-Avraham et al.

10 

individuals' willingness to rely on an AI system without gathering or processing additional information about its capabilities. Reflective 
trust involves the AI trustworthiness based on cognitive belief, finding the AI system trustworthy based on its properties. The findings, 
therefore, suggest that interventions and educational programs should cultivate epistemic knowledge, thus foster reflective trust 
among GenAI users. Regarding attitudes toward AI, positive associations emerged only with content, procedural, and user-related 
knowledge. These findings indicate that individuals with more favorable AI attitudes possess a greater understanding of AI opera­
tions, applications, and everyday manifestations. While the literature presents mixed findings regarding AI knowledge-attitude re­
lationships (e.g., Kovacevic & Demic, 2024; B. Wang, Rau, & Yuan, 2023), positive associations are more commonly reported (Kaya 
et al., 2024; Obenza et al., 2024). Overall, while the two-axial scale yields results that coincide with existing understandings of AI 
literacy and AI knowledge, it provides nuanced insights into the nature of AI knowledge, thus supporting effective AI policy man­
agement, AI education, and public awareness efforts.
Limitations. While the proposed scale offers a theoretically grounded and methodologically sound approach to assessing AI 
knowledge, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the results of the scale evaluation (i.e., the third stage of scale 
development and validation) indicate acceptable psychometric properties, they are not optimal. Second, although using a multiple 
true/false/I don't know scale increases data quality and minimizes cognitive load, multiple-choice questions with four or more options 
might have enabled a more nuanced scoring and better statistical properties. Third, the scale's items are unequally distributed across 
the nine underlying categories resulting from the juxtaposing of the two axes. This might hamper the scale's reliability and create 
unequal (and at times, limited) resolution on the different dimensions. Fourth, the current study employed quota sampling to assemble 
a sample of internet-using adults from a specific cultural context, thus limiting the study's generalizability. Hence, future applications 
of the scale should first consider and verify the scale's suitability, i.e., reliability and validity, to the specificities of each application. 
Notwithstanding, the scale demonstrates satisfying initial validity and robustness, enabling nuanced insights into AI knowledge not 
only in general, but also into distinct knowledge domains and types.
Future research could refine the scale, adapt it for particular populations, or apply it to additional contexts. Further refinement of 
the scale can test whether the use of multiple-choice questions would enhance the scale's reliability and the goodness of fit of the 
constructs. Further validation of the scale would benefit from testing the scale among internet non-users and other sociocultural 
contexts. Further adaptations of the scale can focus on sector specifications (e.g., healthcare, journalism, software engineering), thus 
capturing domain-relevant procedural and epistemic knowledge. Future studies can also harness the scale for repeated national or 
cross-national surveys to measure public shifts in AI knowledge over time.
