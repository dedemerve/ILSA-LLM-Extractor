Abstract
This article investigates the importance of global competence and its incorporation in interventions from Grade 5 to Grade 12 public education in the United Arab
Emirates (UAE) to prepare learners for local and global challenges, such as sustainable development, technological innovation, climate change, etc. Using data from
the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA 2018) and a machine
learning model, the study presented here reveals that three key “global competence
indicators” contribute to student academic performance in both the UAE and member countries of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development
(OECD). These three indicators are (1) self-efficacy regarding global issues; (2)
awareness of global issues; and (3) awareness of intercultural communication. The
study identifies that boys attending public (UAE government-funded) K–12 schools
need particular interventions to improve all three key global competence skills,
while girls would benefit from building stronger self-efficacy regarding global issues
and from improving awareness of intercultural communication. This article details
evidence on which global competence interventions can be built and integrated with
efforts to close achievement gaps in UAE K–12 public schools. The authors recommend intervention designs and target population groups which would benefit most
from the findings of this research study.
Keywords global competence · machine learning · evidence-based intervention
design · PISA 2018 · civic engagement · UAE K–12 public schools

Introduction
During the past decades, the world around us has been experiencing tremendous
changes – digital revolution, global movement of people, global economy, climate change, resource scarcity, etc. What is global could be local and what is local
becomes global, as evidenced by the COVID-19 global pandemic. Industries have
been going through transformations to adapt to these changes, including the shifting demand of skills from the labour market. The International Commission on the
Futures of Education (ICFE), established by the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) in 2019, is tasked with investigating how
education systems need to adapt to the changing context, promote lifelong learning,
and acknowledge the importance of access to knowledge and information (UNESCO
2019; ICFE 2021). These efforts are flanked by the United Nations 2030 Agenda for
Sustainable Development (UN 2015), already in place since 2015:
Education plays a central [role] throughout the 2030 Agenda, which

More accurately, there are seven targets and three indicators. SDG Target 4.7 calls

The concept of student “global competence” has been coined by researchers as
“the capacity and disposition to understand and act on issues of global significance”
(Mansilla and Jackson 2011), and this definition of global competence has been the
most commonly used version thus far. It was American writer and educator Robert
Hanvey who first noted an increasing need for education with a global perspective in
the 1970s. In his article entitled “An Attainable Global Perspective” (Hanvey 1982),
Hanvey proposed five dimensions of the global vision for education: (1) perspective
consciousness; (2) “state of the planet” awareness; (3) cross-cultural awareness; (4)
knowledge of global dynamics; and (5) awareness of human choices (ibid., p. 162).
In 2010, Fernando Reimers further defined global competence as
the knowledge and skills that help people understand the flat world in which
they live, the skills to integrate across disciplinary domains to comprehend
global affairs and events and to create possibilities to address them (Reimers
2010, p. 8).
In a recent study, Veronica Mansilla and Devon Wilson advanced the concept of
global competence to “global competitiveness”, which is “the cultivation of self
and the quest to understand and improve the world” (Mansilla and Wilson 2020,
p. 3). Developing such competencies is a lifelong learning process. The implications of global competence for education, as put forward by the Organisation
for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), is to enable students to
thrive in a changing labour market, to use media platforms effectively and responsibly, to support the SDGs and to live harmoniously in multicultural communities
(OECD 2018, pp. 5–7). Apart from these conceptual advances in global competence, in 2018, the OECD’s Programme for International Student Assessment
(PISA) implemented the OECD PISA Global competence framework (OECD
2018) by including a test component on global competence for the first time.
In light of the above global trends and education initiatives for cultivating globally
competent citizens, UAE federal governments have set up visionary policy guides
for the education sector to adapt to local and global issues and trends, namely, UAE
Vision 2021 (UAE 2010); Abu Dhabi Future Skills 2030 (ADSW 2019); and Emirati
Al Sana’a Curriculum and Principles. The latter, for instance, aim at
developing the capabilities of individuals toward global citizenship, encouraging them to participate, enlightening them and enabling them to interact,
communicate with different cultural and social categories, and individuals
with different ideas and views (MoE n.d., online).

UAE for targeted intervention on global competence. Moreover, UAE students have
performance gaps in core subjects, and it is the government’s priority to close the
achievement gap and build a first-rate education system that ranks Emirati students
among the best in the world (UAE 2010). According to the PISA 2018 report on
UAE students’ performance (OECD 2019a), student mean performance in reading,
maths and science has been well below the OECD average since 2012. Student mean
scores in reading and science have continued to decline from 2012 to 2018, while
maths mean scores remain mostly stable, with a small fluctuation in 2015 over a
range of less than 15 score points. The gap between the highest-achieving and lowest-achieving students widened in all three subjects (ibid.).
Hence, we felt a need to investigate whether building global competence skills
could be combined with efforts to decrease the academic achievement gap. Finally,
it is important to note that PISA data on the UAE take into account both public and
private schools across all seven emirates.2 In the UAE, public (government-funded)
schools are for UAE Emirati citizens and most public schools are gender-segregated;
while private schools, many of which are co-educational, are designed to accommodate expats from various countries and charge school fees. Among the seven
emirates, Abu Dhabi Emirate has the highest number of public schools with Emirati
students (Kippels and Ridge 2019). It is the UAE federal government’s priority to
cultivate its own human capital.

For our model input, we used student questionnaire responses from the PISA
2018 data set (OECD 2019b). We did not use global competence cognitive test data
because the UAE did not participate in the cognitive test. Features with more than
50% missing data over the whole dataset have been excluded from our analysis. In
our machine learning model, we included all 80 survey features from the PISA 2018
data sets as the base of the model, six of which are global competence indicators.
The reason for including all features was that the Shapley analytical model takes into
account the contribution of global competence indicators towards academic performance among the contributions from other factors measured by PISA.

Descriptions of the measurement of these indicators can be found in the PISA 2018
Technical report (OECD 2019c). For instance, GCSELFEFF measurement used six
questionnaire items to measure student self-efficacy in terms of explaining or discussing complex global issues such as climate change, globally connected economy,
and refugees. Each question provided a four-point Likert scale to choose from: “I
couldn’t do this”, “I would struggle to do this on my own”, “I could do this with a
bit of effort”, and “I could do this easily” (ibid., pp. 59–63, 67). All the scores are
standardised by PISA to have an OECD mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1
(OECD 2019c, pp. 14, 22).

Model output – academic performance score
For the model output, we used a per-student estimate of their PISA 2018 academic
performance score for the three main subjects: reading, mathematics and science.

The PISA framework does not estimate a single score for the cognitive tests for
each student, but rather estimates a probability distribution for student achievement
and draws a number of plausible values (PVs) for the student’s performance from
this probability distribution. In order to compute unbiased statistics for the performance, it is necessary to take a weighted average for the subpopulation for each
single PV before taking the average over the PVs.
The academic performance score per student that we define as model output corresponds to taking the mean of all PVs of the cognitive tests for the three main subjects. We computed all averaged scores by taking the weighted average first for each
PV and then taking the mean over PVs.
Analytical model: Shapley application using PISA 2018 data
The SHapley3 Additive exPlanations (SHAP) approach (Lundberg and Lee 2017;
Lundberg et al. 2020), a method from coalitional game theory, determines the contribution of a feature by comparing what a model predicts with and without the feature. It is the average marginal contribution of a feature value across all possible
coalitions. In this article, all PISA 2018 learning factors (i.e. “features”), including
the six global competence indicators, contribute to the prediction of student academic performance score, and the SHAP value of one feature is the average change
that this feature contributes to the student academic performance score in PISA. For
example, if the expected SHAP value for 25 minutes of learning time is +10, this
indicates that 25 minutes spent on learning increases the student performance (the
model output) by +10 scores. A SHAP value of zero indicates no contribution. The
SHAP approach is about feature contribution to the model output, which does not
have any causal inference.
Elsewhere (Nadaf et al. 2021), we (the first two authors of this article) have
reported on our training a boosted tree model to predict the complex and nonlinear relationship between students’ learning features and their academic performance
score in PISA 2018. In that article, we also used the SHAP approach to explain the
complexity of the model and measured the contribution of each of the learning features in predicting academic performance (ibid.). In the present study, we used the
same approach to identify the contribution of global competence indicators to student academic performance score based on the PISA 2018 data.

Conclusions and future steps
This article presents results of an empirical investigation of whether student
global competence contributes to their academic performance in both the UAE
and other PISA 2018-participating countries, and how such contribution varies
among different population groups within the UAE. The aim of our evidencebased study is to inform intervention designs to integrate global competence and
other related competencies in intervention focus groups in K–12 public-school
reforms in the UAE.
Based on our findings, there is evidence that three indicators of global competence (GCA​WAR​E, GCSELFEFF and AWACOM) do indeed contribute positively
towards student academic performance in both the UAE and OECD countries.
First, this means that student academic performance would benefit from interventions designed to improve student awareness of global issues (GCA​WAR​E),
awareness of intercultural communication (AWACOM) and build stronger selfefficacy regarding global issues (GCSELFEFF). This evidence also suggests that
improving global competence skills would be conducive to efforts aiming to close
performance gaps. Second, our findings identify a population group which would
benefit the most from potential interventions designed to improve both global
competence skills and academic performance. UAE K–12 public-school boys
emerge from our findings as the priority target group, since the average contribution of all three global competence indicators to these students’ academic performance score is negative. For UAE public-school girls, our findings indicate
that building stronger self-efficacy regarding global issues and intercultural communication skills should be the focus, because the contributions of GCSELFEFF
and AWACOM towards their academic performance scores are negative. Privateschool students, on average, record higher levels of global competence contribution to academic performance than public-school students in the UAE. However,
we do not envisage private schools as intervention targets for reasons highlighted
in the previous sections.
For future research, it would be interesting to investigate the reasons why most
UAE public-school girls present a positive SHAP value of GCA​WAR​E while the
trends for boys are polarised, either on the extreme positive side or negative side.
Research efforts are needed to understand in what schooling context the SHAP
value of GCA​WAR​E is extremely positive and in what schooling context it is
extremely negative for public-school boys. This would also help to design and
implement effective interventions in the future.
For potential global competence intervention designs, we recommend taking
two key factors into account: (1) the OECD PISA Global competence framework
(OECD 2018); and (2) specific characteristics of the UAE K–12 public-school
learning context and students’ existing learning challenges. According to the
Global competence framework, global competence requires students to be able
to (a) “examine issues of local, global and cultural significance”; (b) “understand
and appreciate the perspectives and worldviews of others”; (c) “engage in open,
appropriate and effective interactions across cultures”; and (d) “take action for

such local and global challenges.
A case in point is climate change, which has been caused mainly by industrial
revolution in developed countries, yet takes its toll on developing countries as well.
Finding solutions to combat climate change, however, requires all nations to work
together. As citizens and leaders in the decades to come, today’s students do need to
be prepared for these global issues. For potential intervention designs, literacy lessons could be designed to cover topics which are significant both in the UAE context
and globally. Besides climate change and sustainability (e.g. clean energy, desalination of seawater), other global issues are innovative technologies such as artificial
intelligence (AI), 21st-century agriculture (i.e. farming with no soil), migration of
people, diversity of the 21st-century workforce, etc.
Another reason for choosing these topics is that there are local informal learning
resources relevant to these topics that students in the UAE can take advantage of
to apply such knowledge in solving real-world problems. These learning resources
include, for example, UAE’s large solar panel fields producing clean energy to
reduce carbon pollution as one way to combat climate change. Local students can
visit these fields in the context of educational programmes, and they can easily see
how climate change is tackled locally. As a result, interventions aiming to improve
global competence and academic performance could consider project-based learning. Before effective interventions can be designed, however, initial research needs
to be done to better understand the learning context and existing challenges. We
need to know, for instance, to what extent local students are able to read material in
English; whether they are interested and prepared to engage in project-based learning, etc.
To allow such interventions to have a bigger impact and reach a wider audience,
one last recommendation we would like to make is using technology platforms. A
key element of global competence is to encourage children and youth from different
cultures to exchange opinions on how to address a shared problem. Building digital
reading lessons and projects on technology platforms could be shared with students
from various countries, and potentially the same platform could enable children to
discuss these issues and topics online.
