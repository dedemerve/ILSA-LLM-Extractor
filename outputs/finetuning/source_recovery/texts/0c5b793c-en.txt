Abstract

With each survey cycle, the measurement of socio-economic status in PISA must
strike a delicate balance between at-times conflicting goals: accuracy, coverage,
cost-efficiency, trend continuity, relevance (keeping up with changes in society)
and comparability across countries. Over the past decade, a number of changes
were introduced in the instruments and scaling processes; new related questions
were added; and experiments were conducted with alternative question formats. In
light of these changes, new variables, and experiments, we examine the quality of
the indicators and scales that contribute to the index of economic, social and
cultural status (ESCS) in PISA. We conclude with implications for questionnaire
development, recommendations for scaling, and suggestions for future research.

1 Introduction
In international large-scale assessments in education, including the OECD’s Programme for International Student
Assessment (PISA), comparisons of performance across education systems are enhanced through measures of
students’ access to economic, social, and educational resources within their families (“socio-economic status”). In
PISA, this information is summarised in the composite index of economic, social and cultural status (ESCS). Such
measures allow to contextualise student achievement within broader societal differences, and to address, within each
system, questions about differences in educational opportunity, inequalities in learning outcomes, and how these
differences and inequalities evolved over time. Cross-country comparisons of the relation between test scores and
ESCS also allow to examine how education policies such as grade retention, tracking, access to early-childhood
education, the allocation of resources across schools and the grouping of students in schools and classes contribute
to education equity and social mobility.
However, the elusive nature of economic, social and cultural status – a composite variable that is difficult to define
clearly, and whose components are hard to measure with questionnaires – limits the strength of the evidence that
builds on it. Two important challenges for the definition and measurement of ESCS result, in particular, from the
nature of PISA as an international trend study, built around a survey of 15-year-olds:
•

While “status” evokes an individual’s position within a well-defined political community, at a particular
point in time, an international study such as PISA must identify relatively universal components of status as
target constructs for measurement, and focus on collecting evidence about time-invariant markers of these
components; or otherwise address the changing relationship between indicators and target constructs across
countries and over time.

•

Because the status of 15-year-olds mainly depends on the status of adult members of their household, PISA
must rely heavily on proxy reporting (e.g. of parents’ occupation by students) and grapple with variations in
the definition of “household” across cultural boundaries and over time.

Traditionally, measures of socio-economic status used in North American education research have included three
components: parental educational attainment, parental occupational status, and household or family income, with
appropriate adjustment for household or family composition (Cowan et al., 2012[1]; Willms and Tramonte, 2019[2]).
The PISA index of socio-economic status (ESCS) follows this tradition: it is an average of three indices based on
student reports: parental educational attainment (in years) (PARED), parental occupational status on the
“International Socio-Economic Index” (ISEI) scale (Ganzeboom, De Graaf and Treiman, 1992[3]; Ganzeboom,
2010[4]),1 and an index of “household possessions” (HOMEPOS), derived using item-response-theory (IRT) models
(the latter index is related to household income, but also emphasises the presence of educational resources – such as
books – in the home).
A previous review of the measurement of ESCS (Avvisati, 2020[5]), based on data up to PISA 2015, examined the
validity, reliability and comparability of each component, in light of the intended use of the composite index for
cross-country and trend comparisons of the relationship between socio-economic status and test scores. PISA 2018

2 What’s new in the measurement
of socio-economic status in
PISA?
This section presents the changes made in 2018 and 2022 to the measurement of socio-economic status. It
distinguishes between changes in questions or answer formats of ESCS components; changes made to the scaling
procedures for ESCS, whose effect on trends can be ascertained through comparisons with alternative scaling
methods; and changes in other related constructs.
In many cases, changes to trend constructs were preceded by field-trial experiments, to ensure that their impact on
trends would not be disruptive. The PISA 2022 assessment was originally due to be conducted in 2021 but was
delayed by one year because of the COVID-19 pandemic. For the same reason, fewer changes than originally intended
were actually made to the questionnaires: indeed, many participants were unable to complete the field-trial data
collection in either 2020 (according to original plans) or 2021 (according to the revised timeline). Under these
circumstances, and given the importance of monitoring change in what was perceived as a volatile environment, the
use of unmodified instruments was, in general, preferred. The experiments that were conducted in the field trial for
PISA 2022 are presented in the last part of this section.

Changes to the questionnaires used in the main study to measure parental education,
occupation, and household possessions
Only limited changes were made to the main-study questionnaires, as far as ESCS-related constructs are concerned.
The main change concerned the sequence and position in which these questions appeared in 2022: in contrast to prior
surveys, questions about home possessions were asked before questions about parents’ educational qualifications and
their jobs (Table 1).

Parental occupation
No changes were made, in 2018 or 2022, to the questions that collect information from students about their parents’
occupation (ST014 and ST015), using an open-response format.

Household possessions
Household-possession questions (questions ST011 and ST012) and questions about “books in the home” (ST013)
were identical in 2015 and 2018.
For PISA 2022, the household possession items were re-organised into smaller, more coherent sets, with filter
questions for “book” items and for items related to digital devices. An introduction was added before these questions
to clarify how respondents should respond in cases where they lived in multiple homes (‘The following questions
concern your home. If you live in multiple homes, please consider the <home> you spend most of your time in.”);
several items were re-worded for clarity or to adapt to a new response scale (e.g. a change from “yes/no” to “how
many…”); a small number of new items were added; and other items, previously used in the home possessions scale,
were dropped from the questionnaire. Table A.1, in Annex A, compares PISA 2015/18 and PISA 2022 items used in
the home-possessions scale.

Changes to scaling procedures
While the questions asked to students remained nearly identical in 2015 and 2018 (with more changes introduced in
2022), the ways in which students’ responses were transformed into numeric values and combined into a composite
index changed significantly in 2018, then again (but to a lesser extent) in 2022. In the empirical analyses presented
in the next sections, the 2022 procedures for converting student responses to education, occupation, and household
possession indices were applied also to earlier years, and the revised (“trend”) values of these indices used instead of
those originally included in the PISA database. These rescaled indices are available at
https://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/2022database/.

Parental education
In order to convert the highest, non-missing level of education among both parents into years of education, countryspecific conversion tables were used up to 2015: as a result, the same level of education (e.g. an upper-secondary
qualification) resulted in different values on the PARED and ESCS indices when comparing students from different
countries. In 2018 a single, international conversion for all countries was used, as suggested in the previous review
(Avvisati, 2020[5]). The same procedure was then applied in 2022; with the newly distinguished levels (based on
ISCED-2011) collapsed in order to make them compatible with the previous mapping, whose values were used (3, 6,
9, 12, 14.5 and 16).

The procedure to determine the highest educational qualification of each parent was somewhat modified. In 2018,
responses about post-secondary qualifications were ignored if the student indicated, in a previous question, that the
same parent had not completed at least lower-secondary education. In 2022, this “filter” was not applied, similarly to
2015.

Parental occupation
No changes were made to the mapping of ISCO codes to the ISEI scales for regular occupations in 2018 or 2022.
However, in 2018, in order to reduce missing values, an ISEI value of 17 (equivalent to the ISEI value for ISCO code
9000, corresponding to the major group “Elementary Occupations”) was attributed to pseudo-ISCO codes 9701, 9702
and 9703 (“Doing housework, bringing up children”, “Learning, studying”, “Retired, pensioner, on unemployment
benefits”). The same procedure was followed in 2022.

Household possessions
The procedures used to derive a unidimensional scale based on item-response-theory (IRT) models from students’
responses to questions about household possessions did not change in 2018 or 2022. However, different sets of
responses were considered to calibrate the model, resulting in different parameters. In each cycle, only the responses
of countries and students that participated in that cycle were used to derive the home possessions scale.
In 2022, prior to scaling, several response categories were collapsed to align them with those available in prior
questionnaires. Indeed, the intention was to use the 2022 parameters to recompute a scale for previous cycles, based
on 2012, 2015 and 2018 data, to be used in trend comparisons with 2022.
The “trend” scale for Household Possessions was derived by fixing the item parameters of the common items to the
parameters that had been estimated for each language-by-country group in 2022, a procedure called fixed parameter
linking. Goodness-of-fit evaluations sometimes resulted in re-estimating the item parameters, either for all countries
or for a particular group; the algorithm followed in these cases is described in Chapter 19 of the PISA 2022 Technical
Report (OECD, 2024[6]).
This procedure implies that “raw” trends affecting certain items in the scale – such as trends in the proportion of
households with an Internet connection – do not necessarily translate into higher values on the scale index, as their
contribution to the scale may be discounted by a different factor depending on the year.

New questionnaire variables related to socio-economic status
The description of the socio-economic context of students was enriched, in 2022, with the inclusion of new variables
in the questionnaire. In particular, the following items were added to the student questionnaire:
•

Food insecurity (ST258: “In the past 30 days, how often did you not eat because there was not enough money
to buy food?”)

•

Subjective socio-economic status (ST259: The scale below represents how society in <country of test> is set
up. At the top of the scale (value 10) are the people who are the best off. They earn the most money, receive
the best education, and have the most respected jobs. At the bottom of the scale (value 1) are the people who
are the worst off. They earn the least money, receive no education, and have no jobs or the least respected
jobs. Now think about where you would place your family on this scale.”

•

Household composition: (ST230: “How many siblings (including brothers, sisters, step-brothers, and stepsisters) do you have?”)

The school questionnaire items that collect information about the composition of the student body were also modified
in 2022: the question stem was rephrased,2 and new items (about immigrant students) were added. While the third
item remained identical (“Students from socio-economically disadvantaged homes”) a definition, accessible through
a help button, was now included ("Socio-economically disadvantaged homes" refers to homes lacking the basic

Questionnaire experiments in the field trial of 2021
The field-trial questionnaires for the eighth cycle of PISA, which were due to be administered in 2020, included two
survey experiments and some unique features, which were not upheld in the main survey. Their analysis can enrich
the available evidence about the validity and reliability of student reports about ESCS components beyond what is
possible based on main survey data alone, and allows to evaluate the impact of changes in question format and
presentation ahead of their possible introduction in future main survey questionnaires.
In particular, the field-trial student questionnaires included:
•

A survey experiment (employing a between-subject design) aimed at assessing the effectiveness of guardianfocused versus parent-focused questions in eliciting information regarding parental/guardian education and
occupation;

•

A survey experiment (employing a within-subject design) to evaluate the effectiveness of multiple-choice
questions in generating parents’ or guardians’ occupation data in comparison to traditional free-response
questions, as used in prior PISA cycles

In addition, the field-trial parent questionnaires included multiple items (related to ESCS components) that matched
items present in the student questionnaire: parents, and not only students, were asked about the education level and
occupation of parents or guardians, as well as about digital devices and books present in the household.

In 2015 and 2018, “Please estimate the percentage of students in <national modal grade for 15-year-olds> at your school who
have the following characteristics.” (SC048); in 2022, “Approximately what percentage of students in <national modal grade for
15-year-olds> in your school have the following characteristics?” (SC211).

3 Trends in socio-economic status
and its components (2015-2022):
real changes or measurement
artifacts?
This section reports on trends in the database variables from which the PISA index of socio-economic status is built.3
It explores to what extent these trends reflect real changes (including changes in the composition of the PISA target
population) or may, instead, be considered measurement artifacts.

Trends in parents’ occupational status
Indicators based on parental occupations provide a useful reference to begin the analysis of trends. Indeed, no direct
changes were made to question wording, response format, or coding procedures; and only a minor, reversible change
was made to the conversion of (nominal) ISCO codes into an (ordinal) ISEI scale for students whose parents are all
unemployed or inactive. The observed trends in parents’ occupational status can therefore be expected to reflect
mostly real changes in the composition of the PISA target population, or changes in the response behaviour of students
which cannot be attributed to changing instruments, especially when the same rules for converting ISCO codes into
ISEI values are applied to all cycles (as is the case in the analyses that follow).

The proportion of missing and invalid occupation variables is increasing
Reports about parents’ occupation are the most frequently missing component in the construction of ESCS (Avvisati,
2020[5]). Missing ISEI values may result from the open-ended response format for reporting occupations, but could
also reflect the presence of empty, ambiguous, or invalid entries among the raw responses.
Between 2015 and 2022, the proportion of students for whom the “highest ISEI” score (HISEI) could not be computed
because valid occupation data were not available, neither for the father nor for the mother, increased significantly on
average across trend countries and economies: from slightly less than 7% in 2015 to more than 10% in 2018
(Figure 1). While this increase reflects a tendency that can be observed in most countries and economies over this
period, the proportion of missing responses increased significantly faster in a few countries: in 2022, it exceeded 20%
in the United Kingdom, Georgia, Bulgaria and North Macedonia (as well as in Germany, where the increase however
was less marked, and the high rate is related to the implementation of a general filter on parent-related variables for
data-protection concerns).

A few countries and economies nevertheless exhibited diminishing missing rates over this period, bucking the general
trend. A remarkable reduction was observed in particular in Thailand (from close to 20% to less than 10%) and
Chinese Taipei (from about 12% to 7%). Finally, in some countries (including Australia and Mexico), an unusually
high missing rate was observed in 2018, but the missing rate returned to lower levels in 2022.
The average increase therefore was not observed across all individual countries. Its causes may include factors that
are common to all countries, but also a number of causes that are unique to “outlier” countries.

Common patterns
The HISEI indicator results from the combination of reports about the student’s mother and father; it is missing only
if both ISEI scores (for mother and father) are missing. Since 2018, an ISEI score is assigned also to responses that
are coded as unemployed or inactive (such as “stay at home mom”), and, as a result, an ISEI score for mothers is
more frequently available than for fathers (the opposite was true for previous years, on average) (Figure 1).
On average, the proportion of codes that reflect either missing or non-valid reports about parental occupation
increased both for fathers and for mothers. More in detail, the overall increase in missingness over the 2015-2022
period reflects mainly an increase in non-response (empty responses), rather than an increased share of responses that
were coded as “vague” or “invalid” (Figure 2). The latter proportion remained relatively stable over this period, both
for mothers and for fathers. Also noteworthy is that the proportion of respondents which provided information only
about one of their parents (mother or father, but not both) remained relatively stable, suggesting that the increase in
missingness is not driven by a higher proportion of single-parent households.

The increase in non-response, in turn, may reflect (for 2022) the fact that the question on parents’ occupation was
moved to a later position in the questionnaire, after the household possessions batteries. It also reflects a more general
increase in missingness in questionnaire variables: indeed, an increase in missingness is also observed for
the household possessions scale (see below), in spite of the fact that the corresponding questions were moved closer
to the beginning of the questionnaire.

Country-specific patterns of missingness
Among the countries with remarkable changes in the rates of missingness, the United Kingdom (where missing rates
increased significantly in 2022), Australia and Mexico (where they peaked in 2018) do show similar patterns in terms
of missingness across all questionnaire variables – including the indicators of household possessions, which, due to
their position in the questionnaire and their simple response format, are rarely missing. This suggests that general
questionnaire-administration issues, not specific to occupation variables, lie behind these patterns. But whereas in
Australia, missing answers in 2018 are mainly concentrated in a small number of schools in which questionnaire
variables are entirely missing (a possible explanation is that questionnaires were not administered in these schools),
in the United Kingdom and Mexico higher rates of missing responses are observed across most schools.

Average occupational status scores are increasing
A steady increasing trend for the occupational status scores (HISEI) is observed among the remaining (non-missing)
observations: mean scores in 2018 were, on average, higher than in 2015; and, in turn, 2022 scores were higher than
in 2018. This increase in the average status score is also observed separately for fathers and mothers, and reflects a
higher proportion of student responses classified as Professionals (major ISCO group 2) (Figure 3).

In the presence of high and growing rates of missingness, this increase in average observed status levels does not
necessarily reflect a true change in the composition of the target population. Indeed, on average across countries, the
likelihood of missingness is related to the index of household possessions, which in turn is rarely missing and is
expected to be strongly correlated with the HISEI index: students with lower socio-economic status (as indicated by
their position on the household possessions scale) tend to have higher rates of missingness on occupational variables.
However, two findings suggest that differential selection into non-response, by skill level, cannot account for the
observed increase in mean occupational status levels:
•

At the country level, changes in average HISEI scores are, in fact, negatively (although weakly) correlated
with changes in missing rates (r = -0.27, for 2015-22 changes): in countries where the proportion of students
covered by this indicator decreased the most, average status levels did not increase more than in
the remaining countries.

Country-specific patterns in average HISEI scores
Of particular interest are also those countries/economies where the average HISEI scores increased significantly faster
than observed on average across countries and economies (including Denmark, the Dominican Republic, Ireland,
Korea, and Romania); and those where the average HISEI score declined significantly between 2018 and 2022
(Indonesia and Jordan) or between 2015 and 2018 (Kazakhstan). In general, changes in parents’ activities are expected
to be slow and predictable: the extent to which the patterns observed in average HISEI score reflect plausible changes
in the underlying occupational data can therefore be used as an indication of face validity for the patterns observed
in HISEI scores, which ultimately affect the trend-ESCS indicator.

Declining occupational status scores: Indonesia, Jordan and Kazakhstan
The three countries with declining occupational status scores exhibit more problematic patterns; these declines may
be driven by changes in student response behaviour and in coders’ coding practices more than by real societal
changes.
In particular, it is noteworthy that in Jordan and Kazakhstan, the major changes in mean HISEI scores coincided with
a change in delivery mode for the survey (which became computer-based in 2018 in Kazakhstan, and in 2022 in
Jordan). This change modified the way in which students entered open-text responses (with a keyboard, rather than
a pen or pencil), and the tools used by coders to code these responses.
In Jordan, the proportion of responses that are either entirely missing, or considered vague or invalid, increased
significantly in 2022; as a result, the share of students for which no HISEI score is available increased from about
3% to 13%. Furthermore, unexpectedly large changes were observed in the proportion of “valid” responses assigned
to the various occupational categories: for example, 14% of responses about fathers were classified as “Clerks”
(Major group 4) in 2015, but only 1% in 2022; 17% were classified as “Technicians and associate professionals”
(Major group 3) in 2015, but only 7% in 2022. These changes could originate from the lack of familiarity of students
with typing, but also from varying standards applied during coding operations, when students’ answers are converted
into ISCO codes.
In Kazakhstan, the main difference between 2015 and 2018 with respect to the information about parents’ occupation
in the PISA database is the absence of students who were assigned one of the “special PISA codes” for inactive
parents (housewives, social beneficiaries,…) in 2015. Their subsequent use could explain the decline in mean HISEI
scores observed in 2018.
In Indonesia, the proportion of fathers whose job description was considered either “vague” or “invalid” increased
significantly in 2022 (from about 5% to more than 15%), and as a result, more than 20% of fathers did not receive an
ISEI score in 2022 (compared to about 8% in 2018). Because most mothers in Indonesia are housewives, in the
absence of valid information about father’s occupation, the “highest” ISEI score often corresponds to one of the
lowest possible values.
Summing up, there are reasons to doubt that the declines in average status scores observed in Indonesia, Jordan and
Kazakhstan reflect the economic reality of these countries; rather, these declines appear sensitive to changes in coding
standards and in students willingness, or ability, to describe their parents’ occupation. While the resulting error in
the measurement of students’ socio-economic status may be attenuated in the presence of valid information about
parents’ education and household possessions, it will be important, moving forward, to facilitate the occupational
coding operations and to monitor the consistent implementation of high-quality procedures to ensure valid trends.

Trends in household possessions
The household possession scale represents a measure of material wealth, or permanent income, based on consumption
patterns (reports on goods and services available in the homes of 15-year-olds). The underlying items include mostly
indicators of household wealth, but also items that ask students to report about the presence of educational resources
– such as books – in the home.
In the PISA 2022 questionnaires, numerous changes were made to the presentation and wording of householdpossession items, including a greater use of routing, a shortening of item batteries (but not of the scale, overall), and
changes in response categories. Despite these changes, an effort was made, prior to scaling, to map item responses
across all recent cycles to common trend versions (e.g. by grouping response scales into common options, and by
distinguishing between new items and items that were only subject to small, lexical changes to improve clarity).
Even in the absence of changes in wording and presentation, items contributing to the household possession scale can
be expected to exhibit a different relationship to the underlying construct (“differential item functioning”, or DIF) not
only across countries, but also across years. Country-level DIF may result, for example, from differences in culture,
climate, or urbanisation, which affect both demand and supply of certain goods or services. DIF across years may
result from goods and services that become, over time, considerably cheaper, relative to others that contribute to the
index.
The scaling plan for household possessions was intended to address linking issues arising from both the “softer”
definition of trend items (which ignores smaller lexical changes) and the inherently unstable relationship of certain
items to the target construct (a measure of household material wealth/permanent income). It did so through a twostep scaling procedure, aiming at producing a trend scale based on partially invariant parameters, i.e. a scale where
linkages in space, and over time, may rely on specific subsets of items only. This was achieved by first estimating
the item parameters using PISA 2022 observations (with variables recoded to a common trend version), then applying
these item parameters – both international and country-language specific ones – to previous vintages, while assessing
item fit, and freely estimating misfitting items and items that were not administered in 2022. All decisions that led to
the final selection of linking items were data-driven.
In a fully invariant scale, the trends observed in scale values reflect the trends observed, on average, across items
from which the scale values are estimated (the contribution of individual items to the trend is moderated by their
discrimination parameter). In contrast, in a partial invariant scale, the items that deviate most from the common trend
pattern no longer contribute to the overall trend in scale values.

Self-reports vs proxy-reports of parents’ occupation and educational attainment
One of the challenges of measuring students’ socio-economic status is obtaining complete and accurate information
from students regarding what essentially constitutes their parents' socio-economic background. Direct data collection
from parents can be impractical in large-scale studies due to its associated costs. Moreover, parents' restricted
availability and willingness to participate may render parent reports substantially incomplete. Proxy-reporting by
students about their parents’ occupations and educational attainments is a pragmatic solution, but concerns about the
completeness and accuracy of the collected data can be raised. Adolescents might not have complete knowledge of
their parents' schooling and qualifications, or about their occupations. Moreover, students’ reports on parents’
educational and professional background might be influenced by their personal biases or what they perceive as
socially desirable, especially when students are faced with uncertainty. This can result in omissions and inaccuracies,
potentially leading to misclassification of students into incorrect socio-economic categories.
Therefore, while student-reported data about parental education and occupation can offer valuable information, it is
useful to assess the accuracy of these proxy reports, for example by comparing them to reports obtained directly from
parents. The Programme for International Student Assessment introduced an optional parent questionnaire in 2006,
administered in approximately one-fourth of participating countries and economies that participate in PISA. The
parent questionnaire for the field trials conducted in 2020/2021 included multiple items that matched items present
in the student questionnaire.5 Both the parent questionnaire and (one or both of the versions of) the student
questionnaire had similar items related to the education level and occupation of parents or guardians, as well as digital
devices and books present in the household. In the 2020/2021 field trial, 15 countries and economies administered
the optional parent questionnaire: Belgium (Flemish Community), Brazil, Colombia, Costa Rica, Croatia, Dominican
Republic, Georgia, Germany, Hong Kong (China), Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Panama, Portugal, Saudi Arabia. None of
the questions about parental education, parental occupation, digital devices and books in the home were administered

The proportion of missing values is (not) the same under the pre-coded and post-coded formats

Agreement
Pre-coded and post-coded occupation data are weakly to moderately in agreement. Across all student reports about
parents’ (booklet 2) and guardians’ (booklet 1) occupations, the agreement rate between the pre-coded and post-coded
formats at the major ISCO group level is 51%, reaching only 67% even when mismatched occupation levels that are
adjacent in terms of first digit, are considered as being in agreement. Agreement is higher for mother’s occupation
(54%) than for father’s occupation (51%) and for guardians’ occupations (50%).
Alternatively, the agreement between pre-coded and post-coded occupation data can be evaluated in terms of the
associated occupational status, measured on the “International Socio-Economic Index” (ISEI) scale. The overall
Pearson correlation between the pre-coded ISEI values and the post-coded ISEI values is 0.66, indicating a moderate
relationship between occupation data obtained under the two formats. The multiple-choice questions on parents’ or
guardians’ jobs and the free-response questions on parents’ or guardians’ jobs yield occupation data generally in the
same direction, but with substantial discrepancies. The correlation is of similar magnitude for mother’s, father’s and
guardians’ occupations.

Free-response questions about occupations enable nuanced descriptions and accommodate unexpected occupations.

multiple-choice questions, especially when the latter offer imperfect response options due to the inherent challenge
of categorising diverse occupations into higher-order categories. Fixed response options that do not align with
students' perceptions of their parents' occupations can heighten the cognitive demand placed on students as they
attempt to select the most appropriate category.

Comparability of parent- vs guardian-centred questions
The 2020/2021 field trial also included a survey experiment aimed at assessing the effectiveness of guardian-focused
versus parent-focused questions in eliciting information regarding parental/guardian education and occupation. This
experiment employs a between-subjects design, wherein approximately half of the students completed the traditional
parent-focused questions (mother and father; booklet 2), while the remaining half completed the new guardianfocused questions (applicable to up to three guardians, depending on their answer to an early question about the
number of parents and/or guardians; booklet 1). Consequently, student responses regarding parental/guardian
education cannot be directly compared at the student level. Instead, we assessed and compared the distribution of
responses obtained under each response format. Furthermore, given that guardians are not differentiated based on
their parental roles, and the number of guardians reported on (ranging from one to three) may not even align with the
number of parents (two, by design), the comparison can be made only for the highest occupational status (HISEI) and
highest level of education (HISCED) among parents/guardians, not for the occupation groups or education levels of
individual parents/guardians.
As a preliminary step in this assessment, we investigated discrepancies between the count of parents or guardians
reported by students and the count reported by parents.

How many parents or guardians?
Students and, in countries where the parent questionnaire was administered, parents, were asked how many parents
or guardians the student has. The student questionnaire explicitly defined a “parent or guardian” as “someone who

Unclassified

34  EDU/WKP(2024)16
raises and takes care of a child and/or provides financial resources to support him/her on a daily basis”, and students
were asked how many parents and/or guardians they have, based on this definition. Conversely, in the parent
questionnaire, the question itself referred to parents or guardians who “take care of your child and provide money or
resources”. As such, the parent questionnaire does not define who is considered a parent or guardian, but rather,
implicitly refers to a (sub)set of parents of guardians that are actively involved in parenting.
Broadly, both formulations refer to the fundamental aspects of caregiving and provision of financial support for the
child, but there are some subtle nuances. The student questionnaire formulation emphasises the broader responsibility
of raising a child and the constancy (daily routine) of parental responsibilities. The student questionnaire formulation,
by using “and/or”, furthermore suggests a parent or guardian can contribute either caregiving or financial support,
whereas the parent questionnaire question suggests a parent or guardian should contribute both caregiving and some
(financial or other) resources. The parent questionnaire formulation uses the ambiguous term “resources” which could
encompass various forms of emotional, social and cultural support beyond financial support.
The two formulations may lead to diverging interpretations about who counts as a parent/guardian: should a noncustodial parent, who only contributes child support money, be included? And what about a relative living in the
same house (such as a grand-mother), who participates in childcare but does not contribute financial resources? The
implicit definition in the parent questionnaire allows for more flexible interpretations, as it relies on the perceptions
of the reporting parent or guardian regarding the involvement of other parents or guardians in parenting and resource
provision. This could lead to a wider range of responses, reflecting different family dynamics and interpretations of
parental roles.
About three out of four students (76%) reported having two parents or guardians (from 44% in Morocco to 91% in
Croatia). About one in seven students (14%) reported having one parent or guardian (from 4% in Iceland to 44% in
Saudi Arabia), and about one in twelve students (9%) reported having three or more parents or guardians (from 1%
in Croatia to 45% in Georgia). Overall, parents were less likely to report that their child has two parents or guardians
(59%) than students were to report they have two parents or guardians. Parents were more likely to report being the
sole parent or guardian (22%) than students were to report they have one parent or guardian, and were also more
likely to report that their child has three or more parents or guardians (18%) than students were to report they have
three or more parents or guardians.
When both student and parent reports on the number of parents or guardians were available, these reports were equal
in 63% of cases (from 50% in Dominican Republic to 85% in Belgium and Croatia). Discrepancies occur in both
directions, but overall, more students overreported (21%) than underreported (17%) their number of parents or
guardians, compared to parent reports. It is not the case, then, that discrepancies are due to motivated underreporting
by students aiming to reduce the number of questions to answer.
Notably, the pattern of discrepancies indicates that misreporting is not due to random measurement error either.
Instead, the most common discrepancies involve students reporting they have two parents or guardians while their
parent or guardian reports either that they are the sole parent or guardian, or that their child has three or more parents
or guardians. Figure 15 demonstrates the prevalence of student reports indicating two parents or guardians,
irrespective of the corresponding numbers provided by parents. Among students whose parent who completed the
parent questionnaire reported that they are the sole parent or guardian, 56% reported that they do have two parents or
guardians. Among students whose parent who completed the parent questionnaire reported that their child has three
or more parents or guardians, 46% reported that they have two parents or guardians.

Among students reporting two parents or guardians while the parent who completed the questionnaire indicated sole

guardian. This suggests that two parental figures may be present in the home, even if not recognised as fulfilling an
active parental role by the parent or guardian who completed the parent questionnaire. Similarly, among students
reporting two parents or guardians while the parent or guardian who completed the parent questionnaire indicated
sole guardianship, 24% indicated they live only with either a mother/female guardian or a father/male guardian. This
suggests that these students recognised a parental figure outside the home while their parent who completed the parent
questionnaire did not.
This shows that discrepancies between student and parent reports may indicate variations in family structures or living
arrangements being perceived differently by students and parents. In particular, the student may recognise two
parents, even if one parent, who is completing the parent questionnaire, may consider themself as the sole parent or
guardian due to skewed involvement in caregiving and/or legal or custodial arrangements.
All in all, these results suggest that the question in the parent questionnaire elicits a more subjective understanding
of parental or guardian roles, emphasising an active involvement in parenting. Conversely, the question in the student
questionnaire tends to evoke a more traditional interpretation, wherein the roles of mother and father as guardians are
primarily defined by their presence in the household or by their biological relationship to the child, rather than their
active engagement in parenting duties.
Even though the discrepancies between student and parent reports suggest caution in using a single question about
the number of parents or guardians to filter follow-up questions, the nature of the discrepancies suggests that students'
reporting about parental education levels and occupations need not be affected by the framing of the questions as
parent-focused or guardian-focused.

The questions about parents’ education levels, on the other hand, differed in ways other than being framed as parentfocused or guardian-focused. In the student questionnaire booklet 2, students completed the trend education items for

experimental, integrated education items listed primary, secondary and post-secondary qualifications (below ISCED

questions were framed in a guardian-focused or parent-focused manner. Specifically, the guardian-focused questions
resulted in a larger proportion of responses at ISCED levels 2 and 4 and fewer responses at higher education levels
compared to the parent-focused questions.
It is challenging to determine whether the differences in the distribution of parental education levels are due to the
integration of education level options or the inclusion of a "Don't know" option in the questionnaire. However, based
on the results observed for parental occupation (which showed similar distributions regardless of question framing),
it is unlikely that the differences in parental education distributions are solely due to whether the questions were
framed as parent-focused or guardian-focused. Rather, we suspect that the presence of a “Don't know” option may
be an important factor. Higher education levels might be more difficult for students to accurately discern. Therefore,
when students are unsure about the specific (post-secondary) education level attained by their parent or guardian,
they may opt to select "Don't know" if this option is available, rather than guessing among the higher ISCED levels.
The addition of a “Don’t know” option may thus have led to the observed differences in the distribution of parental
education.

5 New socio-economic variables:
What do they measure, and how

With each cycle, new questionnaire items related to socio-economic status are included in the questionnaire,
sometimes contributing directly to the measurement of household possessions, other times to be used independently
in analyses or as part of validation efforts. This section explores how the student-reported measures of subjective
socio-economic status, food insecurity and household composition, and the school reports on the proportion of
students from socio-economically disadvantaged homes, can be used in analysis and reporting.

Subjective socio-economic status
While the subjective report of students about their socio-economic status can clearly not be compared across national
boundaries – students are instructed to compare their families to other families in their country only – student
responses appear valid: students seem to anchor their subjective assessments in objective characteristics of their
family, captured by the current indicators of socio-economic status used in PISA. Indeed, student reports about their
family’s position on a socio-economic status ladder have sufficient, and meaningful variability in every
country/economy that administered this question.
In all countries and economies, at least 5 levels of the 10-point scale were selected by 5% or more of the population;
and in no country and economy was a single level chosen by more than 40% of the population (see Figure A.1 in
Annex A). In a majority of countries, the modal choice was selected by less than 25% of students. Three groups
of countries can be distinguished:
•

In the majority of OECD countries and in many partner countries and economies, levels 7 or 8 were the most
frequently chosen options;

•

In Albania, Baku (Azerbaijan), Kosovo, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the Slovak
Republic, the United Arab Emirates and Uzbekistan, the most frequently chosen option corresponds to the
top of the ladder (10 out of 10), and many students self-described their families as among the “best off”;

•

The collection of more detailed information on household composition in student questionnaires opens up
opportunities for adjusting household possessions reports prior to scaling. This may be particularly interesting for
items which can be expected to have a different relationship to income and wealth, depending on the number of

among students from the most advantaged households, but with two or more siblings.

components of socio-economic status. In several countries and economies, the linear correlation coefficient with the
average value of the HOMEPOS index indicates a strong association (r < -0.70): including Bulgaria, Germany, the
United Kingdom and the United States (in the United Kingdom, this is also the case for the association with the
average value of the HISEI index).
Figure 20 shows the proportion of students from the bottom quarter of the HOMEPOS index (disadvantaged
students), across categories of school disadvantage. While the share of disadvantaged students, based on household
possessions, is by definition 25% on average for all countries and economies, within countries and economies it tends
to increase monotonically – though with varying slopes – according to principal reports of school disadvantage. Steep
slopes (and therefore strong correlations between the two indicators) are observed in Bulgaria, Czechia, Germany,
Hungary, Italy, the Slovak Republic, Spain and the United Kingdom, for example. Flat slopes, and, in some cases,
non-monotonic relationships, signaling weak correlations, are observed in Brazil, Cambodia, Costa Rica, the
Dominican Republic, Iceland, Jamaica, Kosovo, Malaysia, the Philippines and Uzbekistan, for example. This
variation in slopes may result both from differences in the reliability of the student and school indices (e.g. because
of low reliability of student reports, or of poor targeting of the existing item bank for HOMEPOS), but also from
institutional factors independent of measurement quality, because the two measures may refer to different target
populations (15-year-olds vs students in the modal grade for 15-year-olds).
In the construction of comparative indicators, the use of school reports about the share of students from socioeconomically disadvantaged homes to identify “advantaged” and “disadvantaged” schools (e.g. using tertiles or
quartiles, and assigning students to three or four groups of equal size), as an alternative to the use of “average school
ESCS” values, seems therefore a valid way of exploring questions of resource allocation across schools. In particular,
its main advantage is that the sampling specificities of PISA (the age-based target population, and the varying sample
size across schools) do not interfere with the measurement properties. In contrast, average student-level variables
have different reliability depending on the student sample size, and may be biased in countries where 15-year-olds
who are behind track (e.g. because of grade repetition) are mostly found in lower secondary schools, while on-track
students are in upper secondary schools.

6 Conclusion
The previous sections examined, based on a variety of analyses, the validity, reliability and comparability of
indicators related to socio-economic status. They were organised by source of data. In this concluding section, we
summarise the main results for each component of the PISA index of economic, social and cultural status, integrating
the findings from different sources and analyses, and draw implications for questionnaire development,
recommendations for scaling, and suggestions for future research. The guiding question remains: how can the
validity, reliability and comparability of the three indices underlying the ESCS composite be strengthened?

Measuring occupational status
The instruments and procedures behind the measure of occupation status have remained unchanged over recent
cycles; nevertheless, the validity and comparability of the resulting measure may vary across countries and over time.
There are three main potential threats to the measurement stability and quality for the occupation status index:
•

High levels of missing rates (of the three components of ESCS, occupation status is the most frequently
missing, with rates of missingness varying over time and between countries);

•

Reliance on proxy reporting (by 15-year-olds) of parents’ occupations: can 15-year-olds accurately report
their parents’ jobs?

•

Quality of occupational coding: how reliable are (human) coders, and how consistent are coding standards
across countries and over time?

In this respect, the empirical analysis of main survey data from 2015, 2018 and 2022, and from field-trial data from
2020-21, resulted in the following main findings:
•

The proportion of missing reports about parents’ occupations has increased, on average across countries and
over recent cycles (Figure 1); but an alternative, multiple-choice response format, experimented in the Field
Trial for PISA 2022, did not reduce the rate of missingness, and most likely would have negative
consequences for the accuracy of information about parents’ occupations if implemented (lower agreement
with reports based on parent questionnaires, where available: see below).

•

Students from more disadvantaged backgrounds (as indicated by their position on the HOMEPOS scale) are
less likely to report their parents’ occupation using the (traditional) open-ended format, compared to more
advantaged students (Figure 4); but this pattern may be even exacerbated by the multiple-choice format.

•

The agreement rate between pre- and post-coded major occupation categories for the same student is low
(51%) and varies depending on occupational status. Among students whose reports about their father’s or
mother’s job were classified (by expert coders) in the category of professionals, 63% selected this category
among the choices given in the pre-coded format; meanwhile, only 44% self-selected the category assigned
by expert coders among students whose reports were classified in the category of “plant and machine
operators”, and only 18% among those whose reports were classified as referring to “technicians
and associate professionals” (Figure 12).

•

The overall agreement rate between measures of the highest parental occupational status based on (postcoded) job descriptions collected from students and parents is relatively low (39%) in field-trial data (202021) but increases to 59% when mismatches within major occupational groups are ignored. Meanwhile,

Unclassified

46  EDU/WKP(2024)16
the agreement rate between students and their parents based on pre-coded (multiple-choice) categories at the
same level of detail is only 51% (Figure 8).
•

In the vast majority of countries, trends in average occupation status, and in the underlying occupational
structure, reflect a plausible, and gradual increase in the share of “professionals” (Figure 3). In a few cases,
the change from paper- to computer-based delivery of questionnaires, or varying standards for occupational
coding, have, however, resulted in implausible trends.

•

At the individual level, the occupational status implied by a student’s description of his or her parents’
occupations correlates strongly with direct, subjective reports (on 10-point scale) about his or her family’s
position on a socio-economic ladder (Figure 17).

•

The experimental framing of occupation questions in terms of guardians (preceded by a filter question aimed
at determining the number of parents or guardians, up to three) resulted in almost identical distributions for
the highest occupational status, compared to the traditional formulation in terms of “mother” and “father”
(Figure 16).

The following implications for questionnaire development, recommendations for scaling, and suggestions for future
research can be drawn from these analyses:
•

Pre-coded, multiple-choice response formats do not represent a viable alternative to the open-ended format
for collecting information about parental occupations.

•

To reduce the amount of missing responses, efforts to shorten the measurement of household possessions
(and therefore move questions about occupations closer to the start of the questionnaire) and soft reminders
if the question is being skipped may be considered.

•

In general, efforts to strengthen the quality of occupational coding, and reduce its costs, should be prioritised
over changes to the measurement instruments. This may include investing in computer-assisted coding tools
(more ergonomic coding interfaces, and reducing the coding effort through automatic coding, where
applicable); in tools to train and select coders; but also documenting the coding design and coder-reliability
indicators more thoroughly.

•

Alternative mappings of occupational codes into a social stratification scale may be considered. These
conversions could be either based on evidence internal to PISA (e.g. based on the relationship with other
components of socio-economic status or with subjective socio-economic status), or based on evidence from
PIAAC. If new mappings are adopted, their consistent application to data from prior cycles for the purpose
of trend analyses must be ensured.

•

A guardian-focused framing can be introduced without disrupting trends in the measurement of socioeconomic status components.

Measuring parental education attainment
Over recent cycles the measure of the educational component of ESCS experienced more changes than the measure
of occupational status:
•

In 2018, the mapping of response options to scale values (which are approximately equivalent to years of
education) changed: starting with PISA 2018 a single international mapping of student responses to PARED
values was used for all countries and economies (the education component of ESCS was renamed from
PARED to PAREDINT, to reflect this).

•

In 2022, questions about parents’ education were moved back, in the student questionnaire, to after the item
batteries collecting information on household possessions.

•

Questionnaire items were also modified to align response categories with the distinctions, among tertiaryeducation qualifications, highlighted in the ISCED 2011 classification. When new response options were

Unclassified

EDU/WKP(2024)16  47

added in 2022, care was taken, however, to maintain a mapping to PAREDINT values that could preserve
trends with prior cycles; several levels of tertiary qualifications were mapped to the same scale value.
The main threats to the measurement stability and quality for the education attainment index are:
•

Reliance on proxy reporting (by 15-year-olds) of parents’ educational qualifications: can 15-year-olds
accurately report their parents’ qualifications?

•

Changes to students’ response behaviour in response to changes in item order and format.

The empirical analysis of main survey data from 2015, 2018 and 2022, and from field-trial data from 2020-21,
resulted in mixed findings about the accuracy and reliability of the education attainment index:
•

Agreement between student and parent reports tends to low, when considering nine distinct levels of
education: 40% across the 13 countries/economies that administered the (optional) parent questionnaire in
the Field Trial prior to PISA 2022 (Figure 8 and Figure 9). Agreement increases to 69% when distinguishing
only three broad education categories (less than upper secondary, upper secondary, and tertiary education).

•

Nevertheless, at aggregate levels and for countries where the level of tertiary-educated 35-44-year-old men
and women is also available based on labour-force surveys (LFS) in the Education at a Glance (EAG)
Database, this indicator (for the year 2022) correlates strongly with the share of fathers and mothers with
tertiary qualifications, as reported in PISA 2022 (Figure 6).

•

According to the reports of 15-year-old children, the share of parents (mothers, in particular) with a tertiary
qualification has increased between 2015 and 2022, in most countries. Over a similar time period (2011-22),
labour-force surveys also indicate an increase in tertiary-educated 35-44-year-olds: however, the magnitudes
of changes for individual countries do not correlate across sources (Figure 6).

•

Within PISA, mean values of PAREDINT for countries and economies correlate similarly over the years
with mean values of occupational status scores; and trends in these two indicators (HISEI, PAREDINT) are
also positively correlated (r = 0.45) over the full 2015-2022 period (p. 19).

•

In the context of the field-trial experiment around guardian-focused questions, an explicit non-response (“I
don’t know”) option was offered to students. While the analysis of occupation-related questions confirmed
that parent- and guardian-focused questions yield equivalent information about the highest occupational
status, this finding could not be confirmed for the highest educational attainment. In particular, fewer tertiary
qualifications were reported when the “I don’t know” option was present (guardian-focused questions), than
in the more traditional format for father/mother where this option was not offered (Figure 16).

The following implications for questionnaire development, recommendations for scaling, and suggestions for future
research can be drawn from these analyses:
•

The validity of student reports about their parents’ education remains fragile, but no clear alternative to the
current procedures emerges from the study of past data.

•

While the introduction of new response options in the question about parental educational qualifications does
not appear to have disrupted trends, the introduction of an explicit non-response option (“I don’t know”)
would likely have a more disruptive impact.

Measuring household income and wealth
In the PISA 2022 questionnaires, numerous changes were made to the presentation and wording of householdpossession items, including a greater use of routing, a shortening of item batteries (but not of the scale, overall), and
changes in response categories. Despite these changes, an effort was made, prior to scaling, to map item responses
across all recent cycles to common trend versions (e.g. by grouping response scales into common options, and by
distinguishing between new items and items that were only subject to small, lexical changes to improve clarity).
The main threat to the measurement stability and quality of the household possessions index are:

Unclassified

48  EDU/WKP(2024)16
•

Changes to students’ response behaviour in response to changes in item order and format.

•

The lack of a theoretical framework for item selection and scale validation, and the reliance on data-driven
algorithms for defining the construct.

•

The absence of adjustments for household composition.

Due to the directly observable nature of the home possessions (as opposed, for example, to parents’ educational
qualifications), concerns about the quality of student reports are less salient. Yet, the analysis of field-trial data from
2020-21, for countries which administered the parent questionnaire, suggests that the agreement between student and
parent reports about the number of books and of digital devices in the home (both reported in broad categories) is
also below 50%, suggesting that even direct reports are not exempt from a substantial amount of misreporting errors
(Figure 13).
With respect to the comparability of the index of household possessions across countries and over time, the analyses
of past data reveal the following main findings:
•

Changes to item presentation and response formats do not appear to have disrupted trends at the item level;
for example, the reports on the number of screen devices owned remained similar to previous years
(Figure 7). At the index level, the correlation between HOMEPOS scale values and occupational status
scores (HISEI) remained similar across cycles (p. 22).

•

However, in contrast to the average trend observed for parental occupational status and education levels,
mean values of the household possession index declined in 2022 (p. 22). Declines were driven by lower
reports on the number of books at home; in contrast, items that became more widespread, such as educational
software, did not result in higher values of the household possessions index, because these items were
frequently “unlinked” following goodness-of-fit evaluations?

•

When aggregated at the school level, most items included in the household possessions scale show negative
correlations with the percentage of students from disadvantaged homes, as reported in the school
questionnaire. A few, however, show only weak or inconsistent links, in some countries and economies
(Figure 21).

•

The newly available information on household composition confirmed the existence of differential item
functioning (DIF), depending on the number of siblings, for the indicator of having “a room of your own”:
students from similar socio-economic background were more likely to report having a room of their own if
they had no other siblings living with them. Interestingly, other indicators of housing quality (such as the
number of bathrooms and toilets) did not show the same pattern (p. 40).

The following implications for questionnaire development, recommendations for scaling, and suggestions for future
research can be drawn from these analyses:
•

As a preliminary step to a more thorough validation process for third (“income”) component of ESCS, this
component would need to be explicitly defined, and the intended uses of the scale delimited.

•

While items that refer to books and technological devices provide interesting information, their relationship
to economic affluence or deprivation appears more volatile (over time) than for items that relate more directly
to basic necessities of life, such as housing or nutrition. In the future, more items on housing, food
(in)security and other dimensions of (absolute) deprivation could contribute to the scale.

•

In addition to the other components of ESCS (with which positive correlations are expected), the share of
students from disadvantaged families (reported by schools) and the subjective socio-economic status
(reported by students) can be used as more direct indicators of concurrent validity. A stronger theoretical
grounding could also guide the selection of items and their treatment in scaling: rather than relying on
a hodgepodge of disparate items and data-driven decisions, the subset of items that shall be used as anchors
for trend scaling (with appropriate consideration for country-level differences) may be identified ex ante, and
scaling proceed in two steps – first using anchor items only, then, after fixing item parameters for anchor

Unclassified

EDU/WKP(2024)16  49

items, by adding the remaining items. Good candidates for the role of anchors would be items that refer to
non-technological, durable goods, or which reflect students’ ability to meet to the basic necessities of life.
•

The collection of information on household composition opens up opportunities for adjusting household
possessions reports prior to scaling (e.g. treating reports of “a room of my own” as two distinct items,
depending on whether siblings are present or not); however, such adjustments are not possible for past cycles.
Where anchor items for trend scaling are affected, the potential gains from such adjustments must be weighed
against the loss of a linking item for trend scaling.
