A B S T R A C T  
The study focuses on the comparability of the digital and print test modes of a German reading 
comprehension test. 249 third graders (50.5% girls, 20.2% L2 learners) worked with both test 
modes (two weeks between the measurement periods). Test mode order was controlled. The data 
was analyzed in terms of (a) test score comparability, (b) validity, and (c) the impact of test order, 
gender and language on test mode effects. The results revealed that the two test modes were not 
equivalent. Differences in mean scores were particularly large for those subtests whose task 
format differed significantly in the two modes. While gender and language had no influence on 
the test mode effect, test mode order did play an important role.   
1. Introduction 
Digitalization is increasingly finding its way into the classroom. The number of schools equipped with information technology is 
steadily increasing, as is the number of “digital natives” (Prensky, 2001) among students, i.e. the number of those who have experience 
using various digital devices (BitKom Research, 2017). Naturally, digitalization has also been the focus of increasing attention in the 
field of school assessment over the last few years. However, although research on digital assessment and its comparability with print 
has been around since the 1980s in the field of second language acquisition (Sawaki, 2001), digital assessment and research on mode 
effects are still relatively recent phenomena in German-speaking countries. 
From the test-takers’ perspective, digital assessment is sometimes perceived as being easier (e.g Blumenthal & Blumenthal, 2020.; 
Paleczek, Seifert & Sch¨ofl, 2021) and more motivating (Blumenthal & Blumenthal, 2020; Paleczek et al., 2021; Wang, Young & Brooks, 
2004) when compared to print assessments. There is no doubt that digital assessment tools offer several advantages for teachers as 
well, especially in terms of administration, evaluation and documentation processes (Cheung & Slavin, 2012; Neumann, Anthony, 
Erazo & Neumann, 2019). It is also true that, as a rule, and in theory, teachers are quite willing to accept the use of digital assessment 
techniques (e.g., F¨orster & Souvignier, 2014). However, teachers in German-speaking countries (especially in primary school) are 
often forced to rely on traditional, print-based assessment procedures. The reasons for this are twofold: (1) in many schools equipment 
and internet connections are inadequate (Forsa, 2019; Lassnigg et al., 2019); (2) many teachers do not feel competent enough to guide 
and teach students in the use of digital devices (e.g., Schaumburg, 2015). Irrespective of such constraints, and as the number of

2.1. Reading comprehension in digital and print 
2.1.1. Mode effects 
Students’ ability to read is not only an essential prerequisite for the acquisition of further competences at school, it is also 
fundamental to the development of a person’s wider knowledge and skills and to the ability to participate adequately in cultural and 
social life outside an institutional context (Naumann, Artelt, Schneider & Stanat, 2010). 
Perfetti, Landi and Oakhill (2005) defined reading comprehension as the ability to understand the meaning of a written word, 
sentence or text. These processes are hierarchical, meaning that word comprehension is necessary for sentence comprehension, and 
sentence comprehension is a prerequisite for text comprehension (e.g., Mullis & Martin, 2015; Richter & Christmann, 2009). However, 
all three levels (word, sentence and text comprehension) are based on other skills or knowledge such as decoding, language processes, 
vocabulary, and prior knowledge (Kintsch, 1998; Richter et al., 2009; Stahl & Hiebert, 2005). 
Despite the growing impact of digitalization, there has been no decline in the importance of reading comprehension as a universal 
cultural tool (Jude, Hartig, Schipolowski, B¨ohme & Stanat, 2013). However, the specific medium used for reading, does exert an 
influence on students’ reading comprehension. Two recent meta-analyses (Clinton, 2019; Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman & Salmer´on, 
2018) have addressed the effect of medium (paper/ print vs. screen/ digital) on reading comprehension. Both studies showed that, 
compared to reading on paper, reading from screens has a negative impact on reading comprehension, especially when it comes to 
expository texts and reading under a time constraint (Clinton, 2019; Delgado et al., 2018). This on-screen inferiority is seen to derive 
from factors such as relatively shallow information processing, reduced levels of attention and concentration, and the tendency to do 
more multitasking when reading on screen (Baron, 2017; Delgado & Salmer´on, 2021; Delgado et al., 2018). 
Such mode effects (for paper vs. digital) have also been studied in assessment research. Testing for test mode effects often involves 
students taking the same test in both test administration modes. Beside reading, other areas were studied as well (e.g., mathematics: 
Blumenthal & Blumenthal, 2020; Wang, Jiao, Young, Brooks & Olson, 2007; vocabulary: Paleczek et al., 2021) and test modes were 
seen to have little or no impact on student’s outcomes. Studies on reading comprehension assessment have also been undertaken with 
similar results. For example, in the meta-analysis of Wang, Jiao, Young, Brooks and Olson (2008), synthesizing the results of 36 
primary studies with students from preschool to grade 12, no statistically significant testing mode effect was found. When a test is 
analyzed for the mode effect engaging students to perform both modes, it is repeatedly stressed that it is important to control the order 
of test administration (such that half of the students start with the print mode and the other half with the digital format). For example, 
Wang et al. (2008) found that the order was considered in 90% of the studies they included in their meta-analysis. However, the 
influence of test order on test mode effect remains unexplored in this study. Generally, this research question is rarely researched. We 
only found Bergstrom (1992) to address this question in her synthesis. Although in general she found no significant differences be­
tween the means of digital and print mode, test order seemed to influence this effect, revealing higher mean scores when the print 
mode was administered first. She does not explain this result. However, it could be related to a low familiarity with the response format 
and input devices when working with a test in the digital mode. 
Other studies do not test the same students in both modes, but have some students work with one mode and other students with the 
other mode and then contrast their results. For example, Robitzsch et al. (2017) and Zehner, Goldhammer, Lubaway and S¨alzer (2018) 
contrasted German PISA results (focusing on students aged 15–16) from 2012 (print mode) and 2015 (digital mode) in this way and 
found a slight advantage in favor of print mode. Lenhard, Schroeders and Lenhard (2017) examined the test mode effect in the same 
manner in the context of the German reading comprehension test ELFE II (Lenhard, Lenhard & Schneider, 2020) and found younger 
children in particular to be faster but less accurate (i.e., making more mistakes) when using the digital mode). As potential moderators 
of the described test mode effects, both test taker characteristics (e.g., motivation: Ekl¨of & Knetka, 2017; familiarity with digital 
devices, demographics: Wang et al., 2007) and properties of test administration (e.g., item layout, response modes, input devices, 
presentation characteristics, scrolling: Bridgeman, Lennon & Jackenthal, 2003; Kr¨ohne & Martens, 2011) are discussed. 
Even if many studies suggest that the effects of test mode are small or negligible, it should not be concluded, as Lenhard et al. (2017) 
point out, that paper and digital test scores are generally interchangeable. Since each measurement is dependent on the test takers, the 
software and hardware used, it should be specifically investigated for each test context whether the test mode has an influence. Hence, 
for the recently developed German reading comprehension test GraLeV, which is available in both print and digital mode, the 
investigation of a potential test mode effect is necessary. Additionally, test order should not only be controlled (i.e., let the half of the 
students start with the print mode first, while the other half begins with the digital mode), but the influence of order of administration 
on the potential test mode effect should also be investigated. 
2.1.2. Gender and language background as moderators of the mode effect in reading 
Demographics (such as gender and language background) are discussed as potentially moderating or mediating the test mode effect 
(e.g., Kr¨ohne & Martens, 2011.; Wang et al., 2007). Previous research focusing on subject matters different than reading has shown 
that some years ago, female students performed slightly better on print mode than on digital mode (e.g., language tests: Gallagher,

Bridgeman & Cahalan, 2002; mathematics & science tests: Jeong, 2014) which was attributed to girls’ lower familiarity with digital 
devices. However, this gender gap in computer skills seems to have vanished which means that girls are no longer at a disadvantage 
when using the digital mode (Punter, Meelissen & Glas, 2016). Girls even show higher information literacy than boys in the Inter­
national Computer and Information Literacy Study 2018 (ICILS: Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Friedman & Duckworth, 2020), suggesting 
that the gender gap has even reversed. In terms of reading, studies directly analyzing the impact of the variable gender on mode effects 
are rare. However, a gender gap in favor of girls has often been reported in print assessments in reading comprehension (i.e., girls have 
been reported as scoring higher in reading comprehension assessments). Mullis, Martin, Foy and Hooper (2017) suggest this gender 
difference to be smaller for digital assessment. Horne (2007) reported that the gender gap in literacy (reading and spelling; Grades 5–8) 
disappeared using digital assessments and argued that this might be due to gender differences in motivation (boys being more 
motivated by the computer than girls), computer experience (boys making more use of computers in and outside the classroom), ICT 
skills (boys often outperforming girls), and competitiveness (girls preferring collaboration over competition). In contrast, McElvany 
et al. (2019) found that girls also outperform boys in reading (in Grade 4) in the digital mode and found that girls’ higher motivation 
with respect to reading fully offset the impact of boys’ supposedly greater interest in digital tools. Given the current research 
attributing higher reading and digital literacy skills to girls, it would be interesting to look at how this affects the test mode effect in the 
area of a reading test. 
Concerning student language background, i.e., comparing first language (L1) and second language (L2) learners, considerable 
research has shown that, in general, L1 learners are ahead of L2 learners in terms of reading comprehension (e.g., Melby-Lervåg & 
Lervåg, 2014). Research on differential test mode effects in L1 and L2 learners, however, is scarce. 
For students learning a foreign language in higher school levels (which is not the same as being an L2 learner), Schroeders et al. 
(2011) revealed differences between test modes, with better, albeit quite small, results being found for the print mode. They argued 
that these differences were primarily due to student differences in prior familiarity with digital devices and to differences in motor 
skill. This result could possibly be transferable to L2 learners, as they have a significant lower level of digital competence than L1 
children, as revealed by ICILS 2018 (Fraillon et al., 2020). Given their internationally recognized lower level of information literacy, it 
would be interesting to find out whether the difference between the test modes is greater for L2 learners than for L1 learners. However, 
to date, to our knowledge, there has been no research concerning mode effects for L1 and L2 learners at the beginning of their reading 
development. Thus, the present paper attempts to analyze differences in digital and print assessment, taking account of both gender 
and language background, and of how these factors may impact test mode effects in primary school students. 
2.2. Assessment of reading comprehension in German-speaking countries 
Reading assessments vary in terms of their intended purpose, target audience and form (Afflerbach, 2016). In terms of audience, 
assessments vary mostly with respect to the age of the children. For primary school children, there are several test procedures in 
German-speaking countries that can be used to assess reading comprehension skills (Lenhard, 2013; Paleczek & Seifert, 2019). Here, a 
distinction needs to be made between the more extensive procedures used to test the different sub-processes of reading literacy (e.g., 
ELFE II: Lenhard et al., 2020), and the shorter procedures that provide a rough overview of the class reading level and which quickly 
identify students with specific deficiencies (e.g., SLS 2–9: Mayringer & Wimmer, 2014). Additional tests, employed as part of CBM 
(curriculum based measurement), also serve to monitor learning progress (e.g., VSL: Walter, 2013). 
In addition to audience and purpose, reading assessments may also be distinguished in terms of measurement method. Such as­
sessments either measure reading comprehension as a product of the reading process (e.g., answering multiple-choice questions after 
reading a text) or directly during the reading process (e.g., filling in gaps in a text). There is a strong link between measurement method 
and skill measurement (Keenan, Betjemann & Olson, 2008). For example, it has been shown that the characteristics of the test and the 
items used in a reading comprehension test affect how much or little other skills (e.g., decoding ability, reading fluency and linguistic 
skills) influence the result in the test (Brasher, 2017). 
On word and sentence level, tests address reading comprehension as a product of the reading process. Deciding which word 
matches a picture or sentence (e.g., in ELFE II on word and sentence level, respectively: Lenhard et al., 2020), or deciding whether a 
sentence is right or wrong (e.g., in SLS 2–9: Mayringer et al., 2014) are common tasks in German reading tests. 
On text level, tests often use questions concerning text read by the student (e.g., in the review of educational standards in Austria). 
In this task format, answers are strongly related to language comprehension and linguistic skills (Keenan et al., 2008). The difficulty of 
the task varies depending on question type (e.g., direct information retrieval, simple linking of information, forming inferences), as 
well as on the answer mode available (yes-no questions vs. open questions) (Guthrie, Seifert, Burnham & Caplan, 1974). This method is 
also used in many standardized procedures for measuring reading comprehension, where the question type is controlled and the 
response mode is usually in the form of a multiple-choice format (e.g., ELFE II: Lenhard et al., 2020). 
Only few procedures in German-speaking countries measure reading comprehension during the reading task itself (i.e., by 
requiring responses during reading). The maze procedure is one such method of measurement. Students have to read a specific text 
passage within a given time limit. Usually, there is a gap at the position of every seventh word in the text and students need to identify 
the target word by choosing from among several options (including one target word and distractors). This method of measuring 
reading comprehension has been extensively used in English-speaking countries (for an overview: Wayman, Wallace, Wiley, Tich´a & 
Espin, 2007). Using this method, reading comprehension is influenced by a number of skills, in particular, those related to vocabulary, 
reading fluency and decoding (if a time limit is given) (Brasher, 2017; Kendeou, Papadopoulos & Spanoudis, 2012; Muijselaar, 
Kendeou, de Jong, & van den Broek, 2017; Spear-Swerling, 2004), with decoding skills being particularly significant in the lower 
grades (Garcia & Cain, 2014). To enable better differentiation between low and high reading comprehension, tests vary in terms of

maze approach are often emphasized in research (Brasher, 2017): it allows for quick and easy evaluation and implementation (as a 
group test), and it can also be easily digitalized. However, one needs to note that text comprehension and student ability to draw 
inferences may not be adequately assessed using this method (Muijselaar, Kendeou, de Jong, & van den Broek, 2017). Thus, in order to

As Francis et al. (2006) point out, reading comprehension in its natural state is dependent on many different skills and abilities. 
Thus, in order to guide further instruction, tools used in the assessment of reading comprehension need to employ test procedures 
capable of differentiating between the various underlying processes involved. The reading comprehension test GraLeV, presented 
below, represents just such an assessment tool. Originally developed as a digital assessment tool, the GraLeV is now also available as a 
paper-and-pencil (print) version. This makes it (like ELFE II: Lenhard et al., 2020; and VSL: Walter, 2013) one of the few hybrid 
standardized reading tests in German-speaking countries. 
2.3. Aims and research questions 
Wang et al. (2008, p. 17) concluded in their study, that “analyses for mode effect are necessary for any test offered in both modes” 
(i.e., in print and digital), a conclusion shared by many researchers (e.g., Lenhard et al., 2017). Therefore, after piloting the GraLeV in 
previous studies (Seifert & Paleczek, 2021; 2020), in the present study we now aim at comparing GraLeV print and digital test modes 
by examining score differences, correlations, and convergent and discriminant validity values. As pointed out in chapter 1.1.1, 
research results in the field of reading tests do not show a clear picture in this respect (e.g., no mode effect: Wang et al., 2008; better 
results in print compared to digital mode: Lenhard et al., 2017; Robitzsch et al., 2017; Zehner et al., 2018). If we find a mode effect, we 
assume that it is also present in favor of the print mode. 
Usually, in studies examining the mode effect, the sample is divided into two groups with different orders of mode administration 
(half of the test-takers working with the digital mode first, the other half working with the print mode first) to avoid test order effects. 
However, most recent studies are not looking for the moderating effect of test order on test mode effects. Thus, we wanted to examine 
the influence of test order on potential mode effects and assume that if we find an influence of the test order at all, it will show better 
performance when the print mode was shown first, as in Bergstrom (1992). 
Previous research offers interesting starting points for the investigation of test mode effects, considering the demographic variables 
gender and language at the test-taker level. On the one hand, a digital gender gap (in favor of girls; Fraillon et al., 2020), but also the 
repeatedly reported better reading skills of girls compared to boys (in both digital and print mode; McElvany et al., 2019) could have 
an influence on a potential test mode effect in the area of a reading comprehension test. On the other hand, higher digital literacy skills 
attributed to L1 learners (Fraillon et al., 2020) may also influence the test mode effect. We would assume that due to higher digital 
skills, there is less or no difference between digital and print mode for girls as well as for children with L1 German, but for boys and 
children with L2 German. 
The following research questions are addressed in this paper:  
(1) Is there any difference between both assessment modes, and to what extent do results correlate with each other?  
(2) Does the order in which the test modes are presented affect the comparability of the test mode results?  
(3) Does gender (male vs. female students) or language (L1 vs. L2 learners) affect the comparability of the test mode results?  
(4) Are the test validities (in terms of convergent and discriminant validity) of both modes comparable? 
3. Method

years; SD=0.38) took part in both assessment periods (digital and print), half (50.6%) of them doing the print version first and the 
other half doing the digital version first (49.4%). Teachers did not provide any background information for 31 of these students (4 in 
the print first group, 27 in the digital first group). Table 1 presents the descriptive characteristics for the remaining 218 students.

3.2. Instruments 
3.2.1. GraLeV 
The GraLeV is a comprehensive reading comprehension test for Grades 3 and 4 and was used in a digital (app) and print version. It 
assesses the three different levels of reading comprehension (word, sentence and text level) using four different subtests (Word, 
Sentence, Text I and Text II). Each of the subtests first presents sample items before students are requested to begin the actual subtest 
and complete it within the specified time limit. 
For word level, there are 12 item-sets, each consisting of three target words and three distractor words (phonologically-graphe­
matically related to the targets). Children are presented with three pictures and six words. In each item-set, students need to match the 
three target words with the three pictures. In print, they have to draw a line from word to picture, and, in the app, they need to drag and 
drop the matching words (see Fig. 1). For the Subtest Word, there is a time limit of three minutes. The retest-reliability of the print 
version of the GraLeV was assessed with a sub-sample of the norming sample in autumn 2022 (n = 112, 64.3% 3rd grade, 35.7% 4th 
grade). For this subtest, retest-reliability was r = 0.81. 
The Subtest Sentence consists of 16 items, each presenting a picture of a situation and four sentences. One sentence is the target 
sentence, matching the picture, and three sentences are distractors. One distractor is syntactical, one semantical, and one is a mix of 
both. While all sentences make sense (in German), only one sentence accurately describes the situation in the picture. In print, students 
must tick the matching sentence, and in the app, they have to click on the appropriate sentence (see Fig. 2). Children have three 
minutes to solve as many items as possible. The retest-reliability (print mode) of this subtest was r = 0.80. 
For text level, two different subtest formats are used: Subtest Text I assesses whether students can extract information from short 
texts (answering short questions). The texts presented in this subtest are eight nonsense stories or descriptions (dealing with non- 
existing things, animals, or actions). Each nonsense story is followed by two questions, one requiring direct information retrieval 
and one requiring inference. In the print version, students tick the right answer, and in the app, they click on it (see Fig. 3). Children 
have three minutes for this subtest. For this subtest, the retest-reliability was r = 0.84 for the print mode. 
Subtest Text II uses a maze procedure where every seventh word of two texts (each 100 words) needs to be chosen out of three 
possible words, i.e. the target word and two phonologically and syntactically related distractors. In print, the three words are presented 
together in a line and children have to encircle the most suitable one. In the app, students need to choose the proper word by clicking 
on it in a drop-down menu (see Fig. 4). Children have 100 s to solve as many items as they can. For this subtest (print mode), the retest-

3.2.2. Teacher questionnaire 
Teachers were asked to answer an online questionnaire on each child before receiving the children’s subtest scores. The purpose of 
the questionnaire was twofold: (1) to help us gather relevant student background information (on age, gender, L1, special educational 
needs (SEN)), (2) to help us check the validity of results. Teachers were asked to rate each child’s reading comprehension skills (for 
assessing convergent validity) as well as mathematical and socio-emotional skills (for assessing divergent validity) on a 7-point Likert 
scale (ranging from 1 “low” to 7 “high”). Reading comprehension was rated in three subcategories (word, sentence and text 
comprehension). The intercorrelations here were found to be between 0.87 and 0.94 (Cronbach’s α=0.96). Mathematical skills were 
rated in two subcategories (numerical understanding, spatial-visual perception), and the intercorrelation was found to be 0.86 
(Cronbach’s α=0.92). Socio-emotional skills were rated in five subcategories (recognizing one’s own emotions, naming one’s own 
emotions, recognizing emotions of others, adequate reactions to emotions of others, regulation of own emotions), and the corre­
sponding intercorrelations lay between 0.67 and 0.91 (Cronbach’s α=0.94). As a result of these high intercorrelations and of the 
internal consistency of each scale, the mean values were calculated for the teacher assessments of reading comprehension, mathe­
matical skills and socio-emotional skills and then used for analyzing the validity of the GraLeV in its two test modes. 
3.3. Design and procedure 
Classroom teachers administered the GraLeV in Spring 2021 after having received appropriate online training, and after parents 
had consented to their children taking part in the study. 
Additionally, teachers were asked to fill out an online questionnaire for each child. However, not all teachers returned the ques­
tionnaires and not all returns were complete. In the end, teachers provided such assessment information for 173 students. 
In total, 16 Grade 3 classrooms participated in the study, eight started with the print version and eight with the app, with two weeks 
in between the two measurement periods. We used codes to match student GraLeV scores from period 1 and 2 with the data on the 
teacher questionnaires. Teachers only received the student scores from the first measurement period after both measurement periods 
had been completed and after they had filled out the questionnaire. As the test was presented as a game to the students they found 
participation to be quite enjoyable.

3.4. Statistical anaylsis 
To compare the means of the GraLeV’s results in the two test modes (print vs. digital), we conducted multivariate variance analyses 
(MANOVA) with repeated measurement. The test order (digi first vs. print first), gender (male vs. female) as well as the language (L1 
vs. L2 learners of German) were used as group factors. As dependent variables, the achievement of the students in the four subtests of 
the GraLeV (word, sentence, text I, text II) in both test modes was inserted as raw scores in the analyses. First, all subtests were included 
in one analysis and then the analyses were carried out separately for each subtest in order to identify subtest-specific effects. 
The analyses were calculated with the program SPSS 27 (IBM Corp., 2020). 
4. Results 
4.1. Differences and correlations between digital and print mode 
The means and standard deviations of the GraLeV subtests are presented for both test modes in Table 2. The variance analysis with 
repeated measurement including all subtests showed a significant main effect for the test mode (F(1, 205) = 43.89, p < .001, η2 =
0.18). Conducted separately for the subtests, the variance analyses with repeated measures confirmed that print and digital scores 
differed significantly for all subtests except sentence level (see Table 2). Student scores in Subtest Word and in Subtest Text I and II 
were significantly higher in the print mode. 
We found high correlations (Cohen, 1988) for children’s scores in the print and the digital mode (between 0.56 and 0.70, see 
Table 2). Although all four subtest print/digital correlations were compatible, the biggest difference (and the only significant one as 
well) was found to exist between those for Subtest Sentence and Subtest Text II (z(234) = 2.62, p < .01). However, in using these 
correlations as test-retest reliability measures, only Subtest Text II reaches an acceptable value. According to Bühner (2011), values of 
0.7 or higher are considered to be acceptable, and those of 0.8 or higher are considered to be good.

4.2. Mode differences in relation to the order of test presentation 
Table 3 presents the means and standard deviations of the GraLeV results in both modes for the Digital First and the Print First

4.3. Mode differences in relation to gender and language 
Table 4 presents the means and standard deviations of the GraLeV results in both test modes separately for male and female 
students. As confirmed by the MANOVA with repeated measures (interaction test mode x gender), gender had no influence on the 
mode effect (F(1, 205) = 0.57, p = .45). This was true for all subtests, which was revealed in the separate variance analyses with 
repeated measures for every subtest (see Table 4). 
Table 5 gives the means and standard deviations of the GraLeV results in both test modes separately for L1 and L2 learners. The 
MANOVA with repeated measures (interaction test mode x language) showed that language, as well, did not influence the mode effect 
(F(1, 205) = 0.13, p = .72), which was revealed for all subtests as well (see Table 5). 
4.4. Convergent and discriminant validity values for both modes 
To check for construct validity, we first measured the correlation between the GraLeV (digital and print) results and the teachers’ 
assessments (TA) of student reading comprehension (convergent validity) and of student mathematical and socio-emotional skills 
(discriminant validity). Table 6 shows the descriptive statistics of the TA and the correlations with the GraLeV subtests in the two 
modes (for the correlations conducted separately for each classroom, see Tables A1 to A3 in the electronic supplement). Even though 
the correlation between the GraLeV and the TA of mathematical skills, as well as the GraLeV and TA of socio-emotional skills are 
significant, a Fisher’s z-test revealed that they are still significantly lower than the correlation between the GraLeV and the TA of 
reading comprehension (e.g., GraLeV digital and TA of reading comprehension, r = 0.53, vs. GraLeV digital and TA of mathematical 
skills, r = 0.43; z(173) = 1.97, p < .05) (Eid, Gollwitzer, & Schmitt, 2017). This shows that the values of convergent validity are higher 
than those of divergent validity, both for the digital and print mode. The correlations of the GraLeV subtests Word, Sentence and Text I 
with TA of reading comprehension differed significantly for the two modes, revealing higher correlations for the digital mode on the 
sentence level (z(173) = 1.73, p < .05) and text level (text I: z(173) = 3.011, p < .01), and a higher correlation for the print mode on the 
word level (z(173) = −1.83, p < .05). 
The correlation between the TA of different areas (reading comprehension, mathematical skills, socio-emotional skills), however, is 
rather high (e.g., TA of reading comprehension and TA of mathematical skills: r = 0.67; TA of reading comprehension and TA of socio- 
emotional skills: r = 0.51; TA of mathematical skills and socio-emotional skills: r = 0.51). 
5. Discussion 
The present paper adds to our stock of knowledge concerning the comparison of digital and print test modes in primary school by 
examining the comparability of the comprehensive reading comprehension test GraLeV in its two modes: digital and print. Four 
research questions were explored. 
The first research question addressed the score differences in the two modes and their correlation. In three of the four subtests, 
children scored significantly higher in the print mode than in the digital mode. Hence, separate norming must take place for the two 
modes (a result we have already considered when norming the GraLeV in autumn 2021). Especially large differences were detected in 
the Subtest Word. One possible explanation for this finding is the different nature of the tasks students need to perform when

2008). While students need to draw lines between the words and the matching pictures in the print version of the Subtest Word, in the 
digital version a drag and drop task is used. Students seem to need more time when using the digital response format. In addition, 
corrections within one item set in the digital format of the Subtest Word can be somewhat difficult and time-consuming as any 
incorrectly selected word must first be returned to its place in the selection bar before a new word can be selected and dragged. The 
response format also differed between digital and print mode in the other subtests. Our findings are in line with those of Lenhard et al. 
(2017), who also evaluated a German reading comprehension test in terms of mode effects by examining children from Grades 1 to 6. 
Lenhard et al. found that mode effects appear to be particularly relevant for young readers (those in Grades 1 to 4) and argued that 
different ways of indicating the correct answer (thus, the response formats) in the two modes contribute to raw score differences. 
According to Lenhard et al. (2017), this difference should be most prominent in subtests where response time is relatively rapid, for 
example, where students are only asked to read single words instead of sentences or complete texts. Some evidence for this phe­
nomenon was also found in our study (quick item responses are possible in Subtest Word). 
However, in the study by Lenhard et al. (2017), corrections were only possible in print mode, and this may have led to more 
mistakes being found for the digital mode. As we have not addressed the question of accuracy (i.e., share of correct answers as a 
percentage of all submitted answers) in this paper, we cannot tell whether the lower scores obtained in the digital test version stem 
from students’ slower editing speed or from a lower level of student accuracy. Besides, since we used tablets instead of PCs in our study, 
different motor skills were involved. Thus, we can neither confirm nor reject the Lenhard et al. (2017) hypothesis, i.e. that clicking with 
the mouse (which is required in the digital version of the reading comprehension test in their study) is faster but less accurate than 
selecting using a pen. Besides, the results of our study are limited to the GraLeV’s tablet version in comparison to the print version. The 
moderating impact of other input devices (such as the mouse at the PC instead of the touchscreen at the tablet; Kr¨ohne & Martens, 
2011) could be of interest in future studies. 
The second research question addressed the issue of whether order of test mode presentation had any impact on the mode effect. 
The results revealed an interaction of test order with mode, showing that the group receiving the digital mode first, performed 
significantly better in the second measurement period (where they worked with the print mode). In contrast, the group which worked 
first with the print mode showed only relatively small gains in the second measurement period in two subtests (Sentence and Text I), 
and even performed worse in the other two subtests (Word and Text II). This can be attributed to the different nature of the task 
responses in these latter subtests. As mentioned above for the subtest Word, the task responses also differ considerably between the 
print and digital version of the Subtest Text II. In the print version, the correct word needs to be encircled, and all options remain 
visible. In the digital version, a drop-down menu first needs to be activated with a click. Only then do the possible selections become 
visible to students. Here, the drop-down menu obscures a part of the text and this may disturb the flow of reading. Students, who 
worked with the print version before working with the somewhat more time-consuming digital version had the advantage that they

were already familiar with the tasks (Subtest Word: matching a word to a picture; Subtest Text II: choosing the word fitting into the 
text). Thus, they could probably remember a few items and then only had to concentrate on the change in the response format rather 
than on the task content. In general, this result shows that future studies should not only control for test order. It is rather interesting to 
dig deeper into this matter and examine the influence of test order on the test mode effect to verify our results. 
Beside the properties of test administration (such as response modes, input devices), mode effects can be moderated by numerous 
variables at the level of the test taker (e.g., Kr¨ohne & Martens, 2011). The third research question deals with two demographic var­
iables at this level. Both the subgroups of male and female students and the L1 and L2 learners were examined in more depth. Our 
findings revealed that both variables had no effect on the test mode differences. Thus, the mode effect was found for boys and girls 
equally, and also equally for L1 and L2 learners. In terms of gender, our results thus contradict the results of Gallagher et al. (2002), 
who found girls, but not boys, performing better in print than in digital mode. Our assumption that the difference between digital and 
print mode is less for the girls, possibly due to better digital skills attributed to them (Fraillon et al., 2020), could not be confirmed 
either. In contrast to McElvany et al. (2019), our results do also not lend support to the notion of a gender gap, neither with respect to 
the print nor the digital version of GraLeV. Thus, we would rather suggest that perhaps, in our sample, the digital competences were 
the same for boys and girls, which means that gender had no influence on the test mode effect. In future studies, this assumption could 
be investigated by additionally surveying digital literacy. 
Looking at the results of L2 learners, we expectedly find their reading scores to be lower in both modes, as seen in numerous studies 
before (e.g Melby-Lervåg & Lervåg, 2014.). However, the factor language had also no impact on the test mode effect. Due to the 
reported lower digital competence of L2 learners (Fraillon et al., 2020), we would have expected a less or no difference between the 
two modes for L1 learners. Again, the digital competence might not have been different between L1 and L2 learners in our sample. 
However, as the sample is rather small (N = 44), it is not possible to generalize. Larger samples and the assessment of digital 
competence are needed to address this question. 
The fourth research question was dedicated to the comparison of convergent and discriminant validity values for the print and 
digital modes. The correlations between the teachers’ assessment of reading comprehension skills and the GraLeV scores (an indicator 
of convergent validity) were significant in both modes, and significantly higher than the correlations between GraLeV scores and 
teachers’ assessment of students’ mathematical skills and socio-emotional skills (thus indicating sufficient divergent validity). The 
convergent validity for Subtest Word was higher in the print mode, suggesting again that the digital mode of this subtest also assesses 
competences other than word level reading comprehension (e.g., digital skills). The high correlation between the teachers’ assessments 
of reading comprehension and mathematical and socio-emotional skills is in line with previous studies. Teachers do not seem to find it 
easy to differentiate between the performance aspects of different areas and students’ personality or visible behavior (Lorenz, 2012; 
Paleczek et al., 2021; Paleczek, Seifert & Gasteiger-Klicpera, 2017; Schmitterer & Brod, 2021; Südkamp, Kaiser & M¨oller, 2012). 
Apparently, teachers tend to have a more holistic view of their students and have difficulty differentiating assessment in terms of 
specific student skills (Paleczek et al., 2021; Paleczek, Seifert, Schwab & Gasteiger-Klicpera, 2015). 
Student level of familiarity with electronic devices, and especially their familiarity with different digital response formats (clicking, 
using drag-and drop and drop-down menus) may have had some influence on all the results presented above. Student response speed 
and reading comprehension can easily be hindered as a result of limited experience with digital formats (Lenhard et al., 2017). 
However, as seen in Pommerich (2004), explicit practice of a digital task prior to testing is likely to help diminish any difference 
between paper and screen testing. In order to record the influence of digital application skills in the future, we are currently developing 
an instrument that assesses students’ competence in using the various digital task formats included in the GraLeV, an instrument which 
does not involve reading comprehension (i.e., GraDig: Grazer Test zur Erfassung Digitaler Anwendungskompetenz (Graz test for digital 
competences); Seifert, Paleczek, Sch¨ofl & Hinterberger, in prep.). GraDig is to be used in future research in order to compare relative 
accuracy and competence in print and digital modes. It is hoped that this will enable us to disentangle whether mode differences stem 
from the on-screen inferiority effect found in reading comprehension under rapid response conditions (Clinton, 2019; Delgado et al., 
2018), from low accuracy of task completion, as demonstrated in Lenhard et al. (2017), or from students’ prevailing digital skills. 
6. Conclusion 
When assessments are offered in some hybrid form, e.g., in both digital and print mode, the results produced by each assessment 
method need to be sufficiently comparable so that any conclusions drawn can be considered valid. As revealed in the present study, 
although the validity of the print and digital test modes is more or less equivalent, in most subtests of the GraLeV, student scores 
differed in the digital and print mode of most subtests, with higher scores being found for the print mode. Test score differences 
between the different modes seem to be particularly influenced by the differences in the respective response formats. On the one hand, 
the more similar the response formats are in the two modes, the more comparable the results. On the other hand, in subtests in which 
the response format clearly differs across the two modes, separate calculation of norm values is necessary. 
In the present study, the variables gender and student language background have no influence on the test mode effect. However, 
our research shows that when testing the effects of the test modes, the order in which test modes are presented does play an important 
role. As far as the latter is concerned, we hypothesize that prior student familiarity with the digital environment and/or practice using 
the different digital response formats is likely to contribute to greater comparability of test mode scores among primary school 
students.
