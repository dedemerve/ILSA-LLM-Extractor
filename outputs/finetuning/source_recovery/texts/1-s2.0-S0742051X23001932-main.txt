In this study, teachers’ proﬁles in terms of their instructional and professional practices were deﬁned by
using multilevel latent proﬁle analysis. For this study, Turkish national data from TALIS 2018 (N ¼ 6861
teachers from 443 schools) were utilized. Five teacher proﬁles that differed especially in terms of “clarity
of instruction” emerged. Members of Proﬁle E are the teachers using instructional practices most, and the
members of Proﬁle A are the ones using these practices least frequently. Results indicated the important
role of both the “school climate for innovativeness” and school location as contextual factors in predicting proﬁle memberships.

1.1. Conceptual framework
1.1.1. Teaching and professional practices in the TALIS 2018 study
Teachers' instructional practices and their professional practices
correspond to the policy area related to effective teaching; therefore, they are the focus of attention of educational scientists from
practitioners to policymakers (Ainley & Carstens, 2018).
Studies (Baumert et al., 2010; Creemers & Kyriakides, 2008)
revealing the relationships between teachers' instructional practices and students' cognitive and non-cognitive learning outcomes
indicate that these practices are one of the most important factors
affecting students' learning outcomes. Teachers' instructional
practices along with their professional practices are placed at the
center of teaching and learning processes in TALIS 2018 study.
TALIS, conducted by The Organization for Economic Co-operation
and Development (OECD), provides comprehensive information
on the characteristics of teachers, administrators, and schools that
may affect learning and teaching processes. In TALIS 2018, “clarity
of instruction”, “cognitive activation”, and “classroom management” were considered as the dimensions and indicators related to
the teachers’ instructional practices (Ainley & Carstens, 2018).
The dimension of “clarity of instruction” refers to deﬁning clear
and comprehensive teaching and learning objectives, meaningfully
linking old and new subjects, and presenting summaries to students. In order to enable this, the teacher must provide information
on how the lecture is structured, should re-teach when necessary,
and periodically summarize the content (Ainley & Carstens, 2018;
Bergem et al., 2020). In the literature, it was stated that the clarity
and consistency of the instructional objectives and the related
subjects which are taught have a signiﬁcant impact on student
learning (Ainley & Carstens, 2018; Creemers & Kyriakides, 2008).
Cognitive activation, another dimension of teachers' instructional
practices, comprises the instructional activities that require students to explore concepts, appraisal, integration of ideas and information, apply information in the context of problem-solving,
and deep inquiry. Cognitive activation has been shown to be an
important predictor of student achievement, which is associated

2.2. Measures
In this study, TALIS-2018 Turkish national data were used.
Accordingly, the clarity of instruction” (T3CLAIN), “cognitive activation” (T3COGAC), and “classroom management” (T3CLASM) subscales of the “instructional practices” scale along with “teacher cooperation” (T3COOP) and “team innovativeness” (T3TEAM) were
utilized (See Supplementary ﬁle for items). In addition, the teacher's gender (TT3G01, 1:female, 2:male) and the number of years of
experience (TT3G11B, metric) were considered predictors of the
proﬁle membership at the teacher-level. Since it was thought that
the school size, teacher-pedagogical support personnel ratio, and
teacher-administrative or management personnel ratio can affect
teachers' instructional practices and teacher co-operation, these
qualiﬁcations were considered as school-level covariates.
2.2.1. Instructional practices’ subscales
In TALIS 2018 survey, the instructional practices scale is
composed of three subscales named: “clarity of instruction”,
“cognitive activation”, and “classroom management”. Teachers'
instructional practices are assessed using a general question about
how often they implement the activities stated in the scale items
while teaching in a “target class”. Each subscale consists of four
items, and teachers are asked to respond to these items by using a
four-point scale (1: “never or almost never” to 4: “always”). In order
to get reliability evidence for these measures, Cronbach's alpha
reliability coefﬁcients were calculated as a ¼ 0.807 for “clarity of
instruction”, a ¼ 0.788 for “cognitive activation”, and a ¼ 0.863 for
“classroom management” measures.

2.2.2. Professional practices scale
In TALIS 2018 survey, “professional practices” is formed as a
multidimensional scale consisting of two subscales: (1) “exchange
and co-ordination among teachers” and (2) “professional collaboration in lessons among teachers”. Based on the responses to this
scale, the composite score, denoted as “T3COOP”, is obtained for
teacher co-operation. Teacher co-operation is assessed using a
general question about how often the teachers do the activities
stated in the items in the school where they work.
Each subscale consists of four items using a four-point response
scale (1: “never or almost never” to 4: “always”). Cronbach's alpha
reliability coefﬁcient was calculated as a ¼ 0.859 for the measures
obtained from the composite scale (for the whole scale).

2.3.1. Single-level and multi-level latent proﬁle analysis
Latent Proﬁle Analysis is a person-centered approach derived
from traditional Latent Class Analysis (LCA) used to deﬁne subpopulations of individuals, which indicates latent classes that
display speciﬁc patterns for speciﬁc indicator variables. Unlike in
LCA, normally distributed continuous variables are used as indicator variables in LPA (Asparouhov & Muth
en, 2008; Vermunt,
2003). Traditional LCA and LPA are based on the assumption of
independence of observations, but hierarchical data structures in
which students and teachers are nested in schools are common,
especially in educational research. In this case, the multilevel
extension of this analysis should be used, which allows us to
accurately model the nested structure of the data by allowing the
variation of the random means between Level-2 units. This represents the parametric approach to MLPA. However, when the
number of latent proﬁles is three or more, this type of modeling
becomes operationally heavier as the random means will be related
to each other. Accordingly, in the current study, a common factor
model representing the covariance matrix for random means,

5. Conclusion
In this study, examining the instructional and professional
practices of teachers with a person-centered approach provides
insight into identifying the shortcomings and strengths of high
school teachers in Turkey in terms of these practices, and what kind
of interventions should be made in terms of both teacher training
programs and in-service training. The results suggest that an inservice training program for teachers who are members of Proﬁle
B, which aims to provide them with knowledge and experience in
explaining the instructional objectives to students in the context of
the course content and linking old topics with new topics, and
which focuses on the development and implementation of activities that require students to use critical thinking skills and
knowledge in the context of problem-solving, may be useful. In
addition, an in-service training program for teachers who are
members of Proﬁle C would be more appropriate which aims to
improve their skills to develop and implement instructional activities to ensure clarity of instruction. For teachers who are members
of Proﬁle A, it is understood that a more comprehensive in-service
training program would be needed to develop teachers' skills in
terms of all three aspects of teaching practice. In addition, more
than half of the teachers in the study group stated that they never
participated or only once a year or less participated in professional
collaboration activities such as monitoring other teachers' classroom teaching and giving feedback, participating in joint activities,
and teaching as a team in the same classroom. Considering the
results regarding the effect of “team innovativeness” on the differentiation of teachers in terms of instructional practices in the
classroom, this points to the importance of “professional learning
communities” for teachers and the beneﬁt of encouraging them to
work actively in these communities.
In this context, it is believed that “team innovativeness” will
contribute to improving school effectiveness by enabling the
implementation of innovative teaching practices and increasing the
use of information technology to facilitate learning and teaching.

6. Limitations and suggestions for future research
Since this study is based on the secondary analysis of TALIS 2018
Turkey data, the concepts examined in the study and their measurements are based on the conceptualization used in TALIS and the
TALIS study design. In this context, although “team innovativeness”
has been measured at the teacher level, it is also considered an
organizational concept in the TALIS framework; therefore, it indicates the school context. Moreover, teachers' instructional practices were measured based on their perceptions in TALIS-2018.
However, as stated in the literature, the results are inconsistent
when the instructional practices are measured based on teachers'
perception and when measured based on students' perception
(Nilsen et al., 2016). This raises doubts about the validity and reliability of the measures of instructional practices based on teacher
perceptions, and it is considered a limitation of the present study.
Accordingly, in future studies, it may be recommended to deﬁne
teacher proﬁles based on both teacher rating and student rating in
terms of teachers' instructional practices and examine proﬁle
similarity for these proﬁles. In addition, considering “teacher cooperation” as a one-dimensional construct, based on the data of
this study, can be seen as a limitation. Therefore, for future studies,
it may be suggested to deﬁne teacher's proﬁles in terms of teacher’
s instructional and professional practices by considering “teacher
co-operation” as a two-dimensional construct. Another limitation
of the study is the emergence of a proﬁle represented by a small
proportion and unequal proﬁle sizes.Therefore, it is believed that it
would be beneﬁcial to examine whether similar proﬁles emerge in
future studies.
