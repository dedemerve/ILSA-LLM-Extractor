Rethinking Item Fairness Using Single World
Intervention Graphs
Youmi Suk
Columbia University
Weicong Lyu
University of Macau
Since the 1960s, the testing community has striven to ensure fairness in tests
and test items. Differential item functioning (DIF) is a widely used statistical
notion for items that may unfairly disadvantage specific subgroups of testtakers. However, traditional DIF analyses focus only on statistical relationships
in observed data and cannot explain why such unfairness occurs. To fill this
gap, we introduce a novel causal framework for defining and detecting unfair
items using single-world intervention graphs (SWIGs). By leveraging SWIGs
and potential outcomes, we define causal DIF (CDIF) as the difference in item
functioning between two hypothetical worlds: one where individuals were
assigned to one group and another where they were assigned to a different
group. We also connect CDIF to related fairness concepts, including group versus individual fairness and item impact. In particular, we use SWIGs to graphically distinguish between item fairness at the individual level and the
population level. Additionally, we discuss causal identification strategies using
SWIGs and demonstrate how our approach differs from traditional DIF methods
through a simulation study. We further illustrate its application to a controversial item from New York’s Regents math exam and a real dataset from the
Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study, concluding with
broader implications of promoting causal fairness in testing practices.
Keywords: differential item functioning; test fairness; causal fairness; causality; SWIG

Introduction
Differential item functioning (DIF; Pine, 1977; Thissen et al., 1988) is one of
the most widely used notions in the testing community for describing (un)fairness in test items. An item flagged as DIF functions differently between testtakers from different demographic or behavioral groups after conditioning on
their abilities (Bolt et al., 2002; Holland & Wainer, 1993; Magis et al., 2010;
1

Causal DIF
Zumbo, 2007). Since DIF analysis can have ethical or legal implications, it is
essential for test developers to examine whether the performance of test-takers
can be explained by factors beyond their abilities. Importantly, DIF is a statistical
notion that depicts associations between items and test-takers’ characteristics in
observed data, but such association-based notions are limited in explaining the
underlying mechanisms and in uncovering the causes of unfairness. In contrast,
causality-based fairness notions provide more nuanced reasoning about the
mechanisms behind unfairness (Carey & Wu, 2022; Chiappa, 2019; Kilbertus
et al., 2017). They enable researchers to raise counterfactual questions, like what
an individual’s performance would have been if they had been in a different
group, say high socioeconomic status (SES) instead of low SES. Through the
lens of causal fairness, the overall goal of this article is to propose a new framework for DIF by leveraging causal tools from the fields of causal inference and
algorithmic fairness.
In algorithmic fairness research, fairness notions are generally categorized into
two types: association-based and causality-based. In the early stages of this field,
most research efforts focused on association-based measures (e.g., demographic
parity, equalized odds) that describe statistical relationships in the observed data
(Chouldechova, 2017; Dwork et al., 2012; Hardt et al., 2016). These measures
can examine whether an algorithm is unfair based on given metrics with the
observed data at hand, but they cannot explain why the unfairness occurs, even
with an unlimited amount of data. In contrast, causality-based notions (e.g., counterfactual fairness, unresolved discrimination) try to model the data-generating
process and uncover the underlying causal mechanisms behind unfairness, but
they require more complex modeling and stronger assumptions about the data
and causal structures (Carey & Wu, 2022; Kilbertus et al., 2017; Kusner et al.,
2017; Plečko & Bareinboim, 2024). Such notions are typically developed within
two main causal frameworks: the potential outcome framework (Neyman, 1923;
Rubin, 1974) and the structural causal model, along with directed acyclic graphs
(DAGs; Pearl, 2009). Potential outcomes are a key concept in causal inference
that represent the hypothetical outcomes an individual would obtain under different treatment conditions. Under the potential outcomes framework, these potential outcomes are compared to formalize causal effects. In contrast, the structural
causal model uses structural equations and DAGs to represent causal relationships between variables and derive counterfactuals under certain assumptions;
see Chapters 2 and 3 of Morgan and Winship (2015) for an overview of these
two frameworks.
Recently, Richardson and Robins (2013) introduced a new causal framework
that unifies causal graphs and potential outcomes, known as Single-World
Intervention Graphs (SWIGs). SWIGs have been increasingly adopted in statistics and epidemiology but have yet to be applied in education and psychology.
Unlike DAGs, SWIGs illustrate the variables and causal relationships that would

The main goal of this article is to propose a novel causal framework for assessing item fairness using SWIGs. Specifically, by leveraging SWIGs and potential
outcomes, we define causal DIF (CDIF) as the difference in item functioning
between two hypothetical worlds: one where individuals were assigned to one
group (e.g., high SES) and another where they were assigned to a different group
(e.g., low SES). We connect CDIF with related fairness issues, including group
versus individual fairness, item impact, and intersectionality,2 to enhance the
understanding of causal fairness in testing practices. Using SWIGs, we also discuss nonparametric identification strategies under various fairness scenarios. We
then demonstrate how our approach differs from traditional DIF through a simulation study and illustrate its application to a controversial item from New York’s
Regents math exam and a real dataset from the Trends in International
Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS).

Literature Review
Test Fairness: DIF and Equity
The concept of test fairness emerged in the 1960s and has evolved with various association-based measures, like Cleary’s (1968) criterion and Darlington’s
(1971) cultural optimality. Among these measures, DIF has become the most
widely used notion for assessing test fairness at the item level (i.e., item fairness)
within the testing community and is used to detect items that exhibit
discriminatory bias in assessments (Holland & Wainer, 1993). An item exhibits
DIF if individuals with the same ability, but from different groups, have different
probabilities of endorsing the item (Magis et al., 2010; Pine, 1977). Formally,

These test fairness notions above are association-based measures and closely
align with modern algorithmic fairness concepts, such as conditional statistical
parity (Corbett-Davies et al., 2017) and differential algorithmic functioning (Suk
& Han, 2024). However, test fairness has yet to be integrated into causal frameworks. There have been some attempts to use test-takers’ covariates, the propensity score (i.e., a summary score of covariates), multidimensional abilities, or
contextual factors in DIF analysis (Belzak, 2022; Chao et al., 2024; Zumbo,
2007); see Zumbo (2007) for a discussion of three generations of DIF analysis.
However, these efforts are largely explanatory and do not yet provide a formal
causal framework to explain why item unfairness occurs. A causal perspective
allows researchers to move beyond mere associations and explore the underlying
mechanisms of unfairness by asking counterfactual questions, such as: ‘‘Would
an examinee have the same probability of endorsing an item if they were in a different group?’’ or ‘‘Would an examinee receive the same score if they took a different test form?’’

Lastly, by definition, SWIGs make single-world assumptions rather than
cross-world assumptions, which are inherent in structural causal models. Crossworld assumptions0 involve counterfactual variables from two hypothetical worlds,
such as Y a, x ? X a . Because two counterfactuals cannot be observed for the same
unit, such cross-world assumptions are not testable, and thus, it is impossible to
design experiments to verify them. In contrast, single-world assumptions, such as
Y a, x ? X a , are empirically testable through future experiments at least in principle. For example, suppose researchers conduct one randomized trial where both
treatment A and mediator X are randomly assigned and another randomized trial
where only A is randomized. If the outcome distribution in the first experiment
differs from the conditional outcome distribution in the second experiment, the
single-world independence assumption is not met, and an unmeasured confounder may be present between X and Y (Richardson & Robins, 2013; Robins et al.,
2022). See section 38.2.6 of Robins et al. (2022) for more details.

Our Proposal: CDIF

Suppose that Peter comes from the low SES group. Then individual-level item
fairness asks a counterfactual question: ‘‘Would Peter’s probability of endorsing
an anchor item be the same if he were assigned to the high SES group?’’ To
achieve individual-level fairness, Peter’s probability of endorsing the item should
remain the same between the actual low SES world versus the counterfactual
high SES world. If this holds, the subgroup CDIF effect is necessarily absent,
that is, tðuÞ = 0. Unfortunately, individual-level item fairness is not identifiable
as only one of the potential outcomes is observed for each individual in practice.
As a result, individual-level item fairness is neither estimable nor testable. While
some researchers (Kusner et al., 2017) have explored causal fairness at the individual level, such approaches require very strong causal modeling assumptions,

Confounding. So far, the SWIGs presented have included only A, Y, and Y in
the simplest scenarios. However, DIF studies are typically conducted using observational data rather than data from randomized trials. In observational studies,
there is often a set of confounders X = fX 1 , . . . , X p g that affect both the protected variable A and outcome Y . SWIGs express these relationships and allow

Controlled CDIF
Impact. In this subsection, we assume that the protected variable A directly
affects the ability Y (i.e., the path A ! Y exists). When this path is present, the
distribution of the ability Y differs between advantaged and disadvantaged
groups (e.g., high SES vs. low SES). This phenomenon is often referred to as
item impact (Ackerman, 1992). Figure 9a presents a DAG for impact, where the
ability Y is a mediator on the causal pathway between protected variable A and
outcome Y . This figure visualizes that impact is conceptually distinct from DIF.
Specifically, there is no direct path from A to Y (i.e., no effect for any individual
and thus, no evidence of CDIF), yet the total effect of A on Y is not zero due to
the indirect path A ! Y ! Y .
Figure 9b presents the corresponding SWIG Gða, uÞ when impact is present.
In this SWIG, both A and Y are split into random and fixed components, and
the descendants of fixed nodes are relabeled accordingly (e.g., Y becomes Ya ; Y
becomes Y u ).10 In this single-world scenario, all examinees were hypothetically
assigned to group A = a and their ability were set to Ya = u, and only ability
has a direct effect on the outcome. As a result, the potential outcome of interest
in this world becomes Y u .
From a perspective of causal fairness (Kilbertus et al., 2017), the ability Y is
considered a resolving mediator because impact reflects (intrinsic) differences in
ability between groups. Therefore, the path of A ! Y ! Y is viewed as nondiscriminatory. However, we consider (i) the direct effect of A on Y and (ii) any
indirect path from A to Y via non-resolving mediators (i.e., mediators other than
ability) to be discriminatory. We can represent this discrimination graphically by
adding the causal path A ! Y .

DIF. In essence, the CDIF framework accounts for the underlying causal structures among key variables (i.e., A, Y, Y ) as well as other crucial variables (e.g.,

In this section, we demonstrate both conceptual and empirical applications of
the CDIF framework. The conceptual demonstration revisits the Regents math
item, discussed in section ‘‘Motivating Example: Regents Math Item,’’ and the
empirical demonstration is based on TIMSS data.

Applying CDIF to the Regents Math Item
This application focuses on how domain knowledge can be used to develop
SWIGs, and how the CDIF approach conceptually differs from the traditional
DIF approach. Let the group variable of interest A denote students’ SES (high
SES vs. low SES), Y denote their algebra ability, and Y denote their response to
the math item. We assume that SES has an impact on math ability.
Let’s start with drawing a SWIG that reflects CDIF and impact with no concerns about confounding. In this case, a CDIF effect is equivalent to the traditional DIF effect after causal identification. The SWIG uses the observed variable
A and counterfactual versions of Y and Y, as illustrated in Figure 13.
In this SWIG, we target  the controlled CDIF effect, for example,
E Y a = LowSES, u  Y a = HighSES, u . After analysis, we may conclude that there is
strong evidence for CDIF attributed to SES. However, such a conclusion could
be misleading since the SWIG omits other causal structures with confounders.

English proficiency. In this case, ELL status serves as a confounder that affects
both ability and item response. Thus, we revise the SWIG to add the ELL variable, as shown in Figure 14.

meaningfully non-zero. This suggests that the math item still has CDIF driven by
SES, though the effect is smaller than in Figure 13, where the effect is inflated

Discussion and Conclusions
This article introduces a causal framework for DIF analysis to assess causal
fairness in test items. The framework utilizes SWIGs to define causality-based
DIF, where researchers draw SWIGs to represent causal relationships among
variables. We define CDIF as the difference in item functioning between two
hypothetical worlds: one where individuals were in one group and another where
they were in a different group. Specifically, we introduce two types of causal
effects, subgroup CDIF and controlled CDIF, depending on whether item impact
is present. To summarize, our CDIF approach offers the following contributions:
1.

It builds on established psychometric concepts, such as anchor items and
item impact, to make the causal framework more meaningful and relevant
to researchers in psychometrics and measurement.
It visually distinguishes between CDIF and item impact, which are often
conceptually disentangled in the DIF literature.
Using potential outcomes, it provides formal definitions of CDIF effects
(i.e., subgroup CDIF and controlled CDIF), linking psychometrics to the
causal inference and algorithmic fairness literature.
It incorporates and enriches broader fairness considerations, including
group versus individual fairness, nonresolved discrimination, and intersectionality, in the context of DIF studies.
It visually distinguishes item fairness at the individual level versus the
population level using SWIGs.
It clarifies which covariates should be included for identification based
on SWIGs, using d-separation rules (e.g., X for subgroup CDIF; W2 , W3
for controlled CDIF).

In algorithmic fairness research, researchers are increasingly moving beyond
simply finding associations with unfair patterns and now seek to determine
whether algorithms causally lead to unfairness (Su et al., 2022). Using statistical
measures only cannot differentiate between causal mechanisms that transmit
change and those that generate disparity, even with unlimited data (Plečko &
Bareinboim, 2024). In line with this shift, we believe that the testing community
can benefit from adopting causal fairness to facilitate discussions on the mechanisms underlying (un)fairness in assessments, such as DIF in item development
and automated scoring, or equity in test equating. Unlike association-based fairness notions, causality-based notions can examine how and why unfairness
occurs under assumed data-generating processes and provide a nuanced causal
reasoning. Although prior work has explored using covariates for DIF analysis
(Belzak, 2022; Chao et al., 2024; Zumbo, 2007), these are still exploratory and
do not incorporate causality to explain unfairness. In contrast, our CDIF framework is the first to formalize item (un)fairness using causality and to demonstrate

Causal DIF
how SWIGs can serve as a powerful tool for representing and communicating
fairness.
Based on the findings of this article, we provide some suggestions for future
research on causal fairness in DIF analysis. First, we assume that ability scores
are known or can be accurately estimated using anchor items. However, in practice, anchor items may be unknown, so researchers need to estimate the ability
and DIF effects at the same time. Future research would explore how to detect
CDIF with unknown anchor items, potentially using item purification or regularization methods, and how inaccurate estimates of ability would affect our CDIF
analysis. Second, this article focuses on the unidimensional ability, but our framework can accommodate multidimensional abilities by denoting ability as a vector,
that is, Y = ðY1 , Y2 , . . . , YL Þ. Future work would extend the CDIF framework
with multidimensional abilities by connecting them with multiple mediators from
the causal mediation literature. Third, we use a simple detection method based on
outcome regression. Future research could develop other detection methods for
CDIF, such as weighting and doubly robust methods. Fourth, we assume that all
confounders are known and measured, which may be difficult to meet in practice.
Further research would investigate how the CDIF framework performs in the
presence of unmeasured confounding, for example, by conducting sensitivity
analysis or using robust causal methods against unmeasured confounding (e.g.,
proximal inference).
Overall, our CDIF framework highlights the benefits of promoting causal fairness in testing and assessment practices. The proposed CDIF complements the
traditional DIF approach by providing an additional capability to explain the
mechanisms underlying item unfairness. We hope that this causal approach contributes to creating a more equitable testing environment and advancing fairness
in educational assessments.

7. A descendant refers to any node (variable) in the graph that can be reached through a

8. Our framework differs from counterfactual fairness in Kusner et al. (2017). First, the
definition proposed by Kusner et al. (2017) focuses on individual-level fairness, but
our framework targets group-level fairness. Second, unlike ours, their framework does
not consider path-specific causal effects, such as the direct effect of a sensitive variable
(e.g., SES or gender) on item response. Third, they rely on Pearl’s structural causal
models (which often rely on cross-world assumptions), whereas we adopt the SWIG
framework, with single-world assumptions that are empirically testable in principle.
