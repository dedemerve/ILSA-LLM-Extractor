A B S T R A C T
The education sector has experienced transformative shifts with increased integration of artificial intelligence 
(AI) and Learning Analytics in higher education, notably within universities. This evolution necessitates the 
integration of Education 5.0 with Industry 5.0, especially in emerging economies like India, where teaching 
professionals play an essential part in facilitating this transition. Despite the opportunities for skills enhance­
ment, significant obstacles impede the sustainable technological empowerment of academicians. This study 
recognizes these challenges in the context of Indian higher education and explores solutions to accelerate the 
acceptance of Education 5.0. Utilizing a mixed-methods approach, semi-structured interviews with 14 acade­
micians were conducted, followed by thematic analysis using NVIVO and ranking via the Best-worst Multi- 
criteria Decision-Making Process (BWM). The research indicates that resolving these challenges can streamline 
the transition to AI-enabled educational technologies. The study highlights the significance of university man­
agement in formulating targeted policies and training programs that minimize these challenges, ultimately 
enhancing the educational infrastructure and fostering a technologically proficient academic workforce. Theo­
retically, this research enriches the discourse on technology empowerment in education by mapping the inter­
play between educational advancements and organizational change, offering an understanding that can be 
applied to similar contexts globally.
1. Introduction
The education sector’s contribution to the world’s economic growth 
has increased as an outcome of digital transformation and moderniza­
tion (Castro Benavides et al., 2020). This shift has been driven by 
continuous advancements in innovative technologies across various 
domains (Saihi et al., 2023). Notably, the integration of technologies 
such as the Internet of Things (IoT), Artificial Intelligence (AI), Big Data, 
Robotics, and Automation has become central to Industry 4.0 (Ferrigno 
et al., 2023a,b; Ferrigno et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2024). In this regard, 
Culot et al. (2020) provide an in-depth analysis of Industry 4.0, shedding 
light on the complexities of modern industrial technologies. Their 
findings emphasize the importance of educators designing curricula that 
are both technologically advanced and adaptable to ongoing industrial 
changes, ensuring learners are equipped to shape the future of 
technology.
Recently, the European Commission marked the ten-year 
anniversary of Industry 4.0 by announcing the emergence of Industry 
5.0 (Leon, 2023). The transition to Industry 5.0 has resulted in the 
coexistence of two industrial revolutions, each offering unique appli­
cations across sectors (Xu et al., 2021). This new phase, Industry 5.0, 
lays the groundwork for Education 5.0, which aims to align AI-enhanced 
education with human consciousness (Andres et al., 2022, pp. 
2921–2926).
According to Rahim (2021b), the fundamental components of Edu­
cation 5.0 encompass research, teaching, community service, innova­
tion, and industrialization. Lantada (2020) further suggests that 
Engineering Education 5.0 should prioritize ethical considerations, 
values, consciences, and humanitarianism as key performance criteria 
for the next generation of engineers. However, recent studies reveal that 
the implementation of Education 5.0 has been hindered by insufficient 
curriculum updates, a shallow comprehension of the Education 5.0 
doctrine, limited stakeholders’ engagement, unfavorable academicians’ 
capacity, and a limited timeframe allocated for the activity (Munikwa &

Previous studies have examined challenges to the adoption of In­
dustry 5.0 in emerging economies highlighting that the barriers asso­
ciated with costs and funding systems, capacity scalability, upskilling, 
and reskilling of human labor are perceived as the most prominent 
barriers (Karmaker et al., 2023). A comprehensive literature review 
must be conducted using academic databases like Scopus, Web of Sci­
ence, and Google Scholar. The process begins with a keyword search for 
terms such as "Education 5.0," "Sustainable Development Goals," 
"Emerging Economies," and "Mixed-Method Approach" to assess the 
frequency, focus, and context of existing research. Analyzing recent 
studies from the past five years will reveal their findings, methodologies, 
and noted limitations. Moreover, no previous study has used interviews 
and BWM to explore and rank the challenges to the adoption of Edu­
cation 5.0 with a vision toward Sustainable Development (Asif et al., 
2023). Studies have shown that academicians encounter a wide range of 
challenges while implementing technologically based working practices 
(Nascimento et al., 2024). These challenges reduce the rate of progress 
toward attaining the degree of upskilling and reskilling required for the 
purposeful, sustainable adaptation of technology (Mercader & Gairín, 
2020). The study advances research in this area by addressing two key 
questions. 
1. What are the challenges that academicians face when they imple­
ment Education 5.0 in an emerging economy?
2. How can these challenges be addressed based on the relevance of 
their redressal to improve SDGs?
To achieve these goals, this study proposes a mixed-method 
approach that explores and prioritizes the influential barriers to sus­
tainable technology empowerment of academicians in Education 5.0 
implementation. We integrated qualitative thematic analysis with 
quantitative modeling using the Best-Worst Method (BWM) (Vieira 
et al., 2022). The qualitative phase identifies significant challenges that 
academics experience when implementing Education 5.0, and the 
quantitative phase ranks these challenges using BWM to provide an 
organized and prioritized view. We considered alternative multi-criteria 
decision-making such as AHP, ELECTRE, or VIKOR. After a careful 
evaluation (James et al., 2022), we found that their intricacy and the 
increased amount of data they require make them less appropriate than 
BWM for the specific objectives of our study. More specifically, we 
divided our study into two distinct phases.
In Phase 1, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 14 aca­
demicians to identify the problems they experienced in the imple­
mentation of Education 5.0 measures. These interviews were analyzed 
through thematic analysis using NVIVO software. Thematic analysis is a 
qualitative analytical approach that examines categorizations and elu­
cidates themes or patterns pertinent to the data (Ferrigno et al., 2023a,
b). This phase helped organize challenges into 8 distinct dimensions or 
themes, exploring the major challenges faced by academicians in the 
process of technology empowerment.
In Phase 2, we employed Multi-Criteria Decision-Making procedures 
(MCDM), which include both quantitative and qualitative aspects and 
have gained widespread acceptance in educational research in recent 
years (Malik et al., 2021). We specifically utilized the Best-Worst 
Method (BWM), a relatively new MCDM approach that facilitates sys­
tematic and structured contrasts (Vieira et al., 2022). In this phase, BWM 
was used to calculate weights for each of the themes identified in the 
previous phase, prioritizing the most critical challenges faced by aca­
demicians. This method not only requires less information but also en­
sures greater consistency in the comparisons made, thereby highlighting 
the most relevant themes that require immediate attention for resolving 
the most influential challenges to sustainable technology empowerment 
in an emerging economy.
Considering the growing significance of digitalization and its 
disruption in emerging economies, the findings of this study may enrich 
the academic literature on sustainable technological empowerment of 
academicians. Moreover, the method of prioritizing challenges from best 
to worst was employed to rank the identified obstacles effectively, 
enabling the implementation of necessary measures. The increasing 
demand for academicians to adapt to the AI and ICT-enabled education 
sector motivates educational institutions to recognize and address the 
primary challenges towards the sustainable technological empowerment 
of academicians for Education 5.0, tackling the disruptions caused by 
Education 5.0.

2. Education 5.0 in emerging economies
Education 5.0 was first adopted by the Government of Zimbabwe to 
enhance their higher and tertiary education system. Extant studies 
suggest that it should be used by universities and colleges to achieve 
shared development for all stakeholders (Ind et al., 2022). Conse­
quently, educational institutions are becoming increasingly conscious 
that chasing economic gains alone would not help them survive in this 
era, where adaptation to Education 5.0 is at its ultimate expression. 
Therefore, efforts to imbue Artificial Intelligence (AI) with conscious­
ness have started with the introduction of Education 5.0 in 2020 in 
emerging economies.
Education 5.0 represents a pivotal shift in the pedagogical landscape 
of emerging economies, underpinned by the urgent need to equip 
stakeholders with the necessary skills for the 21st century (Kateryna 
et al., 2020). This paradigm emphasizes the importance of conscious 
technological advancement and digital empowerment, recognizing that 
merely granting access to technology is insufficient. Instead, stake­
holders must be empowered through organization-led initiatives and 
external support systems to effectively harness the potential of tech­
nology for educational purposes (Cucino et al., 2022).
By promoting learning, literacy, and life skills, Education 5.0 ad­
dresses the pressing need for upskilling and reskilling initiatives, 
aligning with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly 
SDG 4 (Rahim, 2021b). Information and Communication Technology 
(ICT) serves as a critical catalyst for educational reforms, facilitating 
communication among stakeholders and promoting universal digital 
literacy (Adarkwah, 2021). Collaboration between educational in­
stitutions, governments, and civil society is essential to implement SDGs 
effectively and foster a conducive learning environment (Togo & Gan­
didzanwa, 2021). Through the adoption of Education 5.0, emerging 
economies can significantly enhance their educational systems, ensuring 
that individuals are equipped to meet the challenges and opportunities

While Education 5.0 brings transformative prospects for pedagogy, it 
also presents significant challenges that necessitate strategic manage­

development and execution of reformative strategies (Tabata & Johns­

economies, like India, where the adoption of advanced educational 
models like Education 5.0 is critical for sustainable development (Payel 
et al., 2023).
2.1. Addressing the research gap in the adoption of education 5.0 in 
emerging economies
Despite significant research on the broader facets of Education 5.0, a 
critical gap persists in understanding the specific challenges academi­
cians face when implementing these strategies in emerging economies. 
Previous studies have addressed barriers to Industry 5.0, identifying 
obstacles such as costs, funding mechanisms, capacity scalability, and 
the need for upskilling and reskilling (Mukherjee et al., 2023), but these 
analyses often rely on secondary data and overlook the unique demands 
of Education 5.0. While some research has explored the barriers to 
various aspects of technology integration (Quaicoe & Pata, 2020; Runge 
et al., 2023), there has been limited focus on the practical challenges 
academics encounter in adopting Education 5.0. Additionally, there is a 
notable absence of studies that employ qualitative interviews and the 
Best Worst Method (BWM) to systematically identify and rank these 
challenges within the framework of Sustainable Development. This gap 
is particularly significant given the multitude of challenges educators 
face in integrating technology-driven practices, which slow progress 
toward the necessary upskilling and reskilling required for sustainable 
technology adoption (Mercader & Gairín, 2020). Moreover, although 
scholars like Karmaker et al. (2023) and Mukherjee et al. (2023) have 
explored related topics, they have not thoroughly examined the specific 
influence of Education 5.0 on academics, nor have they provided clear 
strategies to overcome these challenges.
To address this gap, our study will conduct a comprehensive litera­
ture review using academic databases like Scopus, Web of Science, and 
Google Scholar, focusing on terms such as "Education 5.0," "Sustainable 
Development Goals," "Emerging Economies," and "Mixed-Method 
Approach." We aim to enhance the current state of knowledge by of­
fering empirical insights into the challenges faced by academicians and 
proposing a prioritization strategy using a mixed-method approach that 
includes qualitative interviews and the BWM. This innovative frame­
work will provide practical guidance for educational institutions in 
emerging economies, helping them navigate the complexities of 
Education 5.0 implementation and align with the evolving needs of the 
workforce. Our study’s unique contribution lies in bridging the gap 
between theoretical frameworks and real-world applications, offering a 
structured roadmap for sustainable and effective Education 5.0 
adoption.
3. Research methodology
To tackle our research questions, this study utilized a mixed-methods 
approach, i.e., a variant of multimethod examinations that entails the 
integration of multiple qualitative ("qual") or quantitative techniques 
("quan") (Morse, 1991). In this study, we employed a twofold plan of 
action to implement a mixed-methods approach. In phase 1, we con­
ducted a qualitative analysis of 14 semi-structured interviews with ac­
ademicians of the Indian education system. According to Taylor and 
Bodgan (1998), the utilization of qualitative research methodology 
proves advantageous in the investigation of novel and unexplored as­
pects within a given research context, in our case the challenges to the 
implementation of Education 5.0 in an emerging economy. As such, this 
approach supported our study to make informed decisions regarding 
identifying the major challenges. After identifying these challenges, we 
conducted a quantitative analysis of experts’ inputs to conclusively 
prioritize these challenges, laying the foundation for the establishment 
of a redressal mechanism aimed at fostering sustainable technology 
empowerment of academicians. More specifically, in Phase 2 a survey 
instrument was employed to gather data on the identified challenges. 
The challenges that were identified were assessed and ranked using the 
BWM. A comprehensive explanation of the mixed methodology is pro­
vided in Fig. 1 and it will be discussed in the subsequent sections.
3.1. Data collection and response acquisition
During the qualitative analysis phase of the preliminary research 
stage, several semi-structured detailed interviews were carried out. We 
contacted academicians using purposive sampling (Campbell et al., 
2020) and snowball sampling. The use of the snowball sampling 
approach facilitated the identification and acquisition of respondents 
who were well-suited for the research (Ramadani et al., 2022). Given the 
rapid integration of Education 5.0 into the Indian education system, 
particularly during the pandemic, academicians faced the urgent need to 
incorporate technology into their professional practices. To gain 
in-depth knowledge related to the challenges faced by academicians, we 
designed an open-ended questionnaire. The interview questions were 
carefully framed to correspond with the main objectives of the research, 
which aimed to identify and comprehend the challenges encountered by 
academicians when implementing Education 5.0 in a developing econ­
omy. The process of defining these questions involved several key steps. 
1 Literature Review: The literature review focused on doing a thorough 
examination of existing literature on Education 5.0, technological 
empowerment, and specific challenges encountered by educational 
institutions in emerging economies. This review facilitated the 
identification of important themes and areas of research that our 
study sought to investigate.
2. Expert Consultations: In order to improve and ensure the significance 
and comprehensiveness of these issues, we sought advice from ex­
perts in managing educational technology implementation in the 
Universities mainly the Department heads dealing with the imple­
mentation of new technologies. These consultations offered valuable 
perspectives on current challenges and enabled us to formulate 
questions that encompass both theoretical and practical dimensions 
of Education 5.0.
3. Pilot Testing: Before finalizing the interview guide, we conducted a 
pilot test with a small group of academicians who were not part of 
the main study. This pilot facilitated our evaluation of the awareness, 
focus, and consistency of the questions. The feedback received

some HOIs, we expanded our network through their recommendations, 
employing snowball sampling. Email invitations were distributed to 
academicians in positions of authority, commonly referred to as decision 
makers (DMs), to conduct a survey. Out of the 20 academicians who 
accepted the interview invitation, four were unable to participate due to 
work commitments while two were not able to generate complete 
transcripts as they were not quite well suited for the topic of study. 
Finally, 14 individuals provided consent to actively contribute to our 
research, reaching data saturation at this stage (Dell’Era et al., 2020).
Upon receiving a favorable response from the 14 academicians, a 
mutually agreeable time was scheduled for the interview. A concise 
overview was presented to aid each respondent in comprehending the 
objective of this research (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007). Following the 
primary explanation of the session, semi-structured interviews were 
conducted between March to September of 2023 with each participant 
to gain a deeper understanding of the problem that had been discovered. 
The list of the interview questions is reported in the Appendix. The in­
terviews were conducted using various platforms, including Zoom, MS 
Teams, Skype, and in person. The recorded conversations were tran­
scribed to identify overarching themes and subthemes (Jason & Glen­
wick, 2016). According to Charmaz (2006), the process of data 
collection can be concluded when the categories or themes reach a state 
of saturation. This occurs when the inclusion of additional information

3.2. Establishing the parameters and scales for rating
Braun and Clarke (2006) acknowledged that the utilization of the­
matic analysis facilitates the systematic organization and categorization 
of overarching themes derived from a collection of data. Identifying 
common themes enables the researcher to document the fundamental 
significance of the subject matter. The trustworthiness of the data was 
ensured through the application of Lincoln and Guba’s (1985) criteria, 
which include transferability, credibility, confirmability, and depend­
ability. The incorporation of these characteristics is frequently sup­
ported by qualitative researchers, and therefore, they were included in 
the study to ensure the integrity and quality of the data. The adaptability 
of thematic analysis is one of its most celebrated attributes, allowing it to 
be customized to the specific demands of varied research projects. For 
example, a study undertaken by Wang et al. (2022) employed thematic 
analysis to identify recurring themes or barriers in circular product 
design. This approach allowed for a nuanced understanding of the ob­
stacles faced and aids in the development of targeted interventions for 
promoting sustainable design practices. Since Education 5.0 is a rela­
tively new topic and has not been researched in depth, interviews with 
14 academicians helped in exploring the nuanced perspective of chal­
lenges to Technology empowerment.
These challenges were ranked in order of relevance using BWM to 
determine which are the most significant obstacles standing in the way 
of the successful implementation of sustainable technological empow­
erment of academics for Education 5.0.

4. Case implementation
This paper attempts to explore the challenges to adoption of Edu­
cation 5.0 and suggests the best order to build an efficient redressal 
mechanism. This section highlights the case background in sub-section 
4.1. Further, it explains the necessity of undertaking this research along 
with the steps taken to conduct the interviews in sub-section 4.2. Sub- 
sections 4.3 and 4.4 detail the process for data collection and extrac­
tion of themes/challenges.
4.1. Case background
Digitalization and modernization of working patterns have increased 
the education sector’s contribution to the global economy, and Educa­
tion 5.0 is the latest transition to connect the AI-enabled education 
system with consciousness. Industry 5.0 serves as the foundation for 
Education 5.0, which adopts many features such as personalization, 
innovation, sustainability, and cost optimization (Andres et al., 2022, 
pp. 2921–2926). Academicians should be equipped to create a new 
curriculum in line with Education 5.0 (Munikwa & Mapara, 2022). 
Academicians are essential stakeholders in India’s education system, 
and they play a pivotal role in ensuring that Education 5.0 is adopted 
and successfully implemented. While coping with technology-based 
work patterns, academicians encounter a variety of challenges. These 
barriers reduce the rate of progress towards attaining the quality of 
upskilling and reskilling required for sustained technological adapta­
tion. Hence, universities must comprehend the important issues that 
they encounter in sustainable technological empowerment for Educa­
tion 5.0 to adapt to the forthcoming expectations of academicians. They 
need to understand the disruptions that integration of fifth-generation 
education would present on the sustainable technology empowerment 
of academicians.
The study was conducted with the help of academicians. These ac­
ademicians were a mix of assistant professors, associate professors, and 
professors, and they are experiencing different facets of Technological 
empowerment for Education 5.0. They have observed the disruptions 
caused by the education sector’s adoption of Education 5.0.
4.2. Case explanation
The increased rate of technology adoption in light of academicians’ 
sustainable technological empowerment enables the interplay of phys­
ical and digital knowledge exchange patterns, resulting in significant 
innovation in the Education sector. Increasing acceptance of Education 
5.0 necessitates educators’ upskilling and reskilling with an emphasis on 
academic and mental well-being. The sample comprised 14 academi­
cians and we observed data saturation after these interviews. The DMs

4.3. Selection of challenges for sustainable technology empowerment of 
academicians for education 5.0
We conducted 14 semi-structured interviews to identify the pre­
vailing issues. The basis for each interview was a pre-established ques­
tionnaire. Open-ended questions were used to elicit a diverse range of 
concerns from the interviewees. The participants were reached via 
various means including Microsoft Teams, Zoom, Skype, or in-person, in 
order to conduct comprehensive interviews. The interviews were 
recorded and thereafter transcribed.

4.4.1. Theme 1: research challenges (RC)
Academicians highlighted that research is one of the most essential 
factors for academic development. They noted that there are now more 
constraints to research because of poor response rates for data collection 
and a shortage of research training time for academicians, both of which 
have a negative effect on the quality of research and, by extension, the 
number of academic papers that are published. The following excerpts 
from the interviews provide more support for the aforementioned 
assertions: 
“The recent uncertainty has expanded the scope of research and inno­
vation and we expected that the rate of online data collection would in­
crease and become more precise, however the response rate from industry 
has declined and due to busy teaching requirements, genuine fieldwork- 
based research is difficult to conduct” (Respondent 1).
“Academicians new to the field of research face major problems in 
learning data analysis software which has impacted their research out­
comes and publications opportunities. Research is an important aspect for 
our growth and Online research training lacks the needed amount of 
personal mentorship for novice researchers” (Respondent 5).
“Absence of data analysis tools and paid databases, such as Scopus, 
impedes the research of academicians and hinders the adoption of Edu­
cation 5.0” (Respondent 9).

4.4.4. Theme 4: Organizational Challenges (OC)
Educational institutions need to adapt to the changing educational 
patterns and grant higher levels of Autonomy and Authority. An 
example of this challenge is contained in the following data extracts: 
“As a result of the recent pandemic, the academicians’ role has expanded, 
and their job demands have increased their stress levels. Universities 
should construct tech-enabled stress-relieving zones where wellness 
training, meditation, yoga, and other extracurricular activities may be 
adopted to increase the degree of conscious technology empowerment, 
hence promoting the adoption of Education 5.0” (Respondent 2).
“Universities should provide a certain level of autonomy to academicians 
where they are not bound by fixed hours with attendance on campus. This 
impacts an academicians’ sense of freedom to indulge in constructive 
activities and networking” (Respondent 6).
4.4.5. Theme 5: Student-Teacher Involvement Challenges (STC)
Aside from academicians, students and their parents are the most 
significant stakeholders in the education sector, and their interaction is 
crucial for the adoption of Education 5.0. The following extracts were 
collected from the interviews: 
“The mutual understanding between educators and learners has been 
influenced by new patterns of technology-based teaching and learning. 
The digital involvement of students is less as compared to a physical 
classroom. Hence, universities should embrace more interactive platforms 
to accelerate the rate of academicians’ technological empowerment” 
(Respondent 7).
“Students, being one of the most important stakeholders in the education 
sector, need to be addressed with the utmost priority. Broad instructional 
platforms such as Zoom and MS Teams impact the emotional connection 
with learners. For sustainable technological empowerment for Education 
5.0, universities must invest in improved technologies such as augmented 
reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) gadgets to assist academicians in 
forming emotional bonds with students” (Respondent 13).
4.4.6. Theme 6: Pandemic-Based Teaching Challenges (PTC)
The pandemic has completely altered the Teaching-learning patterns 
in the education sector thus impacting academicians’ sustainable tech­
nology empowerment. The following extract is included in the data 
sample: 
“The pandemic has had a significant influence on work patterns and has 
unwittingly accelerated the adaptation of measures to promote Education 
5.0. The rapid expansion of the education industry as a result of the 
pandemic has overwhelmed educators with digital innovations. Empow­
ering academicians with technology will greatly assist them in responding 
to the volatility of any future crises” (Respondent 8).
4.4.7. Theme 7: Technology-Based Challenges (TBC)
To build better educational platforms, the latest technologies like 
augmented reality, artificial intelligence, virtual reality, etc. should be 
introduced, and academicians need to be reskilled about such platforms. 
As technology is one of the most prominent factors in the adoption of 
Education 5.0, almost all the interviewees highlighted these challenges. 
As an illustrative example, this challenge can be found in the following 
data extracts: 
“For sustainable technology empowerment of academicians, Proper 
technical and well-being trainings need to be conducted more frequently. 
The rate of technological development is rapid, and educators are not 
entirely tech-savvy. They require adequate technological training and 
refresher programs, along with a competent technical department for 
assistance” (Respondent 1).
“Network or connection issues are the most often cited difficulty during 
interviews” (Respondent 14).
4.4.8. Theme 8: academicians’ personal challenges (APC)
Education 5.0 promotes the well-being of academicians. Sustainable 
technology empowerment not only means an advancement in 
technology-related aspects but also overall empowerment, which in­
cludes all the personal shortcomings faced by the academician. An 
illustration of this can be found in the following data extracts: 
“The recent pandemic has led to a realization among educators that along 
with financial and physical well-being, academicians also require training 
for improving their consciousness related to themselves as well as their 
work” (Respondent 4).
“Any change requires a personal acceptance of the change and Educators 
need to develop a high degree of Self-efficacy to adapt to changing cir­
cumstances. I have seen educators taking a VRS 5 years before their 
retirement due to the sudden adoption of Technology and I have also seen 
academicians in their extension period trying to adapt technology. So, I 
strongly feel that an academician self-efficacy plays a vital role here” 
(Respondent 13).
In this section, we identified the 8 themes that, according to our data, 
represent the most challenging problems encountered in the area of 
sustainable technology empowerment of academicians for Education 
5.0. Nevertheless, we will further analyze them. In the subsequent part, 
we will examine these themes by using a rating scale to determine the 
challenges that should be prioritized. Moreover, a recommended 
approach for effectively tackling these issues will be elucidated.
4.5. Ranking sustainable technology empowerment challenges based on 
best-worst method
The BWM was utilized to evaluate and prioritize the shortlisted 
challenges to determine the final ranking. The response collection and 
results obtained using the method are explained in the following 
subsection.
4.5.1. Response collection from DMs
The data inputs for the best-worst method of response collecting 
were obtained from recognized academicians. Based on their experien­
tial knowledge, observational data, and professional skills, a group of 9 
DMs was requested to identify the most favorable and unfavorable ob­
stacles. The DMs, here denoted as Experts, are the Department Heads in 
various Indian Universities. Out of nine department heads, three were 
also considered in Phase 1. They were chosen as experts, as they were

In BWM consensus was obtained through a structured process where 
decision-makers selected the most important (best) and least important 
(worst) criteria from a predefined set. The experts were asked to make a 
comparative analysis of the most significant challenges about other 
challenges, followed by an examination of other challenges about the 
least favorable challenge. The comparison involved evaluating the 
relative merits of the chosen optimal factor against other factors, as well 
as comparing other factors to the identified most challenging obstacle. 
These pairwise comparisons formed vectors that were used in an opti­
mization problem to minimize inconsistency in judgments. After this, a 
pairwise comparison was performed to obtain the mean weights, which 
helped in delineating ranks more precisely. For group decision-making, 
individual judgments were aggregated, often using geometric mean or 
consensus-building techniques. Consensus was achieved when the 
inconsistency in comparisons was low, meaning decision-makers pro­
vided consistent judgments, and the aggregated weights reflected a 
unified prioritization of criteria. If inconsistency was high, feedback 
loops allowed for revisions of judgments until consensus was reached, 
indicated by a low consistency ratio and agreed-upon weights, ensuring 
a coherent and unified ranking of criteria for decision-making.
4.5.2. Findings derived from the best-worst approach
Following the BWM (Best Worst Method) method described in step 
3.4, we conducted a ranking of the challenges based on their level of 
significance. As a reduced number of comparisons are involved in BWM,

icant criterion. According to the expert team’s agreement, challenge 
TBC has been identified as the most favorable criterion, whilst STC has 
been deemed the least favorable criterion. By adhering to the procedures 
outlined in steps 3.4.2 and 3.4.3, DMs were able to express their choice 
for the best criteria to others (BO) and express their choice for the Worst 
criteria to others (OW). As an example, the DM4 identified RC as the

one side, addressing the training needs for advanced technologies has 
become a significant obstacle in the adoption of Industry 5.0. 
Conversely, prioritizing research and personal challenges takes prece­
dence over financial challenges in the overall assessment of urgent 
issues.
Second, to the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first 
attempts to develop a mixed method integrating thematic analysis and 
BWM to identify and rank the most relevant challenges to implementing 
I5.0 in the education sector. As such, this paper responds to the call of 
recent works that advocate that a broader evaluation of Industry 5.0 
technologies can take place through prioritization and rankings (Asif 
et al., 2023). By scrutinizing the diverse challenges faced by academi­
cians, the research expands the theoretical understanding of the im­
pediments to technology empowerment. It not only identifies these 
challenges but also goes a step further by proposing a systematic 
redressal mechanism by prioritizing the challenges for targeted in­
terventions. This nuanced approach ensures a strategic and effective 
response to multifaceted issues inhibiting the full integration of tech­
nology into academic practices. Our findings demonstrate that in addi­
tion to the challenges impeding the adoption of Industry 5.0 in emerging 
economies (Mukherjee et al., 2023), Education 5.0 also faces challenges 
with academics’ reskilling and upskilling, funding, expansion, and 
innovation. Additionally, academicians have multiple roles to play in 
the education sector, leading them to deal with challenges related to 
research and social and industrial connectivity. As such, this study un­
derscores the critical need for educational institutions to explore inno­
vative approaches geared toward facilitating the seamless adoption of 
digital transformation by educators. This exploration exceeds conven­
tional perspectives, urging institutions to engage in forward-thinking 
strategies that align with the evolving nature of technology in educa­
tion. By doing so, the theoretical framework positions itself as a guide for 
institutions seeking to navigate the complexities of the digital landscape, 
offering insights into transformative measures that extend beyond con­
ventional paradigms. Furthermore, the theoretical contribution em­
phasizes the indispensable role of learning opportunities and continuous 
skill development in fostering lasting resilience within university-based 
academic teams (Çipi et al., 2023). It calls for strategic investments in

5.2. Practical implications
This study offers educational institutions with a comprehensive un­
derstanding of the various challenges to the sustainable technological 
empowerment of academicians for Education 5.0, allowing for the 
development of appropriate policies. Potential upliftment should be 
emphasized to enable academicians to acquire the skills necessary to 
work successfully in the present and in the future, with the goal of 
removing barriers to technological empowerment. Urgent action is 
required to alleviate the skills gap by providing suitable development 
opportunities (Çipi et al., 2023). Policies must be created for diverse 
academicians based on their levels of technological expertise. According 
to the findings of this study, the current curriculum should put more 
emphasis on developing key skills and competencies that match the 
requirements of Education 5.0. Universities should focus on building 
tech-empowered infrastructures. These are the numerous policy pro­
posals for effective adaption to Education 5.0 by empowering acade­
micians with technology and assisting the organization’s sustainable 
growth.
6. Conclusion
This study explored multifaceted challenges impeding sustainable 
technology empowerment among academicians within the context of 
Education 5.0. We have identified significant barriers across different 
areas, ranging from technological challenges to research challenges, that 
require immediate intervention in order to fully harness the potential of 
educational progress. It is crucial that we resolve these challenges in 
order to improve the quality of education and realize the global Sus­
tainable Development Goals.
The results of our research highlight the need of addressing these 
challenges in order to create a smooth transition to an educational 
environment that is empowered by technology. The study’s findings 
indicate that in order to effectively empower academicians, it is crucial 
to implement strategic interventions and provide supporting frame­
works. These implications have direct relevance for policy and practice.
Looking ahead, further research should explore specific strategies for 
addressing the highlighted challenges while investigating the long-term 
impacts of these interventions on educational outcomes. By persistently 
prioritizing these crucial areas, stakeholders may guarantee that the 
development of the educational sector corresponds to the requirements 
of the 21st century and beyond.
6.1. Limitations of the study
The study has some limitations. First, it is limited to examining 
university academicians, thereby not including insights from school­
teachers, college educators, administrative staff, and students. Second, 
while the recognized challenges to sustainable technology empower­
ment are ranked using the Best-Worst Method, the study does not extend 
to additional quantitative testing methods that could further validate or 
refine these findings. Third, geographical details are not explored, as the 
research does not differentiate results by region. Despite these limits, the 
study provides a solid foundation for comprehending the essential 
challenges that academicians face in the context of Education 5.0, as 
well as actionable insights for higher education institutions in emerging 
economies.
