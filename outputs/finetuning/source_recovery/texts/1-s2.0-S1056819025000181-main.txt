A B S T R A C T
This paper investigates the role of regional specialization in ICT in fostering AI patenting performance and inter- 
regional spatial spillovers across China’s provincial-level regions. Using panel fixed effects estimators, a Spatial 
Autoregressive Regression model and by adapting the technological frontier IV strategy on a comprehensive 
database covering 2006–2021, we find that positively selecting areas where regional ICT specialization is 
leveraged – the “picking the fittest” approach – can increase AI patenting performance while exacerbating 
regional disparities. Furthermore, we find that geographical proximity to developed AI regions impedes AI 
patenting progress in neighboring areas. The findings highlight the need for collaborative regional strategies and 
urge policy-makers to achieve a balance between strengthening regional specialization and promoting 
cooperation.
1. Introduction
Spatial spillovers significantly influence regional innovation dy­
namics and economic development (Antonelli and Colombelli, 2017). 
The recombination of existing local knowledge and capacities leads to 
the emergence of new technological activities and innovation (Frenken 
and Boschma, 2007). However, research on regional innovation tends to 
consider regions as isolated entities, neglecting inter-regional spillovers 
(Boschma et al., 2017). While empirical research in different European 
contexts has highlighted that knowledge diffusion is geographically 
constrained, confirming the importance of agglomeration effects and 
their diminishing effects with distance (Moreno et al., 2005; Lehmann 
and Menter, 2018), few studies have focused on the negative aspects of 
such agglomeration and inter-regional knowledge diffusion (Wang et al., 
2022). For instance, Montmartin et al. (2018) investigated the effect of 
policy mix on private R&D investments and found significant negative 
spatial dependence among R&D investments. This leads to resource 
crowding and market rivalry forcing regions to focus more on 
out-competing their neighbors rather than engaging in cooperative 
innovation efforts (Bloom et al., 2013).
This is particularly relevant in the case of artificial intelligence (AI). 
The need to pursue market dominance in many AI-related sectors such as 
deep learning and robotics has generated a competitive race (Cockburn 
et al., 2019), not to mention AI’s high reliance on data, providing a 
significant competitive advantage to those capable of collecting, 
analyzing, and utilizing it (Klingenberg et al., 2019). At the regional 
level, AI has the potential to drive technological diversification by 
enabling the recombination of complex knowledge, thereby allowing 
regions to develop and sustain new specializations over time (Montresor 
and Quatraro, 2017). This process hinges on the ability of AI to integrate 
diverse local knowledge bases, reinforcing the role of regional capabil­
ities in fostering innovation (Bresnahan, 2010; Brynjolfsson et al., 
2018).
Despite this potential, the extent to which AI-driven agglomeration 
yields positive regional outcomes remains underexplored. While 
research at the European level acknowledges the broader implications of 
Industry 4.0 technologies, of which AI is a part, and AI on regional 
economies, more in-depth investigations are required to assess its long- 
term effects (De Propris and Bailey, 2021). In particular, current 
empirical analysis on AI-related clustering makes it difficult to deter­
mine whether such concentrations foster sustainable growth or exacer­
bate regional inequalities (De Propris and Bellandi, 2021). Furthermore, 
the geography of Industry 4.0 technologies remains highly uneven, with 
regional capabilities playing a decisive role in determining whether 
regions can successfully integrate into new knowledge production net­
works (Balland and Boschma, 2021). Given these disparities, policy

Additionally, while policy interventions are widely recognized as crucial 
for regional adaptation, further research is needed to identify the most 
effective governance strategies for ensuring that AI-driven innovation 
benefits a diverse set of regions rather than concentrating in a few highly 
specialized hubs. To address these gaps, this research examines the 
following research questions: what are the effects of previous knowledge 
base specialization, positive selection, and inter-regional spillovers in 
the development of AI innovation?
We use the Chinese case as an empirical setting to address the 
research questions. China has set clear and ambitious goals to become a 
leading global innovator in AI (State Council of the PRC, 2017). These 
efforts are evident not only in the prominent role of the Chinese gov­
ernment with its numerous AI-related policies (Yang and Huang, 2022) 
but also in the interplay of different actors like government institutions, 
universities, and industry (Jacobides et al., 2021). One of China’s key 
strategies for developing AI is the “National New Generation Artificial 
Intelligence Innovation and Development Pilot Zones” (hereafter “AI 
zones”) policy, introduced by the Ministry of Science and Technology 
(Ministry of Science and Technology of the PRC MOST, 2019) in 2019. 
These AI zones were designed to leverage local assets and foster AI 
development at a national scale. The policy’s ambition is twofold: to 
enhance AI capabilities at the regional level and to drive overall national 
AI technological progress.
However, China’s AI innovation strategy faces significant obstacles 
in achieving balanced regional growth. One concern is the inherent bias 
in China’s policy experimentation processes. Many pilot projects are 
implemented in economically advanced regions where local leaders, 
driven by short-term political incentives, may exert extra effort during 
the pilot phase, but the long-term sustainability of these initiatives re­
mains uncertain (Wang and Yang, 2021). This “picking the fittest” 
strategy has the potential to widen existing disparities by reinforcing the 
competitive advantage of leading regions while sidelining less devel­
oped ones. Moreover, differences in institutional proximity across 
neighboring regions can limit inter-regional knowledge spillovers, 
thereby further hindering innovation diffusion and deepening regional 
disparities (He et al., 2019; Xie et al., 2023). These dynamics can result 
in “club convergence,” where regions with similar initial conditions 
cluster together, exacerbating technological and economic inequalities 
(Jimenez-Moro et al., 2023; Rui et al., 2023).
This pattern highlights a paradox within China’s national AI strat­
egy: while fostering regional hubs can promote advanced specialization, 
it may inadvertently hinder collaborative spillovers necessary for sus­
tained innovation development (Bloom et al., 2013; Montmartin et al., 
2018; Wang et al., 2022). Understanding this tension is essential for 
aligning regional AI initiatives with China’s broader innovation goals. 
Despite extensive research on China’s national AI strategy and its global 
ambitions, the regional dimension remains underexplored. Prior studies 
have primarily focused on the national innovation system, focusing on 
the role of major corporations (Jacobides et al., 2021), the triple helix 
model of government-industry-university collaboration (Arenal et al., 
2020), and the global interconnections driving AI growth (Yu et al., 
2022). However, since the introduction of the “2006 Medium and 
Long-Term Plan for Science and Technology Development” (Liu et al., 
2011), China’s strategy has increasingly emphasized regional innova­
tion, particularly through provincial-led programs (Heilmann et al., 
2013; Sun et al., 2018).
Therefore, the objective of this paper is twofold: first, to investigate 
the contribution of past knowledge specialization in enhancing AI pat­
enting performance in AI zones; and second, to comprehend the impact 
of inter-regional spillovers in the context of AI technologies in China.
To address these goals, we construct a database encompassing Chi­
nese provincial-level region data spanning the period from 2006 to 
2021. Using a linear panel regression with fixed effects estimators, a 
Spatial Autoregressive model, and by adapting Acemoglu and Restrepo’s 
(2020) technological frontier Instrumental Variable strategy, we show 
that the AI zone program significantly strengthens regional AI patenting 
performance and it emphasizes the effects of a “picking the fittest” 
strategy. This essentially means that China selected the regions with 
specific capabilities and pushed them even further by simultaneously 
accentuating regional disparities in AI patenting. Moreover, geograph­
ical proximity to regions with developed AI patenting may hinder the 
progress of neighboring regions. To mitigate these effects, we recom­
mend policies that enhance the absorptive capacity of lagging regions 
through investments in education, R&D infrastructure, and institutional 
reforms, while encouraging more collaborative, cross-regional innova­
tion initiatives. Creating AI Regional Collaborative Hubs could help 
align regional capabilities with national innovation objectives, reducing 
the inefficiencies of competitive spillovers and fostering inclusive 
growth across China’s AI landscape.
The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the 
relevant literature and the theoretical background and formulates the 
appropriate research hypothesis. Section 3 describes the data and 
methodology. Section 4 presents the analyses and the findings. Section 5
discusses the results, provides the scholarly and policy implications, and 
concludes the paper by offering areas for future research.
2. Literature background
2.1. Regional specialization, local experimentation and positive selection
Previous studies set in Europe and in the USA have found that regions 
that built solid foundations during earlier technological waves are better 
equipped to drive technological breakthroughs (Boschma et al., 2015; 
Laffi and Boschma, 2022). In particular, strong information and 
communication technologies (ICT) infrastructures can lead to AI 
development (Xiao and Boschma, 2022). Advanced manufacturing hubs 
with strong prior capabilities in ICT and Industry 3.0 are also more likely 
to adopt AI, as seen in the concentration of Industry 4.0 technologies in 
these areas (Corradini et al., 2021). At the heart of this process is the 
concept of recombinant growth, where innovation does not arise in 
isolation but from the recombination of existing knowledge and tech­
nologies (Weitzman, 1996, 1998). This aligns with the broader under­
standing of regional innovation studies, where new technological 
activities build on existing capacities and local knowledge base (Frenken 
and Boschma, 2007). Beyond their technological assets, these regions 
benefit from cognitive proximity, which enhances their ability to absorb 
and recombine knowledge and apply AI innovations effectively (Cohen 
and Levinthal, 1990). Thus, the relationship between prior technological 
capabilities and new innovations is not just a matter of resources but also 
of how well regions can recombine existing knowledge into new, pro­
ductive uses.
The regional variation in technological capabilities helps to explain 
the uneven distribution of AI innovation across different regions in 
China. China’s strategy for AI development heavily relies on the capacity 
of local governments to foster innovation, particularly through policy 
experimentation and the strategic selection of regions with strong 
technological foundations (Wang and Yang, 2021). The government’s 
approach is rooted in selecting these regions, granting them autonomy 
to experiment with AI applications. This decentralized model leverages 
regional strengths to fuel national technological progress (Liu et al., 
2022). This can lead to significant heterogeneity in the performance of 
science and technology parks in China, where regions with advanced 
technological infrastructures form elite innovation clusters that

Moreover, China’s economic development, particularly in key in­
dustries, has long been tied to regional policy initiatives (Li, 2015). 
Economic and Development Zones (EDZs) and High-Tech Development 
Zones (HTDZs) are prominent examples of regional policy imple­
mentations. EDZs focus on developing extra-local linkages, while HTDZs 
prioritize establishing domestic R&D institutes and collaborations for 
indigenous-based R&D (Howell, 2019). Recent evidence suggests that 
EDZs enhance economic growth through industrial clusters and 
improved firm performance (Liu et al., 2020), but they may not always 
generate high-quality growth at the regional level (Ma et al., 2023). In 
contrast, HTDZs generate learning effects and Marshallian-based exter­
nalities (Howell, 2019). China’s focus on AI has further amplified these 
efforts, as the government introduced cluster policies, industry enabler 
policies, and knowledge diffusion initiatives. The “AI zone” policy issued 
by the MOST in 2019 aims to establish 20 pilot zones by 2023, each of 
which will demonstrate replicable AI applications for the nation’s 
overall benefit (Wu et al., 2020). These zones are selected based on 
certain criteria, including a region’s past innovation capacity and their 
capabilities as ICT and AI innovators, among others. This positive se­
lection process ensures that only the most technologically capable re­
gions receive resources, maximizing the likelihood of success in AI 
adoption (Wang and Yang, 2021).
The AI zone policy exemplifies China’s hybrid implementation 
model, combining centralized oversight with decentralized, region- 
specific execution. By reinforcing local embeddedness in AI innova­
tion, this approach ensures that the most capable regions lead in AI 
while contributing to national technological advancement (Liu et al., 
2022). China’s AI development strategy is centered on selecting regions 
with strong ICT foundations, granting them the autonomy to experiment 
and innovate while maintaining alignment with national goals. By 
focusing resources on regions with proven technological capabilities, 
China enhances the likelihood of successful AI adoption and diffusion. 
The positive selection of these regions, combined with their ability to 
recombine existing knowledge into new applications, reinforces their 
role as key drivers of AI innovation. This strategic approach ensures that 
regional strengths are harnessed to fuel both local advancements and 
national leadership in AI. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis: 
H1.
Regional ICT specialization generates higher AI regional patenting 
performance in the candidate regions for AI development zones.
2.2. Negative knowledge spillovers and regional competition
Regional innovation is shaped by the inter-regional knowledge 
spillovers of its neighboring areas (Boschma et al., 2017), often arising 
through patenting and R&D activities in adjacent regions (Moreno et al., 
2005). While knowledge can spread through both intentional and un­
intentional channels (Antonelli and Colombelli, 2017), recent studies 
have started to address a research gap highlighting the negative side of 
innovation interactions between regions (Wang et al., 2022). As Mont­
martin et al. (2018) highlight, spatial dependencies can generate posi­
tive spillover effects through the sharing of knowledge but also 
exacerbate negative effects, such as market rivalry and competition for 
technological advantage between regions, leading to a redistribution of 
innovation resources. This tension is particularly pronounced in AI, 
where spillovers do not operate in the same way as in other technology 
sectors. Unlike traditional innovations, AI’s development is deeply 
reliant on large-scale data success, specialized computational infra­
structure, and high-skilled labor, which are often concentrated in a few 
leading regions (Cockburn et al., 2019). As a result, rather than pro­
moting broad-based innovation, AI spillovers can reinforce existing 
regional disparities by disproportionately benefiting regions with su­
perior access to these critical resources. Market rivalry in AI further 
exacerbates this dynamic, as regions compete not just for patents and 
talent but also for the underlying data that fuels AI advancements 
(Klingenberg et al., 2019). This differs from other sectors, where spill­
overs often lead to cumulative knowledge diffusion, whereas in AI, 
competition over exclusive data access and algorithmic advancements 
can limit the extent to which neighboring regions benefit from one an­
other’s progress. Consequently, while inter-regional competition may 
drive incremental innovation, it can simultaneously constrain break­
through advancements in AI, shaping an innovation landscape where 
proximity does not necessarily translate into shared progress (Byun 
et al., 2021; Drivas, 2021).
The Chinese framework presents a unique subject for analyzing inter- 
regional competition. Despite being governed by a single-party system, 
the 34 administrative regions present different access to resources and 
socio-economic conditions (Wei, 2000). China’s decentralization during 
the reform era that started in the 1970s gave local governments control 
over spending, resulting in intense inter-regional competition for mar­
kets and resources and a strong protectionism approach (He et al., 
2008). This decentralization encouraged regional governments to focus 
on local industries and innovation systems, which has led to fragmented 
policy diffusion and resistance to centralized directives as regions pri­
oritize their own interests (Gu, 2016). This competition has led to sig­
nificant regional divergence in innovation outcomes. Some regions, 
particularly those with stronger institutional environments and market 
conditions, have been able to attract more resources and talent, creating 
a feedback loop of increasing innovation performance (Gao et al., 2021). 
Jimenez-Moro et al. (2023) provide evidence of innovation convergence 
clubs, where regions with better institutional frameworks cluster 
together and outperform their neighbors leading to varying levels of 
patenting activities (Wang et al., 2015) and innovation misallocation (Li 
et al., 2017). Within this competitive framework, Chinese local gov­
ernments effectively utilize market forces to attract and sustain in­
dustries that complement their existing capabilities, encouraging 
regional specialization (He et al., 2018). This, however, increases 
inter-regional competition, as regions race to capture limited resources 
and position themselves as leaders in particular sectors since local 
governments are incentivized to protect their knowledge and resources 
from competitors, inhibiting positive knowledge spillovers (He et al., 
2018).
The role of institutional proximity is critical in these dynamics. 
Differences in institutional proximity limit inter-regional knowledge 
transfer, with varying regional governance structures preventing effec­
tive collaborations (He et al., 2019). Cao et al. (2019) emphasize the 
importance of both geographical and institutional proximity for effec­
tive inter-organizational collaboration, but in the Chinese context, these 
proximities are often unevenly distributed, exacerbating inter-regional 
innovation gaps. Furthermore, when the central government focuses 
on a significant industrial project or technology program in one prov­
ince, neighboring regions may reduce their own investments, leading to 
a crowding-out effect (Wang et al., 2022). In such cases, the central 
government’s focus on a particular region exacerbates inter-regional 
disparities, as surrounding regions invest less, perceiving diminished 
returns on their innovation efforts. Moreover, as found also in studies 
applied in Europe, local competitive policies may act as a “beggar-­
thy-neighbor” strategy, redistributing innovation resources from one 
region to another and intensifying the uneven competition for resources 
(Lehmann and Menter, 2018). The effectiveness of local government 
reforms and support structures plays a crucial role in determining 
regional innovation performance. Regions that have embraced institu­
tional reforms and aligned their innovation strategies with market de­
mands have shown higher levels of innovation, while those lagging in 
reforms face further constraints in a highly competitive inter-regional 
environment.
Therefore, while knowledge spillovers often benefit innovation, the 
inter-regional competition for AI-related resources may lead to negative 
spillover effects, diminishing the innovation performance of neigh­
boring regions. This competition, driven by both market rivalry and 
resource misallocation, reinforces regional disparities and limits the

3. Data, variables and methodology
3.1. Data sources
We conduct the empirical analysis on 30 Chinese provincial-level 
regions1 observed over the period 2006–2021. The starting year is 
chosen because of the introduction of the “2006 Plan” which is widely 
recognized as a turning point in China’s innovation journey (Liu et al., 
2011). We collected patent data from the Orbis Intellectual Property 
Database and the Annual Report of Patent Statistics. Then we collected 
provincial-level regions’ administrative data from the China Statistical 
Yearbook and the China Statistical Yearbook on Science and Technology2, 
which are all datasets previously and effectively used in empirical 
studies applied to China (Chen et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2023; Wang et al., 
2022; Wei, 2022).
3.2. Variables
3.2.1. Dependent variable
We measure provincial-level regions’ AI patenting performance in 
terms of patents applied to the China National Intellectual Property 
Administration (CNIPA). By relying on variables derived from Chinese 
domestic patent data, the study ensures comparability between regions. 
Consequently, these variables serve as consistent measures of both 
technologically and economically significant innovations across the 
provincial-level regions under examination (Acs et al., 2002). Our 
analysis concentrates on patent applicants, driven by the complexities of 
identifying inventors in China. The CNIPA lacks consistent documen­
tation of inventor details (Yin et al., 2020), and the homonym problem 
complicates the disambiguation of Chinese names, a widely recognized 
challenge in disambiguation methodologies (Li et al., 2014; Yin et al., 
2020). While acknowledging this limitation, previous studies have 
successfully utilized applicants’ locations rather than the inventors’ to 
conduct their analysis (Kroll, 2016; Suzuki, 2023).
Within the term “AI” there can be found different technologies 
(Cockburn et al., 2019), making the identification of what can be 
included within the label discussed in the literature (Benassi et al., 
2022). However, there are different ways in which AI can be codified 
and identified which have been used in the literature. We followed the 
search strategies used by Liu et al. (2021), Liu et al. (2021)) that rely on 
the union of selected keywords in the title, abstract or claim, IPC and 
CPC codes (expressed in Boolean terms as: keywords OR IPC codes OR 
CPC codes).3 The data are only limited to invention patents. Design 
patents and utility model patents are excluded due to their lower in­
ventive step, attributed to their legal characteristics (Huang, 2017). For 
each AI-related patent, we collected the application date to the CNIPA 
and the applicants’ information on their regions of affiliation. Then, we 
computed the sum of AI-related patents for each region i and year t by 
partly attributing patents that list applicants from different regions 
(fractional counts). Fig. 1 reports the regional average distribution 
between 2006 and 2021 of AI-related patents per population.
3.2.2. Independent variable
Our independent variable is regional specialization in ICT, proxied 
by Revealed Technological Advantage (RTA) measured for each 
provincial-level region. RTA is an index that measures the relative 
technological strength (or weakness) of the regions (Soete, 1987) and is 
a proxy of regional specialization in a technological field. We compute 
RTA by considering ICT-related patents since the knowledge base of 
such technology influences the emergence of AI and since AI represents 
developments of the ICT system (Igna and Venturini, 2023; Xiao and 
Boschma, 2022). ICTs themselves are often regarded as GPTs, and a 
substantial knowledge base in ICTs not only fosters high-tech innovation 
but also acts as a catalyst for regional innovation (Sergio et al., 2023). 
Hence, we collected ICT-related patents applied to CNIPA by using 
specific IPC codes following Inaba and Squicciarini (2017)4 and we 
retrieved the applicants’ information on their regions of affiliation, and 
we computed the sum of ICT-related patents for each region i and year t 
by partly attributing patents that list applicants from different regions 
(fractional counts). Then, we collected the total amount of patents 
applied to CNIPA in each region i at year t. RTA in ICT is computed as

steps. First, we instrument China’s provincial-level RTA in ICT using an 
analogous measure constructed for the federal states of the United States 
of America (US), based on USPTO patent data, as the US represents the 
country that sets the bar for the global advancements in ICT patents.

which are the 5 US states with the highest number of ICT applications

ICT due to idiosyncratic and unobserved Chinese regional characteris­

source of variation related to global technological advancement in ICT.

3.2.3. Control variables
Then, we introduce a set of control variables. As a proxy for regional 
knowledge, we focus on intramural high-tech R&D expenditures of 
Chinese-owned enterprises rescaled for GDP to control for the different 
economic sizes of each region. Since the early 2000s, high-tech com­
panies have been pivotal in China’s pursuit of becoming an innovation- 
driven nation (Chen et al., 2018). These companies have significantly 
contributed to the growth of emerging industries, particularly the 
new-generation IT sector, by actively engaging in activities such as 
building ICT infrastructure, advancing R&D commercialization and 
industrialization, and acquiring technology (Li et al., 2021).
We also measure the ratio between employees in high-tech industries 
and total employees of a region, as a proxy for the endowment of 
regional human capital, which fosters, adopts, and diffuses technolog­
ical change (Nelson and Phelps, 1966), but also it absorbs and imitates 
foreign advanced technologies (Verspagen, 1991). Xie et al. (2018) have 
found that there are positive relationships between the absorptive ca­
pacity of Chinese high-tech companies and their ability to innovate. 
They specifically underline how the dimensions of knowledge trans­
formation and knowledge exploitation play a pivotal role in cultivating 
core competencies. In line with Furman et al. (2002), we also introduce 
GDP per capita to assess the capability of a region to convert its internal 
knowledge stock into an accomplished regional of economic and tech­
nological development. Moreover, knowledge flows can arise from 
foreign direct investment (FDI) (Coe and Helpman, 1995), hence, we 
introduce the share of FDI per GDP. This is particularly relevant in the 
case of China, which has greatly benefited from international knowledge 
diffusion in building its initial technological capabilities (Fu and Hou, 
2021). An overview of the variables, the descriptive statistics, and the 
correlation matrix are provided in Table 1.

4. Results and discussion
In this section, we present our econometric results and assess the 
economic significance of the estimated effects of regional specialization 
in ICT and inter-regional knowledge spillovers on AI patenting 
performance.
4.1. Linear regression results
Table 3 displays the results of the linear panel model with fixed ef­
fects and the 2SLS. Column 1 shows the results of the regression without 
the interaction between RTA ICT and AI zones as a baseline model. The 
coefficient of RTA ICT is positive and statistically significant (β =
0.2964, p-value<0.05), which reveals that regional specialization in ICT 
is positively correlated with AI patenting performance. Column 2 reports 
the results of the regression shown in Eq. 1. We find that the coefficient 
of RTA ICT of regions with AI zones is positive and statistically signifi­
cant (β = 0.3302, p-value<0.01), while the coefficient of regions 
without AI zones is still positive, but not significant (β = 0.2059, p- 
value>0.1). Then, we performed a 2SLS model. In Column 3 we find a 
statistically significant correlation between IV and the independent 
variable (β = −0.1385, p-value<0.1; β =
0.4535, p-value<0.01). 
Finally, Column 4 reports the regression results of 2SLS. In the 2SLS 
model, RTA ICT is positive and statistically significant for regions with 
and without AI zones (β =0.6705, p-value<0.01, and β =0.4984, p- 
value<0.01, respectively). The magnitude of the coefficient is higher for 
regions with AI zones, thus confirming H1. Moreover, the difference in 
magnitude between the OLS and 2SLS estimates for both the RTA ICT ×
no AIzone and RTA ICT × AIzone interaction term suggests that the OLS 
estimates reported in Column 2 were downward biased due to potential 
endogeneity. Specifically, OLS may underestimate the effect due to 
measurement error in ICT specialization, omitted variables related to 
regional innovation capacity, or reverse causality, where regions with 
stronger AI activity also enhance their ICT capabilities over time. The 
2SLS reported in column 4 corrects for these biases, revealing a stronger 
positive effect of ICT-related specialization in both groups of regions 
with respect to OLS. This suggests that the role of ICT in AI-driven 
regional innovation is even more pronounced than initially estimated, 
reinforcing the importance of ICT capabilities in fostering AI develop­
ment, especially in AI zones.

Negative inter-regional spillovers may exacerbate the technology 
and innovation gap between regions in developing AI, which could also 
hamper AI patenting performance at the national level. While regional 
specialization can drive localized technological leadership, our findings 
suggest that it also intensifies market rivalry, resource concentration, 
and uneven knowledge diffusion, which in turn constrain breakthrough 
innovation at the national level (Montmartin et al., 2018). These nega­
tive spillovers could undermine the potential for inter-regional knowl­
edge recombination—a key mechanism in fostering the radical 
innovation necessary to advance along the technological frontier, 
particularly in AI, a GPT with widespread transformative potential 
(Cockburn et al., 2019). The fragmentation of AI capabilities across re­
gions may diminish opportunities for such recombination, slowing the 
pace at which China advances toward the technological frontier. While 
competition among AI-leading regions can fuel incremental progress, it 
may also undermine collaborative innovation dynamics that are crucial 
for major technological breakthroughs (Byun et al., 2021). The observed 
negative spillovers suggest that inter-regional AI competition is more 
likely to lead to duplication of efforts rather than productive 
knowledge-sharing, 
particularly 
if 
leading 
regions 
prioritize 
self-sufficiency over cooperation (Bloom et al., 2013). Specifically, these 
spillovers may take distinct forms. First, resource crowding may occur 
when regions compete for limited R&D funding and skilled talent, 
generating a dynamic in which one region’s gain comes at the expense of 
another’s innovation capacity (Montmartin et al., 2018; Wang et al., 
2022). Second, strategic isolation may arise when regions, in their 
pursuit of self-sufficiency, limit opportunities for cross-regional knowl­
edge recombination, hindering collaborative efforts that are vital for 
breakthrough innovations (Montmartin et al., 2018). Third, institutional 
lock-in may develop as regions become entrenched in their existing 
development paths due to historical specialization, making it difficult to 
diversify and adapt to emerging technologies. Such over-specialization 
creates self-reinforcing cycles in regional innovation systems, making 
it difficult for local actors to diversify or adopt new technologies 
(T¨odtling and Trippl, 2005). In the context of AI development, this 
lock-in may prevent even leading regions from transitioning toward 
more innovative, collaborative, or diversified technological trajectories 
(De Propris and Bailey, 2021). Yet, regional lock-in is not deterministic 
since proactive policies can foster regional renewal and counteract 
excessive specialization through entrepreneurial and institutional 
agency (Newey and Coenen, 2022). Corradini (2019) further empha­
sizes the role of local knowledge bases and institutional support as 
critical determinants for technological entry, suggesting that while 
specialization provides a foundation for new technology development, it 
can also reinforce disparities unless regions broaden their capability 
portfolios.
Such disparities are particularly concerning for a country like China, 
where the government’s independent innovation strategy emphasizes 
the need for technological self-sufficiency and national competitiveness 
(Chen et al., 2018). The focus on AI may require stronger attention to the

5. Conclusions, implications and limitations
In this study, we show the role of regional specialization in ICT and 
negative interregional knowledge spillovers in China’s AI patenting 
performance by using multiple and reliable sources to construct a 
comprehensive dataset of 30 Chinese provincial-level regions from 2006 
to 2021. Previous literature shows the importance of developing the 
right capabilities for successful specialization strategies in Industry 4.0 
(Labory and Bianchi, 2021), whether these technologies widen the gap 
between leaders and laggards (Corradini et al., 2021) or promote the 
creation of new islands of innovation (Capello and Lenzi, 2021). We 
contribute to this debate by exploring the role of regional specialization 
in ICT and positive selection in fostering AI patenting performance, 
thereby further reinforcing the competitive advantage of specialized 
regions and widening the technological gap between regions that excel 
and those that lag behind. We also provide evidence of negative 
inter-regional spatial spillovers within Chinese regional AI patenting 
performance, contributing to the literature on inter-regional spillovers 
and their negative effects on innovation (Byun et al., 2021; Drivas et al., 
2021). Then, we contribute to the literature by analyzing China’s 
regional dimensions, which have been overlooked by regional analysis 
on AI (Capello and Lenzi, 2021; De Propis and Bellandi, 2021; Xiao and 
Boschma, 2022), despite its growing prominence in the AI domain and 
being a developing country.
Our findings have significant implications for policy-making. By 
limiting the diffusion of knowledge across regions, China risks not only 
exacerbating internal regional inequalities but also slowing down its 
progress toward global technological leadership. The strategy aims to 
mitigate these risks through targeted investments in regional innovation 
ecosystems, promoting intra-national collaborations to ensure that AI- 
driven growth is inclusive and maximizes national patenting and tech­
nological achievements. Aligning inter-regional innovation dynamics 
with indigenous innovation goals can enhance the country’s capacity to 
leverage AI’s GPT characteristics, secure long-term competitiveness, and 
maintain an upward trajectory within the global technological 
landscape.
To address these disparities, promoting inter-regional cooperation is 
imperative to strengthen the innovation outcomes of local contexts 
(Rong et al., 2021), and customized innovation policies are essential in 
achieving this alignment with the unique capabilities and requirements 
of each region (Brown, 2021). However, policymakers often choose to 
invest in the fittest region. While prioritizing the fittest may enhance 
regional competitiveness, it contributes to global and regional in­
equalities. The fittest regions, with technological advancements and 
flexibility, thrive, leaving the others behind. Given these dynamics, 
policymakers must consider a balanced regional development strategy 
that goes beyond focusing exclusively on leading AI zones. While in­
vestment in leading regions can drive AI breakthroughs, neglecting the 
lagging regions exacerbates the negative spillover effects we observed. 
We recommend policies that strengthen the absorptive capacities of 
lagging regions through targeted investments in education, R&D infra­
structure, and institutional reforms. Drawing on the work of He et al. 
(2019) and Zhang and Rodríguez-Pose (2024), enhancing the institu­
tional proximity of lagging regions to leading AI zones will enable them 
to absorb spillover benefits rather than suffer from market rivalry and 
resource crowding-out effects.
Our findings suggest that merely establishing AI development zones 
without fostering cross-regional collaboration may lead to inefficient 
resource allocation and redundancies. The evidence of negative spill­
overs implies that neighboring regions need targeted support to engage 
collaboratively and productively. Without intervention, market rivalry 
and resource concentration may further exacerbate regional disparities, 
limiting knowledge diffusion and reducing opportunities for break­
through innovation (Montmartin et al., 2018; Bloom et al., 2013; Byun

Regional Collaborative Hubs, modeled after successful innovation eco­
systems that link leading AI regions with adjacent lagging ones.
A key lesson from European innovation policy strategy is that 
competitive schemes often favor leading regions, whereas collaborative 
frameworks can reduce regional innovation gaps (Archibugi et al., 
2022). Similarly, AI hubs should not merely intensify competition but 
actively promote cooperative strategies, such as cross-regional joint 
ventures, inter-regional funding schemes, and coordinated AI research 
clusters (De Propris and Bellandi, 2021). However, for these hubs to be 
effective, regions must possess a certain level of absorptive capacity, 
including technological capabilities, institutional quality, and human 
capital—factors critical to ensuring meaningful participation in inno­
vation networks (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Filippetti and Zinilli, 
2023).Aligning AI hubs with local strengths and complementary capa­
bilities will help prevent the formation of exclusive innovation clubs, 
where only top-tier regions benefit while others remain excluded 
(Filippetti and Zinilli, 2023). Instead, structured collaboration mecha­
nisms—such as shared R&D infrastructure, data-sharing platforms, and 
targeted talent mobility programs—can ensure that AI knowledge 
spillovers contribute to balanced regional development rather than 
reinforcing existing inequalities (Balland and Boschma, 2021; Capello 
and Lenzi, 2021). By embedding these principles into China’s AI strat­
egy, AI Regional Collaborative Hubs could serve as a model for pro­
moting both technological leadership and regional cohesion, mitigating 
the risks of excessive competition while maximizing the potential of AI 
as a transformative general-purpose technology.
To summarize, while our study sheds light on the critical role of 
regional specialization and the negative effects of inter-regional spill­
overs in China’s AI development, it also underscores the need for 
comprehensive policy interventions to mitigate regional disparities. To 
foster a more balanced and inclusive AI innovation development, poli­
cymakers must prioritize strengthening absorptive capacities in lagging 
regions, promoting regional collaboration, and adopting a stronger co­
ordinated national strategy. This approach will help reduce the negative 
externalities of competition and ensure that all regions contribute to and 
benefit from China’s AI advancement.
To conclude, we acknowledge that our study has certain limitations 
that should be addressed by future research. First, future work should 
analyze different AI innovation indicators, such as scientific publica­
tions, startup activity, and productivity related to AI. While patenting 
activity is generally a good proxy to measure innovation, it does not 
always capture everything. AI, in particular, is driven by data accumu­
lation, algorithmic improvements, and large language models – many of 
which are not always patentable or are protected through trade secrets 
and copyrights. Second, while we employ fixed effects and an IV strategy 
to mitigate potential biases, we recognize that certain time-varying 
contextual factors, such as digital infrastructure quality and local pol­
icy interventions, could still play a role in shaping AI development. 
Future research could explore these dimensions further by incorporating 
high-frequency data on infrastructure investments, policy changes, and 
institutional frameworks to better disentangle their effects. Third, 
further investigation into the time-varying behavior of the effect of ICT 
specialization on AI performance is warranted. We encourage future 
research with more granular datasets (i.e., prefectural- or city-level 
data) to explore these variations in greater depth. Finally, our paper 
focuses exclusively on geographic proximity as a proxy for competition 
and institutional differences in influencing knowledge spillovers, so 
future research should broaden the scope by analyzing various channels 
that could facilitate or hinder knowledge diffusion, such as labor 
mobility, co-patenting, research and academic collaborations, and trade 
patterns. Finally, another strand of future research should also take a 
broader perspective and examine the contributions at a more granular 
level, such as prefecture and firm levels. However, these tasks are 
beyond the design and purpose of the present paper.
