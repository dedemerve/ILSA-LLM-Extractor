ABSTRACT
Examining cognitive dynamics in the creative process is crucial for advancing creative theories and creativity education but has 
been historically understudied due to the technical challenges in collecting fine-­grained data underlying cognitive processes. 
The PISA 2022 Creative Thinking assessment has the potential to address this limitation by offering rich, technology-­based 
process data that capture cognitive and affective dynamics. To explore this potential, we outlined key topics and theoretical 
foundations in creativity research related to process data, followed by an illustrative example demonstrating how process data 
can illuminate creative thinking processes simply using descriptive statistics of released items. Furthermore, we delineated 
model-­based approaches, including data mining techniques and extended item response theory models, to uncover various be-
havioral patterns in creative thinking through integrating process data and item responses together. While PISA 2022 process 
data provide valuable insights into creative thinking, strong theoretical support is recommended to compute new variables from 
original process data metrics and choose appropriate statistical methodologies for nuanced and valid interpretations. We further 
discussed challenges and recommendations for utilizing the PISA 2022 CT process data.
Cognitive dynamics—the moment-­to-­moment mental processes 
and interactions occurring during creative thinking—under-
lying the creative process is a topic of significant attention be-
cause it refines and provides insights into creative theories while 
also informing creative education (Barbot  2018). Research on 
the cognitive dynamics of the creative process has been doc-
umented, including the interplay between ideational fluency 
and novelty, as well as idiosyncratic profiles in creative think-
ing (e.g., Beaty and Silvia 2012; Paek and Runco 2018). Looking 
into how students behave and operate mental processes unveils 
unique insights about creative thinking beyond general com-
petency scores. For instance, creative thinking is an effortful 
process as illustrated in a serial order effect in which original re-
sponses take more time to arise than mundane responses during 
divergent thinking, one of the key elements of creative thinking 
(Beaty and Silvia 2012). The effortful process can be measured 
by response time, the number of actions, and the frequency of 
visits, which can then be used to examine the dynamics of cre-
ative thinking (Barbot 2018).
While deeper exploration of the creative process is needed, 
most previous studies in creativity research have primarily fo-
cused on simple divergent thinking tasks (e.g., Alternate Uses 
Tasks [AUTs] using mundane items; Runco and Acar 2012), 
small adult populations (Beaty and Silvia 2012), and limited 
types of data capturing the process (e.g., mainly time on task; 
Paek et al. 2021). Although these studies contribute to under-
standing cognitive dynamics underlying the creative process,

Process data refers to students' digital traces of performance, 
encompassing multiple types of data collected through assess-
ment technologies (OECD 2023a, 6). For example, eye-­tracking 
data can be gathered through video recordings, action logs (e.g., 
mouse clicks, mouse tracking, keystrokes), and timing data 
(e.g., time on task, time to first action) can be recorded through 
technology-­based assessments. Advances in technology-­based 
technologies have transformed educational assessments, mov-
ing from traditional paper-­based formats to technology-­based 
assessment (OECD  2023a). Building on this shift, the PISA 
2022 Creative Thinking (CT) assessment provides various pro-
cess data of the creative process—extracted from log files that 
capture students' interactions with items—which offer valu-
able insights for studying the dynamics of creative thinking 
(OECD 2024b).
Process data can inform which elements are predictive of CT 
scores in addition to discovering patterns in the cognitive (e.g., 
problem-­solving strategies) and affective (e.g., engagement) as-
pects of creative thinking (OECD  2023a). Process data alone 
does not constitute evidence; rather, it requires interpretation 
grounded in theory and supported by empirical validation to un-
cover meaningful patterns in creative thinking. Furthermore, 
researchers should consider sophisticated analyses that enable 
them to handle process data appropriately, accounting for the 
complex nature of the data.
Therefore, this review aimed to (1) depict the process data avail-
able in the PISA 2022 CT assessment, (2) delineate the topics 
and theoretical foundations of creativity research relevant to 
process data, (3) outline model-­based approaches for analyzing 
process data to address the creativity research, and (4) provide 
challenges and recommendations for utilizing the PISA 2022 CT 
process data.
1   |   PISA Creative Thinking Assessment and 
Process Data
In the PISA 2022 CT assessment, three key facets of creative 
ideation and four domain contexts were evaluated (See the de-
tails in Appendix A, Table A1). The assessment focused on three 
distinct ideation processes: generating diverse ideas, generating 
creative ideas, and evaluating and improving ideas. The four 
domain contexts—written expression, visual expression, social 
problem solving, and scientific problem solving—reflect differ-
ent types of knowledge, skills, and modalities involved in cre-
ative ideation across various real-­world settings.
PISA 2022 CT assessment provides five types of process data: 
total time on item, time to first action, number of actions, num-
ber of visits, and number of short visits. First, total time on task 
records the total time a student spends interacting with an item, 
excluding overhead such as loading or introductory screens. If 
a student revisits the same item multiple times, the total time, 
measured in milliseconds, is calculated by summing the time 
spent on the item across all visits. Second, time to first action 
refers to the time elapsed from when the item becomes avail-
able to the student's first recorded action. Third, the number of 
actions represents the total count of meaningful actions (e.g., 
clicks, double-­clicks, key presses, and drag/drop events) per-
formed on an item across all item visits. Fourth, the number of 
visits indicates the total frequency with which a student visited/
revisited an item during a session. Lastly, the number of short 
visits captures the count of visits where the interaction time was 
less than 0.5 s, suggesting minimal engagement. Among these, 
only response time per item is used in official PISA analyses 
to derive engagement indicators, while other metrics—such as 
time to first action, number of actions, and visits—are reserved 
for exploratory research. Process data (including timestamps) in 
PISA 2022 were captured locally in the offline Student Delivery 
System and exported via dedicated tools. The data management 
contractor implemented stringent quality-­control procedures 
to ensure integrity (see Chapter 12 in the PISA 2022 Technical 
Report).
Process data metrics support valid, fair assessments and re-
veal behavioral patterns often overlooked in traditional testing. 
Process data is used to refine test construction by identifying 
issues in item design and interface, and to ensure fairness by 
detecting construct-­irrelevant factors differentially affecting 
groups (Ercikan et  al.  2023). More importantly, process data 
offers additional diagnostic insights into how students ap-
proach tasks, engage with them, and solve problems (Ercikan 
et al. 2023; OECD 2023a). Advanced techniques, such as data 
mining or mixture modeling, enable researchers to uncover 
how student groups approach problem-­solving tasks differently 
(Han et al. 2023; Park et al. 2023; Teig 2024). For instance, Han 
et al. (2023) applied latent profile analysis to response time (i.e., 
time spent on the last visit to an item) and the number of actions 
in PISA 2015 collaborative problem-­solving tasks. They identi-
fied two groups with two different profiles of problem-­solving 
behaviors: students in one group used efficient strategies and 
maintained shared understanding, while students in the other 
group struggled with task adaptation and showed impulsive be-
haviors. These findings underscore the unique utility of process 
data in providing nuanced insights into how students tackle 
complex tasks. In the following section, we delineated key top-
ics and theoretical foundations in creativity research that may 
benefit from the use of process data, illustrated with selected 
examples.
2   |   Process Data and Creativity Research
2.1   |   Cognitive Dynamics
PISA CT process data can contribute to creativity research in 
two key areas: uncovering behavioral patterns that explain (1) 
cognitive and (2) affective dynamics underlying the creative pro-
cess. First, cognitive dynamics underlying the creative process 
gain attention because they refine creative theories and inform 
creative education (Barbot 2018). For instance, the serial order 
effect is a robust finding in creativity research suggesting that 
later responses in a divergent thinking (DT) task tend to be 
more original than earlier ones (Christensen et al. 1957). Beaty 
and Silvia  (2012) confirmed this principle by time-­stamping

simply compared divergent thinking performance across timed 
vs. untimed test conditions (e.g., Antes and Mumford 2009) or 
fixed amounts of time (e.g., Johns et al. 2000). To our knowl-

PISA CT process data may offer opportunities to reexamine es-
tablished principles of the creative process, such as the serial 
order effect, and uncover understudied patterns. Analyzing 
scored responses (i.e., creativity of generated responses) from 
earlier and later items in the cluster in PISA CT, along with total 
timing or time to first action, can provide indirect evidence of 
the serial order effect. For example, if responses rated as more 
creative systematically appear in later-­positioned items across 
units within the cluster or if total time spent on an item is posi-
tively associated with the creativity of responses, this may sug-
gest that generating original ideas requires greater cognitive 
effort—aligning with the serial order effect. To further examine 
this, one could test whether the relationship between time on 
task and creativity is moderated by item position, investigating 
whether this effect progresses linearly or non-­linearly over time 
(e.g., U-­ or J-­shaped patterns), thereby offering a more nuanced 
understanding of the serial order effect.
However, facets and domain contexts should be considered 
when comparing the creativity of responses across items assess-
ing different tasks and domains, since task structure and do-
mains strongly influence outcomes. Creativity is shaped by task 
type, such as those eliciting convergent or divergent thinking 
(Acar and Shen 2025), and by stimuli, whether verbal or graphi-
cal (Barbot et al. 2016). The domain-­specific nature of creativity 
is further evidenced by groups with domain-­based achievements 
(Silvia et al. 2009) and theoretically supported by the domain 
learning model of creativity development (Dumas et al. 2024).
Beaty and Silvia (2012) further challenged the serial order effect 
by proposing that creative thoughts are driven by executive and 
strategic processes rather than merely by spreading activation, 
a classical explanation of the effect. This highlights the impor-
tance of strategic approaches in creative thinking over linear or 
spontaneous approaches. When combined with the creativity of 
responses, PISA CT process data, such as response times, the 
number of actions, and visits, can help identify different strat-
egies in generating creative ideas or creative problem-­solving 
employed by highly creative performers and compare them to 
those used by less creative performers. For instance, PISA CT 
process data can explore idiosyncratic characteristics in cre-
ative thinking by differentiating creators and refiners: creators 
focus on ideation and bringing new concepts to life, while refin-
ers are skilled at improving or optimizing ideas incrementally. 
Refiners may revisit the same items or units more frequently 
than creators, despite producing equally creative responses. 
This exploration of visit patterns adds contemporary insights 
to the traditional confirmatory factorial evidence (e.g., Kim 
et al. 2006; Krumm et al. 2014) supporting Kirton's Adaption-­
Innovation Theory. Such comparisons could provide deeper 
insights into the executive and strategic processes underlying 
creative problem-­solving.
Another robust finding in creativity research is the diversity 
in cognitive styles when processing and expressing creativity. 
Previous research has identified distinct DT profiles predic-
tive of different creative outcomes using static data (Paek and 
Runco 2018). For example, one group with moderate fluency and 
low originality excelled in creative achievement, while another 
with moderate fluency and high originality excelled in creative 
ideation. These findings highlight the unique strengths of dif-
ferent DT profiles. PISA CT process data offers opportunities for 
exploring various profiles in the creative process, contributing 
to and expanding the existing body of knowledge on the diver-
sity of creative processes. For example, response time can help 
identify rapid thinkers who respond quickly to the first valid 
action (scored as 1 or higher in coded responses) regardless of 
the facet of the item, and incubators who take longer to produce 
their first valid response.
2.2   |   Affective Dynamics
Affective aspects of the creative process have been understud-
ied compared to the extensive research on cognitive aspects, 
though this does not imply they are less important. While intrin-
sic motivation is a critical element of creativity (Amabile 1983), 
authentic measures of intrinsic motivation are challenging 
to be operationally define. The roles of motivation in the cre-
ative process and performance have been indirectly examined 
in contexts where competition or evaluation were manipulated 
(e.g., Amabile 1982; Cheek and Stahl 1986; Conti et al. 2001). 
However, direct measures of intrinsic motivation, such as en-
gagement and persistence, remain understudied due to a lack of 
authentic assessment contexts capable of quantifying intrinsic 
motivation.
While authentic measures of intrinsic motivation are limited, 
engagement and disengagement behaviors provide observable 
indicators that can be captured through process data. These in-
dicators help operationalize motivation more directly in creativ-
ity assessments. Disengagement is often inferred from response 
patterns in academic assessments, such as responses with ex-
tremely short times, flatlining responses, skipping, and drop-
ping out, which can be adapted for use in creativity assessments. 
PISA 2022 uses both total timing and missing values to identify 
disengagement with CT items (OECD 2024a). Responses sub-
mitted in under 15 s were treated as missing and considered in-
dicative of careless responding, suggesting a possible indicator of 
disengagement because no deliberate creative process occurred 
(OECD 2024b). Additionally, PISA defines rapid responding as 
submitted responses submitted in under 30 s. Additionally, per-
sistence indicators—such as irregular item visits or action pat-
terns across units—can shed light on student motivation during 
the creative process. They also enable analysis of how engage-
ment or disengagement relates to creative performance and how 
these relationships vary by demographics such as age, gender, 
and country.

3   |   Model-­Based Approaches for Process Data in 
Creativity Research
This section summarizes model-­based approaches for utiliz-
ing process data to address the research questions, focusing on 
data-­driven methods to uncover behavioral patterns in creative 
thinking and integrating process data into measurement mod-
els. Most of these approaches aim to group individuals based on 
their response and/or process data and to identify distinct pat-
terns in test-­taking behavior.
3.1   |   Data Mining
Data mining uses data-­driven approaches to uncover unknown 
patterns, complementing theory-­based methods focused on pre-
determined hypotheses (Ercikan et al. 2023). It is broadly di-
vided into unsupervised and supervised methods. Unsupervised 
methods group individuals by shared characteristics when 
group membership is unknown, while supervised methods train 
classifiers to assign subjects to predefined categories or predict 
outcomes (Qiao and Jiao 2018).
Clustering, one of the most common unsupervised methods, 
groups similar data points into clusters based on shared char-
acteristics, revealing hidden patterns in data and providing 
valuable insights. Clustering methods include K-­means, which 
minimizes variance within clusters, and hierarchical clustering, 
which organizes data into a tree-­like structure based on similar-
ity (Xu and Tian 2015).
Flexible modeling is a strength of data mining, enabling the 
exploration of interesting patterns in students' cognitive or 
behavioral processes during testing, which have been previ-
ously understudied. (Ercikan et al. 2023; He et al. 2019; Park 
et  al.  2023). For example, He et  al.  (2019) clustered students 
based on process data from one item including 34 action types, 
response time, and total actions in the Programme for the 
International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) 2012 
and found three patterns in problem-­solving: minimal action 
takers (low engagement), efficient solvers (strategic and accu-
rate), and exploratory strategists (highly active and exploratory).
Supervised methods, like Random Forest, are used for classifi-
cation and regression tasks (Qiao and Jiao 2018). These methods 
have been applied in educational research to identify features 
for predicting outcomes (Han et al. 2019) and to explore student 
ability and test-­taking effort using response time (Yildirim-­
Erbasli and Gorgun 2024). For instance, Yildirim-­Erbasli and 
Gorgun  (2024) analyzed 39 items from 14,680 PISA 2018 fi-
nancial literacy participants to study the relationship between 
response time and ability levels. They utilized decision tree, ran-
dom forest, logistic regression, and naïve Bayes, using response 
time (measured in milliseconds per item) as explanatory vari-
ables and a binary outcome for low vs. high ability and showed 
that higher test-­taking effort was correlated with better ability 
scores. These supervised methods reflect a variable-­centered 
approach, aiming to identify the most informative predictors of 
outcomes rather than to group individuals.
Unsupervised and supervised methods can be combined de-
pending on purpose. Park et al. (2023) applied both to analyze 
data from 30,453 PISA 2012 participants using elastic net regres-
sion and identified key process data variables related to time in 
explaining the outcomes (i.e., plausible values) in the Creative 
Problem Solving (CPS) domain. Clustering analysis then 
grouped students based on behavior patterns using the selected 
variables. Among 15 time-­related variables, elastic net regres-
sion repeatedly selected five (e.g., total timing) over 100 runs. 
These variables were used to classify students into four groups: 
curious, speedy, repetitive, and strategic problem-­solvers. These 
four groups can be briefly described as follows: Curious students 
explored a wide range of paths, including irrelevant ones; Speedy 
students made quick decisions with minimal exploration; 
Repetitive students repeatedly checked answer-­related paths re-
peatedly to ensure correctness; and Strategic students took their 
time to make careful decisions with fewer but more deliberate 
actions.
3.2   |   Item Response Theory
IRT is a widely used measurement model for estimating indi-
viduals' latent traits (e.g., academic achievement, creativity, 
and proficiency), particularly in large-­scale assessments such 
as PISA. IRT models the relationship between individuals' re-
sponses to items, their latent trait levels, and item characteristics, 
such as difficulty. The modeling process data in IRT enables the 
identification of heterogeneous response patterns and improves 
the accuracy of measurement estimation. Whereas data mining 
approaches often rely primarily on process data to detect behav-
ioral patterns, IRT-­based models also incorporate item response 
patterns to capture underlying ability and response behaviors.
First, mixture IRT, which combines latent class analysis with 
IRT, facilitates exploring heterogeneous response patterns. 
Real-­world populations often exhibit variability in cognitive 
strategies, engagement, or other unobservable characteristics, 
highlighting the need for analyses that appropriately account 
for heterogeneous populations. Wang and Xu  (2015) identify 
solution behavior and rapid guessing behavior from a high-­
stakes large-­scale assessment by jointly modeling response ac-
curacy and response time within a mixture IRT framework. 
Furthermore, their model captures heterogeneous test-­taking 
behaviors, distinguishing between rapid guessing caused by low 
levels of engagement and that driven by time pressure despite 
engagement. Recently, Welling et al. (2024) utilized the number 
of answer changes and test rereads as additional predictors to 
identify disengaged responding in a reading comprehension test 
for the German NEPS (National Educational Panel Study). They 
demonstrated that these additional predictors improved the ac-
curacy of engagement classification. Additional process data, 
such as the number of item visits, the time to the first action, 
and the total number of actions, can be helpful in scrutinizing 
heterogeneous response patterns.
Second, disengagement during assessments threatens valid-
ity, as such responses fail to reflect true abilities. Removing

Mixture IRT can distinguish disengaged from engaged respond-
ers by modeling two latent classes—disengaged and engaged—
assuming item responses and response times are influenced by 
the latent class (e.g., Lu et al. 2020; Ulitzsch et al. 2020). Observed 
response times are modeled as a weighted combination of these 
two latent classes, with their distribution influenced by latent 
traits like speed and proficiency. Integrating response time and 
response models allows for the estimation of both proficiency 
and engagement as person parameters. Another approach uses 
item-­specific response times to predict class membership prob-
abilities (e.g., Nagy and Ulitzsch 2021; Pokropek 2016; Welling 
et  al.  2024), identifying disengagement at the item or person 
level for a nuanced view of disengaged behavior.
In both approaches, the probability of correct responses for the 
disengaged class is fixed at a certain value, such as a probability 
of random guessing (e.g., 0.25 for a multiple-­choice item with 
four options). In contrast, the probability of correct responses 
for the engaged class is defined by IRT. This means that the item 
parameters are estimated exclusively from engaged responses, 
resulting in improved measurement accuracy and more reliable 
latent trait estimates.
4   |   Conclusion
PISA 2022 CT process data provide unique strengths for study-
ing cognitive processes in creative problem-­solving. It captures 
granular metrics, such as response times and actions, enabling 
researchers to uncover behavioral patterns and strategies 
through descriptive statistics and model-­based approaches, giv-
ing shed light on nuanced analysis of creative thinking, includ-
ing effortful processes like the serial order effect, and facilitates 
comparisons between diverse cognitive styles.
Despite its potential, PISA 2022 CT process data poses chal-
lenges that researchers must consider for accurate and nuanced 
analysis. For example, while PISA provides process data metrics 
and rubric-­based creativity scores, these may not fully align with 
individual research goals or established definitions in creativity 
research. PISA defines rapid responding as disengagement or 
effortless behaviors when quick responses result in non-­credit 
answers. This definition aligns with academic assessments but 
may differ in creative contexts, where rapid responding could in-
dicate quick initiators rather than disengaged behavior. If quick 
responses were recorded as credited responses, they could be 
compared to incubators (Wallas 1926). Researchers must care-
fully examine PISA's constructs and definitions to ensure align-
ment between their analyses and creativity theories.
Second, PISA 2022 CT process data includes raw metrics that 
allow researchers to compute novel constructs tailored to their 
studies. For instance, the number of actions across tasks can 
measure persistence, an indicator of intrinsic motivation in cre-
ativity (Amabile 1983). Similarly, visit frequency can reflect a 
willingness to refine ideas, often linked to elaboration or adap-
tation in creative thinking (Alabbasi et al. 2022; Kim 2006). We 
provided more examples in Table A3 in the Appendix C illus-
trating how to map original process data metrics with indicators 
reflecting the creative process, although no theoretical ground 
is elaborated. However, such computations must be grounded in 
strong theoretical frameworks and prior research to permit valid 
inferences about underlying cognitive processes when working 
with process data.
Lastly, researchers should thoroughly understand how PISA 
2022 CT data was scored and structured to conduct nuanced 
and rigorous analyses. This is crucial, as PISA CT provides 
only partial data and limited detail on item and unit struc-
ture. For instance, not all units assess all three creativity fac-
ets, and some items are undocumented. Additionally, process 
data are nested within units, introducing potential depen-
dencies (e.g., testlet effects) despite efforts to minimize them 
(OECD 2024b).
Although there are limitations, the PISA 2022 CT assessment 
still allows researchers to examine how students engage in cre-
ative thinking in various countries and educational settings. By 
aligning process data with theoretical models and employing 
appropriate analytical strategies, researchers can contribute to 
a more precise understanding of how students engage with and 
develop creative ideas—ultimately informing both theory and 
educational practice.

Appendix A
Overview of Assessment Dimensions in the PISA 2022 Creative 
Thinking Test
Table A1 outlines the key dimensions and assessment components of 
the PISA 2022 Creative Thinking Assessment, reflecting the frame-
work established by OECD (2023b). It distinguishes between two over-
arching assessment dimensions: Facets of creative ideation and domain 
contexts.
Facets of creative ideation cover three critical cognitive processes: gen-
erating diverse ideas, generating creative ideas, and evaluating and 
improving ideas. Each facet emphasizes different aspects of creative 
thought. Generating diverse ideas emphasizes flexibility and variety

Domain contexts include four practical areas where students apply cre-

solving, and scientific problem solving. Written and visual expression

contrast, social and scientific problem-­solving contexts engage students 
in generating innovative, practical solutions for interpersonal or scien-
tific challenges, emphasizing functional creativity, problem formula-
tion, and effective outcomes.

Appendix C
Mapping PISA Creative Thinking Process Data With Indicators 
of the Creative Process
Table A3 illustrates how the five original PISA 2022 Creative Thinking 
(CT) process data metrics can be used to compute variables that serve 
as potential behavioral indicators of various aspects of the creative pro-
cess. However, this mapping is intended only as a conceptual illustra-
tion and does not rely on theoretical grounding.
Operational definitions derived from original or computed process met-
rics are not valid unless supported by strong theoretical and empirical 
foundations. Due to space constraints and the broader purpose of this 
study, we did not provide detailed theoretical rationales or empirical val-
idations for each example. Our goal is to offer a framework that guides 
researchers in exploring the cognitive dynamics of creative thinking, 
rather than to prescribe specific operational definitions.
Table  A3 includes a few illustrative examples—without theoretical 
backing—as starting points for researchers to develop and validate 
their own indicators. Since computing meaningful variables from raw 
process data is essential for future research, we leave this task to future 
research efforts.
In Table A3, we provide only one example computation for each original 
metric, although many others are possible. Variables can be computed 
at different levels (e.g., per item, unit, facet, or domain). Additionally, 
process data metrics can be aggregated across units, potentially en-
abling generalization across creative process facets or domains, particu-
larly when units span multiple facets or domains.
Finally, we note that a single computed variable may be conceptual-
ized as multiple behavioral indicators, depending on how it is derived 
and interpreted. Subtle differences in computation and context—when 
theoretically justified—can yield different insights. Therefore, careful 
computation and nuanced interpretation are crucial.
