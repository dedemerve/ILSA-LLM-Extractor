ABSTRACT

Globally, education holds significant value, placing substantial expectations
on its key players – teachers. However, the effectiveness of teachers depends
largely on their job satisfaction, a factor intricately linked to commitment and
the decision to persist in the teaching profession. This study, utilizing selfdetermination theory, explores the predictive role of teacher autonomy,
disciplinary climate, teacher-student relations, participation among stakeholders, and “perceptions of value & policy influence” (independent variables) in relation to job satisfaction (dependent variable) among lower
secondary school teachers in South Africa. Data from the OECD 2018
Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) were analyzed using
Pearson’s correlation and hierarchical multiple regression. Except for disciplinary climate, the results revealed significant positive associations between
the independent variables and job satisfaction. Notably, teacher autonomy
predicted variations in job satisfaction ranging from 3% to 5%, whereas the
combined school climate factors predicted variations between 5% and 28%.
Teachers’ perceptions of value and policy influence also predicted variations,
ranging from 0.4% to 3.0%. These findings underscore the importance of
fostering teacher autonomy, cultivating a positive school climate, and
actively involving teachers in policy discussions to enhance job satisfaction.
These insights provide valuable guidance for principals, policymakers, and
stakeholders in creating supportive environments for educators.

Introduction
Globally, education is regarded as a cornerstone of societal progress, with teachers playing a pivotal
role in achieving lofty objectives, such as eradicating poverty through Sustainable Development Goals
(SDG) – goal 4 specifically focusing on education (UNESCO, 2017). Trained and dedicated teachers
are essential to quality education and form the bedrock of the educational arch (Mmako & Schultz,
2016). However, teachers’ effectiveness depends significantly on their job satisfaction, which is
a crucial predictor of commitment and retention (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2010, 2017). Unsatisfied
teachers exhibit various negative behaviors, including frequent absenteeism, aggression toward colleagues and students, psychological withdrawal, burnout, and a desire to exit the teaching profession
(Mmako & Schultz, 2016). Recent studies, exemplified by Edinger and Edinger (2018), highlight
a global trend of teacher dissatisfaction resulting in higher turnover rates compared to other professions. This trend poses a substantial challenge for school management in retaining teachers, with
potential repercussions on educational quality (Mmako & Schultz, 2016).

The causes of teacher job dissatisfaction are diverse and include factors such as stress, school
disciplinary climate, teacher autonomy, and participation in decision-making (Dicke et al., 2018;
Dupriez et al., 2016). Glazer’s (2018) findings underscore the critical role of teacher autonomy, school
climate, and teacher involvement in educational policy decision-making in shaping job satisfaction.
The study revealed that teachers who experienced intrusion or interference in the classroom expressed
dissatisfaction and were motivated to leave their teaching profession. Moreover, research indicates
a positive and significant relationship between teachers’ job autonomy and career commitment (Hu,
2023; Mulyana & Izzati, 2022). Addressing teachers’ job satisfaction is imperative because of the
financial costs and disruptions associated with high teacher attrition rates (Ronfeldt et al., 2013). In
response, recent studies have shifted focus toward maximizing teachers’ job satisfaction to mitigate
high attrition rates, aligning with the paradigm shift in occupational health psychology toward
building flourishing communities (van der Klink, 2019; van der Klink et al., 2016).
Teacher attrition poses a significant challenge in South Africa. A nationwide study conducted by
the Human Sciences Research Council (HSRC) revealed that numerous South African teachers
resigned from the teaching profession before reaching retirement age (Hall et al., 2005). Although
the average annual number of resignations is relatively low, a substantial number of teachers choose to
permanently exit the teaching profession when the government provides options for voluntary
packages (Louise & Jan, 2011; Mpundu et al., 2023). Teachers’ decisions to quit their jobs are
influenced by various factors, including workplace relationships, as indicated by a study on South
African teachers’ intentions to leave their positions (Usadolo et al., 2020). Furthermore, the capability
sets of teachers, encompassing “using knowledge and skills,” “building and maintaining meaningful
relationships at work,” and “contributing to something valuable,” serve as significant predictors of
South African teachers’ intention to leave the profession (de Wet & Rothmann, 2022). Additional
factors contributing to teacher dissatisfaction and attrition in South Africa include a lack of equal
opportunities for teachers, inadequate discipline among students, escalating violence against teachers,
the perceived erosion of teachers’ authority (Baffour & Achemfuor, 2013), and issues related to poor
management (Louise & Jan, 2011).
Despite South African teachers expressing exceptional eagerness to be involved in decision-making
and taking responsibility for school changes, even in areas traditionally considered the principal’s
domain (Swanepoel, 2009), initiatives addressing teacher attrition have disproportionately focused on
external motivators such as improving working conditions (Mpundu et al., 2023). This emphasis on
external factors comes at the expense of intrinsic motivators such as teacher autonomy, discipline
among students, and teachers’ participation in educational policies. This situation warrants a study on
how job satisfaction among South African teachers can be increased through intrinsic sources.
Existing studies on teacher job satisfaction and commitment to the teaching profession have often
concentrated on specific factors, including school factors (e.g., Al-Mahdy & Alazmi, 2023; Luo et al.,
2022), teacher factors (e.g., Worth & Van den Brande, 2020), and student factors, either individually or
in combination (e.g., Baroudi et al., 2022). Most of these studies, (e.g., McConnell, 2017), have been
conducted in developed countries. Only a limited number of studies, (e.g., Poloni et al., 2022;
Zholchieva et al., 2023), have explored teacher job satisfaction in developing countries, with even
fewer studies, (e.g., de Wet & Rothmann, 2022; Usadolo et al., 2020), delving into the topic in the SubSaharan region. Furthermore, there is a scarcity of studies examining societal factors, such as teachers’
perceptions of value and policy influence, in relation to their job satisfaction and commitment to the
teaching profession.
This study aimed to investigate the predictive role of “perceptions of value and policy influence,”
teacher autonomy, and school climate in relation to job satisfaction among lower secondary school
teachers in South Africa, utilizing data from the 2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey
(TALIS). To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has examined the combined influences of
these factors on teachers’ job satisfaction. Consequently, this study contributes to research on teacher
job satisfaction by delving into societal factors alongside school, teacher, and student factors. The lens
of self-determination theory, emphasizing the three psychological human needs crucial for well-being

The Educational Context in South Africa
Situated at the southernmost point of the African continent, South Africa is renowned for its cultural
diversity, including various ethnicities such as Zulu, Xhosa, and Afrikaner (Morris & Smart, 2018).
This country has a rich historical narrative marked by the era of apartheid, a system characterized by
racial segregation and discrimination. The transition to democracy in 1994, with Nelson Mandela
assuming the presidency, symbolized the end of apartheid (Msimang, 2018). Today, South Africa is
globally recognized for its vibrant cultural tapestry, captivating geographical features, and population
reflective of the nation’s diverse heritage (Nixon, 2022). South Africa’s education system follows a 13year cycle, including eight years of elementary education and five years of secondary education
(Sibanda, 2018). Despite this structural framework, the country faces challenges, from concerns
about the quality of education to issues of accessibility and urban-rural disparities (Ngobeni et al.,
2023; Zulu et al., 2022). Ongoing efforts aim to address these challenges through educational reforms
that target infrastructure improvements, teacher training, and curriculum enhancement. Teachers in
South Africa navigate overpopulated classrooms, limited resources, and historical disparities in access
to quality education (du Plessis & Letshwene, 2020; Nkambule, 2022).
Despite these challenges, teachers exhibit steadfast dedication to shaping positive outcomes in the
lives of their students. Teachers extend their roles beyond the classroom, participate in community
initiatives, and address broader social issues (Cappy, 2016; Soldaat, 2019). In South Africa, teachers
face persistent concerns regarding remuneration, job security, and the impact of government policies.
Teacher unions such as SADTU1 and NAPTOSA2 play a crucial role in safeguarding educators’ rights
and contributing to ongoing efforts to improve the education system. Collective bargaining discussions between teacher unions and education authorities encompass issues specific to lower secondary
education, including remuneration, labor conditions, and regulations affecting the educational landscape (Mokofe, 2021; Msila, 2022). Job security remains a pervasive concern, prompting unions to
advocate initiatives that shield teachers from unjust termination (Logue, 2018; Msila, 2022).
The teaching profession in South African secondary schools is influenced by the ongoing government policies, educational reforms, and initiatives aimed at rectifying historical inequities. Teachers’
commitment to addressing challenges, coupled with the advocacy of teachers’ unions, significantly
shapes the evolving dynamics of labor relations in the country’s secondary education sector.
Furthermore, the teaching culture in South Africa is characterized by profound dedication to rectifying past educational inequalities and fostering inclusivity. Teachers operate within a highly diverse
setting and interact with students who represent a wide array of ethnicities and linguistic communities
(Ndu et al., 2022; Zulu & Mukeredzi, 2021). The post-apartheid period underscores a shared commitment to achieving equal access to education, with teachers actively participating in community
engagement and collaborating with organizations to address broader societal challenges (Mudau,
2020).
Teachers in South Africa demonstrate perseverance and dedication in delivering outstanding
education, even in the face of challenges, such as overcrowded classrooms and inadequate resources.
Professionalism is highly esteemed, with a focus on continuous growth and strict adherence to ethical
principles (Lauria & Long, 2019). Teacher unions play a crucial role in championing educators’ rights

Theoretical Background
The self-determination theory (SDT) proposed by Deci and Ryan (2012) serves as a foundational
framework for comprehending teachers’ job satisfaction and its implications within educational
institutions. This theory posits that both intrinsic and extrinsic motivations play pivotal roles in
shaping teachers’ job satisfaction, with distinct consequences for their well-being and overall job
satisfaction. Environments that foster greater emphasis on intrinsic motivation are anticipated to yield
higher levels of well-being and job satisfaction among teachers, consequently bolstering their commitment to the teaching profession. Conversely, overreliance on extrinsic motivation is perceived as
jeopardizing teachers’ intrinsic motivation, potentially resulting in disengagement, burnout, and
professional attrition.
Deci and Ryan (2012) identified three fundamental psychological needs essential for intrinsic
motivation: competence (referring to the skills required for effective job performance), autonomy
(indicating control over one’s decisions and actions), and relatedness (highlighting connection and
support from colleagues). According to the self-determination theory, the fulfillment of these psychological needs enhances intrinsic motivation, subsequently leading to positive outcomes such as
heightened job satisfaction and overall well-being.
In the ensuing sections, an exploration of the concept of job satisfaction was conducted, delving
into how teacher autonomy, school climate (encompassing disciplinary climate, teacher-student
relations, and participation among stakeholders), and “perceptions of value and policy influence”
either enhance or hinder job satisfaction. This study sought to elucidate the intricate interplay between
the theoretical foundations of self-determination theory and factors influencing teachers’ job satisfaction in the educational landscape.
Concept of Job Satisfaction
Job satisfaction, a complex and multifaceted construct, is a critical aspect of an individual’s well-being
and motivation in the workplace. Drawing on the principles of SDT, which posit that intrinsic and
extrinsic motivations influence job satisfaction, we delve into various definitions and facets of job
satisfaction to understand how teachers’ well-being is intricately linked to their work experience.
According to Locke (1969), job satisfaction can be construed as a pleasurable emotional state that
results from a positive evaluation of one’s job or job experiences. This aligns with the SDT principle of
intrinsic motivation, which emphasizes the importance of internal factors in driving satisfaction (Deci
& Ryan, 2012). Evans (1997) extends this notion by defining job satisfaction as a “state of mind
determined by the extent to which the individual perceives her/his job-related needs to be met”
(p. 328). This echoes the SDT principle of autonomy, emphasizing the significance of individuals
having control over their decisions and actions in the workplace (Deci & Ryan, 2012).
Furthermore, the concept of job satisfaction is intertwined with the working environment, as
highlighted by Mincu (2015), who defines it as “a positive frame of mind reflected by the employee’s
opinion regarding work or the climate of his workplace” (p. 685). This underlines the SDT principle of
relatedness, which emphasizes the importance of connection and support from colleagues in fostering
job satisfaction (Deci & Ryan, 2012). Moreover, recognizing the intricate nature of job satisfaction, it is
essential to consider two main components: job comfort and job fulfillment. Job comfort, focusing on
satisfaction with employment conditions, aligns with external factors and extrinsic motivation, as

individuals find pleasure in favorable working conditions. On the other hand, job fulfillment, linked to
satisfaction with personal accomplishments within the job, resonates with intrinsic motivation,
emphasizing the internal satisfaction derived from meaningful achievements (Deci & Ryan, 2012;
Evans, 1997).
In the educational context, particularly for teachers, job satisfaction is often categorized into
“satisfaction with the profession” and “satisfaction with the current work environment” (Dinham &
Scott, 1998; Sergiovanni, 1967). This division is consistent with the SDT framework, in which
satisfaction with the profession may reflect intrinsic motivation driven by a passion for the work
itself, whereas satisfaction with the work environment may encompass extrinsic factors influencing
overall job satisfaction (Deci & Ryan, 2012). As we explored the dimensions of job satisfaction, this
study integrated SDT principles to gain a nuanced understanding of how teachers’ competence,
autonomy, and relatedness contribute to their overall job satisfaction.
Job Satisfaction and Teacher Autonomy
Teacher autonomy, the control teachers have over their actions and decisions (Jones et al., 2003;
Pearson & Hall, 1993), profoundly influences job satisfaction. Skaalvik and Skaalvik (2014) identified
a positive association between teacher autonomy and job satisfaction and highlighted autonomy as
a strong predictor. Peng et al. (2022) found that high autonomy was linked to elevated teaching
efficacy, job satisfaction, and improved mental health among primary and secondary school teachers.
Aliakbari et al. (2020) in Iran established a positive relationship between job satisfaction, teacher
autonomy, and reflective practice. Liu et al. (2021) in China demonstrated a positive correlation
between teacher autonomy and job satisfaction through distributed leadership.
Worth and Van den Brande (2020), grounded in SDT, affirmed the strong association between
teacher autonomy and improved job satisfaction. Hu (2023) and Dilekci (2022) found that job autonomy
had a significantly positive influence on job satisfaction in China and Turkey. Wang et al. (2020)
emphasized school autonomy as a crucial factor for teachers’ job satisfaction. Baroudi et al. (2022) in
Lebanon revealed intrinsic factors, including teacher autonomy, have a greater influence on job satisfaction. Massry-Herzallah and Arar (2019) in Arab education in Israel highlighted autonomy’s positive
impact on motivation and commitment. However, Dou et al. (2017) found that teacher autonomy has
a weak influence on job satisfaction and organizational commitment, prompting further exploration.
The existing literature consistently supports a positive association between teacher autonomy and
job satisfaction, aligning with the psychological need for autonomy in SDT and predicting a robust
and positive connection between teacher job satisfaction and autonomy. Therefore, we predict
a positive relationship between job satisfaction and teacher autonomy (see Figure 1).

Job Satisfaction and School Climate
The school climate, as defined by Cohen et al. (2009), shapes the well-being of students, parents, and
teachers by incorporating elements, such as safety and relationships (Cohen et al., 2009; Thapa et al.,
2013). Recent studies have linked school climate to diverse outcomes including academic performance
and psychological well-being (Aldridge & McChesney, 2018; Berkowitz et al., 2017; Shakeel &
DeAngelis, 2018). Notably, it is intertwined with teachers’ job satisfaction (Aldridge & Fraser, 2016).
A study by Tekin (2023) in Turkey established a direct link between teachers’ perceptions of the school
climate and job satisfaction.
Agustina et al. (2023) in Indonesia and Nalipay (2023) across Asian societies reaffirm the global
relevance of the positive association between school climate and teacher job satisfaction. Zholchieva
et al. (2023) found a significant positive relationship between organizational climate and teachers’ job
satisfaction in Kyrgyzstan, emphasizing the organizational aspects. Kouhsari et al. (2023) using TALIS
2018 data highlight the substantial influence of school-level factors, including school climate, on
teachers’ professional well-being. The collective literature consistently supports a positive relationship
between the school climate and job satisfaction. Subsequent sections explore specific dimensions of
school climate – teachers’ perceived disciplinary climate, teacher-student relations, and participation
among stakeholders – to offer nuanced insights into their contributions to overall job satisfaction.
Job Satisfaction and Teachers’ Perceived Disciplinary Climate. Teachers’ perceived disciplinary
climate, which reflects their perspective of students’ adherence to school rules, is intricately linked
to job satisfaction. The literature consistently indicates a significant negative association between
teacher job satisfaction and the disciplinary climate within schools (Dicke et al., 2020; García Torres,
2019; Liu et al., 2020; Nalipay, 2023; Poloni et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020). Dicke et al. (2020) found
a positive relationship between teachers’ satisfaction with the current work environment and the
disciplinary climate, suggesting improved job satisfaction when a positive disciplinary climate is
perceived.
Wang et al. (2020) revealed a negative association between classroom disciplinary climate and
teacher job satisfaction, indicating a detrimental impact on overall satisfaction. Liu et al. (2020)
emphasized the critical role of the disciplinary environment in shaping teachers’ contentment. A US
study by García Torres (2019) associated higher levels of delinquency and violence with lower work
satisfaction among teachers. Nalipay’s (2023) cross-cultural study consistently identified disciplinary
climate as a predictor of job satisfaction and workplace well-being. Analyzing TALIS 2013 data, Poloni
et al. (2022) highlighted the substantial impact of classroom disciplinary climate on teachers’ job
satisfaction at both individual and school levels.
The negative association aligns with SDT, where challenges in disciplinary issues disrupt teachers’
autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs, leading to decreased job satisfaction. Conversely,
a positive disciplinary climate supports these psychological needs and contributes to overall job
satisfaction. In summary, the literature converges with the notion that a negative disciplinary climate
is significantly associated with lower teacher job satisfaction. Hence, we predicted a negative relationship between teacher job satisfaction and the school disciplinary climate (see Figure 1).
H2: There is a significant negative association between teachers’ job satisfaction and school
disciplinary climate.

between educators and students, profoundly impact teachers’ job satisfaction. Jonasson et al. (2017)
revealed a positive association between teacher-student relations and job satisfaction, particularly
pronounced for expatriate teachers undergoing slower adjustment. Liu et al. (2020) affirmed that
teacher-student relations significantly predict teachers’ job satisfaction, underscoring the pivotal role
of these relations. Wang et al. (2020) emphasized teacher-student relationships as crucial in determining the degree of job satisfaction. Baroudi et al. (2022) highlighted the impact of fulfilling teachers’

Job Satisfaction and Participation Among Stakeholders. Teachers’ job satisfaction is intricately
linked to participation among stakeholders, an element of educational leadership encompassing distributed, shared, and democratic models (García Torres, 2017, 2019; Wang et al., 2020).
Distributed leadership, which involves diverse stakeholders, is a key contributor to overall job
satisfaction (Wang et al., 2020). Al-Mahdy et al. (2016) found a positive relationship between
servant-leader principals and teacher job satisfaction, emphasizing collaborative and shared
decision-making leadership styles. Principal support, valuing teacher contributions, and
enhancing well-being significantly impact teacher job satisfaction and reduce turnover intention (Al-Mahdy & Alazmi, 2023).
Maheshwari (2021) highlighted the positive impact of transformational leadership, contrasting it with transactional leadership, and indicating that participatory approaches positively
influence teacher satisfaction and performance. Luo et al. (2022) associated perceived transformational leadership with direct positive effects on teachers’ job satisfaction, aligning with
SDT principles. Massry-Herzallah and Arar (2019) emphasized teachers’ participation in
decision-making, leveraging motivation, commitment, and satisfaction. Pietsch et al. (2019)
corroborated the significance of shared leadership in shaping teachers’ commitment and job
satisfaction at the school level. Dou et al. (2017) affirmed the importance of instructional and
transformational leadership in influencing teachers’ attitudes and satisfaction levels. Han et al.
(2022) found that public leadership behaviors positively affect teachers’ motivation and job
satisfaction. Katıtaş et al. (2022) explored principals demonstrating shared leadership behaviors, contributing to increased teacher job satisfaction. Liu et al. (2021) found that distributed
leadership positively affects teacher autonomy, collaboration, and job satisfaction.
These studies collectively establish a significant positive association between job satisfaction
and participation among stakeholders. Participatory leadership, which reflects distributed,
shared, and democratic approaches, consistently influences teachers’ satisfaction and professional wellbeing. This alignment with SDT principles, addressing autonomy, competence, and
relatedness, predicts a positive association between teachers’ job satisfaction and participation
among stakeholders (see Figure 1).
H4: There is a significant positive association between teachers’ job satisfaction and participation
among stakeholders.
Job Satisfaction and “Perceptions of Value and Policy influence”
The interplay between job satisfaction and teachers’ perceptions of value and policy influence,
an underexplored area, has gained insight from SDT. Autonomy, a key SDT principle, is
linked to teachers’ policy influence. When their input is valued, their autonomy needs are
fulfilled, significantly boosting job satisfaction. Competence, another SDT component, is
evident when policies respect teachers’ insights, enhance competence, and positively affect
job satisfaction. Relatedness, the third SDT aspect, emerges in teachers feeling instrumental in
policy processes, fostering connectedness and enhancing job satisfaction. Overall, the literature

Methodology
Research Design
This study adopted a cross-sectional design, characterized by the collection of data from participants at
a specific point in time. In cross-sectional research, data are typically gathered from various populations or participant categories. The primary advantage of this design is its efficiency, which enables
swift data collection across diverse individuals within a relatively short timeframe (Johnson &
Christensen, 2017).
Source of Data
This study utilized an international database extracted from the 2018 Teaching and Learning
International Survey (TALIS). TALIS, conducted by the Organization for Economic Cooperation
and Development (OECD), offers global and cross-sectional data on teachers, teaching practices, and
learning environments for the benefit of researchers and policymakers (OECD, 2019a, p. 12). This
survey included teachers and principals from primary schools (ISCED 1), lower secondary schools
(ISCED 2), and high schools (ISCED 3) in 48 countries. The TALIS 2018 survey ensured the
administration of the ISCED 2 core survey to teachers and principals in all the participating countries
(OECD, 2019b, p. 112).
Participants
This study utilized the “core” TALIS data, focusing on teacher information from South Africa,
involving a total of 2046 lower secondary school teachers. The selection of participants followed the
two-stage sampling design outlined in the TALIS 2018 Technical Report (OECD, 2019b). Initially, the
TALIS 2018 sampling team employed systematic random sampling with a probability proportional to
size to choose a sample of schools. Subsequently, teachers were randomly selected from each chosen
school. The sample consisted of 2046 lower secondary school teachers in South Africa, with the
majority being female (approximately 60%) and holding an ISCED 2011 level 5 qualification (approximately 54%). Level 5 of the ISCED 2011 represents a short-cycle tertiary education that offers
advanced knowledge and skills beyond upper secondary education. Furthermore, a significant proportion of the participants (77%) fell within the age range of 30 to 59 years, and the majority had at
least six years of teaching experience (approximately 52%). Finally, most teachers (81%) held permanent positions, while 17.7% had fixed-term contracts spanning either more than one school year or less
(see Table 1).
Measures
This study examined three independent variables – teacher autonomy, school climate, and
“perceptions of value and policy influence”–in relation to one dependent variable: job satisfaction.

Job Satisfaction (Dependent Variable)
The dependent variable in this study was teacher job satisfaction, encompassing two subscales:
satisfaction with the work environment (JSENV) and satisfaction with the profession (JSPRO)
(OECD, 2019b). The teacher-level assessment covered both the work environment and professional
satisfaction, with the former comprising four components: (a) I would like to change to another school
if possible, (b) I enjoy working at this school, (c) I would recommend this school as a good place to
work, and (d) All in all, I am satisfied with my job. The professional satisfaction measure also included
four items: (a) the advantages of being a teacher outweigh the disadvantages, (b) If I could decide
again, I would still choose to work as a teacher, (c) I regret that I decided to become a teacher, and (d)
I wonder whether it would have been better to choose another profession. All items were measured on
a 4-point Likert scale: “strongly disagree” (1), “disagree” (2), “agree” (3), and “strongly agree” (4). The
Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficients of the JSENV and JSPRO scales were α = .79 and α = .70,
respectively (OECD, 2019a).
Teacher Autonomy Scale (Independent Variable)
The teacher autonomy scale (SATAT) was measured at the teacher level and contains five items: (a)
determining course content, (b) selecting teaching methods, (c) assessing student learning, (d)

agree” (4) (OECD, 2019b). The Cronbach’s alpha for the SATAT scale was .82.
School Climate Scale (Independent Variable)
The school climate scale includes three subscales: teachers’ perceived disciplinary climate (DISC),
teacher-student relations (STUD), and participation among stakeholders (STAKE) (OECD, 2019b).
All three subscales of the School Climate Scale were measured at the teacher level. The teachers’
perceived disciplinary measure contained four items: (a) When the lesson begins, I have to wait quite
a long time for students to quieten down, (b) Students in this class take care to create a pleasant
learning atmosphere, (c) I lose quite a lot of time because of students interrupting the lesson, and (d)
There is much disruptive noise in this classroom. The teacher-student relations measure also comprised four items: (a) teachers and students usually get on well with each other, (b) most teachers
believe that students’ well-being is important, (c) most teachers are interested in what students have to
say, and (d) if a student needs extra assistance, the school provides it.
Additionally, the participation among stakeholders measure was made up of five items: (a) this
school provides staff with opportunities to actively participate in school decisions, (b) this school
provides parents or guardians with opportunities to actively participate in school decisions, (c) this
school provides students with opportunities to actively participate in school decisions, (d) this school
has a culture of shared responsibility for school issues, and (e) there is a collaborative school culture
that is characterized by mutual support. All items were measured on a 4-point Likert scale: “strongly
disagree” (1), “disagree” (2), “agree” (3), and “strongly agree” (4) (OECD, 2019b). The Cronbach’s
alpha reliability coefficients of the DISC, STUD, and STAKE scales were .84, .78, and .91, respectively.
Perceptions of Value & Policy Influence Scale (Independent Variable)
The “perceptions of value and policy influence” scale (VALP) was measured at the teacher level and
contained three items: (a) Teachers’ views are valued by policymakers in this country/region, (b)
Teachers can influence educational policy in this country/region, and (c) Teachers are valued by the
media in this country/region. All items were measured on a 4-point Likert scale: “strongly disagree”
(1), “disagree” (2), “agree” (3), and “strongly agree” (4) (OECD, 2019b). Cronbach’s alpha for the
VALP scale was .72.

Analysis of Data
SPSS version 22 software was used for data analysis. The first research question employed Pearson’s
correlation to assess the significant relationships between independent variables (IVs) and dependent
variables (DVs). For the second research question, hierarchical multiple regression analysis was
applied to examine how various independent variables (teacher autonomy, school climate, and
“perceptions of value and policy influence”) could predict the dependent variable (job satisfaction).
Linear regression was chosen for its suitability in utilizing the relationship between IVs and DV for
predictions, as recommended by Kerr et al. (2002). Moreover, hierarchical multiple regression was
selected due to the involvement of three independent variables (teacher autonomy, school climate, and
“perceptions of value and policy influence”), with each entering at different hierarchical levels (Level 1
- teacher autonomy; Level 2 - school climate; and Level 3 – “perceptions of value and policy
influence”). According to Kerr et al. (2002), in hierarchical multiple regression, the researcher specifies
the order of entry for the independent variables. In this study, the independent variables were entered
based on the extent of prior research on their associations with job satisfaction, with teacher autonomy
being the first, followed by school climate, and then “perceptions of value and policy influence.”

Data Overview and Standardization
All the independent and dependent variables exhibited missing data, with teacher autonomy and
teachers’ perceived disciplinary climate having the highest instances of missing data (320 and 318,
respectively). The remaining variables displayed missing data, ranging from 32 to 24, as indicated in
Table 2 under Column N. Moreover, all variables demonstrated a normal distribution, with skewness
ranging from −0.60 to 0.30 and kurtosis ranging from −0.70 to 1.1. According to established criteria
(Byrne, 2010; George & Mallery, 2010; Hair et al., 2010), researchers can assert that their data are
normally distributed when skewness falls within the range of −2 to +2 and kurtosis falls within the
range of −7 to +7.
Furthermore, TALIS standardized the overall scores for the measures, setting the scale midpoint
at 10. Except for disciplinary climate, an overall or mean score below 10 indicates a lower state of
the measure, whereas a score above 10 indicates a higher state of the measure. For example,
a mean or overall score of 8.42 for teacher autonomy implies that teachers have less autonomy,
and a mean or overall score of 11.45 suggests that teachers have somewhat greater autonomy over
their work. Concerning teacher-perceived disciplinary climate, a mean score of 8.22 indicates
a positive disciplinary climate, whereas a mean score above 10 signifies a negative climate
(OECD, 2019a, p. 210).

Discussion
This study comprehensively investigated the multifaceted determinants of teachers’ job satisfaction and
traversing societal, school, teacher, and student dimensions. This study aimed to discern the unique
contributions of each factor to teachers’ overall job satisfaction. Employing a nationally representative
sample, this study sought to provide an encompassing analysis that simultaneously considered societal,
school-related, teacher-specific, and student-centric elements, thereby contributing a distinctive perspective
to the existing literature on teachers’ job satisfaction.
Student Factor
Teachers’ perspectives on students’ adherence to school rules, encapsulated in the concept of disciplinary
climate, have emerged as a pivotal factor influencing job satisfaction. These findings underscore the
significance of this dimension and shed light on its intricate interplay with educators’ overall contentment.
Consistent with the broader literature, our study revealed a significant relationship between teachers’
perceived disciplinary climate and their job satisfaction. Notably, our results align with prevailing evidence
indicating a negative association between these two variables. The literature consistently emphasizes the
adverse effects of a challenging disciplinary climate on teachers’ overall satisfaction (Dicke et al., 2020;
García Torres, 2019; Liu et al., 2020; Nalipay, 2023; Poloni et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020). This concordance
strengthens the validity of our findings and reinforces the broader understanding of the impact of
disciplinary issues on teachers’ job satisfaction. Moreover, the negative association observed aligns with
the theoretical underpinnings of SDT. According to SDT, challenges in disciplinary issues can disrupt
teachers’ autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs, leading to a decrease in job satisfaction.
Conversely, a positive disciplinary climate is posited to support these psychological needs, thereby contributing to overall job satisfaction. Our study’s alignment with the theoretical framework enhanced the
theoretical coherence of the observed relationship.
Teacher Factor
The examination of teacher-student relations (i.e., teacher factors) in the context of teacher job satisfaction
within both professional and environmental dimensions revealed compelling insights in our study. Teacherstudent relations, signifying the interpersonal connections between educators and their students, emerged
as a significant factor influencing teachers’ overall job satisfaction. Our study revealed a significant positive
association between teacher-student relations and teachers’ job satisfaction. Several studies have consistently

highlighted the pivotal role of these relationships in shaping educators’ contentment (Baroudi et al., 2022;
Jonasson et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2020; Nalipay, 2023; Poloni et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020). Additionally, in the
context of teacher-student relations, the positive association observed in our study resonates with the SDT
principles. The quality of the relationship between educators and students contributes to the fulfillment of
teachers’ psychological needs. Autonomy is satisfied through positive interactions, which allow teachers to
exercise control over instructional approaches and classroom management. Competence is nurtured when
teachers experience success in fostering positive connections with their students, contributing to a sense of
efficacy and mastery. Relatedness is fostered through meaningful interactions, creating a sense of belonging
and connection within the educational environment.
School Factors
Teacher Autonomy
The results of our study indicate a robust positive association between teacher autonomy and job satisfaction, aligned with existing literature grounded in SDT. Our findings resonate with those of Skaalvik and
Skaalvik (2014), Peng et al. (2022), and Worth and Van den Brande (2020), who emphasized autonomy as
a strong predictor of job satisfaction. The positive correlation demonstrated in China by Liu et al. (2021)
through distributed leadership further supported our results. Additionally, studies conducted in diverse
educational settings, such as Iran (Aliakbari et al., 2020), China (Hu, 2023), Turkey (Dilekci, 2022), and
Lebanon (Baroudi et al., 2022), collectively reinforce the positive influence of teacher autonomy on job
satisfaction. The literature consistently underscores the psychological need for autonomy within SDT,
predicting a positive connection between teacher job satisfaction and autonomy. Our study aligns with this
theoretical framework, suggesting that providing teachers with control over their actions and decisions
significantly contributes to their job satisfaction. This is particularly relevant in the context of educational
policies and practices that prioritize autonomy and support teachers in fostering a sense of competence and
relatedness in their professional roles.
Participation Among Stakeholders
Our study established a significant positive association between teacher job satisfaction and participation
among stakeholders, which is consistent with existing literature. Distributed leadership, which involves
diverse stakeholders in the decision-making processes, is a key contributor to overall job satisfaction. The
positive relationship between servant-leader principals and teachers’ job satisfaction found by Al-Mahdy
et al. (2016) underscores the importance of collaborative and shared decision-making leadership styles.
Participatory approaches are further highlighted by Maheshwari (2021), Luo et al. (2022), Massry-Herzallah
and Arar (2019), Pietsch et al. (2019), Dou et al. (2017), Han et al. (2022), Katıtaş et al. (2022), and Liu et al.
(2021). Collectively, these studies reinforce the positive influence of participatory leadership on teachers’
satisfaction, professional well-being, motivation, commitment, and collaboration. Moreover, alignment
with SDT principles, particularly addressing autonomy, competence, and relatedness, predicts a positive
association between teacher job satisfaction and participation among stakeholders. Recognizing the impact
of leadership styles that value teacher contributions and involve them in the decision-making processes is
crucial for educational policymakers and administrators. Creating environments that foster collaborative
and democratic models of leadership can enhance teachers’ job satisfaction by fulfilling their psychological
needs and fostering a sense of belonging and purpose in the educational community.
Societal Factor
Our study delved into the intricate relationship between teacher job satisfaction and “perceptions of value
and policy influence” (i.e., societal factor), shedding light on a relatively underexplored area within the
framework of SDT. The findings revealed a significant positive association between teachers’ job satisfaction
and their “perceptions of value and policy influence”. Drawing from SDT, the results suggest that autonomy,
competence, and relatedness, the key components of SDT, play a crucial role in shaping teachers’

professional contentment regarding their “perceptions of value and policy influence”. Recognizing teachers
as valued contributors to policy discussions and decisions empowers them, contributing to a sense of control
over their professional environment and bolstering their job satisfaction. Acknowledging teachers’ competence in shaping educational policies contributes to their professional well-being, creating a positive feedback loop between competence and job satisfaction. Moreover, when teachers perceive themselves as
integral to policy discussion and implementation, they foster a sense of connectedness. This relatedness
positively affects job satisfaction, emphasizing the importance of collaborative and inclusive policymaking
processes.

Implications, Limitations, and Future Studies
Implications
This study holds substantial significance for diverse education stakeholders by providing valuable
insights into the determinants of teachers’ job satisfaction across societal, school, teacher, and student
dimensions. These findings offer guidance to policymakers, educational leaders, and practitioners in
fostering a supportive environment for educators. First, recognizing the importance of teachers’
involvement in policymaking is crucial, emphasizing their contribution to policy formulation.
Policymakers are urged to prioritize teachers’ insights, aligning with the principles of autonomy,
competence, and relatedness from SDT, thereby fostering increased job satisfaction. Second, school
leaders play a pivotal role in shaping work environments. This study suggests that adopting participatory leadership styles involving diverse stakeholders can positively affect teachers’ job satisfaction.
Additionally, efforts to enhance teacher-student relationships and provide avenues for professional
development are highlighted as effective strategies for school administrators. These implications
extend to professional development, emphasizing the need for initiatives that promote autonomy in
instructional policies and collaboration among teachers. Establishing mentorship programs and
learning communities emerges as a constructive approach to supporting both novice and experienced
teachers, positively influencing teacher self-efficacy and overall job satisfaction. Furthermore, addressing societal perceptions of the teaching profession is crucial. Recognizing the intrinsic value of
teachers and their pivotal role in shaping future generations can significantly contribute to increased
job satisfaction. Policies aimed at improving the societal image of teaching are seen as potential
contributors to the long-term positive effects on teacher morale.
Limitations, and Future Studies
The study identifies key factors influencing teachers’ job satisfaction but lacks an exploration of mediation or
moderation effects within these factors. Future research should investigate these effects to provide a more
nuanced understanding of the dynamics shaping teachers’ job satisfaction. Additionally, examining principal job satisfaction as a separate latent factor and its potential direct or indirect impact on teachers’ job
satisfaction is recommended for further investigation. Moreover, the reliance on self-report measures
introduces the possibility of response bias, as teachers may offer socially desirable responses or not fully
capture the complexities of their experiences. To enhance validity, future studies should incorporate diverse
data sources such as administrative records or peer evaluations. Furthermore, the study primarily employed
linear regression models, potentially limiting the ability to capture the complexity of relationships among
the identified factors. Future research could benefit from advanced modeling techniques such as Structural
Equation Modeling or Hierarchical Linear Modeling to uncover the intricate interplay between variables.
