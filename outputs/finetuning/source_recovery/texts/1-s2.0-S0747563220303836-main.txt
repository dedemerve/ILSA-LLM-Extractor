Both teachers’ pedagogical and basic ICT competence beliefs are antecedents of ICT implementation in class.
Research has developed instruments to assess teachers’ pedagogical ICT competence beliefs. However, there is a
paucity of research on the assessment of teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs. The purpose of this study was to
develop and validate an instrument to assess teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs. Our analyses based on a
sample of 372 teachers in Germany (51.2% women, age M = 44.48, SD = 10.77) who participated in an online
survey. We used exploratory factor analyses to test the factorial structure and confirmatory factor analyses to
investigate the multidimensionality of the measure. Construct validity was analyzed using teachers’ attitudes
toward ICT, ICT use, and socio-demographic variables as validation variables. Our analyses identified six dimensions of teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs: information and data literacy, communication and collaboration, digital content creation, safety and security, problem solving, and analyzing and reflecting. Three of
these dimensions had a second-order structure. The six dimensions are differently correlated with teachers’ attitudes towards ICT, private and professional use of ICT, student-centered ICT teaching practices, and teacher
gender.

1. Introduction
Both teachers’ digital competence and their competence beliefs
related to information and communications technology (ICT) are
important for the successful integration of digital technology in teaching
and learning settings (Hatlevik, 2017; Hobbs, 2017; Knezek & Christensen, 2016; Petko, Prasse, & Cantieni, 2018; Wozney, Venkatesh, &
Abrami, 2006). In particular, teachers’ beliefs about their ability to deal
with ICT are closely related to the implementation of ICT in
school-related learning environments (Hatlevik, 2017; Klassen & Chiu,
2010; Pareja Roblin et al., 2018). A substantial body of studies have
examined specific dimensions of teachers’ ICT competence beliefs
related to teaching and instruction (e.g., Chai, Ng, Li, Hong, & Koh,
2013; Elstad & Christophersen, 2017; Knezek, Christensen, & Furuta,
2019). However, there is a paucity of research regarding teachers’
competence beliefs related to their basic ICT skills (for further discussion
see Hatlevik, 2017; Rubach & Lazarides, 2019). Although theoretical
and empirical work has identified multiple dimensions of teachers’ basic

ICT skills related to the use of digital tools and new technologies
(Tourón, Martín, Navarro Asencio, Pradas, & Íñigo, 2018; Wei, Piaw,
Kannan, & Moulod, 2016), this multidimensionality is rarely applied to
assess teachers’ beliefs about their skills. It is, however, important to
investigate the multidimensional structure of teachers’ basic ICT
competence beliefs as such investigations enable a more detailed understanding of these beliefs as an essential antecedent of ICT implementation in class (Pareja Roblin et al., 2018). Furthermore, it is crucial
to investigate whether different dimensions of competence beliefs
related to basic ICT skills vary in their importance for ICT teaching
practices. Against this backdrop, the present study aims to develop and
validate an instrument of teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs in
various theoretically described ICT competence dimensions.

2.1. Teachers’ basic and pedagogical ICT competence and competence
beliefs
Competence beliefs are defined as self-perceived and self-evaluated
competence with respect to certain activities in the respective domain,
to activities in other domains, and other people (Muenks, Wigfield, &
Eccles, 2018). On a theoretical level, the situated expectancy-value
theory of achievement motivation (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) states
that beliefs about the self, i.e., competence beliefs, together with
task-related value beliefs, explain an individual’s achievement-related
behaviors. Applying these assumptions to the context of teachers’ professional competence, research has shown that teachers who are more
confident about their skills and knowledge in a specific domain are also
more likely to make use of these skills in their learning and instruction
(see Bandura, 1997; Watt & Richardson, 2015). These theoretical assumptions also pertain to teachers’ ICT competence beliefs, which have
been shown to be related to the successful implementation of ICT in
teaching settings (Alt, 2018; Hatlevik, 2017; Knezek & Christensen,
2016; Pareja Roblin et al., 2018; Wozney et al., 2006).
Over the last decades, a substantial body of research has identified
and validated instruments to assess teachers’ and student teachers’
digital competence beliefs (Brandhofer, 2017; Elstad & Christophersen,
2017; Herzig & Martin, 2018; Knezek et al., 2019; Koh, Chai, & Tsai,
2013; Scherer, Tondeur, & Siddiq, 2017). Many of these studies have
referred to the technological pedagogical content knowledge framework
(TPCK), a well-known theoretical framework developed by Mishra and
Koehler (2006). The authors proposed in their framework that the
combination of teachers’ knowledge about technology, content, and
pedagogical approaches are primary influences on the successful
implementation of technology in teaching and learning settings. The
model further emphasized that teachers’ basic ICT knowledge about
various technologies is a central prerequisite for the professional
implementation of ICT in class.
Teachers’ ICT knowledge and related competence beliefs have been
further differentiated into basic ICT competence, pedagogical ICT
competence, and related competence beliefs (Krumsvik, 2014). Many
previous studies have assessed teacher competence beliefs, however,
only in relation to teachers’ pedagogical ICT-related skills. Focusing on
instruction, Knezek et al. (2019), for example, developed an instrument
to assess teachers’ perceived confidence in their ability to integrate
technology in learning environments, to prepare their lessons, and to use
new technologies appropriately in class. Elstad and Christophersen
(2017) focused on student teachers’ ICT self-efficacy regarding problem
solving in challenging pedagogical situations, i.e., bullying of students
through digital communication. Herzig and Martin (2018) developed an
instrument that assesses student teachers’ self-efficacy in using ICT for
specific teaching-related tasks (i.e., explaining content to students).
Consequently, whereas most developed instruments refer to Krumviks’
pedagogical ICT competence, instruments that measure teachers’ basic
ICT competence and related competence beliefs are not well-researched.
The few studies that already have addressed this research gap by
assessing basic ICT competence beliefs are often rather general, assessing whether teachers perceive themselves as able to create web pages,
learn technology easily, or have the technical skills they need to use
technology (Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Friedman, & Gebhardt, 2014; Koh
et al., 2013; Scherer et al., 2017; Vanderlinde & van Braak, 2010). In the
International Computer and Information Literacy Study 2013 (ICILS),
for example, teachers’ basic ICT self-efficacy is assessed by asking
teachers about their perceived skills in communicating and collaborating via ICT, producing digital content, or organizing digital data using
ICT (Fraillon et al., 2014; Lorenz, Gerick, Wendt, & Weischenberg,
2016). More detailed measures are needed to assess basic ICT competence beliefs (Cox, 2008; Hatlevik, 2017). More concretely, new instruments are needed that cover the complete range of basic ICT skills

2.2. Dimensions of teachers’ basic ICT competence and competence
beliefs
In the last decade, research has intensively investigated questions
regarding the relations between ICT competence and related competence beliefs. Theoretical models, such as the situated expectancy-value
theory, propose a strong association between competence beliefs and
competence (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Within ICT, there is a large
amount of research that has shown low to moderate associations between ICT competence beliefs and ICT competence for students in K-12
and Higher Education (Aesaert, Voogt, Kuiper, & van Braak, 2017;
Hatlevik, Throndsen, Loi, & Gudmundsdottir, 2018; Prior, Mazanov,
Meacheam, Heaslip, & Hanson, 2016; Rohatgi, Scherer, & Hatlevik,
2016; Siddiq et al., 2016). Some studies also show such associations in
populations of teachers. Hatlevik (2017), for example, indicated a significant moderate association between basic ICT self-efficacy beliefs and
basic ICT competence using a sample of teachers from primary and
secondary schools.
Due to the theoretically assumed association between competence
beliefs and competence (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) and the empirically
demonstrated association between ICT competence beliefs and actual
ICT competency (Hatlevik, 2017; Krumsvik, 2014), we aim to describe
the structure of ICT competence beliefs by reviewing both frameworks of
ICT competence and competence beliefs.
A highly comprehensive overview of frameworks that conceptualize
ICT competence is provided by Siddiq et al. (2016). The authors describe
the following six competence dimensions based on a systematic review
of 66 publication: information [e.g., managing digital information],
communication [e.g., Interacting through digital technologies],
content-creation [e.g., developing content and programming], safety [e.
g., protecting the environment], problem solving [e.g., innovating and
creatively using technology], and technical operational [e.g., basic
technical skills].
Frameworks that have been suggested by educational policy (Carretero, Vuorikari, & Punie, 2017; Ferrari, 2013; Kultusministerkonferenz, 2016) and that have been validated in empirical studies (Tourón
et al., 2018) have described similar dimensions of basic ICT competence:
information and data literacy, communication and collaboration, digital
content creation, safety, problem solving, and analysis and reflection.
All described frameworks further differentiate the overarching dimensions of basic ICT skills into more specific competencies and skills;
for example, the dimension ‘problem solving’ is further differentiated
into the sub-facets ‘solving technical problems,’ ‘identifying needs and
technological responses,’ ‘using digital technologies in a creative way,’
and ‘identifying the boundaries of own competence.’ An overview of
such competence dimensions and subordinated skills are visualized in
Fig. 1.
As Siddiq et al. (2016) pointed out, in order to understand the whole
range of ICT competence (beliefs), next to theoretical frameworks,
empirical studies need to be reviewed. In our work, we tested the
theoretically assumed multidimensionality of basic ICT competence by
developing a 28-item measure that assessed student teachers’ basic ICT
competence beliefs. Referring to the above-described dimensionality of
six different competence dimensions (Ferrari, 2013; Kultusministerkonferenz, 2016; Tourón et al., 2018) and using data on 292 student
teachers, we identified six overarching dimensions of basic ICT
competence beliefs: information and data literacy, communication and
collaboration, digital content creation, safety and security, problem solving,

2.3. Teachers’ ICT competence beliefs, attitudes, and ICT teaching
practices
On a theoretical level, Situated Expectancy-Value Theory (SEVT,
Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) proposes that individuals’ achievement behaviors in a certain domain depend on their feelings of being competent
in the respective domain and on their subjective value beliefs related to
the task. In the context of ICT, the will, skill, tool model of technology
integration (Knezek & Christensen, 2016) similarly proposes that

teachers’ ICT competence beliefs, ICT skills, and positive attitudes, as
well as the availability and accessibility of ICT in schools and pedagogical approaches to ICT use, are proximal antecedents of teachers’ ICT
use and teaching practices in class. Based on both theoretical models,
research has shown positive associations between teachers’ basic ICT
competence beliefs and ICT implementation in class (Drossel, Eickelmann, & Gerick, 2017; Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010; Ertmer,
Ottenbreit-Leftwich, Sadik, Sendurur, & Sendurur, 2012; Fraillon et al.,
2014; Knezek & Christensen, 2016; Pareja; Petko, 2012; Pareja Roblin
et al., 2018). More specifically, findings by Rubach and Lazarides (2019)
have indicated, for example, that student teachers who felt competent in
the dimensions ‘information and data literacy,’ ‘communication and
collaboration,’ and ‘analyzing and reflecting’ were likely to use a
particularly diverse range of digital devices in their university studies.
Furthermore, student teachers who often used digital devices in university courses to plan teaching- and learning-related activities felt
particularly competent in the overarching competence dimension
‘problem solving’ (Rubach, Lazarides, Brendel, & Krauskopf, 2019).
In line with the assumptions of SEVT (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020), relations between competence beliefs and value beliefs may also be
assumed. Empirical evidence shows that (student) teachers’ basic ICT
competence beliefs are related to their attitudes toward ICT (Ertmer
et al., 2012; Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010; Petko, 2012; Rubach &
Lazarides, 2019; Scherer, Siddiq, & Teo, 2015). Positive attitudes toward academic activities are assumed to enhance effort and persistence,
which in turn leads to high achievement and high achievement-related
competence beliefs (Wigfield & Eccles, 1992). Whereas some studies
exist that examine relations between the dimensions of teachers’ basic
ICT competence beliefs (Petko, 2012; Rubach & Lazarides, 2019;
Scherer et al., 2015), ICT use in private and professional settings, and
ICT-related attitudes, there is a scarcity of research regarding the associations between teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs and the
implementation of ICT teaching practices. Teachers’ ICT teaching
practices can be differentiated into teacher-centered and
student-centered ICT practices (Ertmer et al., 2012; Prasse, Döbeli
Honegger, & Petko, 2017). In teacher-centered ICT teaching practices,
the teacher presents and manages the learning content and mainly uses
technology for instructional purposes. Student-centered ICT teaching
practices can be described as using ICT to create collaborative settings
between teachers and students, in which teachers guide students in the
processes of creating their own knowledge (Ertmer et al., 2012). In such
instructional settings, teachers and students use technology to explore
content, communicate with each other, and solve problems or to analyze
learning content using ICT. Student-centered ICT teaching practices can
be used to support students’ autonomy in class, enhance cognitive
activation, and foster individualized instruction and personalized
learning. Previous research has emphasized that teachers who implement teacher-centered and student-centered ICT teaching practices feel
competent in the competence dimension ‘information and data literacy’
(Ertmer et al., 2012; Gobbo & Girardi, 2001; Schaumburg, Prasse,
Tschackert, & Blömeke, 2007). Moreover, teachers who apply
student-centered ICT teaching practices are shown to feel particularly
competent in the dimension ‘problem solving’ in digital environments,
as well as in the dimensions ‘communication and collaboration’ and
‘digital content creation’ (Ertmer et al., 2012; Gobbo & Girardi, 2001;
Prasse et al., 2017). One explanation for these results could be that
teachers who believe that they are able to competently communicate
with their students using digital tools and who believe that they are able
to solve complex problems with such tools are more likely to approach
their students and to effectively and directly support them by using
digital technology. That might result in a stronger focus on
student-centered ICT teaching practices (see Knezek & Christensen,
2016). Due to student-centered ICT teaching practices are more
collaborative, teachers in their role as collaborators need to help students in various situations in which students use ICT to gain knowledge
autonomously and individually (Ertmer et al., 2012). Teachers might

feel more competent in mandatory competence areas such as ‘communication and collaboration’ or ‘problem solving’ to use such practices.
2.4. Individual characteristics and teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs
As described in Eccles’ et al. SEVT, person characteristics e.g.,
gender, age, temperament, and the cultural milieu (e.g., gender social
roles or stereotypes) influence an individual’s self schemata (Eccles &
Wigfield, 2020). Applied to ICT, research has shown that time-specific
ICT trends and options of access led to different ICT-related socialization processes in different age groups (Palfrey & Gasser, 2008).
Furthermore, different stereotypes based on gender and ICT, such as the
prototypical male computer scientist, have been shown to be associated
with gender difference in ICT interests, competence beliefs, and
behavior (Buchmüller, Joost, Bessing, & Stein, 2011; Cheryan, Plaut,
Handron, & Hudson, 2013; Ferreira, 2017, pp. 1–6).
Previous research has indicated that individual characteristics of
teachers are related to their competence beliefs – for example, younger
teachers reported higher ICT competence beliefs than older teachers
(Fraillon et al., 2014; López-Vargas, Duarte-Suárez, & Ibáñez-Ibáñez,
2017). Results of an empirical study by Fraillon et al. (2014) demonstrate particularly strong relations between age and the competence
dimensions ‘information and data literacy,’ ‘communication and
collaboration,’ and ‘digital content creation.’ Another study indicated
that age is negatively related to perceived competence in sending
e-mails with attachments or participating in online discussion threads
(Lorenz et al., 2016). Teachers’ years of teaching experience is closely
related to teachers’ age. Previous studies have identified teachers’ work
experience in years compared to their age as a more meaningful factor in
understanding ICT use and teachers’ ICT competence beliefs (Drossel
et al., 2017; Gil-Flores, Rodríguez-Santero, & Torres-Gordillo, 2017).
Another individual characteristic that is related to teachers’ basic
ICT competence beliefs is their gender. Existing studies yield inconsistent results, as some findings indicate that male teachers, compared to
female teachers, perceive themselves as more competent to analyze and
reflect on data using ICT and to produce digital content (Gebhardt,
Thomson, Ainley, & Hillman, 2019; Koh, Chai, & Tsai, 2014; Lorenz
et al., 2016). Other results did not demonstrated significant gender
differences in basic ICT competence beliefs or competence among
teachers (Yuen & Ma, 2002), whereas other studies identified gender
differences for specific competence dimensions - for example, male
teachers have been shown to exhibit overall higher basic ICT competence in the areas of web content creation, programming, or information
seeking (Kaarakainen, Kivinen, & Vainio, 2018).
2.5. The current study
Previous studies have shown that both basic and pedagogical ICT
competence beliefs of teachers are central antecedents of effectively
integrating digital technology in educational settings (Hatlevik, 2017;
Pareja Roblin et al., 2018; Wozney et al., 2006). Nevertheless, existing
studies have often focused only on teachers’ pedagogical ICT competence beliefs (Elstad & Christophersen, 2017; Knezek & Christensen,
2016) or assessed teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs without
considering the multidimensionality of the construct (Hatlevik, 2017;
Ramírez-Montoya, Mena, & Rodríguez-Arroyo, 2017; Scherer et al.,
2017). The purpose of this study is to address these gaps in the current
research by developing and validating a measure to assess multiple dimensions and the hierarchical structure of teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs.
The development of the measure is informed by theoretical concepts
in educational psychology (SEVT; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020), by frameworks derived from educational policy (Ferrari, 2013; Kultusministerkonferenz, 2016; Law, Woo, La Torre, & Wong, 2018), and by
empirical research on teachers’ basic ICT competence (Tourón et al.,
2018). Based on theoretical frameworks and previous research (Rubach

3. Methods
3.1. Sample
Our analyses were based on data of N = 372 teachers (age:
M = 44.48, SD = 10.77; 67.5% full-time teachers) in Germany who
participated in the DigiKompEL study (ICT competence beliefs of student teachers and teachers; Rubach & Lazarides, 2017–2019) that
focused, for example, on teachers’ basic and pedagogical ICT competence beliefs, attitudes towards ICT, and ICT teaching practices. Online
survey data were assessed via Twitter and newsletters from teacher
training institutions. Participation in this survey was voluntary and
anonymous. The teachers (51.2% female, n = 12 missing indications of
gender) worked in different school tracks or stages: 79.7% were secondary school teachers, 9.6% were primary school teachers, and 3.4%
were special education teachers. Participants taught in a wide range of
different domains, whereby the most frequently taught subjects were
mathematics (35.6%), computer science (30.1%), the German language
(28.3%), English as a foreign language (21.6%), history (12.5%),
physical education (11.9%), biology (10.1%) and chemistry (9.1%)
(more than one subject could be listed).

Mplus 8.1 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998-2016). We used a stepwise (bottom-up) approach by conducting a series of exploratory factor analyses

of teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs (Jarvis, MacKenzie, & Podsakoff, 2003; MacKenzie, Podsakoff, & Podsakoff, 2011). To examine the

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Exploratory factor analyses
(EFA) were modeled in line with Williams, Onsman, and Brown (2014).
The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin Measure of Sampling Adequacy and Bartlett’s
Test of Sphericity were used to assess the appropriateness of the data to
conduct EFA (Hair, Black, Babin, & Anderson, 2010, p. 7; Malhotra,
1999). A series of EFA models were carried out to examine the factorial
structure of each of the theoretically assumed competence dimensions
separately using the maximum likelihood extraction method with oblique rotation (Eid, Gollwitzer, & Schmitt, 2017; Moosbrugger, 2012). We
used the oblique rotations (Promax) in our EFA models because the
correlation coefficients between the included items for each competence
dimension were above r = 0.32, indicating an overlap of more than 10%
of the variance among the items for each factor (Tabachnick & Fidell,
2007). We considered factor loadings above λ = 0.50 as well as parallel
loadings λ < 0.20 as criteria for an acceptable threshold to include items
(Ferguson & Cox, 1999). We used the χ2‒ test to compare the EFA
models, as well as the root mean square residual (SRMR) and the root
mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) (Fabrigar, Wegener,
MacCallum, & Strahan, 1999, Eid et al., 2017). Furthermore, we evaluated each factor solution based on its theoretical meaningfulness.
Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). One of the aims of using CFA
was to test the hypothesized factor structure derived from our theoretical EFA models for each scale separately. We assumed different factor
loadings of the items, which is why we conducted τ-congeneric models
(Eid & Schmidt, 2014). McDonald’s (1999) ω coefficient was therefore
used to estimate the internal consistency of each scale.
After confirming the factor structure of each dimension separately,
we tested a series of CFA models in which all dimensions were modeled
simultaneously (see Fig. 2). In Model 1, we specified a unidimensional
model, which represented one single factor ‘teachers’ basic ICT
competence beliefs’ explained by all items. In Model 2, we tested the
previously proposed six-factor structure with related subfactors derived
from the EFA modeling procedure (Rubach & Lazarides, 2019). In Model
3, we included a g-factor into Model 2. We tested the g-factor model
because a general factor (g-factor) of domain-specific competence beliefs is described in various theoretical models (Bong & Skaalvik, 2003).
We used the full information maximum likelihood algorithm (FIML)
to handle missing data (Schafer & Graham, 2002). All analyses were
conducted using the maximum likelihood with robust standard errors
(MLR) estimation in Mplus 8.1 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998-2016). To
evaluate the goodness of model fit, the following indicators were used:
the robust χ2 test statistic, comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker and Lewis
index (TLI), root mean square of approximation (RMSEA), standardized
root mean residual (SRMR), Akaike information criterion (AIC),
Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and Akaike’s Bayesian information criterion (ABIC). TLI and CFI values greater than 0.90, RMSEA, and
SRMR values lower than 0.08 were accepted as indicators of an
acceptable model fit (Brown, 2015; Hu & Bentler, 1999). A lower value

4.4. Results for construct validity
Correlations among the six identified basic ICT competence beliefs of
teachers and their ICT attitudes (ICT benefit value, ICT cost value), ICT
use, ICT teaching practices, and socio-demographic characteristics are
reported in Table 7. All dimensions of teachers’ basic ICT competence
beliefs were positively and significantly associated with each other. The
lowest correlations were found between ‘analyzing and reflecting’ and
‘communication and collaboration,’ as well as between ‘analyzing and
reflecting’ and ‘digital content creation.’ The strongest associations were
shown for ‘problem solving’ and ‘digital content creation,’ as well as for
‘information and data literacy’ and ‘communication and collaboration.’
Male teachers reported higher basic ICT competence beliefs, i.e.,
regarding the competence dimensions ‘digital content creation,’ ‘safety
and security,’ ‘problem solving,’ and ‘analyzing and reflecting’
compared to female teachers. No gender differences were found for the
dimensions ‘information and data literacy’ and ‘communication and
collaboration.’ Furthermore, the teachers’ years of experience in school
were not significantly associated with their basic ICT competence beliefs. The more digital media teachers used on average in their day-today life and in classrooms, the higher teachers perceived their own
basic ICT competence in all six competence dimensions. Positive correlations were also found between teachers’ subjective benefit values of
using digital technology in their classrooms and their basic ICT
competence beliefs in all six dimensions (0.24 ≥ r ≤ 0.51). In other
words, the more interesting, important, and useful they perceived digital
technology to be for their profession, the more competent they
perceived themselves in using it. Additionally, as expected, small
negative correlations were indicated between teachers’ subjective cost
values of using digital media in their professional environment and all
six basic ICT competence dimensions (− 0.31 ≥ r ≤ − 0.18).
With a focus on different ICT teaching practices, ICT individualized
instruction, cognitive activation, and autonomy support were weakly or
moderately correlated with each of the six teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs (0.23 ≥ r ≤ 0.48). This suggested that teachers with greater
convictions about their basic ICT competence in all six dimensions also
tended to use ICT for individualized instruction, cognitive activation,
and autonomy support in their classes.

5.1. Factorial structure of teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs
Although teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs are essential antecedents of their ICT implementation in class (Hatlevik, 2017; Pareja
Roblin et al., 2018), the operationalization of this construct has been
fuzzy and not consistent with theoretical and empirical approaches
about basic ICT competence (Ferrari, 2013; Law et al., 2018; Siddiq
et al., 2016). This study considered a broad range of ICT competence
beliefs related to essential 21st-century digital competencies (Ferrari,
2013; Kultusministerkonferenz, 2016; Law et al., 2018; Siddiq et al.,
2016). As expected (Hypothesis 1), we were able to empirically distinguish between the following six factors: ‘information and data literacy,’
‘communication and collaboration,’ ‘digital content creation,’ ‘safety
and security,’ ‘problem solving,’ and ‘analyzing and reflecting.’ The
factorial structure fits the data well, and the overall model showed good
psychometric qualities. With respect to previous assumptions (Bong &
Skaalvik, 2003; Knezek & Christensen, 2016; Krumsvik, 2014; Mishra &
Koehler, 2006), we can reject the hypothesis that the basic ICT competence beliefs of teachers can be explained by a general factor.
Interestingly, our results showed that the three dimensions
‘communication and collaboration,’ ‘digital content creation,’ and
‘safety and security’ of teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs had a oneorder structure. In contrast, the three dimensions ‘information and data
literacy,’ ‘problem solving,’ and ‘analyzing and reflecting’ were multifaceted and best represented by a second-order factorial structure. By
testing the factorial structure of the sub facets of teachers’ basic ICT
competence beliefs, our research contributes to a better understanding
of the theoretical construct ‘basic ICT competence beliefs’ of teachers.
Chen, Sousa, and West (2005) point out that testing second-order
structures is particularly important to understand the association between specific factors and external criteria. Specifically, knowledge
about the multifaceted structure of theoretical constructs allows a
fine-grained analysis of its relations to other factors (McInerney & Ali,
2006). For example, in order to examine the process of teachers prepare
instructions with ICT, the competence beliefs of the higher-order factor
‘information and data literacy’ might explain teachers’ behavior
because it involves both searching for and storing information and
material online. When investigating teachers’ selection of online material or their use of search processes in class, the competence beliefs in the
specific dimension ‘searching’ might be a better predictor. Our study

5.2. Relations between teachers’ ICT competence beliefs, attitudes, and
use of ICT
The results of this study are consistent with the existing literature,
which demonstrates significant associations between teachers’ ICT use
and their basic ICT competence beliefs (Drossel et al., 2017; Ertmer
et al., 2012; Pareja; Petko, 2012; Pareja Roblin et al., 2018). In line with
our assumptions (Hypothesis 2), teachers who have higher basic ICT
competence beliefs in all six dimensions also report on average frequent
use of multiple digital technologies in both day-to-day life and their
professional environment. These findings are in line with theoretical
assumptions of the will, skill, tool model of technology integration
(Knezek & Christensen, 2016). Previous research (Alt, 2018; Pareja
Roblin et al., 2018) has supported this argument, as it indicates that

the student-centered ICT teaching practices. These findings might be
explained by teachers’ need to feel familiar and comfortable with ICT
practices (Pareja Roblin et al., 2018). Teachers’ basic ICT competence
beliefs have been indicated as one of the most influential factors of ICT
use in class (Ertmer et al., 2012). For student-centered teaching practices
compared to teacher-centered pedagogical approaches, previous findings indicate that teachers need to be more competent using ICT because
this applies a broader range of ICT usage (Ertmer et al., 2012; Prasse
et al., 2017). Our results might indicate that ICT teaching practices
included in this study (individualized instruction, cognitive activation,
autonomy support) do not require a specific set of competence beliefs. It
might be assumed that teachers who feel generally competent in their
use of ICT will implement student-centered methods using ICT. Nevertheless, it is possible to suspect a ranking of more and less important
basic ICT competence beliefs. All three student-centered ICT strategies
are strongly associated with competence beliefs in the dimension
‘problem solving’, whereas ICT competence beliefs in ‘digital content
creation’ are less associated with these student-centered ICT strategies.
Additional Wald χ2 tests underlined the assumed stronger associations
(see Appendix Table A4).
Future research should focus in more detail on the ranked importance of the dimensions of basic ICT competence beliefs for studentcentered ICT teaching practices. Also interesting is the question of the
ranked importance of the dimensions of basic ICT competence beliefs for
teachers-centered ICT teaching practices, which have been shown to be
related to specific competence beliefs, such as perceived competence in
the dimension ‘information and data literacy’ (Ertmer et al., 2012). It
might also be possible that depending on ICT teaching practices,
different combinations of teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs are
important for ICT implementation in class. Being one of the first studies
that analyzed multiple dimensions of teachers’ basic ICT competence
beliefs and their variously strong relations to ICT teaching practices, our
findings might contribute to a better understanding of the importance of
dimensions of basic ICT competence beliefs, in particular of the dimensions ‘problem solving’ and ‘safety and security’ for
student-centered ICT teaching practices. For future research, it is
important to examine the directionality of effects between basic ICT
competence beliefs and ICT teaching practices. It is also relevant to
examine whether ICT competence beliefs in particular dimensions are of
particular importance for the implementation of specific
student-centered teaching practices, which would be possible by
including a broader range of teaching practices in future studies.
Consistent with our expectations (Hypothesis 4) and previous
research (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010; Petko, 2012; Rubach &
Lazarides, 2019), the current study demonstrates a significant association between teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs and their attitudes
towards ICT. As described in situated expectancy-value theory (Eccles &
Wigfield, 2020), benefit values (interest value, attainment value, and
utility value of ICT) are positively correlated with all six dimensions of
teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs, most strongly with ‘information
and data literacy’ and ‘communication and collaboration.’ In contrast,
cost values are negatively associated with teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs, most strongly with ‘communication and collaboration’ and
‘digital content creation.’ Whereas Pareja Roblin et al. (2018) describe
the perceived costs and benefits of ICT as antecedents of teachers’ ICT
use in class, we can also conclude that cost and benefit values are related
to competence beliefs. The will, skill, tool model of technology integration (Knezek & Christensen, 2016) highlights attitudes and competence beliefs as essential antecedents of teachers’ ICT use in class. Our
results show that these factors are not independent of one another. In
accordance with SEVT (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020), the next step might be
to analyze whether competence beliefs and attitudes (value beliefs)
interact in predicting teachers’ ICT teaching practices.

5.3. Relations between individual characteristics and basic ICT
competence beliefs
Our fifth hypothesis about the negative correlation between teachers’ ICT competence beliefs and their years of teaching experience was
not confirmed. Surprisingly, we found no evidence that teachers with
more years of teaching experience felt less competent than teachers with
fewer years of teaching experience. Previous studies have shown that
teachers with more years of teaching experience are more anxious and
concerned about ICT in school (Fransson, Holmberg, Lindberg, &
Olofsson, 2019; Rahimi & Yadollahi, 2011) and that teachers, in general,
have little knowledge about privacy laws or important methods of
applying ‘safety and security’ in digital environments (Šimandl, 2015;
Šimandl & Vaníček, 2017). Consequently, one might assume that especially older teachers would make efforts to become competent in this
particular area in order to actively counteract their fears of dealing with
this issue.
When interpreting our findings, it needs to be considered that
teachers with fewer than 20 years of teaching experience were overrepresented in our sample.2 Since we used online surveys to collect data,
participating teachers might already have experience in ICT use. That
might be one reason for the missing age-specific associations, and no
evidence has been found that supports the discussion about digital natives and digital immigrants (Prensky, 2001). Another point might be
the type of relationship: Previous research has indicated no linear
relation between teachers’ age or years of experience, but there are hints
on a curvilinear relationship (Cavas, Cavas, Karaoglan, & Kisla, 2009).
For future studies, it might be important (a) to examine the associations
between age and ICT competence beliefs in a larger representative
sample and (b) to test whether these associations are curvilinear.
The present study contributes to the debate on gender differences in
teachers’ ICT competence beliefs. Previous research has shown inconsistent results concerning gender differences (Gebhardt et al., 2019;
Kaarakainen et al., 2018; Lorenz et al., 2016; Yuen & Ma, 2002). The
present study adds to the debate by analyzing gender differences in six
different dimensions of teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs. The results support our hypothesis (Hypothesis 6) that male teachers will
perceive themselves as more competent in ‘analyzing and reflecting,’
and ‘digital content creation.’ Additionally, our findings show unexpected higher competence beliefs for male teachers than for female
teachers in the dimensions ‘safety and security’ and ‘problem solving.’ A
variety of previous studies have argued that gender differences in ICT
competence beliefs are related to men’s positive attitudes toward ICT,
and to their higher tendency to use ICT (Gebhardt et al., 2019; Punter,
Meelissen, & Glas, 2017). Another explanation of the gender-specific
association might be gender stereotypes about ICT (Clayton, Hellens,
& Nielsen, 2009). The ICT domain is defined as masculine and as related
to mathematical, technical, and logical skills (for an overview, see
Clayton et al., 2009). Such stereotypes might also lead to gender differences between male and female teachers in their basic ICT competence beliefs in the ICT domain (Margolis & Fisher, 2002; Wöhler &
Knolauch, 2013). In addition to teachers’ basic ICT competency beliefs,
research has shown gender differences in pedagogical ICT competency
beliefs (Jang & Tsai, 2013; Scherer et al., 2017). We strongly recommend linking teachers’ basic and pedagogical ICT literacy beliefs with
their ICT implementation in class, as well as integrating gender and
stereotypes about ICT as explanatory factors. Such an analysis would
enable researchers to understand the interplay between ICT competence
beliefs, gender, and ICT implementation in the classroom.

Although this study presents several significant findings in the
context of teachers’ ICT competence beliefs, some limitations need to be
considered. First, since the aim of our study was to develop and validate
an instrument to assess teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs, we used
a cross-sectional design. Therefore, a major limitation is that we did not
examine the longitudinal relationships and thus the changes in the
factorial structure between ICT literacy beliefs over time, nor did we
consider the longitudinal relationships between teachers’ basic ICT literacy beliefs and ICT attitudes and behavior. Such an analysis would
make it possible to test the assumptions of the will, skill, tool model of
technology integration (Knezek & Christensen, 2016) regarding the
directionality of effects.
Second, teachers’ pedagogical ICT beliefs were not considered as
additional validation variables. As the next step, future research should
analyze the association between the two types of ICT competence beliefs
in order to test the theoretical assumption that basic ICT competence
beliefs are antecedents of pedagogical ICT competence beliefs (Mishra &
Koehler, 2006; Redecker, 2017).
Since the majority of studies comparing self-reported competence
beliefs of ICT competence with actual performance report only low
correlation for students (Siddiq et al., 2016) or moderate correlation for
teachers (Hatlevik, 2017), it should be investigated further how and
through which mechanisms teachers’ ICT competence beliefs and
teachers’ ICT competence are interrelated.
Fourth, data from the participating teachers were assessed using an
online survey. Consequently, only those teachers might have participated voluntarily in our study who are already interested in ICT usage in
schools, and who already are to a certain level, competent in using ICT.
Furthermore, a high percentage of teachers in our sample are teachers in
ICT-related fields. Due to validate the findings of our study, it would
therefore be needed to test the examined relations in a representative
teacher sample.

6. Conclusions
Guided by various theories and frameworks (Ferrari, 2013; Kultusministerkonferenz, 2016; Law et al., 2018; Siddiq et al., 2016), we are
the first study to analyze the multidimensionality of teachers’ basic ICT
competence beliefs. We understand our study as a first step toward
investigating teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs about a broad range
of 21st-century skills. As expected, we identified six dimensions of
teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs (information and data literacy,
communication and collaboration, digital content creation, safety and
security, problem solving, and analyzing and reflecting), of which three
dimensions had a second-order structure.
Given the fact that teachers’ need to implement ICT in their classes to
foster students’ digital competence (Kultusministerkonferenz, 2016;
Redecker, 2017) and competence beliefs are antecedents of teachers’
ICT use (Hatlevik, 2017; Knezek & Christensen, 2016; Pareja Roblin
et al., 2018), the need for a validated instrument assessing a broad range
of teachers’ basic ICT competence beliefs is paramount. Therefore, the
present study’s findings provide research and educational practice with
a validated instrument to identify teachers’ needs for further education
and support that can be applied in teacher training programs.
