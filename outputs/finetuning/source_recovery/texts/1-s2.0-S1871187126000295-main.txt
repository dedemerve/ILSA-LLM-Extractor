Background: Creativity is a highly desirable general competence in the 21st century worldwide,
but the potential of reading literacy in predicting general creativity competence is understudied.
Materials and Methods: The current study examined the relationship between reading literacy and
general creativity competence at the student and school levels. We used the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022 produced by 5557 Hong Kong adolescents (Mage =
15.81, SD = 0.29, females = 49%) nested within 163 schools. Hong Kong was chosen for its
consistent efforts to integrate creativity into the educational curriculum. We included student
socio-economic status (SES) and gender (0 = female, 1 = male) at the student level and school
SES at the school level as covariates.
Results: A two-level path analysis indicated that: (1) student reading literacy produced a large
predictive effect on student creativity competence; (2) school mean of reading literacy had a large
positive effect on school collective creativity competence; (3) student SES had a small positive
effect on student creativity competence and gender had a medium negative effect on student
creativity competence in favor of females; and (4) school SES had a large positive effect on school
collective creativity competence.
Conclusion: The results showed the importance of fostering creativity competence through reading
interventions and school resource investment.

1. Introduction
By the Oxford dictionary, creativity means “the use of imagination or original ideas to create something”. In research, creativity is
about generating novel and or useful products or ideas (Kaufman & Sternberg, 2019; Runco & Jaeger, 2012). In education psychology,
Plucker et al. (2004) further defined creativity as “the interaction among aptitude, process, and environment by which an individual or
group produces a perceptible product that is both novel and useful as defined within a social context” (p. 90), which was adopted by the
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022 (OECD, 2023). It is believed that creativity plays an essential role in
determining students’ academic achievement, well-being, and career success (Acar et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2015; Egan et al., 2017;
Gajda, 2016). For these reasons, creativity has been deemed a highly desirable general competence in the 21st century and attracted

increasing research attention (OECD, 2024b).
One strand of research on creativity focuses on the resources involved in students’ creativity competence development (Benedek,
2024; van der Zanden et al., 2020). Research on the resources of creativity competence mainly focused on two lines: individual
characteristics and environmental characteristics (Benedek, 2024; Sternberg, 2006).
Individual-level factors include students’ cognitive abilities (e.g., intelligence, knowledge, divergent thinking, associative thinking,
and metacognition), personality traits (e.g., openness to experience, risk-taking), and motivational factors (e.g., creative self-efficacy)
(Benedek, 2024; Sternberg, 2006; van der Zanden et al., 2020). Another promising line of studies attends to the mutual relationships
between learning and creativity, specifically, academic achievement and creativity (e.g., Bicer et al., 2020; Gajda, 2016; Gajda et al.,
2017; Karwowski et al., 2020). This cohort of researchers is interested in the role of prior knowledge and experience in creativity, the
ways that creative insights are converted into new understandings and acquisition, and the socio-situational factors that influence
individuals’ creative learning (Karwowski et al., 2020). However, these studies have mostly focused on the association between
creativity competence and general academic achievement (e.g., Acar et al., 2022; Gajda, 2016; Gajda et al., 2017). The role of specific
literacy such as reading literacy (or skill) in creativity competence development is understudied, with a few exceptions (e.g., Ritchie
et al., 2013; Segundo Marcos et al., 2020; Wang, 2012). Given the unique educational emphasis on reading in Hong Kong, there is a
critical need to investigate whether superior reading literacy or skill (interchangeablly, reading hereafter) predicts students’ creativity
competence.
Apart from individual creativity, many researchers have emphasized the importance of collective creativity competence achieved
by individuals working together in a group (Acar et al., 2023; Amabile & Pratt, 2016; Hargadon & Bechky, 2006). While the study of
collective creativity has a tradition in organizational and business management (e.g., Amabile & Pratt, 2016; Woodman et al., 1993),
its application has expanded significantly into engineering (Ciachorowski & Lis, 2022), computer science (Shiiku et al., 2025), and
educational contexts (Timotheou & Ioannou, 2021), highlighting the need for specific academic skills such as reading literacy in
promoting general creativity competence.
Aside from individual internal resources, a substantial body of study has documented the role of environmental factors that may
exert an influence on students’ creativity, such as students’ social and economic status (SES) (Acar et al., 2022; Alcock et al., 2024),
school type (Castillo-Vergara et al., 2018), school resources (Huang et al., 2021), and teacher support (Hong & Song, 2020). Among
them, SES is regarded as one of the most important environmental factors for creativity. However, attention has been mostly paid to the
individual-level SES-creativity link, ignoring the effect of school SES on the collective creativity at the school level. Given the above
gaps, the current study aimed to utilize a multilevel lens to explore the reading-creativity and SES-creativity link at both the individual
and school levels.
The study aimed to explore the link between reading skill and general creativity competence at the student and school levels by
considering two covariate variables (i.e., gender at the student level and SES at student and school levels). To achieve this goal, we used
the Hong Kong sample of PISA 2022 (OECD, 2023) conducted by the Organization of Economic Co-operation and Development
(OECD). Specifically, we addressed three questions:
• Question 1. To what extent does reading literacy predict student creativity competence?
• Question 2. To what extent does school mean of reading literacy predict school collective creativity competence?
• Question 3. To what extent do the covariate variables (i.e., gender at the student level and SES at student and school levels) predict
school collective creativity competence?
The objectives of these three questions are (1) to examine the unique predictive effect of student-level reading literacy on studentlevel creativity scores among Hong Kong adolescents; (2) to determine the school-level effect of aggregated school mean of reading
literacy on aggregated school creativity scores; and (3) to quantify the predictive effects of gender and SES at the student level and SES
on creativity competence at both student and school levels.
2. Literature
2.1. Reading and creativity
Creativity involves two key processes (Goldschmidt, 2016). Divergent thinking generates multiple ideas, measured by fluency,
flexibility, originality, and elaboration (Runco & Acar, 2019). Convergent thinking evaluates and synthesizes information to find
optimal solutions (Cropley, 2006). Creative individuals use broad knowledge to generate original ideas and critically refine them to
improve usefulness and originality (Cropley, 2006; Guentulle et al., 2024).
Several arguments can explain the link between reading literacy and creativity competence. First, reading is one of the primary
approaches to gain and create knowledge (van den Broek & Kendeou, 2024), which plays a solid foundation for the generation of
creative ideas or solutions (Amabile, 1983; Kenett, 2023; Sternberg, 2006). According to the investment theory of creativity
(Sternberg, 2006), creative people are those who ‘buy low’ (pursue potentially valuable ideas that others overlook) and ‘sell high’
(move on when those ideas are established). This would require creative individuals to invest heavily to acquire domain expertise and
knowledge (Sternberg, 2006), skills largely cultivated through deep engagement in reading (OECD, 2019).
Second, according to the componential model of creativity (Amabile, 1983; Amabile & Pratt, 2016), creative ideas originate, in
part, from prior knowledge, expertise, and experience. Individuals with high reading literacy tend to have a broad base of knowledge,
including factual knowledge, conceptual knowledge, and experiential knowledge (Hattan & Kendeou, 2024). Competent readers

combine their preexisting knowledge with the information of the texts to accumulate and create new knowledge (Hattan & Kendeou,
2024; van den Broek & Kendeou, 2024). The knowledge and experience afforded by the process of reading may increase the diversity of
mental representations required by creative thinking (Ritchie et al., 2013).
Third, cognitive-based theories of reading emphasize that reading is a cognitively complex task (Hattan & Kendeou, 2024). Reading
is defined as the competence of understanding, using, evaluating, and reflecting on and engaging in texts to develop one’s knowledge
and potential (OECD, 2019). A series of cognitive skills are involved in reading, ranging from lower-order thinking skills (e.g., understanding, synthesizing, and applying) (Chen et al., 2022) to higher-order thinking skills, such as evaluating and reflecting (Cai &
Chen, 2022). The cognitive skills involved in reading, as mentioned above, are crucial for creative minds (Cropley, 2006). According to
Bloom’s taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001), creative thinking is conceptualized as the ability to integrate information in an
unconventional way to form a new pattern and propose alternative solutions. It ranks highest among other thinking skills of understanding, synthesizing, and evaluating (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001). Creative people can connect different or even conflicting information to create new ideas and evaluate the appropriateness and usefulness of solutions or ideas based on their knowledge and
experience (Cropley, 2006; Lubart, 2016). Therefore, the cognitive mechanism underlying reading shares many characteristics with
that of creativity (Wang, 2012).
Empirical studies provide evidence for the positive link between reading and creativity (e.g., Ritchie et al., 2013; Segundo Marcos
et al., 2020; Wang, 2012). For example, Ritchie et al. (2013) explored the association between reading ability and creativity. Drawing
on 855 participants in Australia, the results of regression models indicated that reading ability is positively linked to creativity after
controlling for the effects of gender, intelligence quotient, and age. In another quantitative study, Wang (2012) investigated the
relationship between attitudes toward reading/writing and creativity. Participants included 191 university students. The results of the
correlation indicated that three dimensions of creativity (i.e., fluency, elaboration, and flexibility) are positively associated with
enjoyment of reading/writing and hours spent on reading/writing. The study suggested that students who were more emotionally and
behaviorally engaged in reading showed higher performance in creativity tests.
The facilitating role of reading ability in creativity was also observed in an experimental study (Segundo Marcos et al., 2020).
Segundo Marcos et al. (2020) investigated whether students’ creative thinking can be enhanced through reading activities in cooperative learning classrooms. Sixty fifth-grade students from a primary school in southern Spain participated over two months: half
engaged in reading activities within a cooperative learning environment, while the other half followed the standard fifth-grade reading
curriculum. The results demonstrated that after the intervention of the reading program, there was an improvement in the creativity
tests of each participant in both groups.
2.2. The relationship between school mean of reading literacy and school collective creativity competence
Creativity manifests not only as individual creativity but also as group creativity, which represents the emergence of creativity in a
group (Acar et al., 2023; Hargadon & Bechky, 2006). The literature on group creativity documents a set of correlates beyond the
individual level (Acar et al., 2023; Amabile & Pratt, 2016; Woodman et al., 1993). Amabile and Pratt (2016) identify individual and
group-level components of creativity in the dynamic componential model of creativity. Group-level components include organizational motivation to innovate, resources in the task domain (e.g., finance, people with sufficient expertise), and skills in management
innovation. Woodman et al. (1993) argued that group creativity is a function of the creativity of individuals. Group creativity is
influenced by individual characteristics in the group (e.g., cognitive abilities, knowledge, personality traits, and intrinsic motivation),
group characteristics (e.g., diversity, size, norms, and cohesiveness), and organizational level characteristics (e.g., culture, resources,
and technology). The two models emphasized the important roles of individual characteristics constituting a group and collective
knowledge in group creativity (Acar et al., 2023).
The two models above, however, are primarily proposed in the realm of business administration. In education, scholars in creativity
research recently have paid increasing attention to collective creativity (Freund & Holling, 2008; Hong & Song, 2020; Levenson,
2011), or innovation skills aggregated at the group level (Amabile & Pratt, 2016; Woodman et al., 1993). For example, based on the
dynamic componential model (Amabile & Pratt, 2016), Hong and Song (2020) conceptualized classroom science creativity at both
individual and collective levels. The scholars investigated components that may exert an influence on students’ individual science
creativity and collective classroom creativity from the dimensions of students’ and environmental characteristics (Hong & Song,
2020). The students’ characteristics involve disciplinary knowledge, thinking styles (e.g., associated thinking, divergent thinking, and
convergent thinking), and cognitive engagement (e.g., understanding and applying), whereas environmental characteristics include
teacher support, school climate, and school physical environment (e.g., resources). This model emphasizes the importance of students’
characteristics, especially the cognitive abilities of students and environmental factors in students’ collective creativity.
However, the existing literature on collective creativity in education mostly focuses on students’ cognitive and affective characteristics and environmental factors, leaving reading, a literacy deeply intertwined with the cognitive process of creativity, downplayed.
The present study not only explored the role of reading literacy in creativity at the individual level but also the link between reading
literacy and creativity at the collective school level.
2.3. SES, gender, and creativity competence
Creativity is not only determined by individual characteristics (e.g., knowledge and cognitive skills) but also by environmental
factors (Benedek, 2024). It is an individual’s interaction with the contextual environment that determines the generation of creative
ideas and solutions (Amabile, 2018; Glăveanu, 2020). Many models of creativity emphasize that social context plays a crucial role in

shaping one’s creative mind and behavior (Amabile & Pratt, 2016; Glăveanu, 2011, 2020). The literature on creativity has identified an
array of external factors influencing creativity, including social and economic status (SES) (Acar et al., 2022), parental responsiveness
(Zhang et al., 2020), and cultural climates (e.g., individualism; Guo et al., 2023). Among these environmental factors, SES is regarded
as one of the most important contextual factors for students.
It is well established in the literature that socially and economically advantaged students outperform their peers in creative
thinking (e.g., Acar et al., 2022; Castillo-Vergara et al., 2018; OECD, 2024a). Socioeconomically advantaged students have access to
resources (e.g., books, artwork), intellectual exchange, and activities (e.g., creative writing programs), making it easier to make
connections between different ideas and domains to create novel and useful ideas and products (Acar et al., 2022). The positive association between SES and creativity has gained empirical support (e.g., Acar et al., 2022). For example, a meta-analytic investigation
by Acar et al. (2022) shows that SES has a small positive correlation with creativity (r = 0.12), moderated by creativity assessment, SES
indicators, and sample and study characteristics. In addition, the literature shows that SES is positively associated with factors that are
conducive to creativity, such as creative self-efficacy (OECD, 2024b), personality traits (e.g., openness to experience) (Zhang et al.,
2018), intelligence (Alcock et al., 2024), and cognitive intrinsic motivation (Dai et al., 2012).
Nevertheless, a few studies have documented the negative SES-creativity link. For example, Dahlman et al. (2013) found that street
children from low social and economic backgrounds showed higher divergent thinking and creative problem-solving than children
from low-SES families. Acar et al. (2022) argued that the negative link was in line with the idea of asynchronicity that creativity may be
enhanced by tension, discrepancy, and misfit (Acar, 2020). The mixed findings regarding the relationship between SES and creativity
necessitate further examination in different contexts and with diversified data, such as large-scale cross-sectional and longitudinal data
(Acar et al., 2022).
School SES, the average school-level SES, has long been regarded as an important factor for educational outcomes (e.g., Alwin &
Otto, 1977; Marsh et al., 2023). High-SES schools (i.e., schools with high average SES) provide access to more resources, such as
diversifying classes and activities, professional teachers, and technologies that stimulate students’ creative thinking (OECD, 2024b). In
this way, high school SES promotes the average level of creativity for the school. Conversely, socioeconomically disadvantaged students tend to attend under-resourced schools where the curricular focus often sidelines creative activities, leading to lower performance in creative thinking (OECD, 2024a). Examining the school SES and school creativity can shed light on the ecological settings of
school creativity.
Numerous studies (e.g., Abdulla Alabbasi et al., 2022; Cai & Jiang, 2024; Cai et al., 2023) have reported that there is a gender effect
on reading and creativity. These studies have revealed that females outperform males in reading (Cai & Jiang, 2024; Cai & Lin, 2024)
and creativity (Abdulla Alabbasi et al., 2022). Thus, gender is included as a covariate at the student level.
3. Method
3.1. Data and procedure
The current study used the Hong Kong sample of the OECD PISA 2022 (OECD, 2023). PISA has been conducted by OECD since 2000
on a triennial cycle (with PISA 2022 delayed due to the pandemic) to measure the cognitive skills of reading, mathematics, and science
of 15-year-olds globally. In 2022, a test of creativity was added to the PISA package. PISA 2022 data are available from the OECD
website (https://www.oecd.org/en/data/datasets/pisa-2022-database/). For our study, we used student response data on creativity,
reading, gender, and socioeconomic status (SES). Participants involved 5557 adolescents (Mage = 15.81, SD = 0.29, female = 49 %)
nested in 163 schools in Hong Kong. Besides, we used students’ gender information and their family socio-economic status composite
score. School data on SES, reading, and creativity were aggregated from the student-level data.
Measures
Student and school mean of reading literacy. PISA 2018 labels reading as reading literacy and defines it as the ‘understanding,
using, evaluating, reflecting on and engaging with texts in order to achieve one’s goals, to develop one’s knowledge and potential and
to participate in society’ (OECD, 2019, p. 28). The reading test was designed as a scenario-based assessment in which students are
given particular goals within a context. Each scenario consists of one or several tasks that measure one or several of the following
cognitive processes: locating information, understanding (literal and inferential meaning), and evaluating and reflecting. The reading
texts included continuous (organized in sentences and paragraphs) and non-continuous texts (e.g., lists, forms), single- and
multiple-source texts, static and dynamic texts, and mixed texts. Besides, since PISA 2018, PISA reading test has been updated to
integrate traditional reading and digital reading (OECD, 2023). PISA 2022 adapted this framework of reading assessment (OECD,
2023). PISA 2022 did not provide actual scores but 10 plausible values for student reading. For our study, we used all ten plausible
values to compute our two-level path model and reported the average of the statistics. These student reading scores were aggregated to
the school level to represent school mean of reading literacy. This same approach has been applied to previous studies (e.g., King et al.,
2021).
Student and school creativity competence. The PISA 2022 creativity test focuses on adolescents’ malleable processes of creative
thinking capacity developed in school. It is not intended to identify exceptional individuals, but to provide international comparisons
on how creative thinking relates to teaching and school activities. The objective is to generate findings with implications for education
pedagogy and policy (OECD, 2023). The PISA 2022 creativity test decomposes creativity into three facets (i.e., generating diverse
ideas, generating creative ideas, and evaluating and improving ideas) specified in four contextual domains (i.e., written expression,

visual expression, social problem solving, and scientific problem solving). All creativity test tasks are open-ended, which allows infinite
ways for students to demonstrate their creativity (OECD, 2023). PISA 2022 released only a single aggregate score for student creativity,
with 10 alternative plausible values representing this score (Barbot & Kaufman, 2025). We used all plausible values in our multilevel
analysis. Student creativity was aggregated to the school level to represent school creativity. Conceptually, this school creativity
corresponds to the group-level component of skills in innovation in the dynamic componential model of creativity (Amabile & Pratt,
2016) and the function of individual creativity aggregated at the school level in the interactionist model (Woodman et al., 1993).
Student and school SES. Student SES was measured through an index called economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS) in the
PISA database (OECD, 2024c). This index measures students’ responses regarding their home possessions, parents' highest occupational status, and parents' highest level of education. Likewise, student SES was aggregated to the school level to represent school SES.
Data analysis
As students were nested within schools, we used multilevel modelling to analyze the data. Before primary data analysis, cases with
missing data on the two main variables (i.e., reading and creativity) were dropped from our analysis. The analysis dataset only
contained 0.9 % missing values on SES. The missing values were processed using the full information maximum likelihood method
built in Mplus 8.11 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2023).
The multilevel model was run in three steps. First, we assessed the appropriateness of using multilevel modeling by examining the
intra-class correlation (ICC) of the key study variables across schools (Barcikowski, 1981). The ICC values of reading, creativity, and
SES were 0.32, 0.23, and 0.26, respectively. They are all larger than zero and suggest the need for multilevel analysis (Heck & Thomas,
2015). Second, a two-level path model with school as the cluster variable was constructed. This modeling approach, also known as
multilevel modeling (Heck & Thomas, 2020), is utilized for nested data structures (students within schools) as it allows for the
simultaneous estimation of predictive relationships at the within-group (student) level and the between-group (school) level. In our
case, we used reading, SES, and gender to predict creativity at the student level, and school mean of reading and school SES to predict
school creativity at the school level.
To evaluate the model-data fit, we used the following criteria recommended by quantitative methodologists for model evaluation
for structural equation models (Mueller & Hancock, 2019): Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA; Steiger, 1990),
Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR; Jöreskog & Sörbom, 1981), comparative Fit Index (CFI; Bentler, 1990), and
Tucker–Lewis index (TLI; Tucker & Lewis, 1973). A model with RMSEA and SRMR no larger than 0.05 and with CFI and TLI no smaller
than 0.95 was considered to have a good fit (Mueller & Hancock, 2019).
When interpreting effect sizes, we refer to Hattie’s (2009) criteria of 0.05, 0.15, and 0.24 as references for small, medium, and large
predictive effects.
4. Results
4.1. Correlations
Table 1 shows the correlations among the study variables at both the student and school levels. At both the student and school level,
reading was positively associated with creativity and SES. Besides, gender was negatively associated with both reading and creativity
at the student level.
4.2. Results of the two-level path analysis
The two-level path modeling yielded an excellent model-data fit: χ2/df = 0.046/1, p = .833, RMSEA = 0.000, CFI = 1.000, TLI =
1.000, SRMR (within/between) = 0.001/0.000.
Fig. 1 shows the standardized estimates of the path coefficients. At the student level, student creativity was positively predicted by
reading (β = 0.44, p < .01) and SES (β = 0.04, p < .01). Gender (0 = females, 1 = males) negatively predicted creativity (β = − 0.12, p <
.01) and reading (β = − 0.08, p < .01). Student SES had a non-significant relation to student reading (β = 0.01, p = .72).
At the school level, school creativity was positively predicted by school mean of reading (β = 0.74, p < .01) and school SES (β =
0.31, p < .01). Besides, school SES positively predicted school mean of reading (β = 0.60, p < .01).

5. Discussion
5.1. Reading literacy and creativity competence
The present study showed that student reading literacy was a significant predictor of student creativity. This finding is consistent
with existing studies (Ritchie et al., 2013; Segundo Marcos et al., 2020; Wang, 2012). This finding resounds with theories of creativity
that emphasize the importance of cognitive architecture underlying creativity (Amabile, 2018; Sternberg, 2006) and confirms the
complementary relation between learning and creativity (Beghetto, 2021; Karwowski et al., 2020). This finding verifies the essential
role of reading literacy in the cultivation of creativity. It also echoes Bloom’s taxonomy of thinking skills that creative thinking skill is
closely associated with other essential thinking skills in reading, namely, understanding, synthesizing, and evaluating (Anderson &
Krathwohl, 2001; Cai & Chen, 2022; Cai & Cheung, 2021)
The reading-creativity association suggests that the cognitive mechanism of creativity may be activated in the process of reading,
making it easier and more efficient for readers to generate creative ideas. This implies that when learners perform a reading task, they
would deal with mixed texts that present different or even conflicting perspectives and information (Cai et al., 2024). The positive
reading-creativity association suggests that successful readers are capable of integrating divergent perspectives located in different
pieces of text, making inferences based on their existing knowledge, and engaging in evaluation processes to handle the conflict
(Perfetti et al., 1999). The divergent perspectives and experiences in reading may stimulate students’ divergent thinking by providing
them with diverse directions; the ability to synthesize may allow them to make connections between different directions; and evaluating skill enables them to judge the appropriateness of ideas (Cropley, 2006; Reche & Perfectti, 2020).
5.2. School mean of reading literacy and school collective creativity competence
The study showed that there was a positive link between school mean of reading literacy and school creativity and that the effect of
this association was larger than that at the student level. This finding makes additional contributions to the literature by suggesting
that school-level reading literacy plays a crucial role in school creativity. That is to say, the cognitive abilities and knowledge level of
members in a group matter in the group level of creativity (Acar et al., 2023; Amabile & Pratt, 2016). Higher reading literacy, to a large
extent, represents a higher level of thinking skills, learning abilities, and knowledge base (Artelt et al., 2001). Students who are
surrounded by peers with higher learning abilities and knowledge levels tend to have more intellectual exchanges, thus stimulating
their cognitive flexibility and divergent thinking. When they seek help from peers, they tend to receive more intellectual feedback to
solve problems creatively. Their interaction enables the recombination of ideas, leading to the emergence of diverse and novel ideas
and solutions, thus proliferating collective creativity at the group level (Levenson, 2011; Reche & Perfectti, 2020). This finding also
suggests that students with higher reading literacy tend to exhibit higher creativity when they are surrounded by peers with high
reading literacy. Our finding also confirms the argument of person-environment fit theory that a good fit between student ability and
school environment enhances positive outcomes (King et al., 2021).

5.3. SES, gender, and creativity competence
Our study found that covariates at both student (student SES and gender) and school level (school SES) significantly predicted
creativity. At the student level, students’ SES had a small positive association with creativity. This is in line with the existing work
(Acar et al., 2022; Castillo-Vergara et al., 2018; Dai et al., 2012). This finding echoes the family investment model (Sohr-Preston et al.,
2013), which posits that parents’ investment in their children was influenced by their social and economic status and the investment
plays an important role in shaping their cognitive development (Conger & Donnellan, 2007). Socio-economically advantaged students
have access to more educational and social resources, such as different genres and types of reading materials, cutting-edge technology,
opportunities to engage in creative activities, and intellectual exchange (Conger & Donnellan, 2007). The affordance of rich educational and social resources broadens students’ knowledge and diversifies their experiences (Xu & Pang, 2020), thereby making them
more cognitively flexible to generate creative ideas and products (Ritter et al., 2012).
Besides, well-educated parents tend to cultivate their children’s self-confidence in their creative potential and encourage their
flexibility and imagination (Karwowski, 2011). Therefore, students from higher SES tend to show more confidence in their creative
potential with the belief that their creativity can be developed (OECD, 2024a). The attitudes increase their willingness to engage in
creative activities and achieve higher scores in creativity assessment (Dai et al., 2012).
Thirdly, a notable finding in the present study is that student SES makes no difference in students’ reading literacy. The nonsignificant association between SES and reading achievement in Hong Kong is consistent with the existing studies (e.g., Lee & Manzon, 2014). We argue that the effect of SES widely acknowledged in the literature should have been aggregated to the school level, an
issue to be discussed in our later text. Last but not least, gender negatively predicted both creativity and reading, suggesting females
had higher creativity and reading scores than males. This aligns with the literature of reading (Cai & Jiang, 2024; Cai & Lin, 2024) and
creativity (Abdulla Alabbasi et al., 2022).
At the school level, we found that school SES is a significant predictor of school creativity. The finding contributes to the literature
on creativity by highlighting school SES as an important environmental component for school creativity. It aligns with the OECD
report, which finds that students attending private schools with high SES perform better in creative assessment than those attending
public schools with low SES in most countries (OECD, 2024). This is also true in Hong Kong, where high school segregation exists (Zhou
et al., 2016). Students from advantaged backgrounds tend to attend private elite schools where students have access to excellent
teachers and a diverse set of activities, such as art classes, music, computer programs, and creative writing classes. These benefits are
not only conducive to students’ creativity but also to school creativity (Hong & Song, 2020).
Moreover, an interesting and notable finding in the present study is that school-level SES not only directly predicts school creativity
but also exerts an influence on school-level reading literacy, in contrast to the non-significant SES-reading association at the student
level. This finding confirms the positive compositional effect of school SES on students’ academic achievements (Marsh et al., 2023;
Yeung et al., 2022).
In the end, we must admit that the relationship between reading literacy and creativity competence might display heterogeneity in
specific domains of written expression, visual expression, social problem solving, and scientific problem solving, possibly with a
stronger association with the domain of written expression than other domains like visual expression. However, this study used the
overall composite score of creativity provided by PISA 2022. This decision to model the relationship between general reading literacy
and general creativity competence at the school level prioritizes establishing a high-level understanding of this association beyond the
student level across educational contexts. This general understanding can serve as a necessary foundation for future researchers to
disaggregate the relationship at the student level across specific contextual domains of creativity competence and in specific educational systems.
6. Conclusion, limitations, and implications
The present study showed that poor reading literacy and disadvantaged social-economic status prevent both students and schools
from demonstrating the potential of creativity and that females demonstrated higher creativity at the student level. Our study not only
reveals the significance of individual-level factors (i.e., student SES and reading literacy) on creativity but also underscores the roles of
school environment, namely, school SES and school average reading literacy in nurturing school creativity.
This study bears a few limitations. First, although the cross-sectional design adopted in this study is based on a sound theory of
creativity competence, it cannot fully imply the causal relationship between SES, reading, and creativity competence. Future studies
can apply experimental designs to derive causal inferences. Second, the population of the study is based on Hong Kong secondary
school students. The generalization of our findings to other contexts is cautioned. Specifically, differences in cultures between Hong
Kong and other Western countries should render different the relationship between reading literacy and creativity competence. For
instance, studies have shown that the cognitive (Cai & Zhu, 2017) and motivational processes (Li et al., 2024) are different between
students in Eastern and Western countries. Moreover, a large-scale cross-national study indicated between-country variability in the
effect of SES on creativity performance (Goecke et al., 2025). Future studies need to examine the reading-creativity relationship by
accounting for the predictors of reading and creativity across different contextual and cultural contexts. Third, the present study did
not consider the effects of other individual factors (e.g., motivational variables like creative self-efficacy) and school factors (e.g.,
school climate, teacher support, etc.). Further research could explore the effect of these additional factors.
Despite the limitations, the present study has some significant implications. Theoretically, the study indicates that family SES and
reading literacy are two positive predictors of creativity competence, demonstrating the important roles of socio-economic variables
and reading literacy in creativity research, which has traditionally focused more on the cognitive and motivational contributors of

creativity competence. Moreover, the study uses multilevel modeling to identify the role of SES and reading literacy in creativity at the
student and school levels, providing implications for the ecological development of creative schools.
The study also has policy and practical implications. Given the direct effect of family SES on creativity, low-SES families cannot
provide children with resources such as access to diverse genres of books, multicultural experiences, and extracurricular activities that
stimulate creativity (Alcock et al., 2024; van der Zanden et al., 2020). Policymakers should ensure equal access to these resources for
low-SES students by investing in public libraries and after-school programs. Given the large effect of school SES on school creativity,
schools in low-SES communities should be supported with more financial investment, and scholarships should be provided to students
from low-SES families.
Pedagogically, our study provides implications for educators on creativity intervention to integrate reading into the program of
creativity. For instance, schools may increase the time allocated to reading classes and promote reading in interdisciplinary subjects to
cultivate students’ creativity competence. Besides, our study also provides insight into the instructional design of reading classes. To
cultivate creative thinking, reading classes should focus not merely on the linguistic aspects of texts but also on the thinking skills of
reading.
