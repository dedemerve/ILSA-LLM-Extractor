A B S T R A C T  
Student perceptions are rarely used to compare teaching quality across countries due to the challenge of ensuring 
a comparable construct or achieving exact measurement invariance. The present study aims to evaluate the 
extent to which student perceptions of teaching quality can be used for valid cross-national comparisons. To this 
end, the study aims to overcome challenges associated with traditional measurement invariance by applying a 
novel method – alignment optimization. Based on data from 38 countries from the 2019 Trends in Mathematics 
and Science Study (TIMSS), our results showed limited comparability across countries with variation between 
behavioral management and instructional clarity scales. We review potential causes of non-comparability using 
the estimated mean scores. Implications for further research are discussed.   
1. Introduction 
Teaching quality is among the most effective indicators for 
enhancing student learning outcomes (Seidel & Shavelson, 2007). 
Studying teaching quality cross-nationally can offer insights into its use 
and variation across diverse educational systems, cultures, and world 
regions — hereafter referred to as “countries” (Phillips & Schweisfurth, 
2006). Such insights are instrumental in guiding educational reform and 
development within these countries and can be used to set an interna­
tional benchmark by which countries can compare themselves (Alex­
ander et al., 2000; Phillips & Schweisfurth, 2006). However, bias can be 
introduced at different stages of the study, which challenges the validity 
of cross-national comparisons (van de Vijver & Leung, 1997/2021; He 
et al., 2022). For example, in the sampling, through characteristics of the 
instrument, or the type of analysis employed. Additional research is 
needed to identify cross-national differences and similarities in teaching 
quality and, consequently, to compare and learn from findings across 
countries. 
International Large-Scale Assessments (ILSAs) — such as the Trends 
in Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) and the Programme for In­
ternational Student Assessment (PISA) — offer unique opportunities for 
cross-national comparisons of teaching quality. First and foremost, 
ILSAs have increasingly included items to assess multiple aspects of 
teaching quality in their contextual questionnaires (Klieme & Nilsen, 
2022). Moreover, they (1) assess those aspects across a considerable 
number of countries, (2) use identical items to assess constructs across 
all countries, (3) provide large nationally representative samples, and 
(4) conduct rigorous quality control procedures to ensure consistency 
across countries, for example in translating, scaling, and linking the 
items (Klieme, 2020; Klieme & Nilsen, 2022; Strietholt & Scherer, 
2018). With these extensive procedures, ILSAs have the potential to 
minimize bias and are thus uniquely placed to investigate teaching 
quality cross-nationally. However, ILSAs rely on contextual question­
naires to assess teaching quality. A fundamental challenge of using 
questionnaires for comparative research is establishing their compara­
bility across countries (He et al., 2022; van de Vijver & Leung, 
1997/2021). Furthermore, despite the meticulous design of ILSAs to 
minimize bias in comparative research, bias cannot be ruled out (He 
et al., 2022). 
An important approach to investigate whether and at which level 
valid cross-national comparisons can be made is by testing measurement 
invariance (Davidov, Muthen, et al., 2018; He et al., 2022). Traditionally, 
the extent to which a questionnaire is measurement invariant and thus 
allows for a valid group comparison is examined by gradually imposing 
equality constraints on the measurement structure across the countries 
being compared (Davidov, Schmidt, et al., 2018). This procedure is often

referred to as the traditional or exact approach. However, exact invari­
ance is considered an unattainable ideal that is hardly ever reached, 
especially for the level of invariance required to compare mean scores of 
latent constructs across a large number of culturally and linguistically 
diverse countries (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014; He et al., 2022; Marsh 
et al., 2018; van de Vijver et al., 2019). As a result, most comparative 
studies using ILSA data have either (1) refrained from comparing mean 
scores across countries, (2) only included a small number of culturally 
similar countries, (3) used single-item measures that are not appropriate 
for assessing measurement invariance and do not account for measure­
ment error, or (4) simply ignored possible non-invariance (Avvisati 
et al., 2019; He et al., 2022; Klieme & Nilsen, 2022). With this severe 
limitation in mind, novel approaches to measurement invariance have 
emerged, including the alignment method (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 
2014). The alignment method is an approach of approximate invariance, 
which, instead of requiring parameters to be identical across groups — 
as is done in the exact approach — allows for some flexibility (Muth´en & 
Asparouhov, 2018). In doing so, the alignment method aims to acquire 
comparable and meaningful mean scores, even in the absence of exact 
invariance (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014; Davidov, Muthen, et al., 
2018). 
The present study aims to examine the cross-national comparability 
of student perceptions of teaching quality. Measurement invariance 
analysis will be utilized to investigate the extent to which valid cross- 
national comparisons can be made across 38 countries that partici­
pated in TIMSS 2019. Moreover, the present study aims to acquire 
comparable mean scores by including a more flexible approach to 
measurement invariance — the alignment method. Thereby, the study 
tries to overcome the current limitations of exact invariance. Finally, 
this study delves into understanding the extent of cross-national 
comparability in student perceptions of teaching quality and discusses 
possible factors influencing comparability. Based on the findings, rec­
ommendations to improve comparability are discussed. 
1.1. Teaching quality 
Teaching quality refers to the interactions among students and 
teachers that revolve around content and are intended to promote 
learning (Fenstermacher & Richardson, 2005; Biesta & Sengel, 2016). A 
variety of frameworks and theoretical models exist that conceptualize 
teaching quality. They vary in their aims, the types of instructional 
practice they cover, and their dimensionalities (Authors et al., 2021). 
One widely used framework in German-speaking countries is the three 
basic dimensions of teaching quality. This framework conceptualizes 
three dimensions of teaching quality: classroom management, support­
ive climate, and cognitive activation (Klieme et al., 2009). Classroom 
management focuses on identifying and enhancing desirable classroom 
behavior while preventing undesirable ones (Klieme and Nilsen, 2022; 
Kounin, 1970). Supportive climate refers to the interactions and re­
lationships — both among students and between students and teachers 
— in the classroom that facilitate positive learning experiences. Thus, it 
includes the extent to which everyone in the classroom respects each 
other, listens to each other, and supports each other (Klieme et al., 2009; 
Klieme & Nilsen, 2022). Cognitive activation pertains to instructional 
practices that stimulate students to engage in higher-level cognitive 
thinking (Klieme et al., 2009). All three dimensions are hypothesized to 
promote student learning outcomes (Klieme et al., 2009). 
The three basic dimensions framework provided the theoretical 
foundation for including measures of teaching quality in the TIMSS 2019 
study. Since the framework is not tied to a specific instrument, the 
TIMSS 2019 study developed its own operationalizations (Mullis & 
Martin, 2017; Praetorius et al., 2018). More specifically, the TIMSS 2019 
international student questionnaire included items measuring aspects of 
two dimensions: behavioral management as an aspect of classroom man­
agement and instructional clarity as an aspect of a supportive climate 
(Klieme & Nilsen, 2022; Mullis & Martin, 2017). 
Behavioral management — often referred to as disciplinary climate — 
is an essential aspect of classroom management and revolves around 
strategies teachers apply to handle disciplinary problems and deal with 
disruptions (Emmer & Stough, 2001; Kounin, 1970). It is hypothesized 
that classrooms with fewer disciplinary problems provide more oppor­
tunities for teachers to engage students cognitively and for students to 
engage with the learning content, leading to improved student learning 
outcomes (Klieme et al., 2009; Lipowsky et al., 2009). 
Instructional clarity concerns the teachers’ ability to increase stu­
dents’ understanding and resolve confusion, especially when students 
have difficulty grasping the content (Ferguson & Danielson, 2015; 
Rowley et al., 2019). It also refers to teacher clarity in providing explicit 
instruction, explaining learning goals, asking straightforward questions, 
and answering students’ questions (Neumann et al., 2012). 
1.2. Using student questionnaires to assess teaching quality 
Student questionnaires are commonly used to assess teaching quality 
by acquiring students’ perceptions of teacher or classroom behavior 
(Seidel & Shavelson, 2007). This measure is widely used in ILSAs, which 
have increasingly included items measuring teaching quality in their 
student questionnaires (Klieme & Nilsen, 2022). 
The widespread popularity of using student questionnaires to assess 
teaching quality might partly be explained by their easy and low-cost 
implementation (De Jong & Westerhof, 2001). Additionally, students 
play a central role in the teaching and learning process and experience 
the classroom on a daily basis (G¨ollner et al., 2021; Maulana & Helm­
s-Lorenz, 2016). On the other hand, student ratings might also be subject 
to bias: factors affecting the ratings that are unrelated to any criteria of 
good teaching (Centra & Gaubatz, 2000). Some well-known biasing 
factors include student characteristics, such as motivation and 
achievement (Atlay et al., 2019; Bijlsma et al., 2022; Fauth, Wagner, 
et al., 2020) or teacher characteristics, like popularity (Fauth et al., 
2014). Despite this limitation, student perceptions of teaching quality 
provide valuable and useful information on classroom processes and are 
considered well-suited as an alternative, or complementary, to teacher 
self-reports or observations (Rollett et al., 2021). 
When using student ratings to assess teaching quality, a major 
challenge comes with choosing the appropriate level of analysis (Lüdtke 
et al., 2009). Given that students are clustered within classrooms, two 
different levels of analysis can be considered: (1) the student level, which 
focuses on individual student ratings, and (2) the classroom level, which 
entails aggregating individual student ratings to provide a shared 
perspective of teaching quality in the classroom (Lüdtke et al., 2009). 
Which level is chosen for the analyses depends first and foremost on the 
nature of the data (Marsh et al., 2012). The TIMSS data — which is used 
in the current research — are collected in two stages: Schools are 
sampled in the first stage, and intact classrooms within the schools are 
sampled in the second stage (see LaRoche, Joncas & Foy, 2020). This 
two-stage sample design allows the aggregation of student ratings to the 
classroom level. Whether the classroom level should then be used as the 
main level of analysis depends on the research question under investi­
gation. More specifically, if the research interest is studying differences 
between students or differences between classrooms (Marsh et al., 
2012). The current research aims to evaluate whether individual stu­
dents perceive teaching quality differently when situated in varying 
cultural contexts. Therefore, the appropriate level of analysis for the 
current research is the individual student level. 
1.3. Cross-national comparability of teaching quality 
Theoretical concepts derived from contextual questionnaires — such 
as student perceptions of teaching quality — can be highly specific to 
culture and context (He & van de Vijver, 2013). For example, linguistic 
variations in the questionnaires and general cultural differences might 
shape how students understand or respond to these concepts (Avvisati

et al., 2019; He et al., 2019). Therefore, there is a growing concern about 
the ability to make cross-national comparisons of latent constructs, 
especially across a large number of culturally and linguistically diverse 
countries (Rutkowski & Rutkowski, 2018; Van de Vijver et al., 2019). 
Two concepts play a key role in dealing with challenges of cross-national 
comparability, which are also relevant to ILSAs: the minimization of 
bias, and the evaluation of invariance (van de Vijver & Leung, 
1997/2021; He & van de Vijver, 2012). This current section will deal 
with the first, whereas the next section (1.4) will deal with the later. 
In comparative research, bias refers to the presence of factors that 
threaten the validity of cross-national comparisons (van de Vijver & 
Leung, 1997/2021). When biased, scores obtained from different 
countries do not reflect genuine differences across countries, thus 
jeopardizing the interpretation of score differences (He et al., 2022; Van 
de Vijver, 2018). Cross-cultural research has identified three sources of 
bias that challenge comparability: construct bias, method bias, and item 
bias (He & van de Vijver, 2013; Van de vijver & Leung, 1997/2021). 
Construct bias emerges when the construct has a different definition 
across countries (van de Vijver, 2018). This type of bias might be 
attributed to the exportation of constructs developed in a particular 
context being exported to a different one (van de Vijver & Leung, 
1997/2021). A case in point is the framework of three basic dimensions 
of teaching quality. This framework was initially developed in Germany 
but disseminated across countries worldwide through ILSA studies, like 
TIMSS and PISA. Nevertheless, construct bias is rare in ILSAs, largely 
due to their focus on universally applicable constructs (He et al., 2022). 
Moreover, latent constructs with cultural sensitivity usually do not pass 
the field trial, where they are meticulously evaluated (van de Vijver, 
2018). 
Method bias refers to bias introduced in the sampling, by the char­
acteristics of the instrument, or through the administration (van de 
Vijver & Leung, 1997/2021). Sampling bias is generally not a substantial 
concern in ILSAs due to careful sampling processes emphasizing 
cross-national comparability. However, a degree of “sampling” bias 
emerges at the country-level since the more affluent countries are often 
better represented (van de Vijver, 2018). Instrument bias is considered a 
main challenge when measuring latent constructs in ILSAs, particularly 
culture-specific response styles (van de Vijver & Leung, 1997/2021; He 
et al., 2022). Thus, such bias could be an issue when acquiring student 
ratings of teaching quality. 
Item bias occurs when an item of a measurement scale (e.g., an item 
measuring behavioral management) has a different meaning across 
countries (He et al., 2022). For example, when asking: “The students 
respect the teachers”, the notion of “respect for a teacher” might have 
different connotations for students in classrooms in China compared to 
students in classrooms in Norway. In ILSAs, item bias is often addressed 
through stringent processes, such as expert reviews, field trials, precise 
translation and adaption, and statistical procedures to identify item bias 
(van de Vijver, 2018). These processes are meant to ensure that the items 
are valid and comparable across diverse cultural contexts. 
Thus, ILSAs attempt to reduce bias and enhance cross-national 
comparability. However, despite the meticulous efforts of ILSAs, bias 
might still exist, influencing the extent to which valid cross-national 
comparisons can be made. Therefore, it is essential to test the extent 
to which the instrument used to assess teaching quality measures the 
same construct across countries — that is, the extent to which the 
measurement instrument is invariant (Davidov, Schmidt, et al., 2018). 
1.4. Using measurement invariance to assess cross-national comparability 
Measurement invariance is typically divided into configural, metric, 
and scalar levels. Each level entails certain parameter constraints to the 
measurement model. Configural invariance is established when the factor 
loadings of items measuring a latent variable inhibit a similar pattern 
across groups. Configural invariance indicates that the basic organiza­
tion of the construct is comparable across groups (Putnick & Bornstein, 
2016). Metric invariance is established when the factor loadings are 
invariant across groups, allowing for the comparisons of regression co­
efficients (Davidov et al., 2014; Millsap, 2011). Scalar invariance is 
established when — in addition to the factor loadings — the items in­
tercepts are invariant across groups and allows for the comparison of 
latent means (Millsap, 2011). Thus, the level of measurement invariance 
informs the extent to which we can make meaningful cross-national 
comparisons of relationships or means. 
Traditionally, measurement invariance analysis is conducted by 
employing Multi-Group Confirmatory Factor Analysis (MGCFA) while 
systematically restricting parameters to be exactly equal. The increas­
ingly restricted models are then compared regarding previously pro­
posed cut-off criteria for model fit indices (e.g., Chen, 2007; Cheung & 
Rensvold, 2002; Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014). This traditional approach 
has come under increased scrutiny as the requirements for scalar 
invariance are nearly impossible to meet (Marsh et al., 2018). As a 
result, several alternative approaches have been proposed (see Davidov, 
Schmidt, et al., 2018; van de Vijver et al., 2019, for an overview), 
including the alignment optimization method (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 
2014; Muth´en & Asparouhov, 2018). The alignment method is a rela­
tively novel MGCFA approach of approximate invariance that minimizes 
the total amount of non-invariance across groups and aims to extract 
trustworthy means and variance from the data by accounting for the 
non-invariance of parameters (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014; Cieciuch 
et al., 2018). This approach is especially suitable when comparing a 
large number of groups (Davidov, Muthen, et al., 2018). Moreover, 
whereas the traditional MGCFA provides little information about sour­
ces of measurement non-invariance (Avvisati et al., 2019), the output of 
the alignment analysis presents an overview of (non-)invariant factor 
loadings and intercepts for each item in every group (Muth´en & 
Asparouhov, 2018). The latter could help further scale development by 
indicating which items are more suitable for cross-country comparisons 
(Marsh et al., 2018). 
1.5. Previous empirical evidence 
Studies investigating measurement invariance of student perceptions 
of teaching quality across a variety of culturally diverse countries have 
rarely provided evidence supporting scalar invariance. For example, 
Scherer and Nilsen (2016) found metric, but not scalar invariance of 
student perceptions of instructional clarity across 50 countries partici­
pating in TIMSS 2011. More recently, Herbert et al. (2022) examined 
measurement invariance of four aspects of students’ perceived teaching 
quality. Similar to Scherer and Nilsen (2016), they found evidence of 
metric, but not scalar invariance across Colombia, England, Germany, 
Japan, Mexico, and Shanghai. Another notable international project 
used a previously validated questionnaire — called the My Teacher 
Questionnaire (MTQ) — to assess student perceptions of six aspects of 
teaching quality (Maulana et al., 2015). Using data from this project, 
Andr´e et al. (2020) found evidence of metric and partly scalar invariance 
across the Netherlands, Indonesia, South Africa, South Korea, Spain, and 
Turkey. However, using the same data, van der Lans et al. (2021) found 
issues with differential item function (DIF) and advised against 
comparing mean scores. 
One possible solution to achieve scalar invariance — as showcased 
by several previous studies — is to include only a subset of countries 
with a similar language and cultural background to enhance compara­
bility (Fischer et al., 2019; Klieme, 2020; Scherer & Gustafsson, 2015; 
Scherer et al., 2016). For example, evidence supporting scalar invari­
ance of student perceptions of teaching quality was found across three 
Nordic countries: Finland, Norway, and Sweden (Scherer and Gus­
tafsson, 2015), across three English-speaking countries: Australia, Can­
ada, and the USA (Scherer et al. 2016), and across four English-speaking 
countries: the U.K., New Zealand, Australia, and Ireland (Klieme, 2020). 
Furthermore, Fischer et al. (2019) examined the measurement invari­
ance of student perceptions of teaching quality across 15 countries,

small group of culturally and linguistically similar countries seems to 
significantly enhance the likelihood of obtaining scalar invariance. 
1.6. The present study 
The primary goal of the current study is to examine the extent to 
which student perceptions of teaching quality can be used for valid 
cross-national comparisons. More specifically, we use the alignment 
method to examine whether valid cross-national mean score compari­
sons across 38 linguistically and culturally diverse countries can be 
made. In doing so, the study seeks to extend the work of Fischer et al. 
(2019) by applying the alignment method to an even broader and more 
diverse set of countries. Where Fischer et al. (2019) focused on 15 
countries clustered into five linguistic groups, this study expands the 
sample to include 38 countries across various continents, thereby 
incorporating a wider range of linguistic and cultural diversity. 
In addition, the theoretical framework used in both the Fischer et al. 
(2019) study and the current study — the framework of the Three Basic 
Dimensions — is not connected to a single instrument. As a result, PISA, 
which was used in the Fischer et al. (2019) study, and TIMSS, which was 
used in the current study, have operationalized teaching quality differ­
ently in their respective student questionnaires. This variation in oper­
ationalization is significant because measurement invariance is a 
property of the measurement tool rather than the underlying theoretical 
framework. Therefore, the findings and discussions in these two studies 
will inherently differ. For example, the PISA study, in contrast to the 
TIMSS study, did not use agreement as a response option for any of their 
scales. 
Furthermore, the current study expands previous research by con­
ducting an in-depth analysis of the sources of (non-)invariance using the 
alignment method and the estimated mean scores. This approach offers 
valuable insights for future scale development, particularly in identi­
fying which items or constructs are most and least suitable for cross- 
country comparisons. 
2. Materials and Methods 
2.1. Data 
In TIMSS 2019, a total of 39 countries participated in the Grade 8 
assessment. One country, Singapore, was excluded from the analyses 
because it did not include the behavioral management scale in the stu­
dent questionnaire. Therefore, the data used in the study consisted of 
nationally representative responses from Grade 8 students and their 
mathematics teachers from 38 countries assessed in TIMSS 2019 (Nstud 
= 221,016). The raw data and other extensive TIMSS documentation are 
publicly available at https://timssandpirls.bc.edu/timss2019/. Table 1 
provides more detailed information on the samples of each country 
included in the analyses. 
2.2. Measures 
2.2.1. Teaching quality 
TIMSS assesses two aspects of teaching quality through the student 
questionnaire: behavioral management and instructional clarity (see 
Table 2). The behavioral management scale consists of six items 
measuring students’ perceptions of how frequently disruptive and 
disorderly behavior occurs in the classroom. The composite reliability of 
the scale — as estimated by the McDonalds omega (ω) — was high in all 
countries (median reliability = 0.891). The TIMSS 2019 instructional 
clarity scale included seven items measuring student agreement on the 
clarity of teacher instructions that promote understanding. However, 
one item (“I know what my teacher expects me to do”) was excluded 
from the original scale because — unlike the other six items — the 
reference in this item is “I” instead of “My teacher”. The exclusion of this 
item was justified because the reference in student questionnaire items 
measuring teaching quality likely influences the underlying response 
pattern (Fauth, G¨ollner, et al., 2020). The composite reliability of the 
instructional clarity scale across countries was sufficiently high (median 
reliability = 0.895). For both scales, all items were coded so that the 
higher values corresponded to the most positive responses. 
2.2.2. Missing 
Missing data in the total sample ranged from 2.3% - 8.9 at the item 
level and was handled with the Full Information Maximum Likelihood 
(FIML).

analyses were performed using Mplus 8 (Muth´en & Muth´en, 2017). All

2.3.1. Measurement models 
In a preliminary analysis, we tested the factorial structure of our two- 
factor model on the pooled sample and subsequently on each country 
separately using Confirmatory Factor Analyses (CFA). Model fit was 
evaluated by inspecting fit indices, following general guidelines: CFI ≥
0.95, TLI ≥0.95, RMSEA ≤0.06, SRMR ≤0.08 (Hu & Bentler, 1999; 
West et al., 2012). In addition, we examined whether students perceived 
the two factors as being distinct and unrelated in each country by testing 
whether the factor correlations were less than 0.70 (Brown, 2015). 
Finally, we calculated Mcdonalds’ omega (ω) composite reliability for 
each country (Geldhof et al., 2014). Reliability was considered sufficient 
if it was close to, or greater than, 0.70. 
2.3.2. Measurement Invariance Testing 
To address the question of comparability across countries, we started 
by conducting a traditional MGCFA using the measurement model ob­
tained from the preliminary analyses. First, we estimated the measure­
ment model for all 38 countries simultaneously (configural invariance). 
Next, we restricted the factor loadings to be equal across countries 
(metric invariance). In the last step, we constrained the factor loadings 
and intercepts to be equal across countries (scalar invariance). We 
evaluated which level of invariance was established by comparing 
changes in model fit indices across the increasingly restricted models. 
We used the guidelines for assessing measurement invariance across 
many groups and with varying sample sizes, recommended by Rut­
kowski and Svetina (2014): Compared to the configural model, the 
metric model was accepted if CFI decreases by less than 0.020, and 
RMSEA decreases by less than 0.030. The scalar model was accepted 
when CFI decreases by less than 0.010, and RMSEA decreases by less 
than 0.020 when compared to the metric model. We refrained from 
using the Chi-square statistics due to its sensitivity to sample size 
(Cheung & Rensvold, 2002; Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014). 
In cases where the scalar or even metric model was not accepted, we 
continued to explore measurement invariance using the less strict and 
more flexible approach of approximate measurement invariance: the 
alignment method (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014; Muth´en & Aspar­
ouhov, 2018). R-squared statistics provided by the alignment output 
were interpreted as an indication of the degree of variation in the pa­
rameters explained by alignment, with a higher R-square value associ­
ated with a higher degree of invariant parameters (Asparouhov & 
Muth´en, 2022; Muth´en & Asparouhov, 2018). In assessing measurement 
invariance, we followed the guidelines from Asparouhov and Muth´en 
(2022), who suggested a threshold of 20% non-invariance, on average 
across intercepts and loadings, as a rough rule of thumb to obtain 
trustworthy means. We conducted a Monte Carlo simulation study, as 
recommended by Muth´en & Asparouhov (2014), to assess the reliability 
of findings obtained by the alignment optimization. 
3. Results 
3.1. Measurement Model 
A two-factor model, which consists of behavioral management and 
instructional clarity, showed a good fit on the pooled data: CFI = 0.975, 
TLI = 0.969, RMSEA = 0.036, SRMR = 0.030 and to the data of each 
country separately: CFI = 0.948-0.988, TLI = 0.936-0.984, RMSEA =
0.028-0.064, and SRMR = 0.024-0.060. Correlation between the two 
scales ranged from 0.057 to 0.331 across countries, supporting the 
notion that students perceive the two factors as distinct. A detailed 
overview of the results can be found in the supplementary material 
(Tables S1 and S2). 
3.2. Measurement Invariance across countries 
In the first step, we explored the extent of exact measurement 
invariance of our two-factor model across all 38 countries. As presented 
in Table 3, findings from the traditional MGCFA with exact parameters 
constraints show acceptable changes in model fit indices between the 
configural and metric models (ΔCFI = 0.007, ΔRMSEA = 0.002), 
following the guidelines from Rutkowski & Svetina (2014). Therefore, 
we accept our measurement model to be exact metric invariant. How­
ever, the changes in model fit between the metric and scalar models 
(ΔCFI = 0.036, ΔRMSEA = 0.015) were above the proposed guidelines, 
thus our measurement model did not reach scalar invariance across all 
38 countries. Based on the findings from the traditional MGCFA, we can 
conclude that a comparison of mean scores across countries is not 
justified. 
Next, we tested whether approximate invariance using the alignment 
method could provide us with comparable mean scores across countries. 
The alignment model builds on the configural model and, therefore, has 
the same model fit as the configural model in Table 3. Due to poor 
identification of an alignment model in which the factor means were 
freely estimated, we fixed the factor means of the first country 
(Australia) to zero. The findings indicated that there were more non- 
invariant item intercepts than non-invariant factor loadings, which 
aligns with the results found in the traditional MGCFA (see Table 4).

3.3. Monte Carlo Simulation 
Findings from the subsequent Monte Carlo simulation study (see 
Table 5) provide information on the extent to which factor means and 
variances in our model could be successfully replicated under different 
conditions. The median sample size across our 38 countries was 4500, 
and all sample sizes were always above 3000 (see Table 1). Thus, we 
simulated data with sample sizes ranging from 2000 to 5000. The 
simulated correlations between the estimated and generated factor 
means were sufficiently high and consistently above the recommended 
cut-off of 0.98 proposed by Muth´en & Asparouhov (2018). The corre­
lation between the estimated and generated factor variance was only 
below the cut-off value of 0.98 for the behavioral management scale 
when the sample size was set to 2000. Therefore, we conclude that the 
results obtained from the alignment method can be used to reliably 
interpret the extent of invariance across countries. 
In conclusion, results from the alignment optimization led us to 
conclude that there is stronger evidence to support the cross-national 
comparison of the estimated factor means from the instructional 
clarity scale across countries than there is for the behavioral manage­
ment scale. The subsequent Monte Carlo Simulation confirmed that 
similar results would be obtained under different conditions, confirming 
the reliability of the findings. However, it is crucial to approach these 
findings with caution. Given the limited conclusive evidence, we do not 
recommend using the estimated mean scores for substantive in­
terpretations or cross-national comparisons. 
3.4. Mean scores 
Although results from the invariance analysis do not fully support a 
comparison of mean scores across countries, it is valuable to examine the 
estimated mean scores in order to discuss possible factors influencing 
comparability (see section 4). Therefore, the estimated mean scores can 
be found in the supplementary material (Figure S1). We can shed light 
on using or misusing mean scores obtained from ILSAs for cross-national 
comparisons by critically examining these estimated mean scores while 
considering previous research. 
4. Discussion and conclusion 
The current study aimed to contribute to the field of comparative 
research on teaching quality by utilizing recent methodological ad­
vances to assess the extent to which student perceptions of teaching 
quality are comparable across a large number of culturally diverse 
countries. Similar to previous studies (e.g., Herbert et al., 2022; Scherer 
& Nilsen, 2016), metric invariance was met even when applying exact 
invariance criteria. In addition, changes in model fit between the metric 
and scalar model in the traditional MGCFA were not particularly 
pronounced. 
However, despite these promising first findings, applying the less 
strict criteria of approximate invariance – i.e., using the alignment 
method – did not significantly increase the comparability of students’ 
perceived teaching quality across our 38 countries. Thus, using mean 
scores to examine differences in student-perceived behavioral manage­
ment and instructional clarity across countries was not warranted, even 
under conditions of approximate invariance. This finding is especially 
relevant given that TIMSS 2019 presents the average mean scores of 
each country’s behavioral management and instructional clarity scales 
in their international results (Mullis et al., 2020). Intriguingly, the mean 
scores derived from the alignment method in the current study corre­
lated strongly with the average scale scores provided in the TIMSS 2019 
international results (instructional clarity: r(36) = .942, p < .001, 
behavioral management: r(36) = .959, p < .001). Thus, using these 
mean scores for substantive interpretation or cross-national compari­
sons might yield misleading insights. Therefore, organizations admin­
istering ILSA studies should be more cautious or refrain from reporting 
and comparing the mean scores of instructional clarity and behavioral 
management based on data obtained from the student questionnaires 
(Klieme, 2020).

4.1. Comparability depends on the construct and items 
First, we confirmed results from the previous studies that showed the 
comparability of students’ perceived teaching quality across countries 
depends on the teaching aspect under investigation (e.g., Fischer et al., 
2019; Herbert et al., 2022). In the current study, findings from the 
alignment method outlined the instructional clarity scale to be better 
suited for comparison across our 38 countries. In contrast, we found a 
high level of non-invariance for the behavioral management scale, a 
finding also made in the study of Fischer et al. (2019). 
Additionally, the alignment method allowed us to gain insights into 
the extent to which specific items were invariant across the 38 countries. 
For the instructional clarity scale, no single item stood out to perform 
notably better or worse in the invariant analysis. However, for the 
behavioral management scale, the item “My teacher has to keep telling 
us to follow the classroom rules” (BSBM18F) performed particularly 
poorly, as indicated by a high percentage of non-invariant parameters 
and a low R-square statistic. For this item, the poor performance in the 
invariance analysis might arise from variations between students un­
derstanding across countries due to the interpretative nature of “class­
room rules”. For example, other items that leave less room for 
interpretation — such as “students interrupt the teacher” (BSBM18E) — 
performed better in the invariance analysis. Changing the item 
(BSBM18F) to be less open for interpretation —for example, “my teacher 
keeps telling us to be quiet” — might improve cross-national 
comparability. 
4.2. Comparability depends on the response option and style 
Other factors known to influence comparability are the response 
option of the scale and culture-specific response styles (He et al., 2022; 
van de Vijver, 2018). Most often, the response options of items from 
contextual questionnaires in ILSA studies are either based on frequency 
(e.g., “once every two weeks” to “every day”) or agreement (e.g., “agree 
a little” to “agree a lot”). These different options may trigger response 
processes influenced by cultural norms (Avvisati et al., 2019; van de 
Vijver, 2018). For example, analyses from several cycles of the PIRLS 
(Progress in International Reading Literacy Study) and TIMSS data 
suggested that frequency scales are around half as susceptible to 
country-specific response styles (van de Vijver et al., 2017; van de Vijver 
et al., 2018). 
In the current study, respondents were asked about frequency (e.g., 
“every lesson” or “never”) in the behavioral management scale and 
agreement (e.g., “disagree” or “agree a lot”) in the instructional clarity 
scale. Thus, the instructional clarity scale might be more susceptible to 
country-specific response styles. A possible solution to enhance cross- 
country comparability of the scale is to measure teaching quality vari­
ables using frequency instead of agreement as a response option. This 
approach is currently implemented in all variables measuring teaching 
quality in the PISA study. However, while this approach could make the 
instructional clarity scale less susceptible to country-specific response 
styles, it might not rule out its occurrence, as it is often a stable country 
characteristic (Harzing, 2006; van de Vijver et al., 2017; van de Vijver, 
2018). 
To take a closer look at the extent to which country-specific response 
styles could have affected the estimation of mean scores in the current 
study, we compared the ranking of countries based on the estimated 
mean scores in the current study with country-specific response styles 
found in the previous research. Past studies have indicated that country- 
level characteristics are significantly related to specific response styles 
(e.g., Harzing, 2006; Harzing et al., 2012; van de Vijver et al., 2017). For 
example, van de Vijver and colleagues (2017) analyzed response styles 
in TIMSS and PIRLS data obtained through student questionnaires. The 
authors ranked countries according to response styles and found that 
East Asian and European countries typically responded more modestly. 
In contrast, Middle Eastern countries, countries from the former Soviet 
Union, and Central and South American countries typically gave more 
pronounced responses (van de Vijver et al., 2017; van de Vijver, 2018). 
Especially East-Asian countries stand out because they demonstrate a 
pronounced cultural modesty with high educational achievement. This 
cultural trait leads them to predominantly use a more conservative or 
lower end of the response scale (van de Vijver et al., 2018). 
The mean scores for the instructional clarity scale estimated in the 
current study (see supplementary material, figure S1b) largely align 
with the country-specific response styles found in previous studies: 
Students in TIMSS 2019 rated instructional clarity lowest in South 
Korea, Hong Kong, Japan, and Hungary, whereas they rated instruc­
tional clarity highest in Turkey, Jordan, Egypt, and Georgia. Addition­
ally, the use of country-specific response styles explains the counter- 
intuitive finding that countries which generally perform high in math­
ematics and science in large-scale assessments (e.g., Korea, Hong Kong, 
and Japan) had the lowest perceived instructional clarity among their 
students, whereas countries that perform relatively low (e.g., Turkey, 
Jordan, and Egypt) had the highest perceived instructional clarity. Thus, 
in summary, these findings may indicate that a country-specific response 
style — possibly mediated by the response option of the scale — may be 
a factor contributing to the estimation of mean scores and comparability 
of the instructional clarity scale in the current study. This finding 
highlights the challenges and potential pitfalls of making valid cross- 
national comparisons based solely on mean scores of instructional 
clarity. 
Contrary to the mean scores observed for the instructional clarity 
scale, the behavioral management scale showed less alignment with 
previously identified country-specific response styles. For instance, un­
like the country-specific response styles found by van de Vijver and 
colleagues (2017), our findings showed that students in countries from 
the former Soviet Union, such as Kazakhstan and Russia, along with 
Asian countries like Japan, Taiwan, and Korea, rated their classrooms as 
having very few behavioral problems. On the other hand, European 
countries rated their classrooms as having the most behavioral problems 
(see supplementary material Figure S1a). This pattern is, however, in 
line with the hypothesis that frequency-based scales are less susceptible 
to country-specific response styles (Avvisati et al., 2019; van de Vijver, 
2018). 
Moreover, the mean scores estimated for the behavioral management 
scale are, in fact, consistent with previous research. For instance, 
Wubbels (2011) reviewed international literature on classroom man­
agement and found that Western countries generally appear less orderly 
than Asian countries. Similarly, other studies found that in countries 
where teachers are held in great respect — like China, Taiwan, Russia, 
and India — behavioral difficulties in classrooms were almost 
non-existent compared to countries where managing classroom 
behavior is a frequent challenge for educators — like England, France, 
and the United States (Aldridge et al., 1999; Alexander, 2001; Lewis 
et al., 2005; Wubbels, 2011). However, the absence of exact or 
approximate scalar invariance in the behavioral management scale also 
indicates that not all items are understood and answered similarly across 
our 38 countries (He et al., 2022). Collectively, these insights point to 
the need to further develop the behavioral management scale to improve 
its cross-national comparability. 
4.3. Improving cross-national comparability 
The findings from the current study suggest that measurement

invariance is an essential prerequisite before making valid comparisons 
of mean scores of students’ perceptions of teaching quality across 
countries. Comparing mean scores without achieving an adequate level 
of measurement invariance, either exact or approximate, may lead to 
incorrect conclusions about real differences across countries. Such dis­
crepancies might arise from factors such as country-specific response 
styles rather than true differences. Therefore, we advise against ignoring 
measurement non-invariance or using single measures in cross-national 
research. 
Innovative statistical methods, like alignment optimization or 
Bayesian approximate invariance, have been proposed as remedies for 
addressing issues with exact invariance. However, neither the current 
nor previous studies have successfully enhanced the cross-national 
comparability of student questionnaire scales using these approaches 
(see, e.g., Ding et al., 2022; Fischer et al., 2019; Odell et al., 2021; 
Wurster, 2022). This indicates that the current statistical approaches 
might not be sufficient in dealing with the invariance issues of student 
questionnaire data across countries. Therefore, it might be beneficial to 
address the introduction of bias at all stages of cross-cultural studies to 
improve cross-national comparability. 
In the current study, while the alignment method did not signifi­
cantly improve comparability, it was useful in guiding future scale de­
velopments, for example, by reducing item bias (He et al., 2022). 
Specifically, findings from the current study and the subsequent dis­
cussion seem to point to (1) using frequency instead of agreement as a 
response option to mitigate the occurrence of country-specific response 
styles and (2) revising items with ambiguous wording to leave less room 
for interpretation. However, these changes might not be sufficient for 
valid cross-national mean scores comparison. To further improve the 
questionnaires cross-national comparability, it might be beneficial to 
add qualitative studies, like interviewing students from different coun­
tries and examining how they perceive the questions to ensure consis­
tent translation, interpretation, and comparability (van de Vijver & 
Leung, 1997/2021). This might be especially important for constructs 
like behavioral management, which both the current study and the study 
of Fischer and colleagues (2019) identified as having issues with 
cross-national comparability. Possibly, construct bias was introduced 
due to the export of the concept from Western to non-Western countries 
(Leung and Zhang, 1996; van de Vijver & Leung, 1997/2021). More 
specifically, the three basic dimension framework, on which teaching 
quality in TIMSS and PISA is based, was developed in Germany. How­
ever, classroom management might not be a part of teaching quality in 
countries where students are expected to sit and listen, and disruptions 
are rare (Alexander, 2000; Berliner, 2005). Furthermore, items can have 
different connotations across countries, thus introducing item bias. For 
example, whether students respect a teacher can have different conno­
tations in Chinese classrooms compared to Norwegian classrooms. 
Previous research has also suggested the necessity to include more 
culturally sensitive local or regional measures to enhance comparability, 
especially in light of the increasing participation of diverse countries in 
ILSAs (Rutkowski and Rutkowski, 2018; He & van de Vijver, 2012). 
Others have suggested innovative item formats, such as anchoring vi­
gnettes and situational judgement tests (Avvisati et al., 2019; Marks­
teiner et al., 2019). Moreover, combining positive and negative 
statements may help reduce country-specific response styles because it 
might allow participants to consider the exact meaning of the item more 
thoroughly (Smith, 2003). If nothing else, it might cancel out the 
extreme response styles and better approximate a genuine opinion 
(Smith, 2003). However, mixed-worded scales have also been shown to 
increase the number of inconsistent responders and could increase the 
occurrence of invalid scores (Steinman et al., 2022). Finally, as previous 
research has shown (e.g., Fischer et al., 2019; Klieme, 2020; Scherer & 
Gustafsson, 2015; Scherer et al., 2016), a viable alternative is to keep 
cross-national comparisons of mean scores limited to a small number of 
culturally and linguistically similar countries. 
Ultimately, the decision on how much effort and resources should be 
invested to improve the cross-national comparability of contextual 
questionnaires in ILSAs hinges on the primary objectives of these as­
sessments. Suppose the aim of ILSAs is to ensure valid cross-national 
comparisons using contextual questionnaires among increasingly 
diverse participating countries. In that case, prioritizing comparability 
becomes paramount despite the challenges and costs associated. 
4.4. Limitations and ways forward 
The current study was limited in its representation of countries, 
especially from the global south. Of the 38 countries that participated in 
TIMSS 2019, only three were from the African continent, and only one 
was from South America. In addition, our study showed that the 
comparability of student perceptions of teaching quality across coun­
tries depends on the teaching aspect under examination. Thus, future 
research that includes additional countries and aspects of teaching 
quality is needed. Qualitative research would also be useful, as methods 
of measurement invariance are limited in their ability to identify sources 
of non-comparability more conclusively. Such research could include, 
for example, interviewing students across countries and investigating 
how they perceive the questions to ensure consistent translation, inter­
pretation, and comparability. 
Some methodological limitations also need to be considered. 
Currently, the alignment method is not compatible with multilevel 
modelling, which limits the ability to investigate the comparability of 
teaching quality at the classroom level. However, the current study 
accounted for the nesting by adjusting standard errors and model fit. In 
addition, as the alignment method is relatively new, it lacks the clear 
guidelines that come with repeated application and simulation studies. 
While such clear guidelines have been proposed for the traditional 
MGCFA, they are known to depend on study characteristics, such as 
sample size, number of observations, or type of indicators used (Rut­
kowski & Svetina, 2014, 2017). Thus, a blind application of cut-off 
criteria to assess invariance can lead to an incorrect assessment of 
invariance (Avvisati et al., 2019). Moreover, the amount of 
non-invariant parameters estimated by the alignment method in the 
current study was high despite the traditional MGCFA providing evi­
dence for metric invariance and nearly scalar invariance. The source of 
the high non-invariant results potentially originates from small insig­
nificant differences in parameters across groups, which, due to the large 
sample sizes in the current study, emerge as statistically significant 
(Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2022). If this is indeed the case, it begs the 
question: Should the statistical significance threshold be recalibrated 
when the alignment method is used to analyze measurement invariance 
in ILSA data? There is a pressing need for additional methodological 
research to ascertain whether such an adjustment is essential and, if so, 
how it can be effectively operationalized. if such specification is 
necessary.
