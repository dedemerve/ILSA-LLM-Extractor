This study examines the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the socioeconomic (SES) achievement gap in
Taiwan’s education system. Using Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) datasets of student
math performance from 2006 to 2022 and a difference-in-differences (DID) analytical framework, the study
estimates how school disruptions during the pandemic affected students from lower versus higher SES backgrounds. The analysis reveals a widening achievement gap: although overall math scores rose in 2022, lower-SES
students improved far less than higher-SES students. The results suggest that pandemic-era remote learning
exacerbated pre-existing inequalities, likely due to disparities in digital access and learning support. The findings
align with prior research on educational inequity and online learning, demonstrating consistencies with global
patterns of COVID-induced learning loss. The study concludes with policy recommendations for Taiwan and
beyond, including digital equity initiatives (e.g., ensuring all students have internet and device access) and
targeted academic interventions (e.g., remedial programs and tutoring) to support disadvantaged students. These
measures are critical for mitigating the widened SES gap in the post-pandemic period and fostering a more
resilient and equitable education system.

1. Introduction
Socioeconomic (SES) disparities in educational achievement are a
well-documented challenge across societies. It is generally found that
students from higher-SES families—who typically enjoy greater economic resources, have better-educated parents, and more access to
educational support—consistently outperform their lower SES counterparts on academic performance (see e.g., Harju-Luukkainen et al., 2020;
Muench et al., 2023; Vázquez-Cano et al., 2020). In Taiwan, despite high
overall academic performance internationally, an achievement gap between advantaged and disadvantaged students has persisted. Prior to the
COVID-19 pandemic, research indicated that this SES achievement gap
in Taiwan was substantial (Chen et al., 2017; Huang and Hwang, 2014;
Hwang and Lin, 2016; Sung et al., 2014). For example, an analysis of
high school entrance examination scores found that achievement gaps
were decreasing among ethnic groups but increasing between students
of different SES backgrounds and regions (Sung et al., 2014). Such disparities raise concerns about equity and social mobility, as lower-SES
students may face limited opportunities due to lower academic attainment (Chung and Chen, 2011; Hwang and Lin, 2016; Peng et al., 2011).
The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 brought unprecedented disruptions

to education worldwide. Schools in many countries were forced to close
temporarily and shift to online instruction, raising alarms about exacerbating existing educational inequalities (Golden et al., 2023). Taiwan
initially managed to keep schools open in 2020 through effective public
health measures, thereby avoiding major learning disruptions in the
early phase of the pandemic. However, a local outbreak in May 2021 led
to nationwide school closures and a sudden transition to fully remote
learning for the first time (Lee, 2021). The abrupt shift to online education had the potential to disproportionately affect students lacking
technology or supportive home environments (Blundell et al., 2022;
Haelermans et al., 2022; Korkmaz et al., 2022; Pokhrel and Chhetri,
2021). Early reports from Taiwan highlighted a “digital divide” between
students of different backgrounds: nearly 70 % of students in rural or
lower-SES communities did not have a personal computer or tablet for
online classes. Many had to rely on borrowing devices or using smartphones, and over 40 % had no reliable internet at home (Taipei Times,
2022). Such conditions made effective remote learning far more challenging for disadvantaged students, whereas students from higher-SES
families often had adequate devices, internet access, and parental support for learning at home (Blundell et al., 2022; Haelermans et al., 2022;
Korkmaz et al., 2022; Pokhrel and Chhetri, 2021).

These circumstances suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic could
deepen the SES achievement gap in Taiwan. Lower-SES students, who
were already at an academic disadvantage, risked falling further behind
due to unequal access to online learning and resources (Chen and Lin,
2022; Huang, 2021; Tsai, 2020). On the other hand, Taiwan’s education
system took steps to mitigate these impacts, including strengthening
digital infrastructure, providing online learning platforms, and lending
equipment to students (Ministry of Education, 2020). By late 2022, an
OECD (2023) report even noted that Taiwanese students’ performance
remained robust across social groups, suggesting potential resilience.
Given these mixed indications, a rigorous analysis is needed to quantify
how the pandemic affected the SES achievement gap. Understanding
this impact is not only academically significant for education policy
research but also critical for designing effective interventions to support
vulnerable students moving forward.
This study addresses the following question: How did the COVID-19
pandemic impact the academic achievement gap between lower- and
higher-SES students in Taiwan? The study focuses on math achievement
as a key outcome indicator, utilizing PISA datasets of student performance from 2006 to 2022. The current study employs a difference-indifferences (DID) approach to isolate the effect of the pandemic on the
performance gap between lower-SES and higher-SES students. My
analysis contributes to the literature on educational equity by providing
empirical evidence from Taiwan, a high-performing East Asian system
with proactive pandemic responses. The findings will shed light on
whether the pandemic exacerbated educational inequalities in this
context and inform policymakers and educators seeking to close the
widened gaps in the post-pandemic recovery period.

For instance, Liu et al. (2017) found that elementary students who paid

Building on these structural foundations, recent research has begun
to examine how socioeconomic inequalities have further intensified
amid rapid digitization, education policy reforms, and the COVID-19
pandemic. Evidence from Taiwan demonstrates that school closures
and class cancellations during the 2021–2022 outbreaks disrupted
children’s learning and heightened parental stress, with families with
fewer resources facing greater challenges (Chao et al., 2024). Huang
et al. (2023) found that Chinese students from lower-SES backgrounds
experienced disproportionately larger declines in mathematical
reasoning during school disruptions, confirming that math is particularly sensitive to crises and that pandemic shocks amplified existing
disadvantages. At the regional level, UNICEF’s Learning Recovery report
(2022) highlights that disadvantaged students across East Asia and the
Pacific experienced deeper learning losses and slower recovery, particularly in numeracy. Comparative PISA analyses show that family SES
remains among the strongest predictors of math achievement worldwide
(OECD, 2023). According to OECD (2023), higher-SES students in
Taiwan scored about 119 points higher in math than disadvantaged
peers—larger than the OECD average (about 93) and gaps in Japan
(about 81) or Korea (about 97), comparable to Singapore (about 112).
This positions Taiwan among the East-Asian economies where family
background exerts a particularly strong influence on achievement.
These more recent studies help ground the argument that the SES
achievement gap is not only historically persistent but also acutely
exacerbated by recent shocks such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Taken
together, this body of research demonstrates that socioeconomic
achievement gaps in Taiwan are structurally embedded in family resources and supplementary education, with the COVID-19 pandemic
primarily acting as an external shock that intensified—rather than
fundamentally altered—these pre-existing inequalities.
Beyond these macro patterns, the inclusion of both ninth and tenth
graders in Taiwan’s PISA data introduces additional complexity. These
two groups belong to different educational stages—middle and high
school—where the mechanisms linking socioeconomic background to
achievement diverge. In Taiwan, the transition between these stages is
governed by a high-stakes entrance examination that determines
whether students enter general or vocational tracks (Tsai, 1998). Students from higher-SES families are disproportionately represented in
general high schools, which emphasize university preparation and
provide stronger academic environments, whereas lower-SES students
are more likely to attend vocational high schools that channel graduates
directly into the labor market (Chen et al., 2017; Hsueh, 1996; Huang
and Hwang, 2014; Lee and Hwang, 2010; Tsai, 1998). This stratified
tracking structure means that SES influences achievement not only
directly through home resources but also indirectly through school
placement and curriculum exposure. Consequently, the pandemic’s
impact on learning may have operated through distinct mechanisms
across these educational levels—through digital access and home support among ninth graders, and through institutional or curricular differences among tenth graders—underscoring the importance of
interpreting SES effects in light of Taiwan’s tracking system and the
transition between lower- and upper-secondary schooling, which is
further examined in the robustness tests.

1.1. Literature review
The literature reviewed in this section was selected based on its
relevance to socioeconomic inequality in educational achievement, with
particular attention to studies examining learning outcomes, digital
learning environments, and pandemic-related disruptions. Priority was
given to peer-reviewed journal articles and large-scale international
assessments (such as PISA), while official policy or statistical reports
were included selectively to provide contextual or descriptive evidence
rather than causal inference. Overall, the review aims to synthesize
influential and empirically grounded studies most directly aligned with
the analytical focus of this paper.
1.2. SES achievement gaps in education
Socioeconomic status is one of the strongest predictors of student
achievement in both Western and Asian education systems
(Harju-Luukkainen et al., 2020; Muench et al., 2023; Vázquez-Cano
et al., 2020). Research constantly shows that children from higher-SES
families tend to have higher test scores and grades, partly due to more
learning resources at home, better school quality, and extra academic
support (e.g., tutoring) available to them (Bourdieu, 1986; Coleman,
1968; Lareau, 2000; Teachman, 1987).
In Taiwan and other East Asian societies, one major contributor to
SES-based achievement gaps is unequal access to supplementary education (Byun et al., 2018). Taiwan has a prevalent shadow education
system of cram schools. To give the reader a clearer sense of its scale, the
Ministry of Education (MOE) (2024) reports that Taiwan supports a
large cram school industry, with over 17,000 supplementary education
institutions in recent years, compared to 11,123 schools across all formal
levels from preschool to university. Notably, the number of
test-preparation cram schools has continued to increase despite demographic decline, suggesting that supplementary education remains a
structurally entrenched component of academic competition. Students
from higher-SES families more often enroll in afterschool cram classes or
private tutoring, which can boost their test performance. In contrast,

The COVID-19 pandemic brought about an unprecedented reliance
on online learning, which had uneven effects across different student
populations. Emerging literature has documented that the pandemic
exacerbated educational inequalities in many countries (Blundell et al.,
2022; Golden et al., 2023; Haelermans et al., 2022; Jakubowski et al.,
2025; Korkmaz et al., 2022; Pokhrel and Chhetri, 2021). A review by
Golden et al. (2023) on remote schooling during the pandemic
concluded that the pandemic contributed to the widened achievement
gaps, as students from lower-SES families and other marginalized groups
suffered greater learning losses when schooling moved online. Several
factors drove this trend. First, the digital divide—unequal access to
technology and the internet—meant that disadvantaged students often
could not fully participate in virtual classes (Blundell et al., 2022;
Golden et al., 2023; Gremigni, 2023; Korkmaz et al., 2022). Second,
differences in home learning environments were pronounced:
higher-SES parents could provide supervision, learning materials, and a
quiet study space, whereas many lower-SES students lacked these supports (Blundell et al., 2022; Golden et al., 2023; Gremigni, 2023; Haelermans et al., 2022). Third, schools serving disadvantaged communities
often had less experience or resources to deliver quality remote instruction on short notice (Dean et al., 2023; Rojas Apaza et al., 2024;
Sanfo, 2023; Sumida and Kawata, 2021).
Consequently, studies using standardized test data between 2020
and 2021 found larger declines in math and reading scores among lowerperforming and lower-SES students, compared to their more advantaged
peers (Golden et al., 2023). Recent U.S. evidence further shows that, five
years after COVID-19, math scores have partly rebounded while reading
recovery has stalled, with achievement gaps persisting and in some cases
widening across socioeconomic and racial groups (Kuhfeld and Lewis,
2025). Regional reports reinforce this pattern: UNICEF (2022) highlights
that disadvantaged students across East Asia and the Pacific experienced
slower recovery, particularly in numeracy. A meta-analysis across 42
studies found that pandemic-related learning deficits were consistently
larger in math and disproportionately severe for lower-SES students
(Betthäuser et al., 2023). Using global PISA data from 72 countries,
Jakubowski et al. (2025) found that COVID-19-related school closures
led to an average seven-month learning loss in math, with the steepest
declines among disadvantaged, low-achieving, and immigrant students,
thereby widening socioeconomic inequalities in educational outcomes
worldwide.
In the context of Taiwan, early evidence during the pandemic echoed
international concerns. When Taiwanese schools switched to online
classes in mid-2021, observers noted that not all students could
smoothly transition (Chen and Lin, 2022; Huang, 2021; Taipei Times,
2022; Tsai, 2020). The Child Welfare League Foundation warned in
early 2022 that remote learning had accentuated and potentially worsened the urban-rural education gap. Their survey of rural students
revealed that two-thirds did not own a suitable device for online
learning and over half lacked a stable internet connection at home.
Many rural or lower-SES students had to share one smartphone among
siblings or rely on limited school-provided Wi-Fi hotspots. Additionally,
a majority of these students did not have a quiet space to study, and
nearly 20 % were often home alone during remote learning hours
(Taipei Times, 2022). These conditions contrast sharply with those of
many higher-SES families in urban areas, who generally have ample
devices (computers or tablets), broadband internet, and parents or tutors
available to assist with online coursework (Chen and Lin, 2022; Huang,
2021; Taipei Times, 2022; Tsai, 2020). The foundation’s report
concluded that “remote learning has exacerbated the rural-urban divide
in education in Taiwan” (Taipei Times, 2022). Although this was a
qualitative warning rather than a quantified achievement study, it aligns
with the hypothesis that lower-SES students in Taiwan likely fell behind

more during the COVID-19 pandemic.
Beyond formal schooling, the COVID-19 pandemic also accelerated
the digital transformation of supplementary education, introducing new
online and hybrid forms of academic support whose equity implications
remain uneven. For example, Tran et al. (2023) studied Taiwan’s Digital
Learning Companion online tutoring project and found that it helped
bridge the digital gap for remote children, though challenges such as
technical access and brief tutoring sessions limited its effectiveness.
Complementing this, Lin et al. (2022) documented New Taipei City’s
rapid e-learning pivot and the concrete challenges teachers, parents, and
students encountered (platform unfamiliarity, device/connectivity gaps,
difficulty assessing progress, etc.), underscoring how access and platform readiness mediated learning support during school closures.
Globally, Karakus et al.’s (2024) study, using PISA 2022 data, shows that
asynchronous video-based instruction may provide measurable benefits
for math achievement, especially for lower-SES students, whereas synchronous large-group or remedial tutoring may be less reliably effective.
Together, these newer findings suggest that supplementary education is
becoming more varied (online/hybrid formats), potentially more
accessible, yet still layered by SES in its effectiveness and reach.
1.4. Taiwan’s education system and pandemic response
The Taiwanese government has long recognized educational
inequality as an issue and implemented policies such as the high school
tuition-free policy (高級中等學校免學費方案) and relevant programs
that cover a vast majority of high school students, especially those from
lower-SES families (Ministry of Education, n.d.). When the pandemic
hit, the MOE took steps to support online learning across all schools. In
early 2020, the MOE established an “Edu Cloud” online learning platform and provided guidelines and training for teachers to conduct virtual classes. The government also coordinated with telecommunications
companies to offer discounted internet packages for students and,
through schools, distributed some laptops and tablets to families in
need. All these efforts were aimed at ensuring that no students were left
behind due to COVID-19 disruptions (Ministry of Education, 2020). By
the next school year, most schools had blended learning plans and could
activate remote teaching when needed (Lee, 2021).
It should be noted that the effectiveness of these measures in
reaching all students remains a key question. Notably, in the latest PISA
2022 assessment (conducted after the main pandemic waves), Taiwan
was highlighted as one of a few systems demonstrating “overall resilience,” that is, maintaining high performance and student well-being
across socioeconomic groups (OECD, 2023, p. 227). MOE officials
attributed this to the rapid digitalization of teaching and extensive efforts by schools and teachers during the pandemic (Taipei Times, 2023).
However, it is possible that such resilience reflects a recovery by 2022
rather than the absence of any widening gap at the height of remote
learning.
In sum, prior research and reports suggest that: 1) SES-based
achievement gaps were a persistent issue in Taiwan prior to the
pandemic; 2) the shift to online learning in 2021 likely disproportionately affected lower-SES students due to digital divide issues; and 3)
government interventions might have mitigated some negative effects
by 2022. This study builds on the literature by empirically analyzing
achievement data to determine the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic
on Taiwan’s SES achievement gap. I employ a DID design, as detailed in
the next section, to separate the pandemic’s effect from existing trends.

2.1. Data and sample
I utilize a multiple-year dataset of student academic performance in
Taiwan spanning 2006–2022. The data are compiled from standardized
testing results in math for secondary school students. In particular, I

draw on performance data from the OECD’s Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) for the years 2006, 2009, 2012, 2015,
2018, and 2022. These repeated cross-sectional data provide a broad
view of achievement trends over time in a variety of countries/regions
(OECD, 2009, 2023). Each observation in the dataset represents a
plausible value of a student’s test score (OECD, 2009, 2023). In the
present study, I focus on the effect of the socioeconomic status indicator
provided in the data.
Socioeconomic status (SES) is measured through a composite index
of family background (ESCS, including parental education, occupation,
and home possessions) in the PISA data (OECD, 2023). For clarity, I
define “lower-SES students” as those in the bottom quarter of the SES
index distribution (e.g., bottom 25 % by family background), and
“higher-SES students” as those in the top quarter, consistent with PISA’s
operationalization of socioeconomically disadvantaged and advantaged
groups (OECD, 2023). This dichotomization allows me to compare the
two groups in terms of advantaged versus disadvantaged backgrounds.
The total sample includes more than 20,000 student observations across
the years, with similar numbers of lower- and higher-SES students in
each cross-section, ensuring robust group comparisons (see Table 1).
Table 1 further reveals that while mean scores increased for both
groups in 2022, the standard deviations also rose relative to 2018 (lower
25 %: 96.53–103.76; upper 25 %: 94.41–101.03). This pattern suggests
that inequality not only widened between SES groups but also within
them. Nevertheless, because the present study focuses on between-group
disparities, a fuller examination of within-group variation is left for
future research.

The term β3 on the interaction Lower × Post is the DID estimator of
interest, capturing the differential change in scores for lower-SES students after the pandemic relative to higher-SES students. I include X as a
vector of control variables and fixed effects: this includes year fixed
effects (to account for any general time trends affecting all students,
such as overall improvements or declines in test scores over the years)
and demographic controls (such as gender and urban/rural location) to
reduce residual variance. By including year dummies, I can effectively
compare the 2022 data to prior years while controlling for average
differences in difficulty or scoring across PISA cycles.
The DID approach relies on the parallel trends assumption (Abadie,
2005; Basu and Small, 2020; Bertrand et al., 2004), that is, in the
absence of the COVID-19 pandemic, the achievement gap between
lower- and higher-SES groups would have remained on its previous
trajectory (i.e., any changes observed for the higher-SES group represent
the counterfactual for the lower-SES group). I examined pre-pandemic
data to assess this assumption. As can be seen in Fig. 1, from 2006 to
2018, both groups showed relatively parallel performance trends prior
to the pandemic, with the exception of a notable surge in 2012 among
higher-SES students. Although explaining this anomaly lies beyond the
scope of the present study, it warrants further investigation in future
research. Overall, the pre-pandemic stability of trends lends some
credibility to treating the higher-SES group as a valid control for
time-related effects.

To identify the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the SES
achievement gap, I employ a difference-in-differences (DID) design. In
this framework, the “treatment” is the exposure to the pandemic’s
disruption of in-person schooling (particularly the switch to remote
learning in 2021). All students in 2021 were affected by this disruption,
but I hypothesize that lower-SES students experienced a larger negative
impact (due to reasons discussed above) whereas higher-SES students
were relatively less affected. I treat the lower-SES group as the “affected”
group in my DID analysis and the higher-SES group as a comparison
group that serves as a counterfactual for what would have happened to
lower-SES students’ scores if the disproportionate challenges were absent. The DID approach thus compares changes in math performance
from the pre-pandemic period to the post-pandemic period between
these two groups.

4. Discussion
My analysis provides clear evidence that the COVID-19 pandemic
exacerbated socioeconomic disparities in student achievement in
Taiwan. The math performance gap between lower- and higher-SES
students widened significantly in the aftermath of pandemic-related
school closures. This finding is consistent with emerging research
globally which indicates that disadvantaged students were disproportionately impacted by the shift to remote learning (Blundell et al., 2022;
Golden et al., 2023; Haelermans et al., 2022; Korkmaz et al., 2022;
Pokhrel and Chhetri, 2021). In Taiwan’s case, despite a relatively short
period of nationwide school closure (mainly mid-2021) compared to
many other countries, the disruption was sufficient to amplify existing
inequalities. My results show that those from lower-SES backgrounds
still suffered a larger educational setback during the pandemic.
Although math scores rose overall in 2022 compared to 2018, gains
were disproportionately concentrated among higher-SES students,
resulting in a wider SES gap. This pattern aligns with meta-analytic
evidence suggesting that math achievement is particularly sensitive to
large-scale educational disruptions and socioeconomic disadvantage,
making it a revealing domain for examining pandemic-era inequality
(Betthäuser et al., 2023). In this sense, the observed widening of the
math achievement gap reflects a broader process through which existing
social inequalities were intensified under pandemic conditions. Evidence from East Asia further demonstrates that disadvantaged groups
have been slower to recover, with UNICEF (2022) reporting particularly
deep and persistent gaps in numeracy across the region.
The pattern observed in this study may reflect a combination of
pandemic-specific disruptions and broader structural forces. On one
hand, COVID-19 amplified pre-existing inequalities: students from
higher-SES families were more likely to have access to digital devices,
online cram schools, and parental academic support, which turned the
disruption into an opportunity for enrichment. Disadvantaged students,
in contrast, struggled with limited resources and consequently saw much
smaller improvements (Tran et al., 2023; UNICEF, 2022). On the other
hand, macro-level factors may also have contributed. Taiwan’s declining
student population, curriculum reforms under the 12-Year Basic Education policy, and the continuing expansion of supplementary education
(Ministry of Education, 2024) are all plausible influences that may have
disproportionately benefitted higher-SES households. However, given
the available data, the present study cannot disentangle these structural
dynamics from the pandemic effect; this task should be pursued in future
research.
It is instructive to compare these results with prior expectations and
data. Official reports following the 2022 PISA cycle portrayed an optimistic picture, noting that Taiwan maintained performance across all
social groups and suggesting that digital learning initiatives could help
strengthen resilience (OECD, 2023). Nevertheless, the finding of a
widened SES gap in Taiwan warns us that inequality still exists and has
even been intensified by the pandemic. The MOE also acknowledged
that performance for the poorest students was just around the OECD
average (Taipei Times, 2023), indicating there is still a gap to be
addressed. International comparisons suggest that aggregate resilience
can mask internal inequalities. U.S. evidence, for example, shows that
while average math performance has rebounded modestly, reading recovery has stalled and SES gaps remain wide five years after COVID-19
(Kuhfeld and Lewis, 2025). Similar cross-national analyses of PISA 2022
indicate that shadow education and supplementary support were unevenly accessible, with asynchronous forms benefitting lower-SES students more than others, but synchronous or fee-based tutoring
reinforcing disparities (Karakus et al., 2024). The current study’s

3.3. Robustness tests
First, I examined reading and science scores as alternative outcome
variables (see Appendix). The pattern for reading was qualitatively
similar but the widening gap was slightly smaller (around 11–19 points
increase) and only significant in Model 2. Science showed an even
smaller gap change, but the overall trend was comparable again.
Accordingly, math appears to have been affected the most by the
pandemic. These subject differences, however, should be interpreted
cautiously due to data limitations and are an area for further study.
Second, I used 2015 as a pseudo treatment year to check whether any
significant changes could be detected in the SES gap when there was no
actual intervention (see Appendix). It reveals no significant differential
effect in SES math scores in simpler models (Models 0 and 1), and a
significant but modest effect in the fully specified model (Model 2). This
pattern suggests that the inclusion of structural covariates—such as
school type, region, and grade composition—may capture pre-existing
compositional changes around that time, rather than random noise.
Therefore, the significant placebo result may reflect early signs of
inequality expansion linked to other structural factors, rather than a
purely statistical artifact. This finding cautions against attributing all
observed post-pandemic widening solely to COVID-19. Instead, it suggests that socioeconomic disparities were already beginning to widen
prior to the pandemic, with the pandemic acting as an accelerant that
deepened an existing trend.
Third, I conducted a placebo test checking whether the DID model
spuriously detects a treatment effect on a variable that should not be
affected by the pandemic: in this case, student age (see Appendix). The
absence of a significant interaction effect on age in the test confirms that
the DID model does not falsely detect treatment effects where none
should exist. This strengthens the credibility of the main findings, suggesting they are not driven by model artifacts or mis-specified
covariates.
Through these tests, I am confident that the observed widening of the
SES gap is attributable to the conditions introduced by the COVID-19
remote learning period. In short, the results answer my research question affirmatively: the pandemic had a notable adverse impact on
educational equality in Taiwan, widening the achievement gap between
socioeconomically disadvantaged students and their more advantaged
peers.

severely limit their ability to engage in lessons, complete assignments, or interact with teachers; in contrast, higher-SES students
generally had ready access to multiple devices and comfortable study
environments (Blundell et al., 2022; Golden et al., 2023; Gremigni,
2023; Korkmaz et al., 2022). The unequal access to technology
translated directly into unequal learning time: if a lower-SES student
struggled to get online or follow a class due to connectivity issues,
that is lost instruction that higher-SES peers did not miss. Lin et al.
(2022) found that the rapid shift to e-learning in New Taipei City
exposed gaps in device provision, platform familiarity, and teacher
capacity, with students from less advantaged households struggling
most to adapt. Similarly, the Digital Learning Companion online
tutoring project showed that while digital tutoring could partially
bridge gaps, persistent technical problems and short tutoring durations limited its effectiveness for disadvantaged students (Tran et al.,
2023). My findings mirror this, as the lower-SES group saw minimal
gains in test scores, suggesting greater disruption to instructional
continuity. These observations are in line with global literature that
the digital divide amplified existing unequal access to resources and
affected educational outcomes during remote learning (Blundell
et al., 2022; Golden et al., 2023; Gremigni, 2023; Korkmaz et al.,
2022).
2. Home learning environment and parental support: With schools
closed, learning largely depended on support at home. Lower-SES
parents on average have lower educational attainment and may be
less able to help with academic content (Zhang et al., 2020). In
Taiwan, the survey by Child Welfare League Foundation found that
only about 48 % of lower-SES students had parental help with online
learning, and over 20 % were essentially on their own during school
hours. Under such circumstances, staying on track was a challenge
for these students (Taipei Times, 2022). Chao et al. (2024) further
showed that during the Omicron outbreak, learning disruptions
heightened parental stress and reduced student motivation, especially in disadvantaged households. Higher-SES families, by contrast,
often could supervise studying or hire tutors (Lareau, 2000), which
was particularly crucial during the pandemic. This disparity in support is a likely reason why higher-SES students’ performance was
more resilient. As one teacher noted, families with means can simply
compensate for school closures by arranging extra instruction (e.g.,
enrolling in cram courses). Meanwhile, lower-SES students without
guidance risked disengagement. The pandemic thus magnified the
role of family resources in education: effectively, schooling quality
became dependent on one’s home situation, which is inherently
unequal across SES lines (Lee, 2021).

afterschool tutoring further contributed to unequal learning loss.

as well. Higher-SES students were more likely to continue with their
cram school sessions online or receive one-on-one tutoring via video

barriers or because their families could not afford fees under economic strain (Hum, 2021). Prior research showed that those who can

The widening of the SES achievement gap in Taiwan during the
pandemic aligns with findings from other contexts (see e.g., Blundell
et al., 2022; Golden et al., 2023; Gremigni, 2023; Haelermans et al.,
2022; Korkmaz et al., 2022), reinforcing that this was a global equity
issue. For instance, studies in the United States found that students in
high-poverty schools experienced larger declines in test scores than
those in low-poverty schools during the pandemic (Golden et al., 2023).
Although Taiwan’s overall learning loss was not as severe as in countries
with prolonged closures (OECD, 2023), the internal inequality pattern is
analogous. Without deliberate intervention, these widened gaps can
have lasting consequences. Research on past disasters (e.g., the 2005
Pakistan earthquake) suggests that learning losses can compound over
time for affected students (Lee, 2021). In Taiwan’s case, if lower-SES
students lost ground in foundational subjects such as math, they might
struggle in advanced courses later, potentially leading to higher dropout
rates or lower college entrance exam scores years down the line.
It is encouraging that Taiwan’s educational authorities recognized
these risks and reported some success in countering them (Taipei Times,
2023). My results should not be seen as contradicting those efforts, but
rather as a deeper diagnosis of where gaps persisted. The resilience
observed in PISA 2022 may indicate that policies had an effect; however,
the fact remains that an achievement gap did open during the peak of
remote learning. As such, Taiwan’s experience offers an important
lesson: even in a highly developed, tech-capable society, socioeconomic
factors critically mediate student outcomes during crises. Educational
inequality can worsen rapidly in the face of external shocks, and
maintaining equity requires more than universal solutions: it demands
targeted support for those most at risk.
4.1. Policy recommendations
Closing the widened SES achievement gap in the post-pandemic era
will require concerted policy action. Based on my findings and the
broader context discussed, I propose a number of strategies for policymakers and educators:

1. Bridging the digital divide: Ensuring equitable access to technology
is paramount. The government should institutionalize programs to
provide devices and internet access to all students in need, particularly in lower-SES and rural households. Recent evidence shows that
e-learning rollouts in Taiwan exposed gaps in device provision,
platform familiarity, and training (Lin et al., 2022). Programs such as
the Digital Learning Companion also illustrate that while online
tutoring can partially bridge gaps, persistent technical problems and
short duration limit effectiveness (Tran et al., 2023). Public-private
partnerships with telecommunications companies could expand
broadband infrastructure, while technical support and parent
training can help ensure digital tools are effectively used. By closing
the device and connectivity gap, we can mitigate learning disruptions in future crises and support everyday learning for disadvantaged students.
2. Targeted remedial education: Students who fell behind due to the
pandemic need opportunities to catch up. I recommend scaling up
remedial and supplemental instruction programs focused on lowerSES communities. One approach is to implement free afterschool
tutoring and small-group math instruction at all schools serving large
proportions of disadvantaged students, which has been shown
internationally to help reduce learning loss (Betthäuser et al., 2023;
UNICEF, 2022). These could be funded by the central government
and staffed by qualified teachers or trained tutors. Summer bridge
programs are another strategy, which can help recover lost learning
(Lee, 2021). Taiwan could run summer programs for students identified as lagging behind in core subjects. Such programs should be
accessible (free of charge, with transportation or stipends if needed
to encourage attendance). The curriculum can reinforce fundamental
math and reading skills that were missed.
3. Strengthening support in under-resourced schools: The government
should provide additional resources to schools in lower-SES and rural
areas. Evidence shows that schools with better IT infrastructure and
teacher training transitioned more smoothly to online learning,
while others lagged behind (Lin et al., 2022; Ministry of Education,
2024). Funding could be used for hiring more teachers or teacher
aides to reduce class sizes and allow more individualized attention.
Schools could also use funds to purchase educational materials or
software that aid differentiated instruction (so teachers can cater to
varied learning levels in one classroom). Importantly, professional
development for teachers is needed to equip them with skills to
address learning gaps (e.g., training in diagnostic assessment and
remedial pedagogy, or effective use of technology in blended
learning). The government might consider granting schools with
higher proportions of disadvantaged students to implement
gap-closing interventions of their choice (with oversight). Since my
results showed a particular gap in math, resources could be directed
to improve math instruction quality (e.g., deploying math tutors to
schools in disadvantaged areas).
4. Parental and community engagement: Given the heightened role of
the home environment, policies should also focus on engaging parents and communities to support students’ learning. Surveys show
that many lower-SES students in Taiwan had little or no parental
help during remote learning (Taipei Times, 2022; Chao et al., 2024).
Thus, for lower-SES families, parenting workshops or outreach can
be provided to emphasize the importance of and strategies for supporting children’s education (e.g., setting a regular routine for
homework or reading together). Community centers or local NGOs
can partner with schools to provide study spaces with internet access
for students who lack a conducive home environment. Such safe
spaces can be particularly helpful in rural or indigenous communities
where households may be crowded or lack electricity or lighting for
study (Taipei Times, 2022). Furthermore, mentorship programs
could be developed, pairing volunteer mentors (e.g., university students) with disadvantaged pupils for academic guidance and

encouragement. This kind of social support can partially substitute
for the lack of parental academic help.
5. Monitoring and early warning systems: It is crucial to continuously
monitor achievement gaps and intervene early. Education authorities should enhance their student assessment systems to identify
learning gaps in real time. For instance, periodic low-stakes diagnostic tests in math and reading for all students can help flag if
certain schools or groups are falling behind (Kuhfeld and Lewis,
2025). Data analytics could be used to target aid: if a particular region shows a post-pandemic lag, it is necessary to send additional
support there. Establishing an early warning system that tracks
performance, attendance, and other risk indicators (e.g., struggling
to connect during remote learning) will help allocate resources
efficiently. Transparency in reporting SES disparities each year can
also keep the issue in focus and maintain accountability for closing
the gap.
6. Institutionalize digital readiness for future crises: Finally, to futureproof the education system, the government should institutionalize
the lessons learned by creating a robust digital education plan. This
plan should ensure that online learning platforms are continuously
improved and stocked with quality content, and that every teacher is
trained in hybrid teaching methods. By doing drills or practice of
brief remote learning periods, schools can be ready to adapt without
loss of learning. While this benefits all students, it especially helps
lower-SES students who suffer most when unpreparedness leads to
confusion and missed instruction. The goal is that if another
disruption occurs, the gap will not widen because both teachers and
students, regardless of SES, will have the tools and experience to
continue learning effectively. As UNICEF (2022) emphasizes,
disadvantaged students recover more slowly unless preparedness is
universal; embedding these practices systemwide will prevent SES
gaps from widening in future crises.
Collectively, these policy recommendations aim to create a more
equitable and resilient education system in Taiwan and beyond. They
recognize that simply returning to “normal” in the post-pandemic era is
not enough; special efforts are needed to lift up students who were
disproportionately harmed. Taiwan’s relatively strong performance
despite the pandemic (OECD, 2023) is commendable, but to maintain
that performance for all students, the government and stakeholders must
double down on closing the SES gap. By improving digital equity,
providing targeted academic support, and engaging communities, we
can not only make up for setbacks but also make systemic gains toward
equalizing educational opportunities.
4.2. Limitations and future directions
There are, of course, limitations to my study that should be
acknowledged. First, this study relies on PISA data, which, while
representative and high-quality, are collected cross-sectionally every
three years rather than longitudinally. As such, the analysis cannot
follow the same individuals over time, limiting the ability to observe
causal pathways or long-term trajectories. In addition, although I have
refined the SES classification to compare the bottom and top quartiles
(25 % groups) of the distribution, heterogeneity within these categories
may still be obscured. Students at the very bottom of the SES scale may
experience much more acute disadvantages than those closer to the 25th
percentile, and these nuances cannot be fully explored in the current
framework. Future research could extend this approach by employing
finer gradations of SES (e.g., deciles) or by linking PISA data with
administrative or household-level datasets to better capture withingroup variation.
Second, this study focused on standardized test scores in math as the
primary outcome. While math is widely recognized as especially sensitive to instructional disruptions (Betthäuser et al., 2023; Dorn et al.,
2020, 2021; Kuhfeld et al., 2020), it represents only one dimension of
9

T.-R. Tsou

International Journal of Educational Development 122 (2026) 103536

academic achievement. The pandemic likely affected other learning
domains such as reading, science, critical thinking, and socio-emotional
development, which were not analyzed here due to data constraints.
Future work should broaden the scope of outcomes to include multiple
academic and non-academic domains in order to understand the full
spectrum of COVID-19’s educational impact.
Third, although the DID framework provides a rigorous approach to
estimating the pandemic’s effect, it depends on the parallel trends
assumption. While pre-trend analyses suggest that the assumption is
reasonable, it cannot be definitively proven, and unobserved factors
may have influenced different SES groups unevenly. Moreover, the DID
design may not fully account for gradual, pre-existing changes in the
supplementary education market, digital access, or regional policies.
Future studies could test robustness by applying alternative methods
such as event-study designs, placebo tests, or synthetic control approaches to triangulate findings.
Fourth, Taiwan’s experience must be interpreted in light of its
distinctive context. Taiwan experienced a relatively short nationwide
school closure (primarily mid-2021), robust digital infrastructure, and a
deeply entrenched cram school sector. These conditions differentiate it
from countries with prolonged lockdowns, weaker ICT systems, or less
extensive supplementary education cultures. As such, caution is warranted in generalizing the findings beyond Taiwan. Comparative studies
across East Asia and OECD member states would help clarify whether
the mechanisms identified here, particularly the interaction between
digital access and supplementary education, apply more broadly.
Finally, this study captures only the short-term widening of SES gaps
around the pandemic years. It remains unknown whether these disparities will persist, narrow, or widen further as students advance through
high school and into university entrance exams. Longitudinal follow-up
using administrative records or panel studies could provide insights into
the durability of the observed inequalities. Moreover, the effectiveness
of remedial measures introduced in Taiwan, such as digital learning
initiatives or supplemental tutoring programs, remains to be systematically evaluated. Which interventions succeeded in narrowing gaps, and
for which groups, are questions of both scholarly and policy importance.
Beyond quantitative measures, qualitative research, including interviews with students, teachers, and parents, would enrich understanding of the lived experiences underlying the test score patterns,
shedding light on how digital divides, parental support, and school capacity shaped learning during the crisis.

The study’s contributions are twofold. First, it provides empirical
confirmation of a hypothesis that many educators in Taiwan suspected:
the shift to online education, despite the best efforts of teachers and
authorities, disproportionately impacted students from less advantaged
backgrounds (Chen and Lin, 2022; Huang, 2021; Tsai, 2020). I quantified this effect and demonstrated its significance. Second, I placed Taiwan’s experience in international context, showing parallels with other
societies while accounting for local nuances, such as the cram school
culture and early return to in-person schooling. In doing so, I offer insights that are relevant not only for Taiwan but also for other education
systems seeking to understand and address the fallout from the
pandemic on educational equity.
In conclusion, the COVID-19 pandemic served as a stress test for
education systems worldwide, and the results in Taiwan reveal that even
strong systems must pay close attention to equity. The widening of the
SES achievement gap is a warning sign. However, it is also an opportunity: it is a call to action to address long-standing disparities with
renewed urgency. The policy recommendations outlined in the present
study, from technological equity to targeted tutoring, provide a roadmap for how we can ensure that “no student is left behind” in the postpandemic recovery. By implementing these strategies, we can not only
recover lost learning but potentially emerge with a more inclusive and
robust education system. Bridging the SES achievement gap will yield
significant social benefits, ensuring that all students, regardless of
background, have the chance to reach their full academic potential. As
Taiwan and other nations move forward, the lessons learned from this
pandemic should drive innovations and commitments that narrow the
achievement gap and promote educational equity for future generations.

5. Conclusion

Not applicable

This study investigated the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the
socioeconomic achievement gap in Taiwan, with a focus on secondary
school math performance from 2006 to 2022. Using a rigorous DID
approach, I found that the pandemic period corresponded to a significant widening of the achievement gap between lower-SES and higherSES students. In concrete terms, the math score gap grew by an estimated 26.110 points during the pandemic, indicating that disadvantaged students experienced greater learning loss (more than one year of
schooling according to the OECD 2023) during the remote learning
phase. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence that
global crises like the COVID-19 pandemic can exacerbate pre-existing
educational inequities (Blundell et al., 2022; Golden et al., 2023; Gremigni, 2023; Haelermans et al., 2022; Korkmaz et al., 2022). Taiwan’s
case is especially illustrative because of its high baseline performance
and quick pandemic response; even so, hidden vulnerabilities in equity
were exposed.
