ICT-related behavioral factors mediate the relationship between
adolescents’ ICT interest and their ICT self-efficacy: Evidence from
30 countries
Xueliang Chen, Jie Hu *
Department of Linguistics, School of International Studies, Zhejiang University, Hangzhou City, Zhejiang Province, 310058, China

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Secondary education
Teaching/learning strategies
21st century abilities

In this digital era, information and communication technology (ICT) has become an essential
component of school education. Students’ self-efficacy in using ICT has been shown to contribute
to their ICT literacy. However, research on the correlates of ICT self-efficacy has been insufficient,
resulting in often oversimplified causal statements. This study investigated the relationship between adolescents’ interest in ICT and their ICT self-efficacy at age fifteen based on data from 30
Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries that participated in
the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2015. Altogether, the participants
include 201,652 students from 7708 schools, all of whom completed the ICT Familiarity Questionnaire. Data analysis was performed using multilevel mediation, with interest in ICT as the
independent variable, perceived ICT competence at age fifteen as the dependent variable, and
several ICT-related behavioral factors, including ICT use at school (USESCH), ICT use outside of
school for schoolwork (HOMESCH), ICT use outside of school for leisure (ENTUSE), and ICT use
in social interaction (SOIAICT), as the mediating variables. The results showed a significantly
positive relationship between adolescents’ interest in ICT and their ICT self-efficacy at age fifteen
and that this relationship was partially mediated by behavioral factors. ICT use at home and ICT
use at school were not significant mediators for any of the 30 OECD countries while ICT use for
recreation and ICT use for social interaction were significant for most countries. The practical
implications of these findings are discussed, and suggestions are offered.

1. Introduction
The prevalence of information and communication technology (ICT) has become one of the hallmarks of todays’ educational
landscape. Recent studies have underscored the importance of developing students’ confidence and expectations to learn and use ICT
in formal and informal settings (Hatlevik, Throndsen, Loi, & Gudmundsdottir, 2018). Students’ perception of their own ability to use
ICT in these contexts has come to be known as “ICT self-efficacy”. A heavy body of empirical evidence shows that ICT self-efficacy is a
significant determinant of ICT literacy and academic achievement (Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Friedman, & Gebhardt, 2014; Senkbeil &
Ihme, 2017; Xiao & Hu, 2019; Zhu, Yang, Macleod, Yu, & Wu, 2019); accordingly, efforts to identify potential sources of ICT

However, only limited knowledge exists on the sources of students’ self-efficacy in the context of ICT (Rohatgi, Scherer, & Hatlevik,
2016). A number of studies have revealed that students’ interest in ICT, a content-specific motivational disposition, can serve as an
intrinsic motivation to boost students’ ICT self-efficacy (Goldhammer, Gniewosz, & Johannes, 2016; Juhaňák, Zounek, Záleská, Bárta,
& Vlčková, 2019). Some even stated that more than anything, interest in ICT may be the major contributor to individuals’
self-perceived ICT competence (Juhaňák et al., 2019). Others have documented the critical role of ICT-related behavioral factors in
promoting ICT self-efficacy, such as the use of ICT in school and home settings, ICT use for leisure and recreation, and the extent to
which ICT is involved in daily social life (Areepattamannil & Santos, 2019; Xiao, Liu, & Hu, 2019), etc. As motivational factors have
been shown to be crucial antecedents of students’ ICT use (Areepattamannil & Santos, 2019; Christoph, Goldhammer, Zylka, & Hartig,
2015), interest in ICT can be reasonably assumed to increase the frequency of students’ ICT use, which then bolsters their ICT
self-efficacy.
Against this backdrop, this study has two major objectives. First, it aims to investigate the relationship between interest in ICT and
ICT self-efficacy and explore the extent to which ICT interest can predict ICT self-efficacy. Second, it seeks to elucidate the potential
mechanisms or pathways underlying this relationship, which will provide a clearer picture of the sources of ICT self-efficacy and their
interrelationships.
Given the critical role of ICT self-efficacy in boosting ICT literacy and facilitating academic achievement, it is important that the
potential sources of ICT self-efficacy be clarified to better understand this important construct. Furthermore, to the best of our
knowledge, little research has focused on ICT interest, ICT use, and ICT self-efficacy simultaneously. Therefore, our study could fill this
gap by generating new knowledge about their interrelationships.
2. Literature review
2.1. Interest in ICT and ICT self-efficacy
Both interest in ICT and ICT self-efficacy have been described as the cognitive or motivational aspects of ICT engagement, a
multifaceted construct that emphasizes the development and acquisition of ICT skills in a self-regulated way (e.g., Goldhammer,
Gniewosz, & Zylka, 2016). According to Goldhammer et al. (2016), ICT interest is conceptualized as a motivational disposition that
represents “individuals’ long-term preference for dealing with topics, tasks, or activities related to ICT” (p. 341) while ICT self-efficacy,
or perceived ICT competence, is understood as “the individual’s perception of his or her own knowledge about ICT and about how to
use it” (p. 342). The existing studies have mostly treated these two sub-constructs as predictors of achievement, instead of examining
their intra-relationships.
Within the framework of self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000), behaviors motivated by intrinsic motivations such as
personal interest (e.g. in ICT) represent the prototypical example of self-determination. The traditional literature on interest research
offers strong evidence that interest has a strong predictive power of achievement (Schiefele, 1991, 1998). This has been further
corroborated by recent studies (Alexander, 2004; Zhu et al., 2019), which have shown that a strong interest in ICT can motivate
adolescents to engage more actively in related activities or achieve better performance outcomes. When studying high-level computer
skills and ICT competence among secondary school students, Alexander (2004) found that in developing their professional expertise,
there was a strong relationship between their computer interest and acquired knowledge. In another study investigating the determinants of teenage students’ information literacy, researchers found that the ICT interest of both parents and students facilitated
students’ information literacy (Zhu et al., 2019).
With respect to ICT self-efficacy, a wealth of evidence suggests that ICT self-efficacy, among other things, is a significant determinant of ICT literacy (e.g., Marsh, Dowson, & PietschWalker, 2004; Rohatgi et al., 2016). Mumatz (2001) discovered that adolescents
who considered themselves to be more capable and skilled at using computers obtained higher scores on performance tests related to
computer use. In addition, the level of computer self-concept seems to be an indicator of students’ academic success and their future
career development. This has been shown in studies in which students with higher levels of computer self-concept obtained higher
scores in their school subjects or students’ career choices were positively affected by their computer self-concept (Gewerc & Montero,
2013; Janneck, Vincent-Höper, & Ehrhardt, 2013).
Despite the fact that ICT interest and ICT self-efficacy are both potent predictors of ICT literacy, studies investigating the intrarelationship between these two constructs are scarce. One notable study explored the age of adolescents’ first initiation of computer usage and their perceived ICT competence, namely, ICT self-efficacy, at age fifteen and included ICT interest as an intermediary
variable (Juhaňák et al., 2019). The study found that ICT interest was a significant predictor of the fifteen-year-olds’ ICT self-efficacy.
Moreover, the authors even claimed more than anything, the development of students’ computer self-efficacy may be primarily related
to their ICT interest. Their finding provided the impetus for our study.
2.2. ICT-related behavioral factors and ICT self-efficacy
ICT-related behavioral factors refer to manifest characteristics of adolescents’ ICT usage that emphasize their physical involvement
in ICT (Goldhammer et al., 2016). Such factors include the availability and diversity of ICT at home and school, the frequency of ICT
usage in and outside of school, and ICT use for purposes of recreation or social interaction, etc.
A host of studies have revealed that ICT usage are determining factors for the development of ICT self-efficacy. In a study looking
specifically at this issue, Usluel (2007) found that for both students and teachers their level and duration of ICT usage had a significant

impact on their ICT self-efficacy. After interviewing a group of post-elementary school students who were born as “digital natives”,
Kolikant (2010) concluded that despite their constant exposure to and use of ICT, the students reported lower levels of ICT self-efficacy
compared with the pre-ICT generation. Aesaert and van Braak (2014) sought to identify factors related to primary school students’
self-efficacy using a multilevel approach. They also observed that experience with ICT is a contributing factor to the students’ ICT
self-efficacy. Using data from the International Computer and Information Literacy Study 2013, Hatlevik et al. (2018) also observed
that experience with technology explained variations in ICT self-efficacy. In a more recent study, Juhaňák et al. (2019) studied the
relationship between children’s age of first computer usage and their ICT self-efficacy and autonomy at the age of fifteen based on data
from 23 European Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries. In their framework, they included
several ICT-related factors as mediating variables. Their results suggested that ICT use at home was not a significant predictor of their
ICT self-efficacy while ICT use for recreation purposes and ICT use for social interaction were significant predictors for most countries.
Based on previous review of the literature, a research gap can be filled to examine the relationships among ICT interest, ICT selfefficacy, and ICT usage simultaneously. The effort undertaken in this study not only echoes the call by researchers to highlight the role
of ICT interest (Juhaňák et al., 2019), but also seeks to clarify the mechanisms or pathways through which individual interest can exert
an effect on perceptions of competence. Therefore, it is proposed in the current study that adolescent students’ ICT interest influences
their ICT self-efficacy through their usage of ICT, including their ICT use at school, ICT use outside of school for schoolwork, and ICT
use for recreation and social interaction.
2.3. The present study
Hypothesis 1. Adolescent students’ interest in ICT is significantly positively related to their ICT self-efficacy at the age of fifteen.
As previous studies have shown a positive relationship between intrinsic motivation (e.g. interest) and achievement in general
(Alexander, 2004; Zhu et al., 2019), we hypothesized that adolescent students’ interest in ICT could positively predict their ICT
self-efficacy at age fifteen. Students’ perceived ICT competence was used as an indicator of ICT self-efficacy.
Hypothesis 2. The relationship between interest in ICT and ICT self-efficacy is partially mediated by students’ ICT use at home and
school, their ICT use for entertainment, and the involvement of ICT in their daily social interactions.
In line with the work of Christoph et al. (2015) and Juhaňák et al. (2019), we hypothesized that the relationship between interest in
ICT and ICT self-efficacy is partially mediated by behavioral factors. As Christoph et al. (2015) suggested, interest in ICT serves as an
intrinsic motivation that bolsters more active engagement in ICT-related activities. Furthermore, more experimentation with ICT in
daily life could boost students’ confidence about their ICT knowledge and skills.
Based on these two hypotheses, a diagram was drawn to reflect the relationships among the different variables (see Fig. 1). In this
diagram, X represents adolescents’ interest in ICT, and Y represents their ICT self-efficacy at fifteen years of age. Their relationship is
mediated by four mediators, represented by M1, M2, M3, and M4. The arrows indicate the direction of relationships, namely, X first
affects the mediators, which subsequently affect Y. C1 and C2 are control variables and will be explained in greater detail in Section 3.2.

3. Methods
3.1. Data
The data were obtained from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2015 (URL: http://www.oecd.org/pisa/
data/2015database/). PISA is an international large-scale assessment system that is implemented every three years to measure the
performance of 15-year-olds in reading, science, mathematics, collaborative problem solving, among other things. Altogether, 72
countries and regions participated in PISA 2015. In this study, only data from the student background questionnaire and the ICT
Familiarity Questionnaire, which was designed to identify ICT-related predictors of student performance, were used. This questionnaire was optional and administered via a computer-based mode. Students from a total of 48 countries and regions completed this
questionnaire. For this study, all OECD countries that completed the ICT questionnaire were selected given their similar economic
status and comparability of results, amounting to 30 countries in total. The resulting data include 201,652 students from 7708 schools
(see Table 1). Specific information regarding the design and composition of the questionnaires can be found in the official 2015 PISA
technical report (OECD, 2017).
3.2. Variables
A total of eight variables were used in this study.
The independent variable, namely, students’ interest in ICT, was coded as INTICT in the original dataset and developed for the first
time in the PISA 2015 report. This variable was derived based on six items, which were developed to measure students’ overall
enjoyment of ICT. Students expressed their agreement or disagreement with the statements on a four-point Likert scale, ranging from
“strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” (e.g., Question IC013Q11NA, “I am really excited discovering new digital devices or
applications.”).
The dependent variable was students’ self-perceived competence in using ICT, coded as COMPICT in the original dataset. Also
developed for the first time at PISA 2015, this variable was derived from five items that measured how competent students perceived
themselves to be in using ICT-related knowledge or skills. Students expressed their agreement or disagreement with the statements on a
four-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly disagree”.
The four mediating variables were “use of ICT at school in general” (USESCH), “ICT use outside of school for schoolwork”
(HOMESCH), “ICT use outside of school for leisure (ENTUSE)”, and “students’ ICT as a topic in social interaction” (SOIAICT). These

dependent variables, along with the contextual information, are presented in Table 1. Correlations among the variables were calculated prior to the main analysis, and all were in the acceptable range. The correlation matrices for all 30 OECD countries are provided
in the supplementary materials.
In the case of missing data, expectation maximization (EM) was used to impute missing values for all variables. Originally proposed
by Dempster, Laird, and Rubin (1977), the EM algorithm is an iterative method that produces maximum-likelihood estimates of model
parameters. The EM iteration alternates between an expectation (E) step and a maximization (M) step. The E step creates a function for
the expectation of the log-likelihood value calculated using the current estimate of the model parameters. The M step then computes
model parameters that maximize the log-likelihood value found during the E step. In simple terms, the algorithm works by creating
random values for missing data, which are then used to estimate a second set of data. The newly created data points are used to make a
better guess of the first set of data. This process repeats itself until the EM algorithm converges on a fixed point. A single value is
produced for each missing data point in the original dataset. Data imputation with EM was performed using the statistical package
SPSS 20.

In statistics, mediation analysis is a regression-based technique for testing hypotheses about how an independent variable transmits
its effect to a dependent variable through one or more intermediary variables, which are also called mediators or mediating variables.
Mediation analysis aims to reveal the underlying mechanisms or pathways by which a certain effect occurs. Multilevel mediation
analysis is a combination of multilevel modeling and simple mediation analysis.
Different forms of mediation analysis exist. The one selected in this study is called a parallel multiple mediator model, which
stipulates that the independent variable X influences the dependent variable Y directly or indirectly through the four mediators
(USESCH, HOMESCH, ENTUSE, and SOIAICT) on the condition that no one mediator exerts a causal influence on another (see Fig. 1).
An effect is said to be direct if it passes through no mediator (e.g., a1 indicates the direct effect of X on M1, and b1 indicates the direct
effect of M1 on Y controlling for X and the mediators). An indirect effect reflects the mechanism underlying a relationship, and the
number of indirect effects is the same as the number of mediators in the model. Therefore, the model includes four indirect effects,
which are represented by a1b1 (X to M1 to Y), a2b2 (X to M2 to Y), a3b3 (X to M3 to Y), and a4b4 (X to M4 to Y).
This study was primarily concerned with the direct and indirect effects of X on Y. The symbol c’ is used to represent the direct effect
of X on Y holding all four mediators constant. The indirect effects, as mentioned earlier, are a1b1, a2b2, a3b3, and a4b4. The total effects
of X on Y can be obtained when the direct and indirect effects are summed. To quantify these effects, the equations for the statistical
model are presented as follows:

4. Results
4.1. Assessing the need for a multilevel model
Data from international large-scale assessments are highly clustered in nature, with students nested in schools and schools nested
within countries. To account for the hierarchical structure of the data, a null model was first created for each OECD country with no
predictor variable included. This could determine how much of the total variance of the outcome variable can be attributed to studentlevel and school-level factors, respectively. Toward this end, an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated as a proportion
of the total variance that can be accounted for by the clustering of students in schools (Snijders & Bosker, 2012). This was also performed in R using the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012). As Table 3 suggests, almost all ICCs are smaller than 0.05, with Mexico as the
only exception. As a general rule of thumb, an ICC greater than 0.1 indicates the need for a multilevel model (Snijders & Bosker, 2012).
However, given that multilevel modeling is well suited for hierarchical data, a multilevel model was adopted nonetheless. For all
subsequent analyses, the multilevel model was run separately for each country.

dependent variable, and student gender and ESCS as the control variables. This was meant to examine the relationship between
students’ ICT interest and their ICT self-efficacy without factoring in other variables, while at the same time considering the clustering
of students in schools.

5. Discussion
The objective of the current study was two-fold: to explore the relationship between adolescents’ interest in ICT and their ICT selfefficacy at the age of fifteen and to determine the extent to which this relationship was mediated by several ICT-related behavioral
factors, including ICT use at home and school, ICT use for entertainment, and the involvement of ICT in social interaction. Accordingly,
two research hypotheses were formulated.
Hypothesis 1 postulated that a positive relationship exists between interest in ICT and ICT self-efficacy. This study provides
compelling evidence in favor of this hypothesis. According to the results in Table 3, in all 30 OECD countries a significant and positive
relationship was found between adolescents’ interest in ICT interest and their ICT self-efficacy, with the unstandardized regression
coefficients ranging from 0.2750 to 0.3936 and most with a value of approximately 0.35. According to Cohen (1988), these values
indicate a medium effect size. In the context of educational research, the strength of this effect size strongly indicates that a moderate
positive relationship exists between interest in ICT and ICT self-efficacy. This finding is in agreement with those of Christoph et al.
(2015) and Juhaňák et al. (2019). Focusing exclusively on Internet usage, Christoph et al. (2015) reported a positive relationship
between computer interest and basic computer skills (b = 0.20). Juhaňák et al. (2019) studied the relationship between the age at
which children first started using computers and their self-reported competence in ICT using multilevel mediation analysis, with ICT
interest as one of the mediating variables. The results also indicated that a positive relationship exists between ICT interest and ICT
self-efficacy.
The finding that interest in ICT facilitates adolescents’ ICT self-efficacy can be explained from several perspectives. First, it confirms
the results of previous studies in interest research in general and research on ICT-related motivational factors in particular. According
to these studies, motivational factors serve as an incentive for active engagement, which in turn translates into more experience and
knowledge (Ryan & Deci, 2000). In the same vein, as students become more interested in ICT, they are more likely to experiment with
ICT-related tools or engage in ICT-related activities, thus gaining more experience on a trial-and-error basis. Second, as students with
greater interest in ICT acquire more experience, they are also likely to increase their self-efficacy, projecting themselves as competent

users of ICT. As Juhaňák et al. (2019) noted, “the development of ICT competence is probably primarily connected to interest in ICT”
(p. 12); the results of this study provide further evidence to support the role of fostering students’ ICT interest in developing their ICT
self-efficacy.
Hypothesis 2 postulated that the relationship between adolescents’ interest in ICT and their ICT self-efficacy is partially mediated
by ICT-related behavioral factors. According to the results in Table 4, the proportion mediated ranged from 19.21% to 40.15% for all
30 OECD countries in this study. This finding confirmed that mediation did exist and that a fairly large proportion of adolescents’
differences in ICT self-efficacy could be explained by behavioral factors. Further examination of this combined influence clearly
showed that ENTUSE and SOIASCH were significant mediators of the relationship between ICT interest and ICT self-efficacy in all 30
OECD countries, whereas HOMESCH and USESCH were nonsignificant mediators in most countries. This suggests that ICT usage at
school or for school purposes explains very few differences in students’ ICT self-efficacy. In contrast, the extent to which ICT usage is a
part of students’ leisure activities and social interaction is a more important factor. These results are generally congruent with those of
Juhaňák et al. (2019).
One possible explanation for the non-significant result of school-related factors could be that while ICT usage is integrated as part of
the school curriculum, ICT usage mostly stays at a superficial level. Students are not emotionally engaged in ICT-related activities, thus
missing the opportunity for deep learning (Goldhammer et al., 2016). This finding is also in agreement with previous studies showing
that the school environment does not constitute the main context for the development of students’ ICT competence (Eurydice, 2011;
Wastiau et al., 2013). A similar argument can be made for students’ use of ICT in their leisure activities or as a part of their social
interactions. Several studies have shown that the development of ICT competence occurs not only at school but also in the home and
other contexts (Eurydice, 2011). As digital technology becomes more readily accessible in our modern society and as the age at which
children are first exposed to such technology decreases (Ólafsson, Livingstone, & Haddon, 2014), children’s contact with and use of
ICT has become commonplace even before they enter school (Marsh, Hannon, Lewis, & Ritchie, 2017). More often than not, this
increased contact with ICT is reflected not in formal learning but rather in informal learning, such as their leisure and social interaction. Their involvement in this process thus represents a prime example of deep learning, even if it may be unconscious. This thus
calls into question the role of learning engagement in the application of ICT.
6. Conclusion
ICT self-efficacy is among the most important motivational constructs that contribute to individuals’ ICT literacy and academic
achievement. Interest in ICT provides the intrinsic motivation for experimentation with ICT, which in turn could influence ICT selfefficacy. This hypothesis was investigated in this study to highlight the role of ICT interest in relation to students’ ICT self-efficacy
and to clarify the mechanisms or pathways underlying this relationship.
Accordingly, a 1-1-1 multilevel mediation analysis was adopted in this study to test this hypothesis based on data from 30 OECD
countries from the PISA 2015 datasets. The results showed that adolescents’ interest in ICT was significantly positively related to their
ICT self-efficacy and that this relationship was partially mediated by four ICT-related behavioral variables, including ICT use at school
and home (USESCH and HOMESCH) and for recreation and as part of their social interactions (ENTUSE and SOIAICT). First, these
results suggest that interest in ICT is a potent predictor of ICT self-efficacy, highlighting the seemingly trivial but critical role played by
individuals’ interest in ICT. Second, the results confirm our hypothesis that the effect of ICT interest on ICT self-efficacy was to a large
extent mediated by ICT-related behaviors, suggesting that experimentation with ICT is a major contributor to the development of
individuals’ perceptions of their own ability. In summary, this study suggests that in developing adolescents’ ICT self-efficacy, catering
to their motivational needs is perhaps as important a factor as their use of ICT and that both behavioral and motivational factors should
be considered in practice.
