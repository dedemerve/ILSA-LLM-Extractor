A B S T R A C T
Automated scoring (AS) has become increasingly prevalent in educational measurement. However, applying it to 
international reading assessments remains challenging, particularly due to the length and complexity of the 
required prompting, driven by the need to include lengthy reading passages and detailed scoring guides. Pro­
cessing these lengthy inputs results in high computational costs and may impede the performance of large 
language models (LLMs). This study explored the potential of optimizing AS with prompt compression using 
OpenAI's LLM, GPT-4o. Our results show that prompt compression significantly reduces the length of reading 
passages and scoring guides while maintaining their essential content. Reading passages and scoring guides were 
compressed to approximately 18% and 15% of their original lengths, respectively. Despite this substantial 
compression, the AS showed remarkable performance, with an accuracy of 92.87% and a kappa score of 0.8041, 
closely approximating the results obtained without compression. These findings suggest optimizing AS with 
prompt compression can improve its efficiency and scalability, particularly in international reading assessments.
1. Introduction
Automated scoring (AS) has become an essential tool in educational 
measurement, utilizing computational linguistic methods and statistical 
models to assess examinee responses to open-ended test items (Lottridge 
et al., 2023). Early implementations of AS date back to the 1960s, and 
these primarily focused on simplifying tasks such as grading 
multiple-choice responses, and mostly aimed at reducing teacher 
workload (Foltz et al., 2020). Initially, the focus was on streamlining 
repetitive tasks rather than assessing more complex aspects of student 
performance. Page (1966) and Carbonell (1970) explored the potential 
of computers for scoring essays by analyzing rudimentary features such 
as grammar, syntax, and structure by using early statistical models and 
rule-based systems. A significant leap came in later decades with the rise 
of machine learning and advances in natural language processing (NLP). 
These developments made AS more flexible and powerful, allowing it to 
expand beyond simple grading to more complex tasks from essay scoring 
to spoken response analysis (Shermis & Burstein, 2003, 2013).
Research has explored a wide range of applications for AS, including 
scoring constructed response (CR) items (e.g., Burrows et al., 2015; 
Riordan et al., 2020), essays (e.g., Shermis, 2014; Shermis & Burstein, 
2006), mathematical equations (Fife, 2013), and spoken responses 
(Bernstein et al., 2000; Xi et al., 2008). These explorations expanded in 
various educational contexts and domains, including K-12 assessments, 
licensure, and certification programs, particularly in evaluating profi­
ciency in writing, reading, and mathematics (Foltz et al., 2020).
The rapid growth of artificial intelligence (AI) has led large-scale 
assessments to systematically incorporate AI into AS. The National 
Center for Education Statistics (NCES) recently held a competition to 
address challenges in scoring open-ended math questions. The NAEP 
Automated Math Scoring Challenge tasked participants with building 
models to predict human scoring (HS) for student responses to ten math 
items for fourth and eighth grades, aiming to create reliable and unbi­
ased AS methods (Morris et al., 2024).
While the operational use of AS in international large-scale assess­
ments (ILSAs) like TIMSS and PISA is relatively limited (Shin et al., 
2024), AS has begun to be applied to diverse types of responses, 
including graphical and text-based responses. For example, TIMSS 2019 
included items requiring students to produce grid-based or freehand 
geometry and quantitative skills drawings. von Davier et al. (2023)
demonstrated the successful implementation of AS for such graphical 
responses using convolutional neural networks (CNN), achieving up to 
97.5 % agreement between human and machine scoring. Subsequently, 
this work was expanded to cover multiple dichotomous and polytomous 
items from TIMSS 2019; Tyack et al., 2024). By manually reviewing 
responses where human and CNN scores differed, the study revealed that 
AS models sometimes outperformed human raters, suggesting that AS 
can be as accurate, if not more so, than HS. This level of performance

the graphical responses, text-based response scoring in ILSAs also 
demonstrated significant progress. Jung et al. (2022) successfully 
applied AI-driven scoring to student responses from TIMSS 2019, 
achieving high levels of agreement between human and automated 
scores by showcasing a novel approach to training the model on data 
weighted based on IRT-based scores.
Moreover, integrating large language models (LLMs) brought trans­
formative advancements to AS in large-scale assessments, particularly 
by facilitating multilingual AS in ILSAs. As a considerable number of 
existing NLP tools are most proficient in high-resource languages such as 
English (Sawicki et al., 2023), a viable strategy for implementing AS in 
ILSAs involving over 60 languages is to translate non-English languages 
into English to ensure optimal performance. Technologies like Meta's No 
Language Left Behind, Google Translate, which now supports 133 lan­
guages, including 24 low-resource ones (Bapna et al., 2022), and 
OpenAI's GPT models have expanded the potential for multilingual AS 
(Hao et al., 2024). These models offer context-aware translations, 
allowing more accurate handling of new languages into translation en­
gines. Jung et al. (2024) utilized Google Translate API and GPT-3.5 for 
translating responses from eight countries in six different languages and 
demonstrated that machine scoring achieved a high correlation with 
human raters. This outcome showed the efficacy of artificial neural 
networks (ANNs) trained and tested on ChatGPT-translated responses, 
demonstrating that LLMs can outperform traditional translation engines 
by providing higher-context translation, which is crucial for accurate 
scoring across different linguistic contexts. For instance, AS has suc­
cessfully been applied to short-answer CR items in TIMSS across 42 
languages, with unsupervised learning utilizing LLMs showing prom­
ising results (Jung et al., 2025).
However, adapting these LLM-based models to score responses from 
PIRLS (Progress in International Reading Literacy Study) presents 
unique challenges due to the nature of the reading assessment. PIRLS 
requires students to read one literary and one informational passage and 
respond to a block of questions aligned with these passages. This design 
aims to authentically assess students’ reading comprehension skills, 
using stimuli that closely resemble published texts (Mullis & Martin, 
2019). To implement AS effectively, these questions from a multilingual 
sample need to be part of the prompt for the LLM, together with their 
corresponding reading passage and detailed scoring guides (SGs) 
necessary for evaluating the student responses. This combination results 
in a significantly longer and more complex prompt than a typical prompt 
for items with shorter context, such as those in mathematics or science 
assessments.
A notable limitation of using LLMs for such tasks is the maximum 
context length and the models’ sensitivity to input length. When are 
LLMs accessed through APIs, the user agreement is often based on a by- 
token pricing model, where the cost of processing increases with the 
number of tokens in the input and output. Also, longer prompts require 
more time due to the extensive computational cost of input processing. 
Extensive prompts that include reading passages, questions, and SGs can 
lead to significant increases in token counts, subsequently inflating the 
cost of each API call. This cost factor becomes especially important in 
large-scale assessments where tens of thousands of responses need to be 
processed. Particularly, ILSAs operate at an immense scale, involving 
substantial volumes of multilingual data from diverse countries and 
languages (Okubo et al., 2023). This makes more efficient AS crucial for 
reducing infrastructure and operational costs.
A key challenge, therefore, is the need to handle these long form 
prompts efficiently. To address this, researchers explored various stra­
tegies for prompt optimization and compression (e.g., Jiang et al., 2023; 
Chuang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024) in various other domains outside of 
AS. These strategies offer significant opportunities to improve the per­
formance of AS systems, especially for reading assessments or other 
testlet-based assessments where each question is associated with a 
(long) passage. Zhang et al. (2025) also found that prompt compression 
facilitates LLM's real-world applications by retaining most critical in­
formation while achieving lower time costs and minimal memory con­
sumption. By reducing prompt length and complexity while preserving 
the essentials of the task, prompt compression can reduce the compu­
tational and logistical demands of large-scale scoring. Moreover, opti­
mizing the prompt structure can result in cost savings by reducing the 
number of tokens in each prompt when using LLMs through APIs. This 
reduction in token numbers lowers the cost per API call, thus improving 
the economic feasibility of AS in large-scale applications. This paper 
explores how prompt compression and summarization techniques can 
improve the scalability, efficiency, and accuracy of AS in ILSAs.
2. Background
The context window size of LLMs has expanded considerably in 
recent years, expanding the utility of these models for increasingly 
complex tasks. Early models such as GPT-1 and GPT-2 had relatively low 
token limits, limited to around a thousand tokens, as they were primarily 
intended for processing shorter contexts. In contrast, more recent ad­
vancements have significantly expanded these capabilities. For example, 
Google's Gemini now supports context windows of up to 1 million tokens 
(Reid et al., 2024), Anthropic's Claude 3 models can manage up to 200, 
000 tokens (Anthropic, 2024), and OpenAI's GPT-4-Turbo accommo­
dates 128,000 tokens, equivalent to around 300 pages of text, in a single 
input (OpenAI, 2023). These expanded context windows allow pro­
cessing longer prompts, which are commonly needed in applications like 
document summarization, legal and financial document analysis (Koh 
et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023), and chat agents that 
maintain conversation history (Packer et al., 2023).
However, the use of long-context models brings its own set of diffi­
culties. Long prompts, which usually include comprehensive in­
structions, multiple examples, and detailed task definitions, introduce 
challenges related to inference speed, computational cost, and memory 
usage (Jha et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). These issues are particularly 
prevalent for LLMs accessed through APIs that employ token-based 
pricing, where long prompts can become extremely costly. The sub­
stantial costs pose a barrier to cost-effectiveness, making practical 
implementation challenging, especially for large-scale evaluations 
(Larionov & Eger, 2025). In addition, it has been observed that the 
reasoning abilities of LLMs can diminish when processing very long 
inputs due to the “lost in the middle” effect, where the model struggles to 
utilize relevant context in the middle of a large input (Liu et al., 2024). 
To address these concerns, research has focused on prompt engineering 
methods such as prompt compression and prompt optimization to lower 
the complexity of the input.
Prompt compression is a technique used to reduce the length of a 
long prompt by extracting only the most critical information. This idea is 
grounded in the concept that natural language is inherently repetitive 
and redundant (Shannon, 1951) and thus can be compressed. This can 
be achieved through soft prompt and hard prompt compression 
methods. Soft prompt compression involves encoding prompt informa­
tion into continuous vectors within the model's embedding space to 
optimize the structure of the prompt in a flexible way. This approach 
uses a trainable encoder-decoder architecture, where the encoder 
compresses the prompt into a shorter sequence of specialized tokens, 
and the decoder interprets these tokens to generate responses. GIST (Mu 
et al., 2024) and AutoCompressor (Chevalier et al., 2023) are examples 
of implementing soft prompt techniques, and they achieve high 
compression rates by utilizing attention mechanism adjustments and 
task-specific embeddings. This structure allows soft prompts to maintain 
semantic integrity while reducing the computational load of lengthy 
prompts (Li et al., 2024). However, because soft prompts are highly 
specialized, they often require fine-tuning specific to each model or task, 
which can limit their generalizability across diverse applications (Jha 
et al., 2024). While embedding-based compression methods are effective

in compressing the prompts, they also require significant changes to the 
inference pipeline, which restrict their applicability for LLM models 
used via API access. Additionally, soft prompts are less interpretable for 
humans than hard prompts, as the resulting tokens are not directly 
readable, limiting transparency and explainability (Li et al., 2024).
In contrast, hard prompt compression involves direct manipulation 
of the natural language text of the prompt. Rather than embedding 
prompts as continuous vectors, hard prompt techniques aim to compress 
prompts by removing low information tokens or summarizing text while 
preserving essential meaning and readability. Li, Dong, et al. (2023)
proposed what they call Selective Context. This method quantifies the 
informativeness of each token using self-information or token-level 
perplexity metrics and eliminates those that contribute minimally to 
the overall semantic meaning. LLMLingua (Jiang et al., 2023), a prom­
inent example of hard prompt compression, follows a coarse-to-fine 
approach that iteratively compresses prompts at the token level, 
achieving substantial token reduction with minimal impact on model 
performance without requiring modifications to the model's architec­
ture. These hard prompt methods are especially useful for applications 
where the LLM must interpret natural language text such as black-box 
models that only accept natural language input (Jiang et al., 2023; 
Pan et al., 2024).
In addition to token-level removal, summarization has emerged as an 
effective form of hard prompt compression. Unlike selective token 
removal, summarization transforms the prompt by rephrasing and 
condensing key information, creating a coherent, compact version that 
preserves the main ideas (Koh et al., 2022). Recent studies have exam­
ined “approximate compression,” or the compression of text to retain 
only core semantic information when exact reconstruction is unnec­
essary. This approach has proven effective in balancing information 
retention with a significantly reduced token count, as shown by exper­
iments with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, which achieve high levels of semantic 
preservation even with substantial compression (Gilbert et al., 2023). 
Summarization as compression has shown promise in applications 
where clarity and readability are essential, such as contexts requiring 
user-facing outputs and chatbot development (Dinesj¨o & Floreteng, 
2024). Since summarization produces a streamlined prompt in natural 
language, it ensures that the essential details are both retained and 
easily understandable. This method aligns well with black-box LLMs 
used via APIs, as it allows users to shorten prompts meaningfully as an 
additional pre-processing step without major modifications to the 
model's inference pipeline.
Prompt optimization goes beyond the reduction of the input size to 
enhance the quality and effectiveness of prompts used with LLMs. Key 
approaches in prompt optimization include chain-of-thought (CoT; Wei 
et al., 2022) prompting, and meta-prompting (Reynolds & McDonell, 
2021; OpenAI, 2024). CoT prompting structures complex tasks as a 
sequence of smaller logically connected steps within the prompt. This 
method is particularly useful in tasks requiring multi-step reasoning, 
such as mathematical problem-solving and commonsense reasoning 
(Wei et al., 2022). Recently, Lee et al. (2024) implemented CoT as an 
LLM reasoning strategy in the AS of student responses from a science 
assessment. They demonstrated that CoT paired with item stem and 
scoring rubrics significantly improved scoring accuracy compared with 
non-CoT reasoning.
Within this framework, meta-prompting involves creating a robust 
guideline that instructs the model on performing the task and generating 
the response. Meta-prompting, a technique in which a language model is 
directed to generate or refine prompts for itself or other models, allows 
the model to review an existing prompt, identify potential improve­
ments, and suggest an optimized or modified prompt accordingly (Ye 
et al., 2023). Some studies further refined this approach by assigning 
specific labels to each step of the reasoning path and using a step-aware 
verifier that evaluates each step's contribution to the accuracy of the 
final answer thus controlling the reasoning process of the model (Li, Lin, 
et al., 2023). Overall, meta-prompts optimize the way models interpret 
instructions and interact with input, often resulting in more reliable 
outputs.
Despite advancements in prompt compression and optimization, 
these strategies have seen limited application in AS for educational as­
sessments. AS tasks, especially those involving reading comprehension 
and other complex assessments, often require processing extended in­
puts such as passages, questions, and detailed rubrics. Lee et al. (2024)
introduced prompt optimization methods and discussed the challenges 
of balancing accessibility, technical complexity, and explainability in 
LLM-driven AS. Their tailored prompt engineering approach, which 
includes prompt writing, reviewing, validating, revising, and testing 
(WRVRT), achieved consistent accuracy across different proficiency 
levels, enhancing the interpretability and usability of such tools for 
educators. However, no other study has yet explored prompt compres­
sion and specific AS engineering techniques.
Integrating prompt compression and optimization strategies into AS 
can greatly improve AS systems' scalability and economic feasibility. 
Tailoring these techniques for reading comprehension and other com­
plex tasks could enable models to handle lengthy, nuanced prompts 
more efficiently. This paper aims to explore how these advancements in 
prompt engineering can be adapted to improve the efficiency and pre­
cision of AS in multilingual large-scale educational assessments that 
require mixed language prompting.
3. Method
3.1. Dataset
The PIRLS assessment, conducted every five years since 2001, eval­
uates the reading comprehension skills of fourth-grade students in more 
than 50 countries. It defines reading literacy as the ability to understand 
and utilize written language forms required by society and/or valued by 
the individual (Mullis & Martin, 2019). The PIRLS framework empha­
sizes two primary reading purposes: reading for literary experience and 
reading to acquire and use information (Mullis & Martin, 2019). Students 
engage with narrative fiction through literary reading, drawing upon 
their personal experiences or knowledge. In contrast, informational texts 
are designed to convey information on various subjects, including sci­
entific, historical, and social topics.
This study used five 1-point CR items from the digital PIRLS 2021 
assessment. Since 1-point CR items are a common format in ILSAs, these 
items serve as a valuable testbed for exploring AS feasibility. For 
context, 1-point items (110 items) comprised 65% of all written CR 
items in digital PIRLS 2021. Four items were based on literary passages 
(i.e., Passages A and C) while one item was derived from an informa­
tional passage, Passage B. We selected medium and difficult passages, 
which are more complex and lengthier than easy passages and often 
require more complex open responses in the items associated with the 
texts. These medium-to-difficult passages serve as an ideal ground for 
evaluating GPT-4o's capability to succinctly compress complex text 
without losing essential information. The items used in this study are 
“trend items”, which are kept secure for re-administration in future as­
sessments (Fishbein et al., 2024). We selected these items for AS prep­
aration for the PIRLS 2026, where they will be re-used. To ensure item 
security, the items will remain unpublished. We also have ensured data 
privacy through enterprise-level API that does not utilize data for 
training purposes (OpenAI, 2025). The AS pipeline fully complies with 
the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) guidelines (European 
Union, 2016). Table 1 provides a description of the items.
The dataset consisted of multilingual responses from all participating 
countries in digital PIRLS 2021, covering 27 countries and 29 distinct 
languages (see Appendix Table A). PIRLS 2021 was the transition year in 
which only about 50% of the countries participated in the computer- 
based test, while the other half continued to test on paper. Still, the 
data contained several thousand responses per question across these 29 
languages. Given the scope and the diversity of the dataset, as well as

3.2. Prompt compression & optimization
The generalized scoring prompt template for PIRLS was designed to 
include four main components: task instruction, reading passage, 
question, and SG as shown in Table 2.
First, the task instruction outlines task-specific directives and format 
constraints. Initially, it specifies that GPT-4o will evaluate multilingual 
responses from an international reading assessment, steering its focus 
from a general domain to scoring international reading assessment re­
sponses. Our task instruction is based on the Zero-Shot-CoT, which does 
not require input-output examples and is thus task-agnostic (Kojima 
et al., 2022). This method enhances the generalizability of our scoring 
prompt template, enabling its application to any 1-point CR items in 
PIRLS. The Zero-Shot-CoT instruction includes four steps: translation, 
scoring, validation, and output construction, as shown in Fig. 1. We 
incorporated a validation step to mitigate potential hallucinations, 
following the definition provided by Shakil et al. (2024). GPT-4o occa­
sionally produces hallucinated outputs, such as providing a correct 
answer instead of translating the original student response, particularly 
when responses are incorrect or very short. Consistent with prior 
research, we integrated the internal validation step to reduce these 
potential hallucinations before GPT-4o generates its final output. To 
standardize the outputs, the format was constrained to JSON, with 
structure specified as {“type”: “json_object”} in the API call.
The second component, a reading passage, can be included in its full 
original form or as a summarized version. Summarization, one of the 
primary focuses of this study, involved utilizing GPT-4o to compress the 
original passage while retaining all essential information necessary for 
accurately answering the given question. This method, called query- 
based text summarization, generates a tailored summary that ad­
dresses a specific query, typically a question of the item in a question- 
answering context (Ding et al., 2024; Yu & Han, 2022). In this study, 
GPT-4o produced a question-specific summary for each item using the 
Zero-Shot-CoT summarization prompt, as detailed in Fig. 2. The initial 
prompt included the first two steps (1. Read the Passage; 2. Summarize), 
but the final step (3. Final Output) was added to our final model to 
standardize the format of the summary output. A single summary was 
created for each item, which was consistently used to evaluate all 
multilingual responses for that item. Example passage summarization is 
provided in Appendix A. Using GPT-4o, we created a mock passage titled 
“Tashi and the Great Adventure” along with two mock items, “What does 
Rinzin do before climbing up?” (Mock_Item 1), and “Why is it important 
that Rinzin wants to help Tashi?” (Mock_Item 2). This was necessary as all 
CR items in this study are currently secured for use in future 
assessments.
The third component, the item's question, was directly incorporated 
into the scoring prompt template without any modification. The final 
component, the SG, required particular attention in this study; instruc­
tion on how to score responses could be used in their original version. 
However, these SG were designed for human raters. These guides often 
include extensive information such as detailed descriptions, extensive 
examples, and nuanced explanations. While this level of detail can 
benefit human interpretation, it may be unnecessarily lengthy and less 
effective for AS. To address this, we focused on developing a refined SG 
that streamlined and optimized the original SG to make it more suitable 
for use with LLMs. This refinement reduces the computational and 
resource demands, enhancing the practical application of AS in PIRLS.
Original SGs for 1-point CR items in PIRLS generally consist of four 
parts: descriptions of acceptable responses, examples of acceptable re­
sponses, descriptions of unacceptable responses, and examples of un­
acceptable responses. Fig. 3 illustrates an original SG for Mock_Item 1, 
“What does Rinzin do before climbing up?”. We hypothesized that the 
refined SG could be restructured into two core components: descriptions 
of acceptable and unacceptable responses. Given that the descriptions 
for unacceptable responses were largely consistent across 1-point CR 
items in the original SGs, we chose to use a standard description (i.e., 
**[Score: 0 ]**: Assign this score if the response is off-topic or fails to address 
the question.”) rather than maintaining varied item-specific descriptions. 
Additionally, item-specific examples were excluded to simplify the 
format and reduce redundancy. This Zero-Shot method improves LLMs’ 
generality and flexibility while providing high accuracy and reliability 
in text classification (Chae & Davidson, 2025; Puri & Catanzaro, 2019; 
T¨ornberg, 2023).
Specifically, we compared two summarization techniques for 
refining original SGs: extractive and abstractive methods. The extractive 
technique (ET) identifies and selects key sentences directly from the 
original SG, while the abstractive technique (AT) creates novel senten­
ces by rephrasing or restructuring the content. These refined SGs were 
labeled as RefinedSG_ET and RefinedSG_AT, respectively.
For RefinedSG_ET, our AS pipeline employed custom Python func­
tions to systematically parse and extract acceptable response de­
scriptions directly from the original SG. For RefinedSG_AT, the pipeline 
integrated GPT-4o for generative refinement, where a novel acceptable 
response description is created by rephrasing or improving the read­
ability of the original acceptable response description. Specifically, Py­
thon scripts were used to extract the original SG's acceptable response 
description and then processed by GPT-4o for refinement. This process

prompts were reviewed to ensure the original meanings or instructions 
were preserved before finalization. The generalized scoring prompt

3.3. Automated scoring with scoring prompt templates
In our initial analysis, we compared a baseline model (using original 
reading passages and SGs) to Model 1, which used a refined version of 
the SGs (see Table 3). We chose two CR items, Items 1 and 2, which 
required complex narrative interpretation from four countries where 
native speakers were available. The GPT-4o temperature was set to 0 for 
maximum determinism. We hypothesized that a refined SG, with 
simplified descriptions and less detailed information than the original, 
would be effective for AS while reducing input complexity. The results 
supported this: Model 1 showed a notable improvement in accuracy (i. 
e., human-machine score agreements), increasing from 89.97% to 
85.84% (baseline) to 93.50% and 92.87% for Items 1 and 2, respec­
tively. This initial finding, combined with the prior research that LLMs 
process natural language written for humans in different ways (Chang 
et al., 2024; Schr¨oder et al., 2025), confirmed our focus on refining SGs.

incorporate passage and SG compression step-by-step. This broader 
investigation allowed us to test whether compressing multiple

both. These models employing refined SGs (RefinedSG_ET for Models 1 
and 2 and RefinedSG_AT for Model 3) were tested on five CR items 
across 26 countries. The key difference between Models 1 and 2 is the 
reading passage used; Model 1 used the original passage, while Model 2 
used a summarized version. In contrast, Models 2 and 3 differ based on 
the type of refined SG: Model 2 used RefinedSG_ET, and Model 3 
employed RefinedSG_AT. This stepwise approach from baseline to 
Model 1 enabled us to isolate the impact of SG compression. In contrast, 
progression to Models 2 and 3 allowed us to evaluate the compressing of 
the reading passages and SGs.
3.4. Post-processing steps
After the model evaluations, two post-processing steps were deemed 
necessary: fixing misformatted outputs and correcting hallucinations. 
Although GPT-4o generally generated appropriately formatted JSON 
outputs, occasional errors occurred. For example, instead of the required 
format {“He helps Tashi out of the ditch”: “Score: 1”}, misformatted 
outputs appeared as {“He helps Tashi out of the ditch. score 1”} or {“He 
helps Tashi out of the ditch.” (tab) “Score: 1”}. These errors were auto­
matically identified and corrected using custom Python scripts inte­
grated into our AS pipeline.
Even though GPT-4o rarely generates hallucinations, they can still 
arise, especially in very brief or off-topic responses. For example, GPT- 
4o might generate a hallucinated output such as{“He helps Tashi out of 
the ditch”: “Score: 1”} for a Turkish response “hendek.” (meaning ditch)

3.5. Evaluation metrics
Multiple metrics were employed for model evaluation: compression 
ratio (R), accuracy, Cohen's kappa (κ), and hallucination rates.
Compression ratio (R) was calculated for each model by comparing 
its token count to that of a baseline. Specifically, we considered the 
length of the reading passage (original vs. summary) and SG lengths 
(original vs. refined versions). A smaller R indicates a greater reduction 
of the original length after compression. For example, R = 0.20 means 
the compressed text retains 20% of the original length, reflecting 80% 
compression.

Accuracy, a commonly used metric, is defined as the percentage of 
exact agreement between human and machine scores. This metric pro­
vides a clear and intuitive measure of a model's performance. Further­
more, because the difference in impact between false positives and false 
negatives is relatively small for 1-point items (binary classification) in 
ILSAs, overall accuracy serves as a primary metric.
Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960) measures inter-rater reliability beyond 
chance, calculated as below: 
k = po −pe
1 −pe 
where po is the relative observed agreement among raters, and pe rep­
resents the hypothetical probability of agreement by chance. A kappa 
value of 0 indicates agreement by chance, while 1 means perfect 
agreement between raters. As Cohen's kappa is widely accepted for bi­
nary classification tasks, we chose to use this metric for our analysis of 1- 
point items, which function as a binary scoring.
Hallucination rates, defined as the percentage of hallucinated re­
sponses, were used to check the reliability of the model. Our detection 
method relied on the length ratio between translated and original re­
sponses. For Latin alphabet languages like English and German, a 
response is flagged as hallucinations when it received a machine score of 
1, but the translated response is more than 2.2 times the length of the 
original, indicating the likely addition of non-existent information. For 
non-Latin alphabet languages such as Chinese and Korean, the threshold 
is set at 20 times the original length. This accounts for the natural 
expansion when translating from character-based languages. Once a 
hallucination is detected, the response is flagged, and its machine score 
is automatically corrected to 0. Monitoring these rates allowed us to 
investigate whether the compression-based models, which utilized a 
simplified context, were more prone to hallucination than the baseline 
model. After the optimal model was identified through primary per­
formance metrics, confusion matrices were analyzed to examine the 
sources of misalignment between human and machine scores.

4.1.2. Model performance
Table 6 provides the accuracy and κ value for each model. All three 
models demonstrated consistently high performance across the five CR 
items. On average, Model 3 achieved the highest accuracy at 92.87%, 
while the baseline model and Model 2 had the highest κ of 0.82. Notably, 
Model 3's accuracy on Items 3 and 5 was comparable to the baseline 
model, yet a gap existed in their κ values. This discrepancy arises 
because these two items are very easy, with item difficulties of 0.76 and 
0.81, which led to a highly skewed score distribution. Since kappa relies 
on chance agreement and marginal probabilities, such skewed distri­
butions can suppress its values. Given the low-stakes nature of the PIRLS 
assessment and the binary scoring of 1-point items, we focused on ac­
curacy as our primary metric. This metric provides a direct and practical 
snapshot of AS performance.
Table 7 presents the hallucination rates for the three models across 
the items. All models exhibited minimal hallucinations, with the base­
line model having the lowest rate at 0.05%, followed by Model 3 
(0.12%). Hallucinations typically occurred with very short or mean­
ingless original responses, where the average length was 8.47 charac­
ters. Interestingly, Model 2 showed a hallucination rate of 1.69% with 
Item 2, arising in very brief, incomplete, and incorrect responses (human 
score = 0), particularly in Arabic (43.18%, 19 responses) and English 
(36.36%, 16 responses). The original responses that led to hallucinations 
averaged 10.21 characters in Arabic and 5.75 characters in English, all 
receiving a score of 0 from human raters. The corresponding outputs 
were much longer: the translated Arabic responses averaged 60.11 
characters, while the grammar-corrected English responses averaged 
68.19. Hallucinations in English usually occur when the original 
response is incomplete, such as GPT-4o generating “Her help shows the 
importance of teamwork” from “Her help.” These findings align with 
previous studies indicating that LLMs are prone to hallucinations when 
processing ambiguous inputs with omissions, insufficient context, or 
random words (Huang et al., 2023; Reddy et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2023).
Multi-metric evaluation revealed that Model 3's performance closely 
approximates the baseline. This finding is notable because Model 3 
achieved this comparability despite significant compression, resulting in 
considerable improvements in efficiency and cost-effectiveness. There­
fore, we conducted an in-depth review of Model 3 to identify the pri­
mary sources of misalignment between HS and AS.
4.2. In-depth review of AS with prompt compression
In this analysis, we closely examined the agreement between HS and 
AS by investigating misalignments. The goal was to gain a deeper un­
derstanding of whether AS is more lenient or stricter than HS, or if the 
False Positive and False Negative rates vary randomly across items and 
countries. Additionally, we assessed the running time and cost per item 
to evaluate the cost-efficiency of our AS.
4.2.1. Sources of misalignment
We examined the major sources of misalignment based on the 
confusion matrices (Fig. 8), which categorize responses into True Posi­
tive, False Negative, True Negative, and False Positive. For the five CR 
items, the False Positive Rates (FPR) range from 3.08% to 7.29%, while 
the False Negative Rates (FNR) range from 1.57% to 4.27%. These rates 
serve as valuable indicators for identifying the main causes of 
misalignment, which can originate from both HS and AS issues (see 
Table 8).
A key factor contributing to the observed misalignment between 
human and machine scores is inconsistencies in HS. Notable FPRs 
(human score = 0; machine score = 1) were observed for items that 
required students to construct their interpretations (Item 1 & Item 2) or 
make inferences beyond the texts (Item 5): 5.02% in Item 1, 7.29% in 
Item 2, and 5.40% in Item 5. In these cases, GPT-4o demonstrated 
greater consistency than human raters in scoring responses where the 
correct answer or key idea is expressed in various ways. For example, a 
mock response “She shows why working together matters” is considered a 
correct answer according to the original SG. While GPT-4o consistently 
awarded a score of 1 to semantically similar responses like “Her help 
shows the importance of teamwork,” “Partnering with others is crucial,” and 
“It shows working as a team is necessary,” human raters showed incon­
sistency, mostly awarding a score of 1, but occasionally giving a score of 
0. This shows that HS can vary even for semantically similar responses, 
potentially due to rater effects or differences in human rater cognition 
(Attali, 2014; Livingston, 2009; McClellan, 2010; Myford & Wolfe, 
2003; Zhang, 2013).
Next, AS inconsistencies can also lead to misalignment, affecting 
both FNR and FPR. Specifically, GPT-4o demonstrated inconsistency in 
scoring responses with subtle nuances. Consider the mock responses “He 
discovered nothing valuable” versus “He discovered nothing” (Item 3), and 
“It transported smoothly” versus “It transported over a bridge” (Item 4). 
Human raters typically assigned a score of 1 to the former responses and 
a score of 0 to the latter. While GPT-4o generally aligned with human 
raters for the former responses, it assigned a score of 1 to the latter as 
well. This indicates that GPT-4o may struggle to capture the nuanced 
complexity outlined in the SGs in this case. Unlike automated systems, 
human raters can discern language nuances and provide more context- 
sensitive evaluations. LLMs like GPT-4o may require further tuning to 
better grasp linguistic subtleties and SG intricacies (Latif & Zhai, 2024; 
Weyssow et al., 2024). Also, for Item 3, GPT-4o sometimes had difficulty 
translating responses that were heavily misspelled or written in low- 
resource languages such as Arabic, Croatian, or Czech, assigning a 
score of 0 to these untranslatable responses.

4.2.2. Running time & cost
As Table 9 illustrates, AS based on the compression (Model 3) ach­
ieved a remarkable cost reduction, with an average running cost of 
approximately $3.62 per item, compared to the baseline model's cost of 
$15-17. While Model 3's running time was only slightly shorter, this 76% 
cost reduction is particularly significant given that ILSAs include many 
1-point CR items (110 items in PIRLS 2021). Additionally, compression 
for passages and SGs was conducted once for each item at a cost of only 
around $0.50 and requiring 1-2 s. Such improved cost efficiency pre­
sents a highly promising solution for reducing the substantial opera­
tional expenses associated with HS in ILSAs (Benette, 1991; Braun, 
1990; Ward & Bennett, 2012).
5. Discussion
5.1. Prompting strategy for AS in PILRS
A primary challenge in the application of LLMs for AS in reading 
assessments such as PIRLS is processing long reading passages and 
detailed SGs. This can lead to high computational costs and negatively 
impact the performance of LLMs (Huang et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2024; 
Jiang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). Additionally, designing effective 
prompts for complex tasks such as AS requires substantial human effort 
and time (Lee et al., 2024; Reynolds & McDonell, 2021). AS demands 
intricate reasoning, particularly due to the complexity and subjectivity 
of SGs (Madnani & Cahill, 2018). This study proposes optimizing 
GPT-4o with prompt compression to address these issues.
5.1.1. Prompt compression
This study suggests that prompt compression reduces the complexity 
of AS by significantly shortening lengthy inputs. Our findings indicate 
that Model 3, which utilizes question-specific summaries and Refi­
nedSG_AT, demonstrated impressive performance. The lengths of the 
passages and SGs were reduced to approximately 18% and 15% of the 
originals, respectively. Despite this significant reduction, it achieved 
similar or even superior performance (accuracy = 92.87%, κ = 0.8041) 
compared to Model 1 (accuracy = 92.19%, κ = 0.8059), which used the 
original passages.
First, aligning with prior research (Ding et al., 2024), our 
question-based summarization generates high-quality, customized 
summaries by focusing on question-specific content and omitting 
extraneous details. For instance, in Appendix A, the input was reduced 
from 842 to 138 tokens, compressing the passage to 16.39% of its 
original size. Specifically, it focused on the question of Mock_Item 2, 
Rinzin's willingness to help, while filtering out irrelevant details such as 
the appearance of Tashi and Rinzin's village, the significance of 
becoming a farmer for Tashi, or the path to the carrot patch. Moreover, 
the summary explicitly highlights the key information at the end: Rin­
zin's help demonstrates his bravery and readiness to become a farmer.
Next, our results indicate that original SGs could be efficiently 
compressed while preserving their content. RefinedSG_AT (Ravg = 0.15) 
appears to be the most effective for AS compared to RefinedSG_ET (Ravg 
= 0.25). For instance, in Fig. 4, the RefinedSG_AT method compressed 
the original SGs by shortening the descriptions and removing examples. 
Redundant phrases were rephrased or removed: “reached or tugged the 
ladder” became “interacted,” and “before climbing” was eliminated. 
Ambiguous expressions like “/tested to ensure” were simplified to “to 
ensure.” While the original SG could guide human raters to score 
consistently based on common interpretations, redundancy and ambi­
guity in SGs can undermine the accuracy of AS. This finding aligns with 
Chu et al. (2024), who suggest that using subject expert-crafted SGs for 
LLMs is less than ideal due to LLMs’ lack of domain-specific knowledge 
and sensitivity to prompts. Moreover, subjectivity in SGs might also 
affect HS, even with thorough training.
5.1.2. Prompt optimization
Consistent with prior research (Diao et al., 2023; Feng et al., 2024), 
we found that prompt optimization is an effective strategy for creating 
prompts for complex tasks. Through CoT, prompts for task instruction, 
summarization, and SG refinement were divided into smaller steps 
(Figs. 1, 2 and 4). These CoT-based prompts made each step more 
manageable by leveraging LLMs' specific expertise for each step (Wei 
et al., 2022). For instance, our task instruction prompt was broken down 
into four steps: translation, scoring, validation, and output construction. 
This step-by-step approach resulted in robust translations with minimal 
hallucinations (0.12%) and ensured consistent formatting across lan­
guages. This shows that CoT can be a useful prompting strategy even in 
multilingual settings involving a range of different languages (Huang 
et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2022). Additionally, meta-prompting, as an 
effective scaffolding technique (Suzgun & Kalai, 2024), contributed to 
the rapid fine-tuning of CoT-based prompts. OpenAI's meta-prompting 
streamlined our prompt revision process by immediately generating 
more functional prompts through enhanced language clarity and prompt 
structure. This automated improvement facilitated the quick review, 
revision, and validation of optimal prompt candidates.
5.2. Limitations and future research
This study has several limitations. First, the current findings are 
based on an exploratory analysis of a randomly selected 20% subset of 
the dataset. Although this subset is representative, future research 
should check the generalizability of our AS approach by utilizing the full 
PIRLS dataset and incorporating additional CR items. It is also crucial to 
thoroughly review the methodology and examine any potential sys­
tematic biases and fairness issues across different languages and coun­
tries before implementing AS for qualitative control or operational use.

Second, while we conducted a qualitative review to identify the main

6. Conclusion
This study highlights the effectiveness of optimizing AS with prompt 
compression for evaluating CR items in multilingual international 
reading assessments. Remarkably, even with significantly compressed 
passages and SGs, AS demonstrated robust performance within the 
PIRLS context, exemplified in this current study with scoring tasks 
involving responses in 29 different languages. This success is primarily 
attributed to the strategic use of prompt compression and optimization, 
which effectively compresses the original contents by focusing on 
question-relevant elements. Moreover, using a generalized scoring 
prompt template, enhanced with prompt optimization techniques, easily 
broadens the scope of AS systems. This approach allows scoring a 
broader range of CR items, without the need to fine-tune passages or SGs 
for each item. By addressing the challenges related to cost-efficiency and 
scalability, these advances in AS pave the way for potential innovative 
applications, such as enhancing quality control and streamlining oper­
ational implementation. Ultimately, a well-designed AS system offers 
more accurate reporting, enabling participating countries to make

Title: Tashi and the Great Adventure.
Hello! My name is Tashi, and I live in a small village by the mountains where my family grows delicious fruits and vegetables. Being a farmer is a 
big deal here because our crops help us eat and stay healthy. When I was little, I watched my parents work hard in the fields and dreamed of becoming 
a skilled farmer like them. I loved imagining myself taking care of our land and making everything grow. The fields were my playground, filled with
