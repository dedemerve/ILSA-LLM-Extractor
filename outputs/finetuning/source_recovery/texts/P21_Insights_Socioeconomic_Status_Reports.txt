Overview
The relationship between students’ home resources and academic performance has long been 
of interest to researchers, policymakers, and educators. Access to home resources for learning 
has been shown to have a strong, positive relationship with reading achievement in many 
assessments, and for many cycles of PIRLS.1,2,3,4 PIRLS 2021 expanded upon these findings by 
including a new context scale, the Home Socioeconomic Status scale, created from items in the 
Home Questionnaire completed by the parents or guardians of fourth-grade students. The new 
scale draws its indicators from established definitions of socioeconomic status and demonstrates 
sound psychometric properties and relationships with students’ reading achievement across the 
countries participating in PIRLS 2021.5,6 The development of this scale reflects ongoing interest 
in the predictive power of students’ home environments to explain their academic achievement. 
Despite the desirable properties of the Home Socioeconomic Status scale, its interpretation 
is not without challenges. Although the variables included in the scale faithfully reflect well-
known conceptualizations of socioeconomic status, this construct is challenging to measure 
and even more challenging to use for comparisons across diverse countries and cultures. While 
some basic indicators of socioeconomic status are applicable across countries, more specific 
indicators of cultural capital are likely to vary across country contexts. The scale also poses 
logistical challenges—in some countries, the scale’s reliance on parents’ reports suffers from 
large amounts of missing data, as response rates from parent questionnaires were relatively low 
in those countries, and a small number of countries opt not to administer this questionnaire. 
This PIRLS Insights report begins with a discussion of how socioeconomic status has been 
conceptualized and measured historically and how this is reflected in the Home Socioeconomic 
Status scale. The discussion begins broadly, before narrowing to focus specifically on the 
context of PIRLS 2021. The report then examines the alignment between scores on the Home 
Socioeconomic Status scale and other indicators of home resources in the PIRLS questionnaires—
specifically the Student Questionnaire completed by fourth-grade students following the reading 
assessment, and the School Questionnaire completed by school principals—to explore other 
variables that may be useful for analysis given the high levels of nonresponse observed for

Defining Socioeconomic Status
Socioeconomic status (SES) is defined as an individual’s or family’s relative position within a 
social structure7 and has been shown to be a critical factor associated with students’ educational 
outcomes. Several meta-analyses have highlighted the relationship between SES and academic 
achievement and revealed that a family’s SES significantly predicts students’ academic 
performance.8,9,10,11 Because SES is not directly observable or measurable, a student’s home 
SES must be inferred based on various indicators. SES indicators cover multiple areas, such 
as financial resources, the educational background of adults, and the social environment within 
the household.12 Because the SES construct draws from so many different areas, measuring it 
through individuals’ self-reports typically requires responses to a set of items regarding individual 
indicators rather than a response to a single item asking directly about SES.13,14
One common approach for conceptualizing SES focuses on access to different forms of 
capital. SES can be broadly defined as access to financial, human, social, and cultural  capital 
resources. This classical perspective draws on established economic and sociological theories. 
Financial capital refers to a household’s financial resources and is often approximated by 
available wealth or income.15 Financial resources help families provide support for students’ 
academic achievement—for example, a dedicated study space in the home and materials to aid 
learning. 
Human capital refers to an individual’s skills or knowledge that can be used to obtain 
resources or advantages.16 In addition to  potentially increasing financial resources  (which can 
itself support students’ academic achievement), families’ human capital can contribute to creating 
a supportive cognitive environment for children.17 This can include providing age-appropriate 
materials and toys, as well as spending time reading books, which is positively related to children’s 
cognitive development.18 
Social capital is embedded in social relationships, namely parent-child, parent-school, and 
parent-community interactions,19 and is less tangible than physical resources or human capital. 
It originates from the quality and structure of these relationships and can be leveraged to achieve 
goals or facilitate actions. Social capital provides individuals with access to valuable resources 
such as information, support, and social norms that enhance their ability to succeed within a social 
context.20 Social capital, reflected in parent-child interactions, discussion of academic issues, 
and parents’ involvement in their children’s academic activities, can play a significant role in their 
children’s academic development.21
Cultural capital is a crucial yet often intangible aspect of SES and is interwoven with financial 
resources as well as human and social capital. Cultural capital is posited to exist in three forms. 
The first is the embodied state, which refers to long-lasting dispositions of mind and body and 
includes the skills, knowledge, and habits that a person develops over time. The second is the 
objectified or material state, represented by cultural goods such as artworks, books, dictionaries, 
musical instruments, and machines available within a family. The third is the institutionalized state,

Socioeconomic Status and Academic Achievement
Socioeconomic status often predicts academic achievement, with parental education and 
occupation being key factors in shaping the home learning environment. Parents’ education plays 
a crucial role in the educational environment they create for their children. Parents’ educational 
level correlates with their education-related behaviors and role in creating a supportive cognitive 
environment that can contribute to student learning. Shaping this environment may include access 
to resources, such as books, which are positively related to children’s cognitive development. 
Additionally, higher levels of education may better equip parents to communicate effectively with 
teachers and may lead them to have higher expectations for their children’s education.25 Higher 
educational degrees are also related to better access to valuable resources, such as a greater 
variety of job opportunities, housing options, and social circles.26,27
A parent’s occupation not only reflects their educational level but also serves as a source of 
income and can signify their social standing,28 which in turn can provide educational advantages 
for children. Higher occupational levels and incomes also allow families to access higher-quality 
education than may otherwise be available. Children whose parents have higher incomes typically 
have access to better-performing primary and secondary schools.29 Income plays a key role in 
where a family can afford to live which, in many countries, is related to the quality of educational 
resources available and accessible to children.30 Parents with higher-level occupations often 
benefit from broader social networks within their communities. These networks enable them to 
connect with others in similarly influential positions, creating opportunities for their children to 
access information and resources. These advantages contribute to a cumulative effect, where 
families with higher occupational levels and income can leverage their resources and networks 
to support their children’s academic and social success.31
Measuring Home Socioeconomic Status
Socioeconomic status is often measured in self-report surveys. These surveys traditionally 
include parental income, education, and occupation as indicators of the household’s social and 
economic resources. Additional home resources have also been used as indicators of SES, 
including household possessions such as books, computers, and a designated study area.32,33,34 
Together, these indicators are thought to represent the different forms of resources and capital 
that contribute to the SES construct.
Different classification systems are available for measuring SES indicators. Educational 
attainment, defined as the highest academic degree achieved by a student’s parent, is often 
measured using the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED). ISCED is a 
globally recognized framework designed for the comparable coding, analysis, and reporting 
of data associated with educational programs and qualifications across countries. The ISCED 
classification system organizes educational qualifications into distinct levels that indicate a 
progression from basic to advanced and specialized content, with each level corresponding to 
specific learning outcomes.35

Financial resources can be assessed through household income; however, it is more 
commonly measured by proxy using a person’s occupational status. This is because income 
is generally considered a volatile indicator of financial resources.36 Many individuals also feel 
reluctant to disclose their exact income levels, as sharing personal financial information is often 
seen as sensitive and confidential; this can lead to low response rates for questions about income. 
Occupation is most often measured using the International Standard Classification of Occupations 
(ISCO) categories.37
Several researchers propose including cultural resources as indicators of home SES, 
attempting to assess cultural capital in a material form.38,39,40 The reported number of books 
at home is often used as a proxy of cultural capital for the family, highlighting their importance 
as sources of learning stimulation in the household.41 Reading books together and discussing 
what was read can promote learning interactions between parents and children. Specifically, 
these elements can encourage joint reading activities, storytelling, discussions about reading 
experiences, and other literacy activities at home.42 
Digital options for accessing reading materials (such as e-books) have become more 
accessible in recent years; however, parents may prefer printed books over e-books for younger 
children to limit their children’s screen time.43,44,45 Research suggests that parents engage less 
in content-related discussions during joint e-book reading46 and often find reading printed books 
with their children more enjoyable.47 This difference in engagement and enjoyment may have 
implications for children’s text comprehension. The incorporation of digital books into measures 
of SES is a topic that merits further investigation.

About the Scale
Students were scored according to their parents’ reports regarding the four indicators on the Home Socioeconomic Status scale. Cut
scores divide the scale into three categories. Students with Higher socioeconomic status had a score at or above the cut score
corresponding to their parents reporting they had more than 25 books and more than 25 children’s books in their home, that at least
one parent finished university, and that at least one parent had a professional occupation, on average. Students with Lower 
socioeconomic status had a score at or below the cut score corresponding to their parents reporting they had 25 or fewer books and
25 or fewer children’s books in the home, that neither parent had gone beyond upper secondary education, and that neither parent
was a small business owner or worked in a clerical or professional occupation, on average. All other students had Middle 
socioeconomic status.

Four variables are included in the scale corresponding to different types of capital. Parental 
occupation is broadly representative of financial resources, and parent education is broadly 
representative of human capital. Together, these can also provide an indicator of the household’s 
social capital. Finally, the number of books and children’s books in the home can serve as proxy 
measures of cultural capital and may be particularly salient in the context of PIRLS (a reading 
assessment). It is important to note that while the scale variables reflect classical definitions of 
SES, they are unlikely to provide a complete picture of SES in any individual country, given that 
indicators of cultural capital vary across country contexts.
Based on their parents’ reports, students are classified as having “higher,” “middle,” or 
“lower” socioeconomic status. Procedures for establishing these categories and cut scores are 
described in Chapter 15 of the PIRLS 2021 Methods and Procedures. As presented in the PIRLS 
2021 International Results in Reading, the scale has a strong positive relationship with reading 
achievement both internationally and within countries.
On average, across participating countries, 29% of students were classified as having 
“higher” socioeconomic status, with country-level proportions ranging from 3% to 57%. The 
percentage of students classified as having “lower” socioeconomic status averaged 23%, with a

Overview of Indicators from Student and School Questionnaires
Building on the framework provided by the PIRLS 2021 Home Socioeconomic Status scale (based 
on parents’ reports from the Home Questionnaire, also referred to as the Early Learning Survey), it 
is important to explore how related indicators from other PIRLS questionnaires align with the SES 
construct. While the Home SES scale provides the most direct measurement of socioeconomic 
status in PIRLS 2021, additional variables captured in the PIRLS 2021 questionnaires can provide 
complementary insights into economic, social, or cultural resources available to students or within 
schools.
The PIRLS 2021 Student Questionnaire included several items asking fourth-grade students 
to report on various resources available at their homes. Although these variables do not capture 
the full breadth of the socioeconomic status construct in the same way as the Home SES scale, 
they may still be useful proxies, particularly for financial resources and cultural capital. We 
hypothesize that students from higher socioeconomic backgrounds may have more books at 
home and be more likely to have access to various resources at home than their peers from lower 
socioeconomic backgrounds.
The School Questionnaire, completed by principals of schools participating in PIRLS 2021, 
can also provide insight into socioeconomic background, albeit not at the student level. Principals 
were asked to report the approximate percentages of students in their school coming from 
economically disadvantaged or economically affluent homes. Based on these reports, students 
were classified as attending one of three types of schools. One challenge in interpreting this 
index is that it is not always clear what information principals rely on to classify their students 
when responding to the questionnaire. Some countries may have measures of socioeconomic 
disadvantage (such as receiving free meals or neighborhood classifications) that a principal can 
utilize to come to an assessment; in other cases, responses to these items may be based on 
principal subjective impressions and cultural norms.

Examining the alignment between scores on the Home SES scale based on parents’ reports 
and these related measures from other PIRLS questionnaires can serve two purposes. First, 
understanding the degree of alignment is useful to support convergent validity arguments. At the 
student level, seeing a high correlation between the presence of a particular home resource and 
home socioeconomic status scale score provides evidence that this resource is a good indicator 
of socioeconomic status. At the school level, finding many higher socioeconomic status students 
in more affluent schools (or, many lower socioeconomic status students in more disadvantaged 
schools) would provide evidence that principals’ reports reflect the composition of their student 
populations appropriately. Extending this one step further, if there is evidence that these student 
and principal variables are good proxies for the home socioeconomic status scale, these variables 
may be of particular use to researchers in cases where home socioeconomic status scale scores

Among the student-reported variables, the number of books in the home shows the highest 
level of alignment with home SES, with an international average Pearson correlation coefficient 
of 0.46. Eta Squared (η²) measures the proportion of variance in home SES explained by a 
student-reported variable based on a fitted linear model.b The international average η² value is 
0.23, indicating that, on average across countries, 23% of the variance in parent-reported SES 
can be explained by the student-reported number of books at home. This finding may be partially 
attributed to the home SES scale including a similar item about the number of books, differing only 
slightly in phrasing (i.e., referencing bookcase size in the Student Questionnaire). This suggests 
that parents and students likely shared similar perceptions of the quantity of books in their homes.

access to a shared computer or tablet has an average correlation of 0.09 with home SES across

average η² values for these variables are all below 0.03, indicating minimal shared variance 
between student-reported variables and parent-reported home SES. While these variables may 
reflect some aspects of SES, their relationships with home SES may likely be more indirect and

Alignment Between Parent-Reported Home SES and Principal-Reported 
Socioeconomic Composition of Schools
The correlation between the school mean of parent-reported home SES and principal-reported 
composition of student body by socioeconomic background in their school varies widely across 
countries, ranging from –.09 to 0.75, with an international average of 0.41. Correspondingly, η² 
values range from 0.00 to 0.56, with an international average of 0.21 (Exhibit 5). This variation 
suggests the inconsistency in the alignment between parent and school reports across different 
national contexts.
No clear pattern emerges between countries’ average achievement and the alignment of 
school-average parent-reported home SES with principal-reported student body composition. 
As illustrative examples, higher-achieving countries like Singapore (r = 0.52, η² = 0.27) show 
moderate alignment between school-average home SES and principal-reported school 
composition, whereas in other higher-achieving countries like Lithuania (r = 0.23, η² = 0.05) 
and Poland (r = 0.19, η² = 0.04), lower correlations were estimated. Similarly, lower-achieving 
countries such as South Africa (r = 0.62, η² = 0.39) show higher levels of alignment than other 
lower-achieving countries such as Kosovo (r = 0.02, η² = 0.00). 
The variability in the alignment between school-average home SES and principal-reported 
school composition of student background may be attributed to several factors. One reason may 
be the difference in the scope of reporting: Principals are asked to describe the socioeconomic 
background of their entire school, while parent-reported home SES reflects only the sampled 
students. Discrepancies can arise if the sampled classes do not fully capture the socioeconomic 
diversity of the broader student body. Additionally, the variability may arise from differences in 
principals’ access to objective SES data, the extent of their direct interactions with students and 
families, their perceptions of the school’s socioeconomic status relative to neighboring schools, 
and systemic differences in the availability and use of SES-based indicators and policies across 
countries. Finally, limited variance of parental populations within schools, and differences in cultural 
norms in discussing family background, are other potential factors that may explain this finding.
Further research is needed to understand how these contextual and systematic factors 
interact to influence the accuracy and reliability of principal-reported data. Caution is warranted 
when relying on the principal-reported measure of student background composition to substitute 
school-level home SES data, particularly in countries with high missing rates on the home SES 
scale.

Variance in Home SES Explained by Student- and Principal-
Reported Variables: HLM Analyses
This section focuses on the extent to which student- and principal-reported variables explain the 
variance in home SES based on parent reports. While the alignment analyses provided above give 
insights into the bivariate relationships between home SES and other variables collected at the 
student and school levels, the current section examines the multivariate structure that emerges 
among these variables.
If a substantial portion of home SES variance can be accounted for by a combination of 
student- and principal-reported measures, these combined measures could serve as valuable 
proxies for home SES in situations where parent-reported data are missing. Conversely, limited 
explained variance would indicate that the Home SES scale contains unique information not 
captured by the alternative measures, emphasizing the need to collect data from parents in PIRLS.
Considering the data structure with students nested within schools, a series of HLM analyses 
were performed to properly model the data hierarchy.53 Compared to simpler metrics like η², HLM 
can disentangle the variance explained at the student level (Level 1) and the school level (Level 
2), thereby allowing for a more nuanced understanding of the variance in home SES explained 
by student- and principal-reported variables.

The HLM analyses conducted include a series of models with progressively added predictors 
(Exhibit 7), starting from the null model (Model 0) to the model that incorporates all student- and 
principal-reported SES-related variables detailed in Exhibit 2 (Model 4). Because the relationship 
between the predictors and home SES may vary by country, the models were applied individually 
to each country’s data.

Specifically, Model 0 serves as the baseline and does not include any predictors, only random 
intercepts for schools. This allows for the calculation of the intraclass correlation (ICC), which 
quantifies the proportion of the total variance in home SES attributable to differences between 
schools. A higher ICC indicates that a larger proportion of the variation in home SES is explained 
by differences between schools, whereas a lower ICC suggests that more variation in home SES 
occurs within schools. 
Model 1 examines the variance in home SES explained by principal-reported school 
composition of student background, while Model 2 focuses on the contribution of the student-
reported number of books at home at both the student and school levels. Models 1 and 2 
essentially reframe and extend the alignment analysis by placing it within the HLM framework. 
While they may not yield drastically new insights, quantifying the percentage of explained variance 
in these single-indicator models provides a useful point of reference for comparison with more 
complex models—Models 3 and 4—that incorporate multiple predictors.
Model 3 incorporates both the student-reported number of books at home and the principal-
reported school composition of student background, allowing for an examination of how the 
two SES-related indicators that showed the highest level of alignment with home SES in earlier 
analyses jointly explain variance in home SES. 
Finally, Model 4 utilizes all student- and principal-reported variables related to SES. This 
allows for an evaluation of both the additional contribution of these variables beyond the number 
of books at home and school composition, as well as their overall contribution to explaining parent-
reported home SES. This, in turn, makes it possible to determine how much unique information 
remains in parent-reported home SES beyond what can be explained by student- and principal-
reported measures. More technical details, including information on explained variance measures 
and model specifications, can be found in Appendix B.

The ICC values, derived from estimates of Model 0, vary considerably across countries. For 
instance, the ICC values are 0.60 and 0.55 in Bulgaria and Türkiye, respectively, indicating that 
more than half of the total variance in home SES lies between schools. In other words, there are 
large disparities between schools in student background composition. In contrast, countries like 
Finland (0.08) and Slovenia (0.03) have much lower ICCs, suggesting that the majority of home 
SES variation occurs within schools rather than between them.c
Model 1 examines the variance in home SES explained by principal reports of school 
composition by student background. While this variable does not account for within-school 
variance by design, it explains between 1% and 61% of the between-school variance in home 
SES across countries, with an international average of 27%. As expected, the explanatory power 
of this principal-reported variable is greater in countries where it correlates strongly with school-
average parent-reported home SES.

In general, countries with higher average achievement tend to have a greater proportion of 
variance in home SES explained by student- and principal-reported variables. This is consistent 
with earlier findings that the correlation between student-reported number of books at home 
and home SES tends to be higher in high-achieving countries. However, at the school level, the 
percentage of variance explained appears to be more stable across countries, while it varies more 
with countries’ average achievement at the student level. 
The pattern echoes findings from earlier sections: In lower-performing countries, some 
students may provide less reliable self-reports due to the reading and/or cognitive demands of 
the context questionnaires.54,55 Data aggregated from the student to the school level tends to be 
more reliable possibly due to the wisdom of the crowds effect, where averaging across responses 
in a school reduces individual unreliability and stabilizes the estimates statistically.
Key Takeaways
1.	Students with lower reading achievement are more likely to have missing data for 
the Home Socioeconomic Status scale in countries with high rates of missing data 
for the scale. Given the strong association between SES and reading achievement, it is 
possible that these data are missing for lower-SES students. However, it is not possible 
to know this for certain. In countries with high response rates, this pattern was not 
observed.
2.	Despite the appeal of digital devices as proxies for socioeconomic status, the 
number of books at home continues to be a strong indicator. This finding is 
supported in the construction of the Home Socioeconomic Status scale itself, where 
parents’ reports of the number of books and children’s books in the home work well in 
conjunction with parental education and occupation. It is also supported by the results 
of the correlation and HLM analyses, where students’ reports of the number of books 
in the home consistently outperformed other variables in correlating with or explaining 
variance in SES. Some caution is warranted in making too much of these findings given 
that different respondents were asked to report on the same variable; however, it is also 
worth noting the relative lack of correlation between or variance in SES explained by 
the items related to students’ access to digital devices. These items appear to be less 
related than more traditional measures.
3.	There is great variation across countries in the degree of alignment between 
principals’ reports of school socioeconomic composition and average 
parent-reported SES. There are several possible explanations for this, including 
socioeconomic segregation of students in classrooms within schools and differences 
in principal perceptions of students’ backgrounds across countries. Principals in some 
countries may have documentation of socioeconomic indicators (such as receiving free 
or reduced-priced lunch) for reference when completing the questionnaire, while others 
may be relying solely on their own judgment to gauge students’ backgrounds, and yet 
others may be reluctant to call the parents “disadvantaged” due to cultural norms.
4.	The alignment between parent-reported home SES and student-reported number 
of books at home tends to be stronger in higher-achieving countries. Similarly, 
more within-school variation in home SES can be explained by all relevant 
student-reported variables in higher-achieving countries. This pattern may be

students in lower-achieving countries may provide more random answers due to

5.	Student and school variables can explain some variance in socioeconomic status, 
but they are no substitute for the parent-based scale. Different student and school 
variables had varying degrees of success explaining variance in SES in HLM. While 
these variables may provide some rough indication of resources available to students 
if SES measures are missing, they are unlikely to serve as reliable proxies for Home 
Socioeconomic Status scale scores. The Home Socioeconomic Status scale was 
created to account for different components of the complex SES construct and this is 
not easily replaced with other variables.
Future Directions
The findings presented in this PIRLS Insights report suggest several directions for future 
exploration. Although the PIRLS 2021 version of the Home Socioeconomic Status scale functioned 
well psychometrically and demonstrated a strong relationship between reading achievement, 
efforts could be made to further study the scale and how it functions across countries. Efforts 
should be made to expand the number of items included in the scale, although this may be 
challenging given the different cultural contexts across countries. Examining contributions of 
digital devices to SES across different contexts also warrants further exploration.
As the Home SES scale is such a strong predictor of student achievement, avenues could 
be explored to facilitate higher parental response rates. This is a challenging issue to address, 
and there are unlikely to be universally applicable strategies that will ensure high response rates 
across countries. However, one strategy to consider is making it as easy as possible for students’ 
parents or guardians to complete the Home Questionnaire—for example, by offering multiple ways 
to complete the questionnaire (e.g., on paper or digitally) and ensuring that digital questionnaires 
can be conveniently completed on mobile devices such as smartphones or tablets.
The mechanisms behind missing data for the scale (and Home Questionnaire more broadly) 
also merit further exploration, particularly given the relationships observed between missing data 
and students’ reading achievement on the PIRLS assessment. In addition, further investigation 
into the reasons for the higher alignment between parent and student reports of SES in higher-
achieving countries would be beneficial. It is clear that parent reports of SES make a unique 
contribution to understanding the contexts that shape students’ reading achievement. It will be 
worthwhile to focus future research on better understanding the relationship between parent-
reported SES and other background variables, as well as exploring operational strategies to 
improve response rates.

Appendix A: About PIRLS 2021
The 2021 cycle of the Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS 2021) aims to 
measure and compare the reading literacy of fourth-grade students around the world. PIRLS 
provides valuable insights into reading achievement differences and helps policymakers and 
educators understand factors contributing to students’ reading abilities.
The data presented in this report come from the PIRLS 2021 Home, Student, and School 
Questionnaires. PIRLS 2021 also collected data from teachers and national policy-level data were 
provided by National Research Coordinators from the participating countries and benchmarking 
entities.
There are several important factors to keep in mind when interpreting PIRLS 2021 data. In 
particular, all PIRLS 2021 data likely was impacted to some degree by the onset of the COVID-19 
pandemic, which had implications for both student achievement and the administration of PIRLS 
2021 itself. These potential implications are described in detail in the PIRLS 2021 International 
Results in Reading.
Many resources are available for obtaining more information about PIRLS 2021, accessing 
PIRLS 2021 data, and conducting other analyses. These resources include the following.
•	PIRLS 2021 Assessment Frameworks provide information about the PIRLS 2021 
reading assessment, context questionnaires, and assessment design.
•	PIRLS 2021 International Results in Reading provides a summary of participating 
countries’ reading achievement, as well as information about how different contextual 
factors are related to students’ reading achievement.
•	PIRLS 2021 Encyclopedia contains information about national contexts for countries 
participating in PIRLS 2021, including a chapter written by each country describing its 
educational system and the national circumstances of the COVID-19 pandemic.
•	Methods and Procedures: PIRLS 2021 Technical Report details instrument 
development, sample design and implementation, operations, and analytical 
procedures.
•	PIRLS 2021 Context Questionnaires show the international versions of the contextual 
items to which students, parents, school principals, teachers, and National Research 
Coordinators responded.
•	PIRLS 2021 International Database includes data collected from the reading 
assessment and context questionnaires and achievement and contextual scale 
estimates for the 57 countries and 8 benchmarking entities that participated in PIRLS 
2021. The accompanying User Guide provides information about database contents 
and analysis resources.
•	PIRLS Insights Series is a set of reports exploring different educational research areas 
using PIRLS data. Reports in the series provide an in-depth examination of particular 
topics beyond what can be covered in the International Results.

Appendix B: Technical Details
This appendix describes the technical details of the HLM approach implemented in this study. 
The HLM framework was chosen to account for the nested structure of the data, where students 
are clustered within schools, and to partition the variance in home SES into within- and between-
school components. The analyses were performed using the HLM 8 software.57
Sample
All students with available SES data and their schools from PIRLS 2021 participating countries 
were initially included in this study. For Models 0–3, observations were excluded if they had 
missing data on student-reported number of books or principal-reported school composition of 
student background. In Model 4, additional exclusions were applied, removing observations with 
missing data on any of the student- or principal-reported variables listed in Exhibit 2. As a result, 
Slovak Republic was not included in Model 4 due to the unavailability of school composition data. 
In Israel, data on whether a student has their own room were missing for the entire country, so 
exclusions were applied only if other student-reported variables or school composition data were 
missing.
The differences in sample composition could slightly affect the comparability of performance 
measures between Model 4 and the other models. However, the impact is expected to be minimal, 
as only 6% of the observations included in Models 0–3 were excluded from Model 4.
Variables
A full list of variables used in the HLM analyses is provided in Exhibit 2. In this report, all raw 
student- and principal-reported variables were ordinal and treated as though they have equal 
intervals between categories based on exploratory analysis. This approach is consistent with the 
assumption used in the correlation analyses. While the true intervals between categories may not 
be equal, any inaccuracies introduced by this assumption are expected to be minimal, as the focus 
is on the variance explained in the outcome variable rather than on the regression coefficients.
All student- and principal-reported variables were recoded such that higher values represent 
“more resources” or a “yes” response for owning a specific resource.
In Model 4, standardization was applied to all variables for validation purposes: Since 
standardized coefficients reflect the relative importance of each predictor in the model, variables 
with larger regression coefficients were expected to have stronger correlations with home SES. 
Therefore, it can be used to check whether the results from the HLM analysis align with the 
findings from the correlation analysis. Standardization does not alter the proportion of variance 
explained in home SES.

Model Specifications
A summary of the models is provided in Exhibit 7. Informed by exploratory analysis, which revealed 
minimal between-school variance in the slopes of level-1 variables in most countries, fixed effects 
were applied to the slopes of level-1 variables, while random effects were implemented for the 
intercepts.
To ensure the results obtained from the sample properly represent the population, sampling 
weights were applied at both the student and school levels.
It is essential to appropriately center variables to accurately estimate the explanatory power 
of predictors at both levels.58 In this study, all variables were centered around the weighted grand 
mean. Additionally, the group means of level-1 variables were included at level 2, following the 
approach commonly referred to as the “contextual model” in the literature.59,60
To illustrate, consider an HLM model with a single level-1 predictor, X. The grand-mean 
centered predictor can be expressed as (X – group mean) + (group mean – grand mean). Since 
the first term reflects within-school variability while the second term represents differences 
between school means, the regression coefficient of this predictor can be regarded as a mix of 
level-1 and level-2 effects.61,62 As a result, the level-1 predictor may explain variance at both levels.
By contrast, when adding the group mean of X at level 2, the within-school effects (deviation 
from the group mean) are separated from the between-school effects (differences in group 
means). As such, the explained variance in the outcome variable can be partitioned into the 
variance explained by individual differences in X within schools and differences in the school 
means of X, thereby allowing for a clearer understanding of the explained variance in the outcome 
variable given the data’s multilevel structure.
Model Evaluation
The total R2, and R2 at the student- and school levels, were examined to evaluate the variance 
explained by student- and principal-reported variables. The explained variance at each level was 
calculated as the proportion of variance explained by the model relative to the unconditional 
variance in the outcome variable at that level, while the total R2 was computed as the proportion 
of total variance in the outcome variable explained by the predictors.63
