With increasing digitalisation, inequality research focuses on students’ digital skills. Findings indicate that there
are considerable differences in the acquisition of CIL skills depending on students’ social background. However,
there are also resilient schools that achieve above-average CIL skills despite having a high proportion of low-SES
students. Looking at different conditions of schools, parental home and students, this article explores why some
schools achieve high levels of CIL skills despite less favourable conditions, and why others do not.
Taking the example of Germany, the study uses the ICILS 2018 data to analyse (i) which schools can be
classified as resilient and which schools can be classified as non-resilient, (ii) in which aspects do these schools
differ, (iii) and to what extent school-specific differences in CIL skill levels can be attributed to differing aspects
at student, parent and teacher level. Using regression analysis, this article shows that a significant proportion of
the skill differences can be attributed to the student level and also to conditions outside the school, in the
parental home. However, a significant proportion of the school differences remains unexplained and further
research is needed in this area.

1. Introduction
The ongoing digitalisation of every aspect of life is challenging
schools to equip students with the digital skills they need to handle
future challenges in their private and professional lives. In addition,
digital skills give students the capacity to be independent and engaged
members of society. In this light, initiatives such as the Digital Education
Plan of the European Commission (2020) or bodies like the OECD (2020)
have identified computer- and information-related skills as a central aim
of education today. Furthermore, changes in the wake of the COVID-19
pandemic have additionally revealed the need for digitalisation in the
German education system. The need for online teaching due to social
distancing measures in the COVID-19 pandemic showed that not all
schools in Germany were equally prepared for digital teaching and
learning. Furthermore, already disadvantaged students (migration,
disability, social origin) were not able to participate in online classes in
the same way. Specifically, students from less privileged families face
more difficult conditions (Bonal & González, 2020; Karpiński et al.,
2023; Koopmann et al., 2023).

In this context, the large-scale International Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS 2018) conducted by the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) observed
that eighth-graders differ by socio-economic background in their computer and information literacy (CIL). Students from families with low
socio-economic status (SES) attain significantly lower CIL scores on
average than students from high-SES families (Drossel et al., 2020;
Passaretta & Gil-Hernández, 2023; Senkbeil et al., 2019). Consequently,
prior research shows that schools with a high-SES student body
composition achieve higher CIL skills on average – however, in-depth
research observes that there is a minority of schools having high CIL
assessment scores despite their low-SES student body composition
(Drossel et al., 2020).
Based on the concept of organizational resilience (Kopp & Pesti, 2022;
Soucek et al., 2018), these schools could be described as resilient organizations.1 For Duchek (2020), resilient organizations are characterized by the fact that they anticipate potential threats, cope effectively
with adverse events and adapt to changing conditions. According to this
understanding, resilient schools are schools that cope better with

Research on social inequality and social stratification is more concerned with individual behaviour and the question of how social inequalities in educational participation and skill acquisition arise. Studies
have shown that inequalities have existed for decades in very different
phases of the educational process (Becker, 2003; Blossfeld et al., 2015;
Mayer et al., 2007; Pollak & Müller, 2020; Scharf et al., 2023) and that
these inequalities are also reflected in students’ skills (Weis et al., 2019;
Niemietz et al., 2023). This applies to traditional skills such as reading,
mathematics, and science and to digital skills alike (Skopek & Passaretta, 2021). The underlying mechanisms have also been investigated in
various studies (Jæger & Breen, 2016; Lareau, 2003). A first strand of
research views social inequalities in educational achievement as the
result of class-specific socialisation in the parental home (Kohn, 1969)
and students’ different performance-related starting points at school
entry (Neumann et al., 2014; Skopek & Passaretta, 2021). With regard to
digital inequality González-Betancor, López-Puig, and Cardenal (2021)
find that ICT access at home as well as ICT literacy (Scherer & Siddiq,
2019) are highly influenced by the family’s SES. A second strand of
research focuses on the role of parents and describes them as important
actors in the development of educational inequalities via the support
they provide and their educational expectations (Boudon, 1974; Dumont
et al., 2019; Maaz & Nagy, 2010; Werfhorst van & Hofstede, 2007). A
third strand of research focuses on the role of teachers and sees schools
as active players in the reproduction of social inequality (Bourdieu &
Passeron, 1977; Bradbury, 2014; Ostermann & Neugebauer, 2021). With
regard to our research question, all three perspectives should be
considered to comprehensively understand the emergence of social
differences in CIL achievement. Recent studies have shown that children
from less privileged families are less well equipped and less likely to use
digital tools educationally (Becker, 2022; Micheli, 2015). Accordingly,
Senkbeil et al. (2019) and Scherer and Siddiq (2019) have found
considerable origin-specific differences in digital skills.
While the relationship between social background and skill acquisition has been well studied, only few studieslbs have addressed
organisational resilience of schools in the CIL domain with regard to
social inequality (Drossel et al., 2020). A central result of the research is
that only a minority of schools are resilient in the CIL domain: only 8.6%
of schools in the ICILS 2018 sample were regarded as organisationally
resilient. Since the social composition of schools is sometimes very unevenly distributed and the acquisition of digital skills become increasingly important, the question of what constitutes resilient schools is
highly relevant. However, empirical research has not yet systematically
focused on those student-, parent-, and teacher-level factors that are
correlated with organisational resilience in the CIL domain. Furthermore, previous research has often focused on schools under difficult
conditions – but a comparison with the conditions in other schools is
advisable in order to correctly classify the decisive factors.
The ways in which resilient schools differ from other schools can
therefore be regarded as unclear. This knowledge is relevant for supporting those schools that face challenges in terms of their socioeconomic student body composition but could also indicate how
educational policies and practitioners can support schools that have not
yet met the minimum standards to enable their students to succeed in
their digital professional and private lives.

2. State of research
The state of research can be divided into studies at the organisational
level, which deal with the appearance of school-specific differences in
digitalisation, and studies at the individual level, which deal with the
processes of the emergence of social inequality.
At the organisational level, there are various studies that deal with
school-specific differences in ICT skills. Muijs et al. (2004) reviewed
studies to assess evidence on school factors that support schools in
challenging conditions. Their findings indicate that effective schools in
disadvantaged areas have sufficient resources for teaching and learning.
The authors add that the mere availability of these resources does not
automatically lead to school improvement because schools must apply
them wisely. If we apply this finding to the CIL area, we might find that
the issue of appropriate resources refers to the provision of appropriate
ICT equipment and the supportive instructional use of ICT in classrooms.
Moreover, teachers’ professional development and teacher collaboration have been found to be relevant for resilient schools in the traditional
learning areas. Further important factors mentioned by Muijs et al.
(2004) include the head teacher’s leadership style, the creation of an
information-rich environment and a learning community, the degree to
which parents are involved in school activities, and the support that is
offered by external agencies (companies, churches, education authorities, etc.). Hence, as suggested by these and the aforementioned findings, research already provides a broad knowledge base on supportive
factors in resilient schools when it comes to traditional learning areas.
Results on which factors are relevant for schools’ or students’ resilience
in the area of CIL is not readily available, although interest is growing,
since schools with disadvantaged student compositions have a special
responsibility to enable their students to participate effectively in the
digital age. Research on the characteristics of organisationally resilient
schools suggests that leadership by head teachers and access to educational technology are relevant factors for successful schools in disadvantaged areas (Muijs et al., 2004). Although there is no empirical
research specifically on educational resilience in the CIL domain,
research suggests that several factors are important for students’ digital
achievement. Aesaert et al. (2015) outline various influencing factors at
different levels within the EDC model. At the school level, ICT availability (Petko, Cantieni, & Prasse, 2018), teachers’ attitude towards
teaching and learning with ICT (Eickelmann & Vennemann, 2017), and
teachers’ computer-related self-efficacy (Hatlevik, 2017) have been
found to be important. Furthermore, Fraillon et al. (2019) have

three potential explanatory perspectives, and, in each case, we derive
empirically testable hypotheses.

3.2. Digital conditions at home
The second potential explanation for school-specific differences lies
outside of schools in the different conditions at home. Parental homes
can differ in terms of access to digital media (H2.1), use of digital media
(H2.2), and (digital) support opportunities (H2.3). Since there is a close
link between conditions at home and students’ digital skills, schoolspecific differences in digital skill levels are probably also related to
different conditions at home.
Access to digital resources: According to van Djyk (2000), digital
resource access is important for testing and developing digital skills.
Although this situation has fundamentally improved in recent decades
(Bachmann & Hertweck, 2023), there are still differences in access to
digital media today. However, these are expressed more in terms of
quality, the number of digital devices available and internet speed
(Senkbeil, 2018; Nordrum & Gracia, 2023). In homes with a comprehensive digital infrastructure (internet, computer access), children
should have higher digital skills. Accordingly, we would expect higher
digital skill levels at schools where an above-average number of parents
have a good digital infrastructure at home (H2.1).
Use of digital resources: As already outlined in the previous section,
mere access to digital resources alone is not enough to achieve a high
level of digital competence. The extent to which digital skills are acquired also depends on the experiences students have with digital resources and the way they are used. The way in which digital media is
used and how motivated the pupils are is closely linked to the respective
parenting style of the parents (Gonzalez-DeHass et al., 2022; Senkbeil,
2018). Furthermore, parents are important role models in the use of
digital media (Bonanati & Buhl, 2022). If digital media are used more for
educational purposes and less for leisure in the home, this could be
associated with higher digital skills among children. Schools where
parents use digital media predominantly for educational purposes
should achieve a higher level of digital skills (H2.2).
Support options: Students’ skill levels and academic performance
depend to a large extent on the support they receive at home (Chohan &
Khan, 2010). (Digital) support may differ between students’ homes for
various reasons. On the one hand, there may be a lack of (digital) support because parents do not attach particular importance to teaching
digital skills or education-related topics in general and are less supportive of their children in school matters (Gonzalez-DeHass et al., 2022;

3.1. Student characteristics
A first potential explanation for school-specific differences in digital
skills lies at the student level. On the one hand, the differences in the
acquisition of digital skills may be related to the different sociodemographic composition of schools (H1.1), but on the other hand,
students may also differ in their motivation (H1.2) and the use of digital
devices (H1.3).
Socio-demographic composition: Schools differ in terms of the educational programmes they offer (e.g. the low-track Hauptschule,
intermediate-track Realschule, upper-track Gymnasium, and comprehensive Gesamtschule) and their socio-demographic composition
(gender, social origin, migration). Since the acquisition of skills differs
according to the various socio-demographic characteristics and also
depends on the educational programmes offered due to the early separation of students into distinct educational tracks, these characteristics
must be considered when analysing school-specific differences in skills.
According to Karpiński et al. (2023), boys, students with an immigrant
background, and students from less privileged families have lower digital skills on average. A below-/above-average digital skill level at a
school could therefore be related to the composition of the student body.
In addition, schools with upper-secondary level (Gymnasium) have students with above-average performance, which is associated with a
higher level of digital skills (Passaretta & Gil-Hernández, 2023). We
could therefore expect a higher level of digital skills at upper-secondary
schools (Gymnasium) and schools with a high proportion of girls, students without an immigrant background, and students from privileged
families than at schools with a different socio-demographic composition
(H1.1).
Motivational composition: The socio-demographic composition of
schools is closely linked to the non-cognitive skills of the students at
these schools. As the findings of Karpiński et al. (2023) show,
non-cognitive skills differ according to gender, immigrant background
and social status and are also related to digital skills. This link between
non-cognitive skills and digital skills is based on a different learning
behaviour. In particular students with high educational aspirations
exhibit more favourable learning behaviour and perform better at school

3.3. Digital conditions at school
A third and obvious explanation for school-specific differences in
digital skill levels relates to the different (organisational) conditions at
schools. Schools’ digital equipment (H3.1) and the intensity of use of
these devices (H3.2) can differ between schools. In addition, the (digital)
support opportunities of teachers can vary from school to school (H3.3).
As the results of Eickelmann et al. (2019) indicate, better ratings of
schools’ digital setups are linked to higher digital skill levels among
students.
Access to digital resources: The observable school-specific differences
in digital skill levels may be related to differences in the schools’ digital
infrastructure (internet, computer access). In Germany in particular, it is
notable that only some school head teachers report adequate internet
connectivity and sufficient numbers of available computers (Hofer et al.,
2019). Some teachers have also noted that their use of digital media is
limited due to too few computers (Eickelmann et al., 2019). At schools
with good internet connectivity and sufficient numbers of computers,
students should be able to practise and develop digital skills. Schools
that have a good digital infrastructure should therefore achieve higher
levels of digital skills (H3.1).
Use of digital resources: Just like the availability of digital resources at
home, ensuring access to digital resources at school is not enough to
develop digital skills. Whether students acquire digital skills also depends on the use of digital teaching formats in lessons. The extent to
which digital media are used in lessons depends to a large extent on
teachers’ attitudes towards digital media. In international comparison, it
is notable that digital media are used less frequently in Germany: around
half of teachers report that they incorporate digital teaching formats into
their lessons at least once a week (Fraillon et al., 2019). Due to these
variations in digital media use, we could plausibly expect schools where
digital teaching formats are used more frequently students achieve an
above average level of digital skills (H3.2).
Support options: The (digital) support options provided by teachers
can also vary between schools for different reasons. On the one side, if
teachers attach little importance to digital media, they may not provide
digital support – or if teachers are more focused on getting through their
lessons than supporting lower performing students or students with
more challenging circumstances. On the other side, if teachers do not
have the necessary digital skills, their digital support may be less helpful. The support offered and the quality of the support offered can
therefore differ between schools. According to school administrators,
just over half of teachers in Germany have the necessary skills to use
digital devices in the classroom (Schuknecht & Schleicher, 2020). We
therefore assume that students in schools where teachers have high
digital skills and offer more support will achieve higher digital skills
(H3.3).
Taking the various theoretical considerations together, we can
postulate very different causes of the school-specific differences
described by Drossel et al. (2020). Differences in digital skill levels may
be due to different student characteristics (H1), different conditions at
home (H2), and different conditions in the schools themselves (H3) (see
Fig. 1). In the following sections, we aim to identify suitable indicators
for the above-described theoretical considerations and to empirically
examine the extent to which these considerations explain school-specific
differences in digital skills at resilient and non-resilient schools.

4. Methodology: sampling, instruments, and analytic
procedures
4.1. Sampling in ICILS 2018
In this study, we conducted secondary analyses of data gathered by
IEA-ICILS 2018. Drawing on a fully computer-based assessment of student’s CIL and a large body of background information on those agents
involved in school education, ICILS 2018 researchers were able to provide internationally comparable data on eighth-graders’ CIL achievement and the contextual prerequisites for CIL acquisition in 14
participating countries and benchmarking participants (Fraillon, Ainley,
Schulz, Friedman, & Duckworth, 2020a,b). The sampling strategy of
ICILS 2018 entailed applying a multi-stage clustered sampling approach
in order to gather a representative cross-section of eight-grade students
in all participating educational systems. The study team drew a random
sample of schools in the first step and a random sample of eight-grade
students and teachers at these schools in the second step. Both the
ICILS 2018 student and teacher samples can be regarded as representative for the countries participating the study (cf. Fraillon et al., 2020b).
Due to the varying degrees of digitalisation and the high levels of social
inequality, the following analysis focuses on the case of Germany. The
full dataset includes 210 schools, 2328 teachers, and 3655 students.
However, the sampling approach used in IEA-ICILS 2018 deserves
further clarification, as it was combined with the plausible value
approach of the achievement test. First, digital skills are understood as a
latent construct in the ICILS 2018 study and five plausible values were
calculated for each student. The plausible value approach demands that
researchers conducting analyses involving CIL achievement data undertake calculations with each of the plausible values (in IEA studies,
five plausible values are generally used) and average the statistics
(Braun & von, 2017; Fraillon et al., 2020b; Rubin, 1987). Second, these
analyses have to be weighted in order to take unit non-response into
account. We use the student-level weighting factors provided in the
dataset. Third, in order to obtain robust standard errors and unbiased
significance testing, we needed to impute missing values (item
non-response) as far as possible (MICE). The multivariate analyses based
on 5 imputed data sets, which were integrated according to Rubin’s rules

With regard to conditions in the parental home, we used the available data to determine how many computers were present in the
parental home (H2.1). We also distinguished between desktop computers and tablets. Our assumption was that desktop computers are more
frequently used for educational or work-related purposes, while tablets
are used more often for leisure activities. According to the results of
Feierabend et al. (2020), a tablet is often used for listening to music or
playing games. The type of digital equipment could therefore point to
the type of digital media use. In addition, the data also allowed us to
assess how long students had been using digital media and thus how
their experiences of digital media use differ (H2.2).
In order to find more detailed information about the educational
support available at home, we first use the number of books at home.
Following Ren et al. (2022), this is intended to indicate an educationally
favourable environment at home. Moreover, students were asked to
what extent they learned their digital skills from their parents (H2.3).
Although the ICILS-2018 data did not include a parent survey, the information provided by students did offer us indications of the digital
conditions at home.
We could assess the digital conditions at school by asking the
teachers directly. In the teacher survey, there were eight agree/disagree
items used to determine how teachers rated the ICT equipment at their
school (H3.1) – for example, “the computer equipment in our school is outof-date” (α = 0.87). Teachers were also asked about their attitudes towards digital media, experiences with digital media and to what extent
digital tools were used in lessons (H3.2). The attitudes towards digital
media were measured using ten agree/disagree items such as “distracts
students from learning” (α = 0.82). The experiences with digital media
were measured in years and the use of digital tools in lessons were
measured on a 5-point-scale ranging from ‘never’ to ‘every day’. The
data likewise provided various indications of the support opportunities
at school (H3.3): First, we captured the teachers’ self-assessed digital
skills by considering nine skills such as “use a learning management system” (α = 0.79). Second, in addition to this teacher-level information,
we obtained information from the students on the extent to which they
learned their digital skills from their teachers.
With regard to the theoretical considerations outlined in Section 3,
we have various indicators to explain school-specific differences from
student, parent, and teacher perspective. All metric variables were zstandardised to ensure better comparability of the results. Although, in
some cases, the indicators should be understood as rough proxies, the
available data allows us to obtain initial indications of the existing
school-specific differences.

4.2. Instruments
We operationalised the theoretical considerations using both the
ICILS 2018 student survey and the ICILS 2018 teacher survey. The key
variables for determining resilient and non-resilient schools were the
students’ social composition and the distribution of CIL skills.
The central dependent variable in this analysis are digital skills,
measured via computer and information literacy (CIL). CIL was defined
in ICILS 2018 as a person’s individual ability to use digital media to
research, create, and communicate information in order to participate in
social life. CIL skills were measured by an objective computer-based test
on various areas of digital life (Fraillon et al., 2019). The scale ranges
from 214 to 723 points: the higher the value, the better the students’
computer and information literacy.
Social background is another key variable in our analysis and has to be
understood as a multidimensional construct that is made up of various
aspects such as income, status and education. In this article, we operationalised social background via the highest socio-economic status of
the family as it reflects various key aspects of social origin. For this
purpose, we use the International Socio-Economic Index of Occupational
Status (ISEI), which was determined based on occupational information
provided by students. This ranges from 11 to 90 points and indicates that
the higher the value, the higher the socio-economic status of the family.
We identified organisational resilient and non-resilient schools based
on the composition of social background and average CIL skills. We use
half of the distributions as cutpoints (above and below average) and
obtain four different school types. Some of these schools can be
considered as ‘resilient’ given the social conditions and the level of CIL
skills achieved, some as ‘non-resilient’ and others as ‘unexpected bad”
and ‘expected good’ (for more details on the classification see section
5.1).
To uncover the key differences between these schools, we used
various explanatory variables in the analysis. The student questionnaire
included various socio-demographic characteristics, such as gender
(male, female) and parents’ country of birth (Germany, abroad).
Furthermore, the data allowed us to differentiate between schools with
or without upper-secondary school track (Gymnasium). We used these
variables to assess the hypotheses outlined in H1.1 regarding differences
in socio-demographic composition at schools.
In addition, the student questionnaire contained various information
on the students’ motivational situation: the students’ educational aspirations were operationalised via the desired educational qualification (1
= university degree; 0 = other qualification). We assessed attitudes towards digital media via an index consisting of four agree/disagree items
such as “ICT is valuable to society” (α = 0.70). Students’ self-efficacy in
dealing with digital media was measured using an index of eight basic
skills such as “create a multi-media presentation” (α = 0.80). Based on the
educational aspirations, attitudes towards digital media, and selfefficacy assessments, we could test the theoretical considerations
regarding students’ motivational situation (H1.2). Detailed information
on the individual items included in all indexes can be found in the appendix (table A1 and table A2).
We tested the type of digital media use using two different indices.
The frequency of leisure-related media use was captured via an index
consisting of five items such as “Accessing the Internet to find out about
places to go or activities to do” (α = 0.71). The frequency of educationrelated media use was assessed using an index consisting of nine items
such as “Preparing presentations” (α = 0.80). We used both indices to test
the hypotheses regarding the different use of digital media (H1.3).

4.3. Analytic procedures
In order to find out what the main differences between resilient and
non-resilient schools are and to what extent these lead to differences in
CIL skills, we proceed in three separate analytical steps. The analyses
were carried out using the statistical software STATA 17.
In a first step, we identify the organisational resilient and other
school types based on the HISEI distribution and the average CIL skill
level of schools (Section 5.1). In a second step, we uncover the distinctive features between the different school types descriptively (Section
5.2). Finally, in a third step, we merge the student data with the teacher
data and compute linear OLS regressions on the basis of weighted and
imputed data on student level with clustered schools on level 2. The
analysis focuses on the regression coefficients of the different school
types and to what extent the influencing variables can explain the
observable CIL gap between these schools. For this purpose, the
regression coefficients of the different model steps are compared with
each other and a chi2 test with Bonferroni adjusted p-values is used to
determine the extent to which a reduction/increase is statistically significant. (Δ M0-M4). The stepwise modelling was used to investigate the
extent to which the explanatory variables discussed in the theoretical
section contribute to the differences between resilient and non-resilient

5. Results
5.1. Classification of resilient and non-resilient schools
Looking at the average HISEI composition and distribution of CIL
skills at different schools, we see a positive correlation between social
background and CIL skills (.28; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Schools with a high
proportion of high-SES students also achieve higher CIL skills on
average. However, as reported by Drossel et al. (2020), we also find
some schools that achieve high CIL skills despite having an unfavourable
proportion of low-SES students.
If we classify the schools included in the sample using the HISEI
distribution (50% < 48.8 < 50%) and the CIL skills distribution (50% <
522.2 < 50%), four school types emerge. We set the cut score at 50% in
order to obtain a sufficiently high number of cases in the various scenarios. There are 12 schools with a high proportion of high-SES students
and low CIL skills (Type III), 70 schools with a high proportion of highSES students and above-average CIL skills (Type IV), 77 schools with a
low proportion of high-SES students and low CIL skills (Type I), and 15
schools with a low proportion of high-SES students and high CIL skills
(Type II). Due to the higher proportion of low-SES students we characterize Type II schools as resilient schools and Type I schools as nonresilient schools. Due to the higher proportion of high-SES students we
characterize Type III schools as ‘unexpected bad’ and Type IV schools as
‘expected good’. Type II schools in particular are at the heart of the
following analysis; the question arises as to what these resilient schools
with a less favourable SES-distribution do differently than the other
three types of school.

6. Summary and discussion

Δ-2.6

This article looks at why some schools reach an above-average level
of CIL skills despite having a high proportion of low-SES students. Using
the ICILS data, we identified four school types with different conditions
and used linear OLS regression models to explain the school-specific
differences in the average CIL skill level. The analysis looked at
different student characteristics, different (digital) conditions at home,
and different (digital) conditions at school. The results show that the
considered variables could only partly explain the school-specific differences in the CIL domain. Explanatory potential can particularly be
found at the parent level and at the student level. The different digital
conditions at home play a crucial role in creating resilient schools –
although this is not the only explanation. Even when the various schoolspecific aspects are considered, a significant difference remains.
Future research should address this persistent research gap. This
article approached the topic from a digitalisation perspective and looked
quantitatively at how resilient schools and other schools differ from one
another in digital terms. A closer (qualitative) look at the conditions at
resilient schools could possibly provide more concrete indications of
what these schools do differently – also with regard to compensatory
measures to reduce educational inequalities. Future research should
identify attitudes, concepts and activities in schools to reduce educational inequalities and examine them with regard to the development
and performance of students. The available data were insufficient for us
to do this. The data we used also had certain limitations due to their
cross-sectional nature, and no causal conclusions can be drawn about
the underlying processes. In future research, the aspects we analysed
should be examined in more detail using longitudinal data, and their
long-term impact and skill development over the course of an individual’s life should be considered.
