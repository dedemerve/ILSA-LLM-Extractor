Teachers’ nonverbal behaviors are critical for motivating students and fostering learning. However, there is a
lack of research considering the situatedness of nonverbal teaching behaviors. Further, the question remains
as to how nonverbal behaviors interrelate with dimensions of supportive teaching. We utilized a machine
learning approach to assess teachers’ nonverbal immediacy (NVI) from classroom video data and investigated its variability across learning situations, lessons, and teachers. Additionally, we examined the relations
between teachers’ NVI and observer-rated social–emotional support at the level of the teacher, the lesson,
and the learning situation. We also investigated relations between NVI and student–teacher relationships and
autonomy support focused on the lessons of the speciﬁc teaching unit as reported by the teacher and the students after the lessons. Data stemmed from the German Teaching and Learning International Survey
(TALIS) video study (Klieme et al., 2023), which was implemented as a national sub-study of the international TALIS video study. The sample of the German TALIS video study included 50 teachers (46% women;
Mage = 43 years) and their 1,140 students (53% girls; Mage = 15 years). Machine learning-assessed NVI
showed 22.5% between-teacher and 5.6% between-lesson variance, with 71.7% at the situational level.
Externally observed socioemotional support showed higher variance between teachers (39.9%) and lessons
(25.5%), but lower variance at the situational level (34.5%). Results from three-level modeling revealed
that teachers’ NVI differed substantially between teachers and lessons. Teachers’ NVI was signiﬁcantly positively associated with observer-rated socioemotional support across all levels of analysis. At the teacher
level, teachers’ NVI was signiﬁcantly and positively related to autonomy support. Our ﬁndings indicate
that nonverbal behaviors of the teacher assessed using computational modeling are substantially related
to a supportive classroom climate.

Educational Impact and Implications Statement
We adapted a framework of temporal variability and context-speciﬁcity from motivational psychology
to the context of instructional research. Using machine learning methods to assess teachers’ nonverbal
immediacy (NVI) in class, our ﬁndings revealed that NVI varied between teachers and across lessons.
Teaching thus can be seen as a situated practice shaped by both individual and contextual factors. Our
results further indicate that NVI is linked to a supportive classroom climate and autonomy-supportive
teaching.

Keywords: nonverbal behaviors, support, classroom videos, machine learning

In classroom settings, teachers communicate with their students
through a variety of nonverbal and verbal behavioral social cues,
which in turn affect students’ affective learning and, albeit with
smaller effect sizes, cognitive learning (Babad, 2007; Harris &
Rosenthal, 2005; Witt et al., 2004). Understanding these behavioral
processes that underly positive relationships in class is an important
question in instructional research (Wentzel, 2022). Behavioral processes of teachers in class have been widely researched in reference
to the concept of teacher immediacy (P. A. Andersen & Andersen,
2014; Liu, 2021; Richmond et al., 2017; Witt et al., 2004), typically
deﬁned as the extent to which communication behaviors enhance
psychological closeness and nonverbal interaction (Mehrabian,
1968). Over the last few decades, research has shown compelling
evidence for the positive effects of teachers’ immediacy on student
learning (e.g., Chesebro & McCroskey, 2001; Liu, 2021; Witt
et al., 2004). However, existing research on immediacy is strongly
focused on general behavioral patterns and largely neglects the
potential situation- and context-speciﬁcity of teachers’ behaviors
in class.
Research on emotion and motivation, contrastingly, has taken a
“situative turn” in recent decades (Eccles & Wigﬁeld, 2020;
Moeller et al., 2022; Pekrun & Marsh, 2022), conceptualizing individuals’ motivational-affective experiences as inﬂuenced by the speciﬁc situations and contexts in which individuals learn and interact
with each other. The “person-in-context” perspective posits that
individuals’ cognitions, motivations, emotions, beliefs, and behaviors are shaped by the sociocultural context in which they occur,
as well as by learning situations and the embedded interactions
with others, including related goals, social expectations, and roles
(Volet, 2001). Whereas research has accordingly shown that motivation and emotion differ substantially across different learning situations (Daumiller, Janke, et al., 2023; Dietrich et al., 2019), relatively
little is known about how teachers’ behaviors vary when interacting
with their students depending on different teaching situations and
contexts.
Our study contributes to this lack of research by applying an existing framework of temporal variability and context-speciﬁcity from
motivational research (Daumiller, Janke, et al., 2023) to the domain
of teaching behaviors. Our study extends classroom research on a
theoretical level by exploring how nonverbal social behaviors vary
between teachers and within the same teacher when teaching the
same mathematical topic across two lessons. To address this research
objective, we apply a validated machine learning approach to assess
teachers’ nonverbal immediacy (NVI) and examine the extent of variability in NVI that can be attributed to the level of the teacher, the
lesson, or the teaching situation.

A second major research gap in current immediacy research
addressed in this study concerns the question of how immediacy
might contribute to establishing a supportive climate, autonomy support, and positive relationship quality in classrooms. On a theoretical
level, it has been proposed that teachers’ situated behavioral processes contribute over time to the development of supportive relationships and high-quality teaching in class (Lazarides, Schiefele,
et al., 2025). However, such theoretical assumptions have been
only seldom empirically examined. Our study contributes to a better
theoretical understanding of a (need-)supportive climate in class by
examining the role of teachers’ nonverbal behaviors in the establishment of (need-)supportive teaching (Reeve & Cheon, 2021) and supportive relationships in class (Trauernicht et al., 2025; Wentzel,
2022).
We investigate the relations between machine learning-assessed
NVI and related dimensions of supportive teaching, namely observerrated teacher socioemotional support (encouragement and warmth)
and teacher- as well as student-reported positive student–teacher relationships and autonomy support. The NVI algorithm used in this
study has already been validated in prior publications (Lazarides
et al., 2024; Petković et al., 2024). In earlier studies, the machine
learning-generated NVI scores showed moderate-to-strong correlations with relevant outcomes, indicating their efﬁcacy in reﬂecting
effective teaching behaviors. Longitudinal path models showed that
the machine learning-assessed NVI scores were signiﬁcantly positively related to student-rated enthusiastic teaching behaviors, even
when controlling for teachers’ self-reported motivation (Lazarides
et al., 2024). Enthusiasm is a construct that is closely related to NVI
(Keller et al., 2016). In this study, we applied the validated NVI algorithm to address two central unresolved research questions (RQs)
in the ﬁeld of classroom research: (a) the variability of teaching behaviors at different levels of analysis and (b) the role of nonverbal
social behaviors of the teacher in fostering a supportive climate in
classrooms.

Conceptual Considerations: Teacher Immediacy
Teacher immediacy comprises teachers’ nonverbal and verbal
behaviors that convey availability, attentiveness, and social accessibility to learners, increase learners’ sensory stimulation, and thereby
produce interpersonal closeness and reduce the psychological distance between learner and teacher (J. F. Andersen, 1979). Verbal
immediacy refers, for example, to using vocal expressiveness in
teaching (J. F. Andersen et al., 1979), using humor in class, addressing students by name, encouraging students by asking questions,
providing feedback on students’ individual tasks, or praising

students’ work (Gorham, 1988). NVI includes maintaining eye contact with students, using gestures, employing a relaxed body posture,
and conveying positive facial expressions such as smiling and optimizing physical proximity to engage with students (J. F. Andersen
et al., 1979; Chesebro & McCroskey, 2001). Meta-analytical evidence has shown that teacher immediacy, including both verbal
and nonverbal aspects, fosters students’ affective learning, deﬁned
as the increasing internalization of positive attitudes toward the content or subject matter (Kearney, 1994), with large effect sizes
(r = .48 in Harris & Rosenthal, 2005; r = .49 in Witt et al., 2004),
and cognitive performance, indicated by tests of recall, recognition,
exams, or course grades, with small effect sizes (r = .11 in Harris &
Rosenthal, 2005; r = .17 in Witt et al., 2004).1 Both verbal intimacy
and NVI are assumed to relate to learning outcomes through different mechanisms, and the effects of teachers’ verbal behaviors are
suggested to act as mediators or even be overridden by their nonverbal behaviors (Witt et al., 2004). In the present study, we speciﬁcally
examined teachers’ NVI because of the closeness of the construct to
other dimensions of teacher support. NVI is assumed to make teachers more approachable, enhance students’ feelings of emotional connectedness, foster teachers’ communication with students, and
demonstrate care and respect, ultimately reducing psychological distance (Richmond et al., 2017). NVI conceptually overlaps substantially with teachers’ enthusiastic teaching behaviors, which also
refer to the teachers’ expressive, encouraging teaching style, characterized by relaxation, mobility, stimulating, and energetic interaction
(Keller et al., 2016; Rosenshine, 1970). Despite the great conceptual
similarities, both research strands, however, remained disconnected
for decades. This is mostly because of the different research interests
in the two strands of research—whereas research on displayed enthusiasm focuses on the instructors’ expressive style in instruction,
research on teachers’ immediacy is centered on social interactions
and closeness (Babad, 2007). Only recently, empirical work began
to bring together both perspectives and showed that teachers’ NVI
during lessons, assessed by machine learning algorithms, and their
enthusiastic teaching behaviors, rated by students after lessons,
were substantially positively related but empirically distinguishable
(Lazarides et al., 2024).
NVI is also conceptually similar to student–teacher relationship
quality. Richmond et al. (2017) accordingly summarize that a teacher’s nonverbal behavior in the classroom should improve students’
interest in the subject matter, as well as their liking of the teacher
and of the class. The teacher thus can improve affect through his
or her effective nonverbal behaviors, which in turn then increases
the students’ positive attitudes about learning and the school in
general. NVI here is described as a possible precondition for positive
student–teacher relationships. However, it can be expected that NVI
is only one component contributing to a positive student–teacher
relationship because such relationships encompass a much broader
range of social behaviors that teachers use to create a classroom environment that fosters feelings of belonging, fairness, safety, social
connection, and personal signiﬁcance (Trauernicht et al., 2025;
Wentzel, 2022). NVI further is theoretically related to needsupportive teaching, which, according to basic needs theory (Ryan
& Deci, 2000), encompasses teacher behaviors that foster students’
fulﬁllment of their need for autonomy, competence, and relatedness
in the classroom, thereby enhancing their intrinsic motivation.
Particularly autonomy-supportive practices are central to learning
and student motivation and refer to the teachers’ instructional effort

to address students’ interests and preferences to foster their volitional
engagement in classroom learning activities (Reeve & Cheon,
2024). Such efforts include, for example, asking students what
they want, or providing a rationale to explain why a rule exists
(Reeve et al., 2004; Reeve & Jang, 2006). Despite their conceptual
closeness, it remains unanswered how NVI is interrelated with
dimensions of relationship quality and (autonomy-)supportive
teaching because both strands of research—research on NVI and
research on teacher support and student–teacher relationships—
have largely developed in isolation. On a theoretical level,
Lazarides et al. (2025) propose in their heuristic model that psychological processes (e.g., goal setting, effort) and behavioral processes
(e.g., nonverbal behaviors) are key elements in the establishment of
supportive relationships and high-quality teaching in class, ultimately fostering student motivation. Indeed, experimental work
with instructional videos shows that teachers’ high NVI (body posture, facial expressions, gesture intensity, tone of voice) signiﬁcantly
increased learners’ state motivation and enjoyment, but not posttest
knowledge (Frenkel et al., 2024). One potential mechanism might be
that NVI behaviors contribute positively to both positive interpersonal relationships in class and dimensions of need-supportive
teaching. However, these interrelations remain largely unexplored,
as existing research often treats NVI, student–teacher relationships,
and need-supportive teaching as separate domains.

Methodological Considerations: Assessment Methods
Research on NVI has been limited by methodological constraints,
often focusing on general assessments rather than capturing
situation-speciﬁc variations in teachers’ behaviors, limiting our current understanding of how teachers’ NVI shapes social dynamics in
class and students’ learning. Meta-analyses and literature reviews on
teacher immediacy (Liu, 2021; Witt et al., 2004) point out that
research on NVI is limited by an overreliance on student self-reports
of general teacher NVI. In their meta-analysis, Witt et al. (2004)
found that 91% of the included studies utilized student questionnaires to measure teacher immediacy. Nearly two decades later, in
another meta-analysis, Liu (2021) still found that most of the
included studies (60%) utilized student questionnaires. The other
studies (40%) assessed teacher immediacy through group interviews
and observations, including established but also relatively classic
observer rating scales such as the “Generalized Immediacy Scale”
(J. F. Andersen et al., 1979). Both student ratings and observational
methods face certain limitations. Students’ perceptions of their
teachers’ behaviors are interrelated with factors such as students’
achievement level (Wagner et al., 2016; Wallace et al., 2016), the
achievement composition of the group (Bijlsma, 2022), the proportion of multilingual learners in class (Heinschel et al., 2024;
Hachfeld & Lazarides, 2021), students’ achievement in other
domains (Jaekel et al., 2021), motivation levels in class (Fauth,
Göllner, et al., 2020; Fauth, Wagner, et al., 2020), student–teacher
dyadic effects (Göllner et al., 2018), and the speciﬁc situation
(Goetz et al., 2013). The assessment of teacher NVI by external
observers, in turn, is often time-consuming and requires extensive
training of external observers (P. A. Andersen & Andersen, 2014).

Also, particularly nonverbal communication often comprises a complex interplay of multiple nonverbal cues that only gain plausibility
when considered in combination, leading to a high level of difﬁculty
in distinguishing between the different nonverbal immediate behaviors in teaching situations (Frenkel et al., 2024; Petković et al.,

The methodological limitations of both methods contribute to
the existing neglect of the potential situatedness of teachers’ in-class
behaviors because the methods at hand mostly focus on generalizable behavioral patterns. Thus, new situation-sensitive methods are
needed in current research on NVI that capture both situationspeciﬁc and trait-related variations. The current developments in
the assessment of learning and teaching through artiﬁcial intelligence (AI; Wang et al., 2021) might enable a situation-speciﬁc
assessment of teachers’ NVI, as AI can process large amounts of
data in a short time and autonomously detect meaningful patterns.
One promising approach has been introduced by Lazarides et al.
(2024), who use methods of computer vision to assess teachers’
NVI in class. Their ﬁndings show that machine learning-assessed
NVI was predicted by teacher self-efﬁcacy and meaningfully contributed to student-rated teachers’ enthusiastic teaching behaviors
and students’ interest (Lazarides et al., 2024). However, further
research is needed to validate these methods and explore their utility
for examining the situational and contextual dynamics of student–
teacher interactions.

Bringing Concepts and Methods Together: Situatedness
of Teaching Behaviors
How individuals behave and react to different situations is theoretically assumed to be inﬂuenced by their prior experiences, goals,
identities, socialization processes, and self-schemata, and, ultimately, by their expectancies and values (Eccles & Wigﬁeld,
2002; Eccles et al., 1983). Research has, for example, shown that differences in teachers’ nonverbal behaviors in class relate to their own
teaching motivation (Lazarides et al., 2024).
The extent to which social behaviors of teachers in instructional
situations vary between teachers have been widely empirically validated, particularly in regard to teaching quality. Using three-level
models, research has investigated how much of the variability in students’ perceptions of teaching quality can be attributed to individual
student characteristics (student level), classroom composition (class
level), and teacher characteristics (teacher level). For example, studies have shown that students’ perceptions of supportive teaching
behaviors vary substantially depending on the speciﬁc teacher
(Heinschel et al., 2024; Voss et al., 2022). Heinschel et al. (2024)
indicated that 10% of the variance in students’ perceptions of teachers’ student orientation were located at the teacher level. Voss et al.
(2022) reported that 13%–25% of the total variance in student support was attributable to the teacher. However, the authors found that
about half of the variation in students’ perceptions of high-quality
teaching at the class level was not explained by sharing the same
teacher. The variability in students’ ratings of the same teacher suggests that contextual and situational factors may play a role in shaping students’ perceptions, pointing to the impact of speciﬁc teaching
situations.
Teaching behaviors are thus not only inﬂuenced by stable personal factors, but also by the context in which they occur. On a theoretical level, the psychological “person-in-context” perspective

proposes that individuals’ behaviors are dynamically interrelated
with sociocultural factors, social practices, and organizational settings (Volet, 2001). We deﬁne context-speciﬁcity as the degree to
which a construct and its relationships to other constructs are inﬂuenced by the speciﬁc characteristics and dynamics of a given context, such as a classroom lesson. In the teaching profession,
lessons are an important context in which teachers’ behaviors take
place and where teachers interact with students in regard to different
content, topics, and tasks and in speciﬁc settings in which knowledge is introduced, consolidated, and revised (Malmberg et al.,
2014). Speciﬁc lessons follow a deﬁned content structure (e.g.,
topic introduction, knowledge consolidation) and speciﬁc interaction formats, which together shape the context of social interactions.
Empirically, as one of the few studies focusing on within-student
variations, Talić et al. (2022) showed approximately 50% withinperson variation in student ratings of teacher support, cognitive activation, and classroom management. As students were prompted at
the beginning of their lessons during a 3-week time period, these
ﬁndings indicate that students’ perceptions of the behaviors of the
teacher varied to a great extent within students across lessons
(ICC1 = .45–.47. for student support items). However, the study
focused on students’ subjective perceptions of teaching behaviors
and not on actual teaching behaviors. Previous research on enthusiastic teaching also showed a substantial degree of lesson-speciﬁc and
situation-speciﬁc variance in teachers’ behaviors. Gaspard and
Lauermann (2021) revealed that 9% of variance in student-reported
and 35% in teachers’ self-reported teacher enthusiasm to teach was
lesson-speciﬁc. Keller et al. (2018) found that 28% of the variance in
student-reported enthusiastic teaching occurred within teachers, and
thus was explained by the lesson. As one of the few existing studies
addressing between-lesson variations in teachers’ actual nonverbal
teaching behaviors, Smidekova et al. (2018) found that teachers’
gaze distribution varied substantially from lesson to lesson as the
GINI coefﬁcient, a measure of inequality of gaze distribution,
clearly showed values indicating that the same teacher behaved
very differently when different lessons were compared. The authors
concluded that teachers can be expected to not act the same way in
every lesson.
Apart from personal and context-speciﬁc characteristics, situational characteristics may also play a role with respect to differences
in the behaviors of individuals. According to situated expectancy
value theory (Eccles & Wigﬁeld, 2020), situations impact individuals’ beliefs, behaviors, and motivations. In this study, we deﬁne a situation as a randomly selected moment in which classroom learning
can be assessed. This conceptualization aligns with recent perspectives on instructional quality that do not deﬁne quality based on the
mere presence of speciﬁc pedagogical elements. Instead, quality is
understood as being expressed in every moment of a lesson, through
the ongoing interactions and instructional decisions that shape the
learning environment (Hamre & Pianta, 2010). We apply this temporal approach, as we are interested in the extent of the variability
in NVI that is attributable to the teacher, the lesson, and the teaching
situation. While this temporal approach does not isolate predeﬁned
instructional events, it offers two key advantages: (a) it captures random variability across the full range of classroom episodes, and (b) it
relates to recent conceptualizations in instructional quality research
(Hamre & Pianta, 2010). We consider this approach a suitable
approximation for assessing situational variability in NVI in authentic classroom settings.

TEACHER NONVERBAL IMMEDIACY

Although it can be expected on a theoretical level that NVI behaviors of the teacher ﬂuctuate from moment to moment as social interactions with students change from situation to situation, studies
have rarely addressed the variability of teaching behaviors across situations (not lessons). As one of few studies, Goetz et al. (2013), for
example, found only 7%–8% of within-person variability in
student-reported teaching characteristics including understandability,
pace, and fostering attention. The authors used time randomization
and students were prompted once at randomly selected time intervals
within 40 min of class time. Only one study examined situational variability in the actual NVI behaviors of individuals and their variations
(Patterson, 1973). The study used an experimental approach focusing
on actually displayed behaviors during interviews. The ﬁndings
revealed that university students’ immediacy behaviors were highly
time-consistent throughout the duration of one interview (25 min;
stability coefﬁcients ranged between .58 and .96 depending on the
facet of immediacy) and across the time span of a 1-week interval
(stability coefﬁcients ranged between .51 and .97).
Taken together, whereas studies exist that provide knowledge on
the variability of teaching behaviors between teachers and classrooms
(Hachfeld & Lazarides, 2021; Heinschel et al., 2024; Voss et al.,
2022), evidence is scarce on the lesson- or situation-speciﬁc variability of teaching behaviors. Further, besides of a lack of empirical evidence, on a conceptual level, a comprehensive theoretical framework
is missing that explains why teaching behaviors should vary across
teachers, lessons, and situations. Models that conceptualize such variations have been introduced in research on motivation and emotion
(e.g., Daumiller, Janke, et al., 2023; Moeller et al., 2022; Pekrun &
Marsh, 2022). Daumiller, Janke, et al. (2023), for example, conceptualize four dimensions of stability and variability that affect differences
in motivational constructs within learning environments. The authors
explain stability in motivational constructs by differentiating between
(a) stable-speciﬁc fractions, caused by characteristics of the individual
(e.g., knowledge of the topic of a lesson) and of the context (e.g.,
social interaction formats of a lessons), and (b) stable-general fractions, explained by traits of the individual (e.g., personality) and of
the achievement situation (e.g., evaluation standards). The authors further explain variability in motivational constructs by differentiating
between (a) variable-speciﬁc fractions related to situation-speciﬁc
experiences (e.g., feelings) and changing contextual features (e.g., student behaviors) and (b) variable-general fractions because of changing
characteristics of the student (e.g., mood) and changing circumstances
(e.g., workload).
Building on the framework of Daumiller, Janke, et al. (2023), in
our study, we propose an adapted model for understanding the stability and variability of NVI behaviors, addressing the underexplored
situatedness of NVI in teaching contexts. As depicted in Figure 1,
our model highlights expected differences in NVI behaviors because
of teacher traits (stable-general fractions), lesson characteristics
(variable-general fractions), and situational dynamics (variablespeciﬁc fractions). Substantial differences in NVI behaviors can be
expected between teachers (stable-general fractions), which might
be explained by personal characteristics such as the teachers’ personality traits. Further, teachers’ NVI behaviors can be assumed to differ
across teaching contexts (variable-general fractions), which might be
explained, for example, by the characteristics of lessons, including the
sequencing of the lesson (e.g., topic introduction or ﬁnal summary,
etc.), the workload in the lesson, forms of social interaction, and
the speciﬁc lesson content, as well as lesson-speciﬁc materials and

5

task formats. Situation-speciﬁc differences in teachers’ NVI behaviors (variable-speciﬁc fractions) might be expected, explained by
the dynamics of social interactions, teachers’ individual experiences
and feelings in a speciﬁc situation, or the students’ dynamic interaction behaviors.

Aims and RQs
The objective of this study is to transfer a framework of temporal
variability and context-speciﬁcity from motivational research
(Daumiller, Janke, et al., 2023) to the context of teaching behaviors.
Insights from this study contribute to a better understanding of the
situated nature of teaching behaviors. To examine the situatedness
of teaching behaviors, more concretely of teachers’ NVI, we used
a validated machine learning method to assess teachers’ NVI
based on classroom video data (Lazarides et al., 2024; Petković
et al., 2024). Further, our ﬁndings inform theories concerned with
the conceptualization of (need-)supportive classroom climates
(Reeve & Cheon, 2021; Wentzel, 2022) about the components of
supportive classroom dynamics by examining the role that teachers’
NVI plays in socioemotional support, autonomy support, and student–teacher relationships. In detail, we addressed two key challenges in current work on teaching behaviors.
First is the question of whether and how teachers’ NVI behaviors
differ between individuals (teacher), contexts (here: lessons), and
situations (teaching situation). On a theoretical level, situated expectancy value theory (Eccles & Wigﬁeld, 2020) and psychological
“person-in-context” perspectives in motivational research (Volet,
2001) conceptualize that individuals’ beliefs and behaviors are
shaped by the context in which they occur as well as by the situation
in which they take place. To structure stability and variability in NVI
behaviors, we apply the framework of Daumiller, Janke, et al. (2023)
to the context of teaching behaviors. This enables us to better understand whether and how NVI behaviors are inﬂuenced by the context
of the learning environment and by the teaching situation.
Second, referring to theoretical conceptualizations that describe
teachers’ situated behavioral processes as key elements of establishing a supportive climate in class (Lazarides et al., 2025), we examine
whether teachers who display high NVI also provide a supportive
learning environment. We thereby extend and integrate existing
work on NVI (Richmond et al., 2003), student–teacher relationships
(Wentzel, 2022), and need-supportive teaching (Reeve & Cheon,
2021; Zeinstra et al., 2023) that has often remained disconnected
despite conceptual overlaps.
We tested the following RQs and hypotheses:
RQ1: To what extent are differences in NVI attributable to the
teacher (stable-general), the lesson (variable-general), and the
teaching situation (variable-speciﬁc)?
We expect machine learning-assessed NVI to exhibit meaningful
variability across teachers, lessons, and teaching situations
(Hypothesis 1 [H1]). This assumption is based on the theoretically
presumed situated and context-speciﬁc variabilities in beliefs and
behaviors (Eccles & Wigﬁeld, 2020; Volet, 2001). We adapt the
framework of Daumiller, Janke, et al. (2023), which distinguishes
between stable-general, variable-general, and variable-speciﬁc components, to conceptualize multilevel ﬂuctuations in teacher behavior.
As there is only little prior empirical work on ﬂuctuations of teaching
behaviors across lessons (Talić et al., 2022) and across teaching

In the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) framework, situations were operationalized as 16-min sequences of lessons,
following a temporal approach by selecting speciﬁc time slots. Within
these 16-min sequences, 30-s segments were selected as suitable for
computer vision analysis, for example, those without excessive camera movement and those in which the teacher remained fully visible
and did not leave the video frame.
Again, a temporal approach was used. We argue that the applied
approach is highly suitable to examine situation-speciﬁc variability
in teachers’ behaviors, as using time as selection criteria (along
with suitability for analysis) allows a certain degree of randomization
that is not used when certain situations are preselected because of the
events that occur within these situations. Our RQ in the present study,
accordingly, is “How much difference in NVI is attributable to the
teacher (stable-general), the lesson (variable-general), and the teaching situation (variable-speciﬁc)?” and we can best study such differences with a time-related selection of sequences and segments
rather than with an event-based selection that would not allow us to
capture random ﬂuctuations across time (which we are interested in).
RQ2: How is machine learning-assessed NVI during the lessons
related to dimensions of support—more concretely, to observerrated socioemotional support (encouragement and warmth) during the lessons, included in the models at the level of the teacher,
the lesson, and the teaching situation, and to autonomy support
in class and student–teacher relationships, focused on the lessons of the speciﬁc teaching unit and reported by the teacher
and students after the lessons, included in the model at the
level of the teacher?
NVI manifests itself in the speciﬁc situation through the expression
of teachers’ sensitivity and adaptability, which allows teachers to meet
the immediate needs of the class or of the individual students. For this
reason, testing the relations between teachers’ NVI and the provision
of a positive, encouraging, and need-supportive learning environment
is important to better understand the role that NVI plays in a

supportive learning climate. In line with the theoretical approaches
examining NVI (Mehrabian, 1968; Richmond et al., 2003) and student–teacher relationships (Trauernicht et al., 2025; Wentzel, 2022),
we expected that machine learning-assessed NVI during the lessons
would positively and directly relate to observer ratings of the teachers’
socioemotional support (encouragement and warmth) during the lessons (Hypothesis 2a [H2a]). Referring to the theoretical relevance of
teachers’ NVI for need-supportive teaching practices (Ryan & Deci,
2000), we expected that NVI during the lessons would be positively
related to the provision of autonomy support and to positive
student–teacher relationships, reported by teachers and students after
the lessons with respect to the lessons of the speciﬁc teaching unit,
included in the models at the teacher level (Hypothesis 2b [H2b]).
We controlled for teacher experience and gender, as prior studies
suggest that more experienced teachers and female teachers report
particularly good teacher–student relationships (Lazarides et al.,
2023). We also controlled for school type (academic vs. nonacademic) because students in academic-track schools tend to report
less support from their teachers (Schiepe-Tiska, 2019).

Design
The German TALIS video study was based on the design of the
international TALIS video study comprising teacher and student
questionnaires and achievement tests before and after two videorecorded lessons on quadratic equations in mathematics. The structure followed a pre–post test design. Both video-recorded lessons
focused on the topic of quadratic equations. In the German
TALIS video study, the international study design was extended
by a second follow-up survey and the video recording of another
lesson on a mathematical topic of choice. The design is depicted
in Figure 2. A detailed check of the dates of the ﬁrst two lessons
in the German TALIS data set showed that the mean time distance
between both lessons was M = 20.62 days, with a range of 3–56
days. The posttest occurred after the second lesson, with a mean
time difference of M = 23.64 and a range of 1–103 days. In our
study, we include the data from videos 1–3 to examine the variability of NVI during lessons (RQ1). However, regarding the examination of relations between teachers’ NVI and teacher- and
student-reported support dimensions (RQ2), we did not include
video 3. This is because our analyses for RQ2 included data of
teachers and students on perceived support dimensions from the
posttest, and those measures referred to teachers’ behaviors in the
ﬁrst two recorded lessons (see the Measures section). For most
teachers (39 out of 50 teachers), video 3 was recorded after the
posttest and the teacher and student reports therefore did not refer
to these lessons. We further only used data from the pretest for
background variables such as teaching experience and teacher gender, since teacher- and student-perceived support dimensions were
only assessed in the posttest.

Measures
NVI Model (During the Lessons)
To examine our RQs, we used a machine learning algorithm to
assess the NVI of teachers during the lessons based on classroom
video data that have already been described in prior work
(Lazarides et al., 2024). Video recordings from 46 out of 50 teachers
were included in the NVI algorithm development; four were
excluded because of unavailable or low-quality video material. Of
the 46 teachers included, for 43 teachers three suitable video recordings were available for our analysis. For the remaining three teachers,
only two videos (and thus, two lessons) were available, resulting in a
total of 135 videos used for the analysis. Next, segments within the
videos suitable for computer vision analysis were identiﬁed.
Included were segments without excessive camera movement
where the teacher delivered frontal presentations, remained fully visible, and did not leave the video frame.
To ensure representativeness of the selected segments, a stratiﬁed
sampling across two dimensions was conducted. At the teacher
level, we ensured an approximately equal distribution of video segments across teachers, with slight deviations reﬂecting that three
teachers had only two lessons recorded, resulting in an average of
M = 8.76 segments per teacher (SD = 0.77). At the lesson level, segments were deliberately chosen to be evenly distributed over time. A
slight concentration of segments at the beginning and end of lessons
reﬂects the typical instructional structure, wherein frontal presentations by the teacher occur predominantly during these lesson phases,
while the central part typically involves independent student activities or group work.
These segments were used to develop the NVI model with computer vision analysis (Petković et al., 2024). Following the selection
of the 403 video segments, we randomly selected 3,056 frames from
these video segments. Three trained student assistants evaluated
each of the 3,056 frames on a continuous slider scale with regard
to body posture, gesture intensity, and perceived distance. In addition, raters evaluated teachers’ NVI over each full 30-s segment
using an adapted Generalized Immediacy scale on a continuous
slider. Rather than counting discrete behaviors, this high-inference
approach prompts raters to integrate multiple nonverbal cues—posture, gesture, proxemics—into one holistic immediacy judgment.
After an intensive rater-training protocol to ensure conceptual

Based on human ratings, submodels for facial expressions, gesture

details on the NVI score are provided in Petković et al. (2024) and

The interrater reliability of the human ratings was analyzed with a
two-way random model, ICC(2,3) (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979). All were
acceptable to good (Koo & Li, 2016), with an ICC(2,3) of .68 for distance, an ICC(2,3) of .89 for gesture intensity, and an ICC(2,3) of .68
for NVI. The rating process revealed low interrater reliability for body
posture related to teacher relaxation, ICC(2,3) = .59, even after joint
discussions and multiple rating rounds. Consequently, this variable
was excluded from subsequent analysis. Then, the 403 rated video
segments were partitioned into a training set (324 segments) and a
validation set (79 segments) at the teacher level, ensuring that no
teacher appeared in both sets. Given that each teacher in TALIS
was assigned to a single classroom, this partitioning also prevented
any overlap of students or classrooms between training and validation. Consequently, the validation data differ from the training
data in terms of teacher identity and some contextual variables
(e.g., classroom layout, camera angle, and lighting). This design
tests the model’s generalizability to new teachers and related classroom environments, though generalizability to other dimensions
(e.g., unseen student behaviors or broader cultural contexts) is not
fully covered in this setup. The teacher-level split also aligns with
the hierarchical structure of our multilevel analyses, which treat
teachers as a primary unit of interest. In this validation, the model
achieved a moderate-to-substantial level of absolute agreement
with trained human raters, ICC(2,3) = .690, demonstrating acceptable accuracy. After conﬁrming the model’s accuracy, we then
applied the validated algorithm to automatically annotate an additional 220 video segments—also selected from the TALIS corpus
using the same inclusion criteria but not previously rated by humans.
This yielded a total of 623 (403 human-coded + 220 model-only)
segments whose NVI scores were included in our analyses, combining human annotation with scalable automatic annotation.

Observer Ratings (During the Lessons)
In the international TALIS design, two lessons focusing on quadratic equations were video-recorded for each teacher, with an average lesson duration of 69.3 min (Md = 90 min) in Germany
(Klieme, 2020). In the German national study, a third lesson was
recorded for each teacher. Two trained and certiﬁed observers
rated the lesson videos using the study’s observation codes that
included six domains of quality, each including multiple components that were rated on a 4-point scoring scale and had associated
behavioral examples (for further details, see OECD, 2020a). All lesson videos were rated in 16-min segments (situations) to improve
rater accuracy (Castellano & Bell, 2020, p. 3). Raters were allowed
to use the learning materials from the lessons to better understand
the interactions in the videos.
Socioemotional Support (Encouragement and Warmth).
We included encouragement and warmth, a subfacet of the socioemotional support component, which refers to regular moments of
encouragement and shared warmth and respect in the classroom
and is indicated by the use of respectful language, positive tone of
voice, and shared smiles or laughter (Bell et al., 2020). Interrater reliability was excellent across the three lessons, ICC2 = .92 (Cicchetti,
1994). The coding scheme of observer-rated encouragement and
warmth, adapted from OECD (2020b)and Bell et al. (2018), is summarized in the Appendix A.
Covariates. Teacher gender (1 = female; 2 = male), teaching
experience (1 = 1–10 years; 2 = 11–20 years; 3 = 21–30 years;
4 = 31–49 years), and school type (0 = nonacademic; 1 = academic) were included as covariates at the teacher level.

Statistical Analyses
The data are structured hierarchically (see Figure 2), with situations (Level 1, n = 623) nested within lessons (Level 2, n = 135,
average cluster size = 4.61) and lessons nested within teachers/
classrooms (Level 3, n = 46, average cluster size = 13.54).
Regarding situations, observer ratings of socioemotional support
were carried out in 16-min segments to ensure sufﬁcient context
for reliable holistic judgments as well as high levels of accuracy
in the lesson ratings (OECD, 2020a). By contrast, the computer
vision-based measurement of NVI necessitating a ﬁner-grained
temporal resolution (30-s segments) required shorter segments
(30 s each), optimizing for technical accuracy in capturing
subtle, rapidly changing nonverbal behaviors. To integrate

moment-to-moment computer vision measures with holistic

We applied three-level manifest modeling. For multilevel modeling, a sample size of 30 groups at L2 with 30 units at L1 has been
proposed as a minimum sample size requirement (Maas & Hox,
2005). However, the minimum sample size requirement ﬂuctuates
based on model complexity. In our study, we had a relatively
small sample at L2 of 46 classrooms. Thus, to reduce model complexity, to examine RQ2 we applied a doubly manifest multilevel
modeling approach based on Marsh et al. (2009) with single manifest indicators consisting of the mean-level averaged items for
each variable and the manifest aggregation of L1 constructs to
form L2 constructs. This helps reduce the number of estimated
model parameters and is particularly useful with a small sample.
To address RQ1 regarding the differences in NVI, we calculated
the intraclass correlation (ICC1) coefﬁcients for three-level models
as recommended by Hox (2002) and Snijders and Bosker (2012).
The intraclass correlation is the proportion of group-level variance
compared to the total variance and can also be interpreted as the
expected correlation between two units that are in the same group
(Hox, 2002). The ICC1 values range between 0 and 1, with higher
numbers indicating a higher correlation between the units within
the same group (e.g., NVI ratings within the same lesson). The
ICC1 is computed as the variance at a given level divided by the
total variance across all levels. Speciﬁcally, following Hox (2002,
p. 34) we estimated the variance components for:

To address RQ2, we tested two three-level path models. In each
model, at Level 1 (situation level), we included observer ratings of
socioemotional support (encouragement and warmth) as well as

Across all levels of analysis, NVI was modelled to predict support
dimensions. To ensure an evaluable model ﬁt and avoid oversaturation, we excluded predicted effects of teacher gender and work experience on autonomy support and student–teacher relationships.
Subsequent analyses conﬁrmed that these effects were nonsigniﬁcant, supporting the parsimony of our ﬁnal model. Example code
is provided in Lazarides, Frenkel, et al. (2025).
Mplus Version 8.3 was used for all analyses (Muthén & Muthén,
1998–2019), and maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors was applied. Goodness of model ﬁt was evaluated
using the following criteria: the comparative ﬁt index (CFI),
root-mean-square of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized
root mean residual (SRMRwithin, SRMRbetween). CFI ≥ .95 as well
as RMSEA ≤ .06 and SRMRwithin/between ≤ .10 (Hu & Bentler,
1999) were taken as indications of a satisfactory model ﬁt. There
were no missing data for posttest survey measures, nor for
lesson-speciﬁc data for videos 1 and 2. However, for the third
video/lesson, four teachers did not have data, which was handled
using the full information maximum likelihood method (Enders &
Bandalos, 2001).

differed between lessons and had substantial variable-general

Interestingly, there was a large amount of unexplained variation
in NVI at the level of the teaching situation, also in comparison
to the theoretically similar construct of socioemotional support,
here assessed with observer ratings. Whereas some of these
variable-speciﬁc fractions represent meaningful differences across
teaching situations caused by situational characteristics such as
social interaction dynamics (e.g., disturbances, joyful conversations), the situational variability in machine learning-assessed NVI
in our study might also reﬂect unexplained error variance.
When considering why the amount of unexplained difference
across teaching situations is greater than in similar constructs, several
potential explanations can be offered. One explanation is the method
of assessment—whereas socioemotional support in our study was
assessed via observer ratings, NVI was assessed via machine learning. Machine learning typically detects trends in teachers’ expressions during the teaching process across a large amount of data
including a large array of different teaching situations and lessons
(Shi et al., 2021). NVI is thereby categorized automatically in
each teaching situation, without considering the context of the lesson. Humans, in turn, rate teaching situations in the context of the
speciﬁc lessons in which they occur because each lesson in the classroom video data set is divided into shorter sequences and observers
rate these sequences according to their temporal order, thereby probably considering contextual information from the lessons and speciﬁc within-lesson social dynamics to a greater extent. In the
present video data set of the international TALIS study, for example,
the teacher behavior component codes were rated every 16 min
by the two randomly assigned raters. Thus, human raters rated the
three lessons sequentially rather than rating a whole data set in a
random order like the machine learning algorithm did. This

difference in the methodology might partially explain the higher
situation-speciﬁc variability in machine learning-assessed NVI compared to socioemotional support as rated by humans. Another explanation for the different amounts of variability across teaching
situations in machine learning-assessed NVI and observer-rated
socioemotional support (encouragement and warmth) is that the
ratings of both variables referred to different time intervals, as
NVI during the lesson was assessed based on 30-s video segments
and socioemotional support during the lesson was observed based
on 16-min video sequences. Although the 30-s NVI ratings were
aggregated to align with the 16-min observation intervals, the difference in the reference objects that were rated may still inﬂuence the
situational variability. Moreover, because our approach deﬁnes situations as ﬁxed time intervals rather than speciﬁc pedagogical events,
the estimates of situation-speciﬁc variation likely reﬂect a combination of meaningful instructional dynamics and temporal ﬂuctuations.
Another less methodological explanation for the noteworthy differences in explained variability across teaching situations of
machine learning-assessed NVI behaviors and observer-rated socioemotional support is that nonverbal behaviors of the teacher might
be inﬂuenced more strongly than the general level of socioemotional
support provided by the teacher by situational characteristics of
dyadic interactions with students. This assumption is based on the
conceptualization of student–teacher interactions by Nurmi and
Kiuru (2015), who found that students’ characteristics, such as
their academic performance, motivations, and adjustments, shape
teachers’ behaviors. Empirical evidence for such situation-speciﬁc
bidirectional inﬂuences between teachers and students is provided
by Frenzel et al. (2024), who showed that students’ facial expressions of joy cross-recurred substantially above chance level within
a time window of about −2 s to +3 s seconds relative to the instructors’ expressions.

Relations to Supportive Teaching
In line with our expectations (H2a), our ﬁndings show that, across
levels of analysis, the machine learning-assessed NVI behaviors
were substantially related to observer-rated socioemotional support
(encouragement and warmth). On a methodological level, these systematic within-time correlations between machine learning-assessed
NVI and a theoretically related construct indicate meaningful variations in machine learning-assessed NVI at the teacher, lesson, and
situational levels. The signiﬁcant effects indicate that the situationlevel differences in machine learning-assessed NVI reﬂect genuine
variability rather than random error variance. On a theoretical
level, consistent with our assumptions and theoretical perspectives
on NVI (Mehrabian, 1968; Richmond et al., 2003), high levels of
machine learning-assessed teacher NVI were associated with higher
observer-rated socioemotional support from teachers. The measure
used to assess observer-rated socioemotional support in this study
is closely linked to the dimensions of competence and social relatedness support in self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000).
More speciﬁcally, the observer-rated socioemotional support measure
(Appendix A) used in this study covered the dimension of ‘encouragement and warmth’ within socioemotional support. This dimension

refers, for example, to situational behaviors such as smiling, but
also to the provision of encouragement during instruction. The measure thus assesses the overall climate in class. Our ﬁndings support
that NVI, here assessed using machine learning (Appendix B)
based on social cues like facial expressions, gestures, and teacher
proximity, is one relevant element of this climate.
Although the overlap may partially explain the associations found
in the study, the measures also show clear differences. While the
coding scheme for observers strongly focuses on high-inference
constructs like encouragement and shared warmth which go beyond
nonverbal social cues, the NVI focuses on nonverbal social cues
only. From a content perspective, thus, the ﬁndings suggest that
teachers who consistently display NVI behaviors in class—both
generally and in speciﬁc teaching situations—were effective in conveying warmth and encouragement to their students.
In line with our assumptions (H2b), our ﬁndings also showed that
teachers’ NVI, assessed by a machine learning model, meaningfully
related to need-supportive teaching practices (Ahmadi et al., 2023;
Ahn et al., 2021; Stroet et al., 2013), more concretely, to teachers’
and students’ self-reported autonomy support focused on the lessons
of the speciﬁc teaching unit. Research on need-supportive teaching
(Ahmadi et al., 2023; Reeve & Jang, 2006; Reeve et al., 2004; Stroet

incorporate students’ interests and preferences, or fostering relevance and showing respect for and interest in students’ experiences
and opinions. The items in our study focused on the speciﬁc lessons
within the teaching unit, which may have increased the likelihood of

strong association with NVI. At the same time, conceptual overlap
does not fully explain the association either. The items to assess
autonomy support referred to providing students with different learning materials and tasks and encouraging them to ﬁnd multiple solution methods and to work independently. Our measure of (high) NVI
behaviors included teachers’ friendly facial expressions, adequate

these concepts do not overlap conceptually, they are both centered
around the aim of promoting students’ motivation to learn. One
explanation for the signiﬁcant relation between these concepts
might be that the underlying goal of the teachers to promote student
motivation might have led them to be highly immediate and, at the
same time, to emphasize autonomy support in their classrooms.
Thus, the two different behaviors (NVI and autonomy support)
would be an expression of the same internal motivation and goal.
Support for this assumption comes from empirical evidence showing that teachers’ student-oriented mastery goal orientation in class
is associated with a large number of different but learning-enhancing
teaching practices (Daumiller, Fasching, et al., 2023; Daumiller
et al., 2025).
Unexpectedly, machine learning-assessed NVI behaviors were
not signiﬁcantly related to teachers’ own perceptions or to students’
perceptions of their positive student–teacher relationships. An explanation for the lack of signiﬁcance of associations between machine
learning-assessed NVI and teacher-rated relationships might be
that the machine learning-assessed NVI score focused on nonverbal
social cues like facial expressions, posture, and proximity, whereas
relationships assessed broader aspects of the teacher (e.g., “My
mathematics teacher made me feel she/he really cared about me”).
While such nonverbal signals may contribute to relationship building, they might be insufﬁcient for fostering close student–teacher
relationships because, typically, relationship quality encompasses
a broad array of implicit social behaviors such as promoting feelings
of belonging, fairness, safety, social connection, or personal signiﬁcance (Trauernicht et al., 2025; Wentzel, 2022).

Theoretical Contribution
The theoretical contribution of our study is multifaceted. First, we
transferred the framework of temporal variability and contextspeciﬁcity that was introduced by Daumiller, Fasching, et al.
(2023) and Daumiller, Janke, et al. (2023) to study the variability
in motivational variables to the context of teaching behaviors. Our
ﬁndings revealed that the model is a useful tool to explain variability
not only in motivational variables but also in teaching behaviors. We
were able to show that the total variance in teachers’ NVI consists of
variable-speciﬁc fractions (temporal fractions: differences across
teaching situations), variable-general fractions (context-related fractions: differences across lessons), and stable-general fractions
(between-person differences: differences between teachers). By
transferring the conceptual model from motivational psychology

15

to instructional research, we contribute to a better systematization
of variability in theoretical constructs studied in educational
psychology.
Second, insights from this study extend our understanding of the
situated nature of teaching behaviors. By showing that approximately one-ﬁfth of the variation in NVI was explained by betweenteacher differences (22.5%), ﬁndings indicate that at least a part of
the variability in teachers’ NVI might be inﬂuenced by speciﬁc interactions with students and with the learning materials. In this context,
our theoretical contribution to research on NVI (J. F. Andersen et al.,
1979; Richmond et al., 2003) is to suggest that such behaviors of the
teacher should not be viewed solely as a stable trait possessed by
some teachers but absent in others. Rather, we suggest that NVI is
a situated construct that depends on situational and contextual
factors. The “situative turn” in motivational research (Nolen,
2020) proposed that motivational constructs are inﬂuenced by the
context in which they occur and, thus, by speciﬁc situations, learning
phases, or interactions with others and related desired identities,
expectations, and social roles. Our study showed that this perspective
can also inform research on instruction and behaviors of teachers.
Teaching should be seen as a situated activity shaped by contextual
factors. On a practical level, this direction holds promise for the planning and implementation of personalized feedback for teachers and
students. By helping educators identify situational predictors and
consequences of their behaviors, our ﬁndings and methods support
the application of tailored, learning-efﬁcient approaches in real time,
paving the way for adaptive and effective teaching strategies based
on dynamic and context-sensitive teaching behaviors.
Third, our ﬁndings inform theoretical frameworks of classroom
climate (Wentzel, 2022) and need-supportive teaching (Reeve &
Cheon, 2021) regarding the components of supportive classroom
environments. Our ﬁndings reveal that teachers’ NVI and observerreported socioemotional support were interrelated at the level of the
teacher, the lesson, and the teaching situation. Thus, NVI of the
teacher can be seen as an important component of supportive teaching. Teacher- and student-reported autonomy support, but not
student–teacher relationships, were related to NVI at the teacher
level. These insights extend current knowledge on need-supportive
teaching based on self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2002;
Reeve & Cheon, 2021) by indicating that teachers’ NVI and selfdetermined learning are strongly positively associated.

Implications for Research and Educational Practice
Our ﬁndings show that machine learning-based tools offer valuable opportunities for investigating key questions in instructional
research, such as the situatedness of teaching behaviors. We used
an objective and resource-efﬁcient method to assess differences
across different levels of analysis that validly captured the dynamic
and situation-speciﬁc character of nonverbal teaching behaviors.
Our ﬁndings contribute to the further advancement of relatively
new process-oriented objective and resource-efﬁcient classroom
(video) research approaches that apply machine learning-based
tools to the classiﬁcation of behaviors and social dynamics in learning and instruction (Wang et al., 2021). These tools can enable
researchers to efﬁciently assess teacher behaviors from large data
sets across various contexts in an efﬁcient and valid manner, providing insights into the situational dynamics of student–teacher interactions (Hou et al., 2024; Petković et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2021). Future

research should explore the potential of machine learning-based
methods to analyze both verbal and nonverbal social interaction
dynamics in classrooms (Tang et al., 2021; D. Wang et al., 2024;
W. Wang et al., 2021), also considering their limitations. Current
AI tools in educational research are limited to analyzing single
features (e.g., distance or facial expressions) and lack the ability to
consider contextual factors, such as group disruptions, that might
inﬂuence teachers’ behaviors (Qiao et al., 2021). Additionally, ethical considerations surrounding data privacy and the use of personal
data remain critical in machine learning-based educational research.
In our study, we used a publicly available video data set that had
been prepared and stored in a research data center and we ofﬁcially
received consent to use the video database to develop our computer
vision algorithm. However, in the context of smaller video data sets
that are not handled by ofﬁcial research data institutions, researchers
need to develop standardized protocols for classroom video data
usage in the context of machine learning-based research.
In terms of the educational implications, our ﬁndings emphasize
that NVI behaviors are also shaped by situational factors and not
only by personal characteristics. Such knowledge might help teachers to understand “good teaching” as student-oriented, professional
actions in class that are strongly shaped by social and situational
dynamics, complementing research focusing on personality patterns
of good teachers (Bastian et al., 2017; Burić et al., 2023). Knowing
that NVI behaviors matter for students’ perceptions of teachers’
(autonomy) support in class points to the importance of including
NVI in teacher education and teacher training. This could take
place by having teachers discuss and reﬂect on their own nonverbal
social behaviors, for example, using video recordings of their teaching practice or virtual reality classrooms (Richter et al., 2022). NVI
behaviors and socioemotional support behaviors can potentially be
trained in such learning environments in multiple social situations
with different groups of students.

Strengths and Limitations
Our study has several strengths, such as the substantial multimodal
design including machine learning—based assessment from classroom video data and teacher reports and observer ratings of teaching
behaviors. As observer ratings were carried out for 16 min at a time
and the situation-speciﬁc NVI ratings occurred for 30-s segments of
three lessons each for 46 teachers, we were able to include three levels
of analysis covering situational, contextual, and personal variations.
However, this study also has limitations. First, the sample size on
the teacher level was relatively small, with video recordings of 46
teachers. Although our analyses showed that substantial variation in
the study variables was located at the teacher level and the statistical
literature considers this sample size adequate for multilevel modeling
with sufﬁcient statistical power (Maas & Hox, 2005), we were not able
to carry out complex multilevel modeling approaches and our ﬁndings
need to be replicated in larger samples.
Second, our ﬁndings are limited to a sample of German mathematics teachers and their students. In the sample of the TALIS video
study in Germany, students in academic track schools are overrepresented. At academic track schools in Germany, students’ competences are higher but teacher support as perceived by students is
usually lower than at nonacademic track schools (Schiepe-Tiska
et al., 2023). One potential implication of our ﬁndings is that
mean levels of externally observed socioemotional support, and

possibly also teachers’ NVI, may be higher in more diverse samples.
Although the study sample was comparable to the population in
terms of student gender and country of birth, the ﬁndings should
be validated using more diverse data sets. In more heterogeneous
samples, greater variance in the teaching behaviors examined here
might be observed at both the lesson and situational levels, which
might reﬂect differences in subject-speciﬁc teaching cultures.
A third limitation was that the video clips we considered for the
rating process were limited to mathematics lessons on quadratic
equations and to lessons in which frontal presentations occurred,
and machine learning-assessed NVI behaviors and their relations
to observer-rated socioemotional support might differ in lessons
with different topics or social forms of interaction. Thus, further
studies are needed for different domains and teaching methods.
Fourth, we did not have data on verbal aspects of the social classroom interactions in this study, which would have been an important
additional source of information informing our algorithm and further
contextualizing the social dynamics that we assessed based on the
video data.
Fifth, the identiﬁcation of suitable 30-s video segments was
conducted manually, reﬂecting current limitations of computer
vision in complex, multiperson classroom settings. While several
preselection steps are amenable to automation (e.g., ﬁltering excessive camera motion via optical ﬂow/scene-change detection and
screening for teacher visibility using conﬁdence scores from faceor pose-tracking), several components remain challenging to automate reliably, including robust teacher identiﬁcation among multiple
classroom occupants, and precise detection of frontal-presentation
phases, which requires a higher-level, semantic understanding of
instructional context. Consequently, full removal of human involvement is not yet feasible; however, future iterations integrating automated motion ﬁltering, visibility gating, and improved multiperson
tracking could further reduce manual effort while maintaining data
quality. On the positive side, the algorithm proved robust in extracting information from ordinary lesson recordings by reusing already
existing data. Nevertheless, future studies using a video setup
focused more directly on the teacher would allow for higher degrees
of automated processing.

Conclusion
Our study advances the current understanding of teachers’ NVI and
support behaviors, highlighting the decisive role that dynamic situated
assessment methods play in classroom video research to better understand such behaviors, including their preconditions and consequences. Our contribution to prior work is to show that nonverbal
social behaviors of the teacher vary substantially between teachers,
lessons, and teaching situations, and thus cannot be seen as a purely
person-speciﬁc trait-like construct. Our study further contributes decisively to a better understanding of a supportive climate in class by suggesting that NVI is not a sufﬁcient condition to establish high-quality
relationships in class, but is signiﬁcantly associated with teachers’
autonomy-supportive teaching. Employing machine learning-based
methods, we demonstrated the potential of these tools to explore nonverbal teaching behaviors and their consequences for establishing a
supportive classroom environment. However, further work is required
to ensure the robustness and interpretability of ﬁndings derived from
such approaches. On a theoretical level, similar to research on motivation and emotion (Daumiller, Janke, et al., 2023; Moeller et al., 2022;

TEACHER NONVERBAL IMMEDIACY

Pekrun & Marsh, 2022), instructional research should more strongly
incorporate situated perspectives and develop comprehensive frameworks that account for situational, contextual, and person-related variations in teacher behavior.

Appendix B
Coding Manual for Human Raters of NVI and Its Subdimensions
Rating Manual: Nonverbal Teacher Behavior—
Subdimensions, Version 1.1
Introduction
The present rating process is part of a multistep procedure aimed
at analyzing teachers’ nonverbal social behaviors in classroom situations. In this initial phase, raters will evaluate speciﬁc subdimensions of teachers’ nonverbal behaviors from still images (frames
extracted from classroom videos). These ratings will subsequently
be used to train computer vision models to automatically analyze
teacher behavior.
In this initial rating round, you will focus speciﬁcally on three
individual subdimensions of teacher nonverbal behavior: gesture
intensity, body pose, perceived distance.
In subsequent steps of the project, these ratings will be used to
train a computer vision model, which will later analyze these behaviors automatically. Eventually, the goal is to combine these dimensions into an overall, holistic evaluation of teacher nonverbal

Interpersonal distance is a signiﬁcant dimension of nonverbal

• Body orientation: People maintain greater distances from
other people’s fronts than from their backs. Similarly, people
judge another person to be closer to their front than to their
back. Orientation must therefore be taken into account

same, perceived distance is lowest when the teacher is facing

Body Pose (Level of Relaxation)
Body pose provides valuable nonverbal cues about internal emotional states and social dynamics. You will rate the teacher’s posture
based on perceived overall relaxation.
Relevant cues include (but are not limited to):
• Head and neck alignment (relaxed vs. tense)
• Arm and hand positions (symmetry, stiffness)
• Torso positioning (upright, leaning, slouched)
• Leg and foot positions (symmetry, stance)
Important: The rating should represent a holistic, subjective
assessment of overall relaxation. Do not sum up individual cues
mathematically. Form an overall impression of how relaxed the
teacher appears.

The present rating process is part of a multistep procedure aimed
at analyzing teachers’ nonverbal social behaviors in classroom situations. In this initial phase, raters will evaluate teachers’ overall nonverbal immediacy based on 30-s video clips of classroom
instruction. The aim of this rating phase is to obtain holistic judgments of the degree to which a teacher communicates interpersonal
warmth and psychological closeness through nonverbal behavior.

of automatically assessing nonverbal immediacy in future applications.
This manual summarizes procedures, deﬁnitions, and instructions
needed for accurate and reliable NVI rating. Detailed example clips

teachers appear more engaged, warm, and dynamic; nonimmediate
