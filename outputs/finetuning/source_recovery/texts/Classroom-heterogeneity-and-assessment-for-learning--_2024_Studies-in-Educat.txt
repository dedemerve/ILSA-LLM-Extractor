TALIS

Despite extensive research on Assessment for Learning (AfL) in the classroom, the importance of contextual
factors as perceived by teachers remains underexplored. This study examines the extent to which elements of the
classroom context, in particular heterogeneity, influence the frequency of AfL. Using 2018 TALIS data from 47
countries, this study found that teachers who perceived higher levels of heterogeneity in their classrooms conducted more frequent classroom assessments. The degree of AfL was most significantly influenced by the
perception of heterogeneity among academically gifted students, followed by socioeconomically disadvantaged
students, immigrant students, students whose first language is different from the language of instruction, and
students with behavioral problems. Interestingly, when teachers perceive a higher proportion of low-achieving
students, the frequency of AfL tends to decrease. These findings suggest that AfL can be used not only as a
tool in circumstances where learning processes are challenging, but also as an approach to understanding and
addressing the diverse learning potentials and backgrounds in the classroom.

1. Introduction
The school system strives to provide the opportunities for learning
within the constraints of the given educational context. Teachers, at the
forefront of this process, undertake the crucial roles of facilitating student learning, evaluating outcomes, and promoting improvement. A
primary instrument in their repertoire is assessment, a complex series of
activities designed to gather and interpret information essential for the
enhancement of teaching and learning. Through understanding the
current learning status of students, assessment aims to improve their
learning capacities (McMillan, 2013). Despite certain negative aspects
on assessment practices prevalent, its fundamental role in the
teaching-learning process at the heart of schooling is largely undisputed.
The importance of classroom assessment in promoting both the
teaching-learning process and the consequential educational outcomes
for teachers and students is well recognized.
Classroom assessment, specifically Assessment for learning(: AfL)
plays a pivotal role not only due to its significant impact on students’
academic outcomes (Brookhart & DeVoge, 1999; Pat-El et , 2015;
Rodriguez, 2004), but also for providing immediate feedback on
teaching strategies (Black & Wiliam, 1998). AfL stands out as it does not
follow the pre-planned, curriculum-aligned format of large-scale

standardized tests designed to assess students’ knowledge and skills
(Shepard, 2010; Hamilton et al., 2002). Its distinctive spontaneous and
flexible nature means AfL can be integrated seamlessly into the teaching
and learning process, allowing for adjustments based on real-time
classroom dynamics and teacher judgment (Stiggins & Chappuis,
2005). This adaptability makes AfL especially suited to the varied and
evolving needs of a classroom, enabling educators to tailor their assessments in a way that directly supports and engages every student. The
critical role of teacher perception in identifying the moment for AfL
intervention underscores the nuanced relationship between classroom
context and assessment practice (Brookhart, 2003; Yan et al., 2021).
Despite the recognized potential of AfL in improving student outcomes and providing immediate feedback for instructional strategies
(Black & Wiliam, 1998; Martínez et al., 2009), there is a gap in understanding how AfL practices are influenced by classroom heterogeneity.
Classroom heterogeneity, which encompasses a wide range of student
backgrounds, abilities, and needs, presents unique challenges and opportunities for the implementation of AfL strategies. Existing research
has highlighted the positive outcomes of AfL practices but has primarily
focused on relatively homogeneous classroom settings or the overall
effectiveness of AfL, leaving a gap in our understanding of how teachers’
perceptions of classroom heterogeneity directly shape their engagement

Today’s educational landscape is marked by an unprecedented diversity of student populations, characterized by differences in cultural,
linguistic, academic, and socio-economic backgrounds. This diversity
requires a deeper exploration of the conditions under which AfL becomes a preferred tool for assessment (Gay, 2002; Pat-El et al., 2015).
Teachers navigating this complex environment must make informed
decisions to adapt their AfL practices to effectively meet the diverse
needs of their students (McMillan, 2013; Stiggins, 2002). However, the
literature has not extensively examined the initial decision-making
process of teachers in heterogeneous classrooms. This overlooks a
crucial aspect of educational practice: the adaptive and responsive approaches to assessment that AfL uniquely offers in response to diverse
classroom environments.
This study aims to bridge this gap by investigating the intricate
relationship between classroom heterogeneity and the application of AfL
practices, utilizing data from the Teaching and Learning International
Survey (TALIS) 2018, which provides insights from 47 countries. It seeks
to understand how teachers’ perceptions of the diverse classroom
environment influence their propensity to adopt AfL strategies. Specifically, this research endeavors to shed light on the factors that drive the
effective implementation of AfL by examining how teachers recognize
and respond to the varied educational needs presented by classroom
heterogeneity. The ultimate goal is to offer insights that can inform the
development of instructional strategies and teacher training programs,
thereby enhancing educators’ ability to support diverse learners through
tailored AfL practices in a global context.
This study aims to fill the gap in understanding how classroom heterogeneity affects the use of AfL practices. Specifically, it explores the
complex relationship between teachers’ perceptions of classroom heterogeneity and their use of AfL practices. The research is guided by the
following question How do teachers’ perceptions of classroom heterogeneity influence their frequency and approach to implementing AfL
practices? This question aims to uncover the dynamics at play as
teachers confront varying degrees of diversity in their classrooms and
decide how frequently to use AfL methods to effectively support all
students. Findings from this research will inform the development of
instructional strategies and teacher training programs designed to
enhance teachers’ ability to foster inclusive learning environments. Ultimately, this study seeks to deepen our understanding of the role of AfL
in meeting the diverse needs of students, thereby contributing to the
broader discourse on educational equity and effectiveness in heterogeneous classrooms.

The adaptability of AfL, underscored by the significant role of

Moreover, the practical effectiveness of AfL is largely determined by
teachers’ perceptions and interpretations of the classroom context. Their
awareness and understanding of the diverse composition of the
classroom-reflecting differences in students’ backgrounds, abilities, and
learning styles-directly influence the frequency and manner in which
AfL is implemented (Dee & Henkin, 2002; Deluca & Lam, 2014).
Acknowledging and responding to this heterogeneity with empathy and
precision is essential to the effective use of AfL practices targeted at
meeting the diverse learning needs of the classroom (Darling-Hammond
& Snyder, 2000). To support teachers in developing the necessary skills
to effectively implement AfL in diverse classroom settings, targeted
professional development programs that emphasize continuous learning
and adaptation are crucial (Christoforidou & Kyriakides, 2021).
In the study of AfL, it is clear that a teacher’s perception of the
classroom context has a profound effect on the teacher’s engagement in
AfL practices. In the following section, the critical impact of classroom
context and teacher perceptions on the implementation of AfL will be
explored in more detail.
2.2. Classroom heterogeneity and teacher’s perception on AfL
Teacher engagement in AfL is essential for clarifying and sharing
learning goals, collecting evidence of learning through student performance, and bridging the gap between students’ current levels and their
learning orientations (Wiliam & Thompson, 2007). The practical
implementation of formative assessments such as AfL in classrooms is
crucial, highlighting the important role teachers play in this process
(Black & Wiliam, 2018). However, a fundamental question is raised:
What influences teachers’ decisions to implement AfL more actively in
different classroom contexts? This research explores the influence of
classroom heterogeneity.
Sociologists have argued that the greater the proportion of diverse
backgrounds within a given group, the greater the cultural heterogeneity due to competing systems of meaning (Harding, 2007; Parkin,
1971). This concept of cultural heterogeneity is consistent with classical
research traditions in the study of social stratification that examine the
influence of cultural diversity on educational outcomes (Bourdieu,
2006; Breen and Goldthorpe, 1997). Even within a classroom, the more
students from different backgrounds there are, the more values are
mixed, so the classroom culture may be a heterogeneous mix rather than
a single entity.
Understanding the concept of cultural heterogeneity is essential to
recognizing how it may affect teachers’ AfL practices. It is within this
context that classroom heterogeneity is understood to encompass a
broad spectrum of diversity, including cultural, linguistic, academic,
and socio-economic differences among students (OECD, 2023). A heterogeneous classroom may require teachers to adopt AfL practices that
are sensitive to the diverse needs and backgrounds of their students,
requiring increased communication and interaction (Brown, 2004;
Taras, 2002). The motivation to use AfL in such diverse settings is often

2. Literature review
2.1. Assessment for learning: principles and practices in classroom
What teachers do in the teaching-learning process, which is one of
the most important functions of schooling, could be seen as two parts:
teaching and assessment. In the traditional perspective, teaching and
assessment have been considered as independent elements of educational activities, and assessment that relies on objective tests at the end
of instruction is being supplemented by other assessments that are better
at measuring important outcomes (Darling-Hammond & Snyder, 2000;
Guskey, 2003; McMillan, 2017). In addition, utilized on a broader scale
to gauge the effectiveness of educational systems, aiming to ensure
accountability through the evaluation of academic achievement
(Hanushek & Woessmann, 2012; Kane & Staiger, 2008; OECD, 2013).
Recent shifts in pedagogical focus have underscored the critical
importance of integrating assessment into the classroom to support
formative learning and instructional improvement (Leahy & Wiliam,
2012; Shepard, 2000). This evolution is epitomized by the emergence of
AfL, a methodology that transcends traditional assessment approaches
by fostering a more interactive and feedback-oriented learning environment (Andrade & Cizek, 2010; Earl, 2012; Hattie, 2008). While AfL

the diverse learning needs of their students. This is supported by the
premise that AfL provides teachers with a valuable means of gathering
evidence to inform their pedagogical strategies and decisions, particularly in environments with diverse learning orientations.

3.2. Measurements
The dependent variable is the frequency of classroom assessment
carried by teacher. TALIS 2018 asked a series of questions to teachers on
the frequency of classroom assessments: “I administer my own assessment”, “I provide written feedback on student work in addition to a
<mark, i.e. numeric score or letter grade> ”, “I let students evaluate their
own progress”, and “I observe students when working on particular tasks
and provide immediate feedback”. I analyze the structure of the interrelationship among these four responses, and condensed the information
contained in the original variables into the one composite variable with
a minimum loss of information employing factor analysis. To ensure the
structural validity of these items as a unified scale of AfL, Confirmatory
Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted. The CFA results, including a
Comparative Fit Index (CFI) of 0.905 and a Standardized Root Mean
Square Residual (SRMR) of 0.046, indicate a satisfactory model fit,
validating the composite AfL variable constructed from these responses.
This continuous variable (mean: 0, sd: 1) is the key outcome measure in
this study. The interpretation of this variable is that the higher the value,
the more often teachers carry out classroom assessment.
Given the findings of the literature review, which posits classroom
heterogeneity as a complex amalgam of cultural, linguistic, academic,
and socioeconomic differences (OECD, 2023), this study carefully chose
six items related to classroom context. These items were selected based
on their direct impact on the dynamics of AfL practices, emphasizing the
need for teaching strategies that are responsive to the diverse needs of
students within these heterogeneous contexts. The six selected dimensions of classroom heterogeneity, where the main explanatory variable is the level of classroom heterogeneity as perceived by teachers,
are as follows: (1) Students whose first language is different from the
languages of instruction or from a dialect of this/these languages, (2)
Low academic achievers, (3) Students with behavioral problems, (4)
Students from socio economically disadvantaged homes, (5) Academically gifted students, and (6) Students who are immigrants or with
migrant background. Teachers were asked to select one of five responses
for each item: (1) none, (2) 1–10 %, (3) 11–30 %, (4) 31–60 %, and (5)
more than 60 %.
In addition to a focal explanatory variable, this study includes a total
of eleven control variables that may influence teachers’ classroom
assessment. At the teacher level, this study controls for gender,
employment status (whether permanent or temporary), and educational
attainment (less than some college < bachelor’s degree < more than
master’s degree). At the school level, the study controls for the following
five variables: the frequency with which teachers are evaluated by each
of four agents (principal, other members of the school management
team, assigned mentors, teachers who are not part of the school management team, and external persons or bodies), the extent to which
teachers are empowered to make decisions in areas such as setting
school policy and shaping the curriculum, the student-teacher ratio, the

3. Data and methods
3.1. Data and samples
This study employs data from the 2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS), encompassing 126,330 teachers across 9024
secondary schools in 47 countries (data source: http://www.oecd.org/
education/talis/talis-2018-data.htm). TALIS is conducted approximately every five years, with the 2018 iteration being the most recent,
and provides a contemporary snapshot of educational practices and
teacher perceptions worldwide. AfL practices identified in the TALIS
dataset include engaging students in setting learning goals, providing
feedback for future improvement, and adjusting teaching strategies
based on student responses. These elements are central to the principles
of AfL, which emphasize continuous feedback, active student involvement in the assessment process, and the adaptability of teaching
methods to support diverse learning needs.
The study’s analysis uses a three-level multilevel model to examine
AfL practices. As TALIS followed a stratified two-stage probability
sampling design, the original data structured a two-level system. Note
that the participating countries considerably differ in their socioeconomic level and other characteristics relating to educational system,
offering a useful context with which to estimate cross-national variation
in classroom assessment. For a comparative perspective, the three-level
multilevel data was utilized by collecting the country level information
from other sources: teacher as level-1, school as level-2, and country as
level-3. This approach accounts for the influence of individual, institutional, and national factors on AfL implementation. The sample was
refined to include respondents with complete data on classroom
assessment and the variables essential for analysis. This process reduced
the original dataset from 153,682, with the missing data on the
dependent variable - 17.8 % of the total - showing no systematic association with other variables.
The operationalization of AfL involved the selection of TALIS 2018
items that embodied formative assessment strategies in action. This selection process aims to understand how classroom heterogeneityincluding cultural, linguistic, academic, and socioeconomic diversityinfluences teachers’ use of AfL. The study posits that classroom heterogeneity requires adaptable AfL practices, with teachers’ judgments
playing a critical role in tailoring assessment strategies to meet diverse
student needs and effectively improve learning environments.

5. Results
5.1. Descriptive statistics
Table 1 presents descriptive statistics of ten teacher-level variables,
five school-level variables and three country-level variables for each
country. The perception of classroom heterogeneity among teachers is
indicated by the proportion of those who report that more than 30 % of
their students belong to each of the specified categories of heterogeneity. In the dimension ’Students whose first language is different from the
languages of instruction or from a dialect of these languages’, South
Africa reports the highest percentage at 61.7 %, indicating a significant
level of linguistic diversity in classrooms. Singapore follows with a significant percentage of 58.0 %. At the other end of the scale, Hungary and
Japan have the lowest proportions at 1.6 % and 1.4 % respectively,
indicating less linguistic diversity in their classrooms. For ’low
achievers’, the data show that South Africa has the highest proportion at
77.5 %, indicating a significant challenge with academic performance in
the classroom. Cyprus also stands out with a notable percentage of
68.8 %, showing a higher-than-average proportion of low achievers.
Conversely, the Russian Federation has the lowest proportion of low
achievers at 24.7 %, suggesting a lower prevalence of academic challenges in their classrooms.
In terms of "students with behavioral problems," Brazil has the
highest reported percentage at 62.5 %, indicating a significant number
of students with behavior management challenges. In contrast, Vietnam
reports the lowest percentage at only 4.6 %, suggesting fewer behavioral
problems in the classroom. With regard to ’students from socioeconomically disadvantaged homes’, Colombia has the highest percentage at a staggering 78.5 %, highlighting a significant number of
students from less privileged backgrounds. On the other hand, the Czech
Republic has the lowest reported percentage at 13.3 %, indicating a
smaller proportion of such students in their classrooms. For "academically gifted students," Kazakhstan leads with the highest percentage
(68.8 %), indicating a notable presence of academically advanced students in the classroom, while Portugal reports the lowest percentage at
only 1.4 %, indicating a much less pronounced identification or
reporting of giftedness in the classroom. In Canada (Alberta) and Sweden, the highest proportions were observed, both above 42 %, indicating
a significant presence of students from immigrant or migrant backgrounds in classrooms. At the other end of the spectrum, Hungary and
Lithuania report the lowest percentages, under 1 %, suggesting a more
homogeneous student population in terms of migrant background.
Turning to our main outcome, Fig. 1 shows the average level of AfL
for each country, along with the values at one standard deviation above
and below the mean. Countries are arranged in ascending order of the
average level of AfL, highlighting a spectrum of variability in these
measures. The average level of assessment is lowest in Japan (− 1.78)
and also relatively low in the Russian Federation, Korea and Chinese
Taipei. In contrast, Colombia has the highest average level of AfL (0.97),
with the United Arab Emirates, Portugal and Mexico also showing high

shaping the variability of classroom assessments across countries.

4. Methods
This study applies two statistical approaches to examine the extent to
which classroom assessment vary according to teachers’ perceived levels
of classroom heterogeneity. First, a three-level multilevel model is used
to take a full advantage of the data structure with the variation across
countries and across teachers within schools. The advantage of multilevel approach is that it identifies a country-specific level and trend in
classroom assessment by classroom heterogeneity. The estimates for the
multilevel model are based on the following equation (Raudenbush &
Bryk, 2002):
Assessmentijk = β0 + β1 Heterogenietyijk + β2 Femaleijk + β3 EDUijk
+ β4 Permanentijk + δ1 Appraisaljk
+ δ2 Empowermentjk + δ3 S − T ratiojk + δ4 Publicjk
+ δ5 Urbanityjk + γ1 lnGDPk + γ2 GINIk
+ γ 3 Working hoursk + εijk
where assessmentijk present the level of classroom assessment for
teacher i in school j in country k. The β–parameters is for teacher level,
the δ–parameters for school level, and the γ–parameters for country
level. In multilevel analysis, missing data is a challenging issue, particularly at the upper levels (Gibson & Olejnik, 2003; McCoach, 2010),
since any group-level unit with missing data excludes all individual units
nested within the group-level unit for the analyses. At the teacher- and
school-level, all the variable was cleaned without missing data by using
the listwise deletion method.
Multilevel models have the advantage of being able to account for
characteristics at each unit of analysis (Pat-El et al., 2015; Yan & Chiu,
2023). In this study, the three-level model captures the characteristics of
individual teachers within the school, but also includes school and
country characteristics to explain differences between schools and
countries. Given the potential for unobservable school and country
characteristics to bias the results, this study also apply the school- and
country-fixed effects models. A major advantage of the fixed effects
model is its ability to account for both observed and unobserved characteristics at the school and country level, which are captured by
dummy variables for each school and country, allowing random errors
to remain independent of the observed variables. It’s worth noting that
while fixed effects models potentially reduce the influence of unobservable school and country characteristics, they do not eliminate them
entirely.

study incorporates estimation weights and uses the balanced repeated
replication (BRR) method. Estimation weights adjust for unequal

policies, cultural norms, and national teaching standards have on the
implementation of classroom assessment practices. These findings show
the importance of taking national contexts into account when examining
educational policies and suggest the potential for international policy
learning and adaptation in the area of classroom assessment.

5.2. Results of multilevel models
Table 2 presents the results of three-level multilevel regression
models predicting the level of AfL by four teacher-level variables, five
school-level variables and three country-level variables. These are the
results of classroom heterogeneity across the six domains described in
the Measures section above. Model 1 includes only classroom heterogeneity, which is the key explanatory variable at the teacher-level, and
Model 2 shows the results of including all the control variables such as
gender, working status, and educational attainment, at the teacher level;
the degree of teacher appraisal, the degree of empowerments given to
teachers, student-teacher ratio, school type, and urbanity at the school
level; GDP per capita, GINI index, and teacher working hours, at the
country level.
The main pattern from the results in Table 2 shows that teachers are
more likely to carry out AfL when they perceive a higher level of heterogeneity in their classroom (It should be clarified that this analysis
does not establish causality, but merely shows a controlled correlation).
This trend aligns with what might be expected based on previous studies
(Brown, 2004; Taras, 2002). Even after accounting for potential confounders, this positive correlation persists. AfL appears to be most
frequently when teachers perceive a high degree of heterogeneity
among academically gifted students. Lower frequencies are associated
with perceptions of heterogeneity among socio-economically disadvantaged students, immigrant students, non-native speakers, and students with behavioral problems. Interestingly, the frequency of AfL
increase noticeably when teachers perceive classroom heterogeneity to
be 30 % or more.
Interestingly, the analysis reveals a distinct pattern regarding
teachers’ perceptions of low-achieving students in the classroom. While
an increase in classroom heterogeneity across five dimensions (linguistically diverse, disruptive behavior, socioeconomically disadvantaged,
academically gifted, and immigrant background) generally corresponds
to a higher use of AfL practices, a notable exception is observed in the
presence of low-achieving students. In such cases, teachers are less likely
to use AfL strategies. This divergence suggests a complex dynamic at
play. On the one hand, AfL, with its emphasis on formative feedback and
student engagement, has the potential to address the diverse needs of
low-achieving students, and thereby promote improvements in their
academic performance. On the other hand, the observed decline in the
use of AfL in classrooms with higher proportions of low-achieving students may reflect underlying challenges. These could include a

(Bennett, 2011; Timperley, 2008). Beyond the existing research on AfL,
which has mainly focused on the effectiveness of AfL on students’
educational outcomes (Black & Wiliam, 1998; Hattie, 2008), the
assessment method itself (Stiggins & Chappuis, 2005; Wiliam, 2011),
and teachers’ roles and behaviors for successful implementation of AfL
(Black et al., 2004; Wiliam & Thompson, 2007), this study tried to
explore the classroom context in which teachers implement AfL (Harlen
& Deakin Crick, 2002; Wiliam, 2011). This study attempts to fill this gap
in our knowledge by exploring the moderating role of classroom cultural
context on teachers’ implementation of AfL in classroom teaching and
learning situations.
Using TALIS 2018 data, which allows for cross-country comparisons,
this study analyzed how the degree of AfL practiced by teachers in 47
countries varies with the cultural heterogeneity of their classrooms. The
results can be summarized in two main conclusions. First, teachers are
more likely to engage in AfL when they perceive high levels of cultural
heterogeneity in their classrooms. This finding was consistent across a
range of heterogeneity, including academically gifted students, socioeconomically disadvantaged students, students whose first language is
different from the language of instruction, students from immigrant
backgrounds, and students with behavioral problems. Second, however,
teachers are less likely to use AfL as the level of heterogeneity in the
classroom increases due to low-achieving students. These findings suggest the possibility that teachers do not perceive cultural heterogeneity
in the composition of students based on academic achievement levels.
The strengths of this study lie in its comprehensive approach to
examining the relationship between classroom context and the implementation of AfL practices. This study offers valuable cross-country
comparisons, providing insights that are applicable on a worldwide
scale. This research contributes to the field by highlighting the importance of teachers’ perceptions of classroom heterogeneity as a significant factor in the implementation of AfL strategies. Furthermore, the
findings have practical implications for educational policy and

curriculum development by advocating for the inclusion of culturally
responsive assessment practices that support teachers in effectively
implementing AfL. For policymakers and educators, this study underscores the potential of AfL to improve learning outcomes for students
from diverse backgrounds and calls for the development of supportive
policies to equip teachers with the skills necessary to navigate the
complexities of heterogeneous classrooms. This finding suggests the
need for further exploration of how AfL can be effectively tailored to
meet the diverse needs of all students, particularly those who are
academically challenged.
There are several limitations to the present study that should be
noted. First, the reliance on secondary data derived from TALIS 2018
and the reliance on teachers’ self-reported perceptions introduce an
inherent risk of subjective bias that could influence the interpretation of
classroom heterogeneity. TALIS data might not have been designed to
cover AfL or other variables of interest in this study in the most optimal
way. This limitation highlights the need for future studies to incorporate
more objective measures or diverse data sources, in order to develop a
more refined understanding of how classroom diversity affects AfL
practices. In addition, while this study has clarified the complex relationship between classroom heterogeneity and the use of AfL, it has not
explicitly addressed the potential impact of teachers’ perceptions of
diverse classrooms, an aspect briefly touched upon in the theoretical
framework but not directly measured due to the limitations of the TALIS
dataset. Future research could benefit from integrating direct assessments of teachers’ attitudes and beliefs to determine their influence on
the implementation of AfL strategies. In addition, the use of crosssectional data limits the ability to draw causal inferences about the
dynamic interplay between classroom diversity and AfL practices. Longitudinal research could provide deeper insights into how teachers’
strategies evolve in response to changing classroom compositions and
offer a more comprehensive understanding of the conditions under
which AfL can be most effectively employed.

Despite of these drawbacks, this study is still significant in that it was
an attempt to understand the classroom context in which AfL is conducted. This study contributes to the existing knowledge of AfL by
examining the impact of the cultural context of the classroom, which has
been overlooked in previous studies of AfL (Bennett, 2011; Schellekens
et al., 2021; Stiggins & Chappuis, 2005). The findings highlight the
importance of preparing teachers to effectively implement AfL strategies
in diverse classroom settings, which aligns with the growing emphasis
on developing teachers’ capacity to facilitate deeper learning (Darling-Hammond et al., 2019). This study provides insight into the factors
that influence teachers’ assessment practices and highlights the importance of considering classroom context when promoting effective AfL
strategies. Furthermore, understanding the impact of classroom cultural
context on the implementation of AfL can inform the development of
educational policies and curricula. These programs should emphasize
continuous learning and adaptation, equipping teachers with the skills
and knowledge necessary to design and implement formative assessments that are responsive to the diverse needs of their students (Christoforidou & Kyriakides, 2021). Content-intensive professional
development programs that focus on enhancing teachers’
subject-specific knowledge and instructional practices have been shown
to improve student achievement (Garet et al., 2016). By providing
teachers with ongoing support and opportunities for collaborative

learning, such initiatives can enhance the potential of AfL to create inclusive learning environments and improve outcomes for all students.
Ultimately, the insights gained from this study can guide the development of policies and curricula that promote inclusive and culturally
responsive assessment practices, thereby contributing to the improvement of learning outcomes for students from diverse backgrounds.
