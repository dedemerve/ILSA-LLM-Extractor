Machine learning algorithm for grading open-ended

Abstract
Worldwide, open-ended questions that require short answers have been used in many
exams in fields of science, such as the International Student Assessment Program
(PISA), the International Science and Maths Trends Research (TIMSS). However,
multiple-choice questions are used for many exams at the national level in Turkey,
especially high school and university entrance exams. This study aims to develop an
objective and useful automatic scoring model for open-ended questions using machine
learning algorithms. Within the scope of this aim, an automated scoring model con-
struction study was conducted on four Physics questions at a University level course
with the participation of 246 undergraduate students. The short-answer scoring was
handled through an approach that addresses students’ answers in Turkish. Model
performing machine learning classification techniques such as SVM (Support Vector
Machines), Gini, KNN (k-Nearest Neighbors), and Bagging and Boosting were applied
after data preprocessing. The score indicated the accuracy, precision and F1-Score of
each predictive model of which the AdaBoost.M1 technique had the best performance.
In this paper, we report on a short answer grading system in Turkish, based on a
machine learning approach using a constructed dataset about a Physics course in
Turkish. This study is also the first study in the field of open-ended exam scoring in
Turkish.

1 Introduction
Nowadays, technological developments are progressing rapidly, and this process also
affects education. One of the most important reflections of these effects is the ability to
measure knowledge (Jayashankar and Sridaran 2017). Assessment and evaluation play
a central role in the education process. The assessment and evaluation used in deter-
mining the students’ knowledge and level are very important for both the students and
the teacher. A reliable and valid assessment tool not only reveals what the students are
missing, but also predicts in which subjects or areas they can be successful in the future.
Measurement and evaluation tools such as open-ended, short-answer, multiple-choice,
true/false, gap filling, essay and other methods are used in measuring the level of
student knowledge. Short-answer questions are open-ended questions that require the
students to create an answer limited to between 3 and 5 words and two paragraphs (Ziai
et al. 2012). They are mostly used in examinations to assess the necessary knowledge
and understanding of students. The usage of short-answer questions in measuring the
students’ knowledge is more decisive than multiple-choice questions (Gronlund 1998).
In multiple-choice questions, students choose one of the options offered to them, and
do not specify why they give this answer. On the other hand, open-ended or short-
answer questions allow students to write and explain their answers and defend their
ideas (Burrows et al., 2015). Thus, a more detailed and accurate assessment can be
made about the student’s level of knowledge. While open-ended questions have this
advantage, it is quite difficult to score and evaluate them than multiple-choice
questions.
While multiple-choice questions can be scored automatically with optical readers,
the number of students and the measurement based on human factors (injustice based
on prejudice, fatigue, inconsistency, and validity-reliability) make it difficult to measure
information in the scoring and evaluation of open-ended questions.
The human effort requirement, which is used to evaluate students’ answers to open-
ended questions, increased with large-scale exams (Alvarado et al. 2018). Using
computer-aided automatic scoring systems, they can evaluate and analyze responses
of the open-ended questions in a much more detailed, pre-judged, and objective
manner, and in a shorter time than human teachers. The automatic scoring systems of
open-ended questions have become significant and grown exponentially in the last few
years.
In this research, short-answer scoring will be handled through an approach that
addresses students’ answers in a Turkish Physics course.
2 Related work
The Turkish alphabet is based on Latin letters and contains 29 letters. In particular, the
letters “ç, Ç, ğ, Ğ, ı, ö, ö, ş, ş, ü, Ü” are not used in the Latin alphabet while they are
frequently used in the Turkish language, and these letters are not included in the
database available in the world. In terms of structure, it is among the “agglutinant”
languages which are called as additive. There are no prefixes in Turkish. In addition; in
the syntax, identifiers come first, followed by those defined (for example; adjectives
used before nouns); there is no grammatical gender; word derivation and inflection are

done with suffixes in Turkish. By using suffixes, at least ten different words can be
derived from a root word; therefore, it has a rather complex structure compared to other
languages. This language, which is very difficult to learn and write even for people,
constitutes a much more complex identification problem for machines. The interpreta-
tion of the meaning of a sentence written in Turkish is for the machine a challenging
task. Therefore, there is a limited number of studies on machine learning in Turkish.
However, there are many studies in the world for scoring open-ended questions using
Automatic short answer grading (ASAG) systems for different languages.
Automatic short answer (response) grading (ASAG) is one of the favorite research
areas in the field of educational data mining (Burrows et al. 2015). In general, the most
used ASAG approaches are response-based, reference-based, corpus-based, Machine
Learning, semi-automatic and fully-automatic systems (Sakaguchi et al. 2015). A
response-based approach uses detailed features extracted from the student’s response
itself, and learns a scoring function from human-scored responses. A reference-based
approach compares the student’s response to reference texts, such as exemplars for each
score level, or specifications of required content from the assessment’s scoring guide-
lines (Sakaguchi et al. 2015). A corpus-based approach requires a reasonable corpus,
depending on the domain (Klein et al. 2011). In the Machine Learning approach,
supervised or unsupervised learning models are used through data mining (Shermis
2015). In semi-automatic systems, students’ answers are processed (clustered) to
facilitate the grading process (Basu et al. 2013). Fully-automatic systems produce
grades for each student, with or without additional feedback. The most important of
these studies are presented below;
Alfonseca and Perez (2004) used an algorithm which is called BLEU (Bilingual
Evaluation Understudy), based on n-gram and normalization structure. They carried out
Evaluating Responses with Bleu, and they showed that Atenea is more powerful than
coordinate matching and vector space model baselines (Perez et al. 2004; Perez-Marin
2004). After that, Perez et al. 2004) managed to merge BLEU and LSA scores together,
instead of the individual features as part of a machine learning solution. As a result of
this step, Atenea is considered as a corpus-based method. Bukai et al. (2006) handled
LSA based on an inverted index data structure, which is constructed by content from
the web using topically-relevant documents with SAMText (Short Answer Measure-
ment of TEXT), and discussed that the inverted index and crawling idea is more useful
for short answers compared to long answers. Mohler and Mihalcea (2009) developed
several systems to examine unsupervised grading methods using Knowledge-based and
Corpus-based text-to-text similarity measures. They used a corpus composed from
Wikipedia. They showed a Pearson correlation value of r = 0.463 between the computer
assigned grades and the average of human assigned grades. They also believed in
merging the best student answers with teacher responses in this approach. Klein et al.
(2011) implemented the student answers into the LSA model, and they used three
approaches such as random selection, clustering, or marked selecting. Klein et al.
(2011) stated that the third approach was the most effective, enabling automatic
selection, and showed the minimization of the manually marked set of submissions.
Bailey and Meurers (2008) explored CAM (Content Evaluation Module) based on the
nearest neighbor classifier, and measured the percentage of content overlap using word
unigram, trigram, noun phrases, text similarity thresholds, and parts of speech. Nielsen
et al. (2008) considered their machine learning system based on the C4.5 decision tree

for classification. In this system, the choice of features includes both lexicalized and
syntactic features. Hou and Tsao (2011) performed a system based on four classes of
features which are extracted, comprising POS tags, term frequency, tf. idf, and entropy,
which is combined with an SVM classifier. Basu et al. (2013) applied the k-medoids

combined with dense reference-based features to build another stacked classifier. They
emphasized the performance using quadratically weighted κ between the human and
predicted scores. Zhang et al. (2016) compared Deep Belief Networks (DBN) to some
classifiers. The DBN performed better than the other classifiers in terms of accuracy,
precision, and F-measure.
3 Model of the research
As reported by Madnani et al. (2017), the most suitable approach depends on the open-
ended nature of the question and the language structure. For these reasons, we have
constructed the most suitable model considering the structure of the Turkish language
and terminology of the Physics course.
In this research, short answer grading is handled with a supervised approach which
was based on the students’ answers. This model has been determined as the most
suitable model for the Turkish language. A supervised machine learning algorithm uses
pre-scored training samples from human raters on a written-response prompt. The
algorithm learns the behavior of the raters by analyzing the classification patterns in
the pre-scored answers. In the evaluation of scoring of open-ended questions by using
machine learning algorithms, the systems imitate the field expert. For these reasons, we
believed the method is the closest to human scoring as an alternative to the methods
given above in the Turkish Physics course. The architecture and the machine learning
model of this research is shown in Fig. 1.

Open-ended questions can be scored with analytic, classification, ranking and
overall impression scoring methods. The main disadvantage of open-ended
questions is that they cannot be scored objectively like multiple-choice items,
since the score may differ according to the person who does the scoring.
(Romagnano 2001; Van der Linden and Hambleton 1997). The most reliable
of these methods is analytical scoring (key scoring). In key scoring, the
answers to the questions are prepared in advance, and each answer is analyzed
to the finest detail (Graham et al. 2012). In this context, within the scope of
this study, the answer key of the four university physics questions’ scores was
determined by three physics education experts, and one assessment and evalu-
ation expert. The score limits of each item were determined, and the raters’
opinions were consulted.

to the short answer questions, using the key scoring as 0.87 for Q1, 0.79 for Q2,
0.92 for Q3 and 0.90 for Q4, and 0.87 for the median correlation.

3.3.4 Feature selection
Since redundant attributes (features) mostly cause a negative impact on the
accuracy of the classification algorithms, the feature selection has a great impor-
tance in data mining. The essential problem is to decide on the method for
choosing related features from a set of collected features. There are different
methods for feature selection, such as document frequency, chi-squared statistic,
information gain, mutual information, and correlation coefficient (Hsu and Hsieh
2010). Among them, we used the correlation coefficient method to eliminate some
words as redundant features that have a low coefficient with other features to
increase the accuracy of the classification models in our study. To eliminate these
redundant words, we used the function findCorrelation() with a cutting of param-
eter (cutoff = 0.30). Table 2 shows the maximum number of individual words, and
the number of features generated for each question.
3.3.5 Balancing the dataset
Many machine learning applications are engaged in imbalanced data (Zhang and
Mani 2003). In our study, we observed that there was a class imbalance problem
in the answers to each individual question. An oversampling technique named
Smote (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling) was used to eliminate the imbalanced
data problem (Chawla et al. 2002).
3.3.6 Data partition
After obtaining the balanced dataset, it was split as 75% training and 25%
testing sets at random, when applied boosting and bagging algorithms were
applied, and repeated the process ten times to have ten different predictive
models. After that, k-fold cross validation techniques for Gini, SVM and KNN
were applied.
3.4 Model building
Text classification is an assignment process of the documents into predefined class
(categories) based on their semantic content by using supervised learning algorithms
(Joachims 1999). In our study, Bagging and AdaBoost.M1 (Adaptive Boosting)
algorithms were applied as ensemble learning techniques (Bagging and
Boosting) to define the grade (class) of the exam papers. Besides, SVM, Gini
and KNN algorithms were applied as supervised machine learning techniques.
The algorithms were applied in three stages. The first stage of the application
was the building of an individual predictive model of them on the class labeled
training dataset. The second stage was the class predicting of the testing data
with these predictive models. In the last stage the performance of the men-
tioned classification models were compared, concerning the proportion of the
total number of correct predictions in all evaluated cases (accuracy), the preci-
sion and F1-score.

3.4.1 Bagging (Bootstrap aggregation)
Bagging is an ensemble learning technique. Because of the complexity and scale
of the problem of a large and high-dimensional data, it is impossible to find a
good classification model that can work in one step (Syarif et al. 2012). The
technique is very beneficial for this kind of data. It reduces variance and also helps
to avoid overfitting. The Breiman's Bagging algorithm was applied by using the
bagging () function in the library of R Package named “adabag” in this study
(Alfaro et al. 2015).
3.4.2 Boosting
Boosting is another ensemble learning technique. It creates a strong learner,
combining many weak learners. The main idea of the boosting algorithms is to
train classifiers consecutively to improve their performance (Freund and Schapire
1996). The actual classification task is done by combining the votes of the
different classifiers and weighing them by the error they had on the training set.
The AdaBoost.M1 (Adaptive Boosting) algorithm is one of the ensemble boosting
classifiers invented by Yoav Freund and Robert Schapire in 1996. It was applied
by using the boosting() function in the library of R Package named “adabag” in
this study (Alfaro et al. 2015).
3.4.3 Gini algorithm
Creating binary splits, the Gini algorithm generates both classification and
regression trees by using the function rpart() in the library of the “rpart” R
package. Thus, before using the function, this package must have been installed
to reach its library (Therneau et al. 2017).
3.4.4 Support vector machines (SVM)
Support Vector Machines (SVM) is a supervised learning classification algorithm.
It has been extensively used in text classification problems. SVM is a kind of
Linear Classifiers. A linear classifier classifies a text document based on a linear
combination of feature values (Mowafy et al. 2018). The SVM algorithm is
applied by using the function svm() in the library of the “e1071” R package
(Meyer et al. 2019).
3.4.5 K-Nearest neighbors algorithm (KNN)
KNN is one of the supervised learning algorithms. It classifies the samples
according to the class of their nearest neighbors. In this study, this algorithm
was applied by using the function knn() in the library of the “class” R package
(Ripley and Venables 2019).

4 Findings
In this study, the supervised machine learning techniques Gini, SVM, KNN,
and also Breiman’s Bagging, Freund and Schapire’s Adaboost.M1 algorithms
were applied on four different datasets related to a single Physics question,
respectively. Two partitions were created for each dataset as 75% training set,
and 25% testing set. Following the partitioning, the algorithms were applied on
the training data and predicted the grade of each answer sheet in the testing
data, consecutively.
As mentioned before, to apply Adaboost.M1, firstly we handled ten different
random training and testing datasets with the ratio of 75% and 25% within the
whole data. Then, the algorithm was applied on these datasets to build ten
different predictive models. Each predictive model was evaluated for each
category in the testing data set according to the performance measures called
accuracy, precision, and F1-score performance measures for each category in
the testing data set, as shown in Figs. 3, 4, 5, and 6 in Appendix 2.
The experimental results confirmed the superiority of Adaboost.M1 over the
other algorithms, because of the highest average of accuracy of each predictive
model for each question. Each predictive model was evaluated through the
accuracy performance measures for each category in the testing data set, as shown
in Fig. 2.
A validation test was also performed at ten iterations, using Adaboost.M1 for
each question. Accuracy, precision and F1-Score were presented in Table 3.
According to the evaluation of the test results; the accuracy values ranged
between 0.84–0.99 for Q1 (528 features), 0,65–0.84 for Q2, which is the longest
and most complex question (1037 features), 0.85–0.91 for Q3 (724 features) and
0.87–0.98 for Q4 (316 features) for the Adaboost.M1 algorithm, for ten iterations.
The precision values ranged between 0.78–0.98 for Q1, 0.75–0.95 for Q2, 0.87–
0.95 for Q3 and 0.77–1.00 for Q4 for the Adaboost.M1 algorithm, for ten
iterations. The F1-score ranged between 0.84–0.95 for Q1, 0.87–0.98 for Q2,
0.84–0.91 for Q3 and 0.77–0.97 for Q4 for the Adaboost.M1 algorithm, for ten
iterations.
In this study, four Physics questions with different characteristics were used.
The scoring key of the questions and the scoring method for each question were
determined according to expert opinions. The average number of words in the
given answers was 61 for Q3 and Q4 which had 2 grades (0–1). Similarly, the
average number of words in the given answers was 52 for Q1 which had 2 grades
(0–1). Finally, the average number of words in the given answers was 125 for Q2
which had 5 grades (0–1–2-3-4).
The SVM, Gini, KNN, Bagging and Boosting algorithms were compared for a
total of 984 responses according to their performance metrics. The Adaboost.M1
algorithm was selected as the best algorithm among them. The accuracy of the
mentioned algorithm ranged between 0.84–0.99 for Q1 (528 features), 0.65–0.84
for Q2 (1037 features), 0.85–0.91 for Q3 (724 features) and 0.87–0.98 for Q4

(316 features) for ten iterations. Its precision ranged between 0.78–0.98 for Q1,

Within the scope of the study, we repeated these stages for each different

5 Discussion and conclusion
Worldwide, open-ended questions that require short answers have been used in
many exams such as the International Student Assessment Program (PISA), the
International Science and Maths Trends Research (TIMSS) in many fields of
science. However, multiple-choice questions are used for many exams at the
national level, especially in high school and university entrance exams in
Turkey. Although multiple-choice questions are useful for large groups nowa-
days, they cannot measure the learners’ level of achievement related to the
subject.
This study aims to develop an objective and useful automatic scoring model for
open-ended Physics questions using text classification algorithms. Within the
scope of this aim, an automated scoring model construction study was conducted
on four Physics questions at a University course. Short-answer scoring was
handled through an approach that addresses the students’ answers in Turkish.
We used and compared the methods closest to human scoring as alternative
methods. With this purpose, we developed an application in R language, which
includes five main parts such as; data extraction, data preprocessing, model
performing, and performance evaluation of the models. In model performing,
machine learning classification techniques which are SVM, Gini, KNN, and
Bagging and Boosting were applied after data preprocessing. The score
indicated that the accuracy of each predictive model of the Boosting technique
was acceptable. This study was conducted with the participation of 246
undergraduate students who had taken the Physics course.
Zhang et al. (2016) compared six different classification algorithms using
16,228 short answers (average word length 7) for high school level Physics. In
this study, they stated that DBN (Deep Belief Networks) showed the best
performance with an F1-score of 0.72–0.75. Herwanto et al. (2018) used three
different PISA datasets and UKARA on the Bahasa Indonesian language in
their study. The answers of the students were around 300 words, and there were
dichotomous (0–1) and polytomous (11,12,00) phases in the scoring of the
questions. Six thousand seven hundred seventy-six students’ answers were
scored with the Adaboosting.M1 algorithm. As a result of this study; it was
stated that the F1-score values were in the range of 0.97–0.70, and the
dichotomous phase scoring was seen as more successful. In the Alvarado
3835

et al. (2018) study, 1876 students’ answers (the number of words varied
between 3 and 9 words) were taken into consideration using the corpus. Two
hundred eighteen university students and six medical science questions which
consisted of binary classifications were used in this study. They aimed to
classify the answers as true-false (1–0) automatically. For this purpose, they
compared six different classification algorithms, and stated that N-gram had the
best performance with 0.93 precision value. Despite the results of the above-
mentioned studies, in our study, five different classification models were built
by using the SVM, Gini, KNN, Bagging and Boosting algorithms for a total of
984 responses (average number of words in four questions is 75) for university
Physics subjects. When comparing the performance metrics of these models, the
model of the Adaboost.M1 algorithm was chosen as the best model. The results
of four questions showed that the F1-score of the mentioned algorithm changes
in the range of 0.77 and 0.98 for ten iterations.
Pribadi et al. (2018) used Texas Corpus, and three iterations in their study,
which was applied with 87 questions, and each question contained 7 to 10 sub-
questions. A total of 2442 student responses were used in the study. Student
responses were scored by two different raters in the 0–5 range, according to the
reference answers. The shortest sentence had one word and the longest sentence
had 170 words in the Texas corpus. In Pribadi et al. (2018) study, firstly 261
students closest to the teacher reference answer were classified using the MMR
(Maximum Marginal Relevance), and then 2442 students’ answers were scored
using the GAN-LCS method, according to the 261 reference student answers. In
the results of this study; MMR showed 91.95% accuracy and the Root Mean
Square (RMSE) correlation values of GAN-LCS were reported to be in the range
of 0.884 to 0.468. Galhardi et al. (2018) compared the Baseline, N-gram, Text
Statistics, Lexical Similarity and Semantic Similarity methods for the Portuguese
language. Three thousand six hundred seventy-five students’ answers (the number
of words varies between 1 and 136 words) using 245 primary school students and
15 biology questions which consisted of 0–1–2-3 and 0–1 classifications (grade?)
were used in their study. It was showed that the best performance among the used
algorithms has N-gram with 69% accuracy for the multi-label (0–1–2-3) classifi-
cation and 85% accuracy for the binary (0–1) classification. In our study, there
was a similar categorized question (Q2). It had 5 categories (0–1–2-3-4). The
accuracy of the chosen classification model values ranged between 0.87–0.98 for
this question.
This study was carried out without using a pre-built corpus because a
Turkish corpus in the field of Physics does not exist yet. The achieved results
for each question showed that the algorithm related to the boosting technique
gave more accurate predictions compared to other algorithms. To achieve the
closest performance to human scoring, ten iterations were used in the compar-
ison of each classification. When the results of the studies above are compared
with the results of our study considering the criteria of corpus usage, the
number of iterations, length of student responses, and scoring phases, the

Adaboost.M1 algorithm is believed to be the closest to human scoring and had
the best performance. In addition to these criteria, as it is well-known that
open-ended Physics questions have a language that is difficult to explain even
in exams held by students orally or using paper and pen, since Physics has a
visceral and scientific language structure (VanLehn et al. 2002). In this respect,
with this study, the best automatic scoring algorithm for open-ended questions
used in physics, which is such a complicated field, has been developed for the
Turkish language. Thus, a small Turkish Physics corpus will be built due to
this study.
Consequently, the study shows that it is possible to use open-ended questions
that measure knowledge in some national selection and placement exams in
Turkey. As it is known in the international level, c-rater is technology in the
Educational Testing Service (ETS) used for automatic content scoring for short
free-text responses (Leacock and Chodorow 2003). C-rater demonstrates the
correctness, failing, incorrectness or inadequacy of students’ responses, and it
is not designed to score open-ended questions. In this respect, the c-rater is
suitable for usage in traditional written exams. This is because teachers can
measure whether students understand what they do and how much they can
understand. Our model was aimed at correctly scoring open-ended questions,
instead of multiple-choice questions applied to numerous people. In this respect,
our model has a structure different from the c- rater. The model proposed in
this study is similar to the PISA and TIMMS exams, and it provides a novel
contribution to the literature and can be applied not only in Turkey’s exams but
also in the PISA and TIMSS.
This study is also the first study in the field of open-ended exam scoring in
Turkish. The plan is to implement this algorithm by using a larger sample, in
different courses and different levels of Physics courses in Turkish. This model,
developed by this research, is believed to be successful for different languages
at the international level, as well.
The study was conducted for a Physics course in the Turkish language. Since
there is no Turkish data set in the field of Physics, the accessible data set could
not be used in this study. Therefore, a Turkish Physics data set was created for
this study. When the studies in the field of ASAG were examined, it was seen
that accessible data sets were used, and the results were compared. Since the
data set was used for the first time in this study, the results of the model could
not be compared with the results of other studies in the literature. However, the
performance of the model was presented in detail, and successful results were
obtained for the Turkish Physics course. This study was conducted on the
topics of electricity, conservation of energy, work and energy in Physics. In
addition, the answer to each question was obtained in Turkish. The questions
used in this study were on the level of knowledge and comprehension of
cognitive field taxonomy. Therefore, the performance of the model covers the
knowledge and comprehension levels of cognitive field taxonomy. The above
explanations were accepted as limitations of the study.
