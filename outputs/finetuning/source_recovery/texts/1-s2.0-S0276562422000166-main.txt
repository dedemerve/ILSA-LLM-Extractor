In the present study, we focus on institutional segregation as determined by the school system. We investigate the
effect of between-school tracking on countries’ social segregation across schools as measured by the variation in
the socioeconomic composition of schools. We combine data from international large-scale assessments to estimate difference-in-differences models. Specifically, we regress the degree of social segregation across schools at
the secondary school level on an indicator of between-school tracking, while controlling for the degree of
segregation at the primary school level. The analyses are replicated on 32 different combinations of datasets from
three large-scale assessments: Progress in International Reading Literacy Study [PIRLS], Trends in International
Mathematics and Science Study [TIMSS], and Programme for International Student Assessment [PISA]. These
encompass 16 assessment cycles and 75 different countries or regions. The results provide strong evidence for the
hypothesis that between-school tracking increases social segregation between advantaged and disadvantaged
students.

1. Introduction
Social segregation between schools means that children from
disadvantaged and advantaged families do not attend the same schools.
The unequal distribution of students across schools is linked to student
outcomes because the social context and peers are relevant for students’
cognitive learning outcomes and socialization. For example, evidence
suggests that having a higher proportion of students from advantaged
backgrounds in the same school has positive effects on learning for less
advantaged students (Sacerdote, 2011). Comparative studies have
revealed a correlation between the degree of social segregation and the
social achievement gap (Burger, 2019; Hindriks, Verschelde, Rayp, &
Schoors, 2010). If there is no contact and exchange between children
from poorer and richer families, segregated school systems are also
problematic for the socialization of children. Historically, deliberate
forms of school segregation have existed by social group, such as race or
gender; these have persisted for a long time in some countries and are
sometimes still promoted today (e.g., Brown vs Board of Education in

the US; single-gender schools). Unlike segregation by race or gender,
segregation by socioeconomic background has not been publicly and
politically propagated: instead, it occurs in hidden ways.
Although social segregation is considered an important issue for
children’s socialization and learning, there is little international
comparative research that systematically addresses the topic. This lack of
inter-country research is surprising, since the country level is the only one
at which the degree of social segregation across schools can be observed
and compared. There is also little rigorous research that aims to identify
the institutional factors that explain why there is more segregation in
some countries or school systems than in others. The present paper is a
first attempt to fill this research gap. In particular, we have focused on the
question of whether between-school tracking, that is, the sorting of students into different ability tracks in secondary education, increases the
social segregation across schools in education systems.1
Between-school tracking is one of the most controversial issues in
educational research addressing the structures of school systems. While
proponents of ability tracking have argued that it is easier and more

between-school social segregation in education systems, we applied a
difference-in-differences approach, in which we studied the degree of
social segregation at the secondary school level, while controlling for the
degree of prior social segregation at the primary school level. This
approach allowed us to circumvent the issue of confounding national
context factors, such as residential segregation. In our analyses, we used
all cycles of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development’s (OECD) Programme for International Student Assessment
(PISA), the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational
Achievement’s (IEA) Trends in International Mathematics and Science
Study (TIMSS), and IEA’s Progress in International Reading Literacy
Study (PIRLS). We thus took advantage of the repeated sampling of
student populations at both primary and secondary school levels from
overlapping countries.

the main concepts and previous comparative research on tracking and
school segregation. After this, we outline the analysis plan, that is, how
we aimed to identify the effects of tracking on segregation. In the results
section, we describe international comparative findings concerning the
degree and distribution of social segregation across schools, as well as
our main findings on the effects of tracking on segregation. Lastly, we
discuss our findings.

The analyses reveal a considerable variation in the degree of social

Malmberg & Andersson, 2020). However, the correlation between residential and school segregation is not perfect, which implies that there
are other institutional features within education systems that promote
further segregation processes. The transitions that take place within the
education system are a central factor in social segregation; at transition
points, educational paths diverge between social groups.
2.2. Transitions and school choice
The term “institutional differentiation” covers various forms of
formal, informal, internal, and external hierarchies in the education
system. While there are different forms of differentiation, the most
obvious one is “between-school ability tracking.” Between-school
tracking means that students are sorted into different types of secondary schools that have either an academic or vocational orientation
(Dollmann, 2019; Skopek, Triventi, & Buchholz, 2019). Some authors
(e.g., Hanushek & Wößmann, 2006) call between-school tracking
“ability tracking,” emphasizing that the sorting of students into different
tracks should not be based on their social status but on their abilities.
However, there are reasons why between-school tracking might nevertheless increase social segregation. In this regard, Boudon (1974)
introduced the useful distinction between primary and secondary effects
to explain the mechanisms by which social background influences
educational decisions at transition points between different educational
stages. In the present case, primary effects refer to the association between student background and achievement before transitioning from
primary to secondary school. An achievement-based allocation to
different ability tracks will automatically lead to social segregation if
background and achievement correlate. Secondary effects refer to
background-dependent educational decisions. The basic idea is that,
even when students have the same academic achievement in primary
school, privileged students are more likely to transition to more ambitious academic tracks than socially disadvantaged children.
To understand the mechanisms that underlie secondary effects,
Kristen (2005) developed a theoretical model that incorporates a
sequence of three stages (see also Hallinan, 1994). These include the
perception of different school alternatives, the evaluation of the
perceived alternatives, and the selection of and access to the desired
school. Kristen (2005) has argued that even after controlling for student
achievement, privileged families have more cultural and social resources, better information to evaluate the different educational alternatives, and higher educational expectations than disadvantaged
families. At the same time, other important actors, such as teachers, tend
to believe that students with more privileged backgrounds are more
likely to succeed in academic tracks. Batruch, Autin, Bataillard, and

2. Literature review
We followed Allen and Vignoles (2007) and defined social segregation across schools as the degree to which members of social groups
attend the same or different schools. According to this definition, complete social segregation means that all members of one social group
attend different schools than the members of the other social group. By
contrast, the absence of social segregation across schools means that the
members of both social groups are equally distributed across all schools
in a country. While the definition of social segregation across schools
can apply to different social groups (e.g., segregation by gender,
immigration status, religion; Gorard & Smith, 2004), we focused
exclusively on the school segregation by socioeconomic status. We
acknowledge, however, that social segregation across schools is a
complex concept that cannot be fully captured by one single indicator or
index (OECD, 2019).

Butera (2018) conducted a vignette experiment that varied the
description of students’ social status while holding the achievement
level constant. They observed that university students and teachers
considered a lower track more appropriate for lower SES students than
for higher SES students and a higher track more appropriate for higher

the low SES and high SES groups was the same. Secondary effects are
often considered more objectionable than primary effects. However,
both primary and secondary effects arguably imply that tracking increases social segregation across schools. This argument is presented in
greater detail below. The sorting of students into tracked schools constitutes one of the most contentious issues in education. While all education systems differentiate at some point (e.g., between lower and
upper secondary education), the controversy is mainly about the age at
which students are tracked for the first time. Some countries—like
Germany, Austria, and Hungary—track their students as early as the end
of grade 4. Opponents of early tracking argue that sorting at such early
ages can be considered problematic, since assessments of students’
abilities may not be reliable and the family background and expectations
may have a stronger impact on students’ school choice at an early age
(Horn, 2009; Erikson & Johnson, 1996). For this reason, tracking may be
a driver of social segregation. Especially in tracking countries, where
track allocation substantially depends on teachers’ and parents’ judgements and choices, track allocation may be more related to the child’s
socioeconomic status. In early tracking countries with a more objective
track allocation (e.g., based on central exams), the association should be
less pronounced (Bol, Witschge, van de Werfhorst, & Dronkers, 2014;
Korthals & Dronkers, 2016).
However, there is hardly any robust evidence on the effects of
tracking on social segregation across schools. Some studies have found
that the degree of social segregation tends to be higher in countries with
a tracked lower secondary school system but others have observed high
levels of segregation in late tracking countries (Burger, 2019; Chmielewski & Savage, 2015; Chmielewski, 2014; Gorard & Smith, 2004;
Jenkins et al., 2008; Murillo, Hernández-Castilla, Martínez-Garrido &
Hidalgo, 2018; OECD, 2019). A central problem is that all these international comparisons are based only on cross-sectional comparisons,

3. Research question and hypothesis
The aim of this paper was to determine the effect of between-school
tracking on social segregation across schools. To this end, we used data
from two international large-scale studies to compare the degree of social segregation in tracked and untracked secondary school systems
among 15-year-old (PISA) and grade 8 (TIMSS) students. Previous
research on school choice suggests that the sorting of students into
different ability tracks differs depending on students’ social background.
Following this research, we hypothesized that between-school tracking
would increase the degree of social segregation across schools.
4. Methodology
4.1. Data sources: combining primary and secondary school information
To identify the effect of between-school ability tracking on segregation, we compared the degree of segregation between countries that
had tracked or comprehensive secondary school systems. We controlled
for the degree of segregation at the primary school level in order to
circumvent possible bias from confounding factors. For this purpose, we
combined primary and secondary school data from three international
large-scale assessments: PISA, TIMSS, and PIRLS. PIRLS was conducted
in 2001, 2006, 2011, and 2016, with a target population of grade 4
students. TIMSS assessed a target population of grade 4 students (Population A) and grade 8 students (Population B) in 1995, 1999 (only Pop.
B), 2003, 2007, 2011, and 2015. PISA was administered in 2000, 2003,
2006, 2009, 2012, and 2015 and tested 15-year-old secondary school
students. We constructed pseudo-panel datasets at the country level by
combining primary and secondary school data to study changes in
segregation before and after the transition from primary to secondary
school (Cordero, Cristóbal, & Santín, 2018).
Following previous research (Strello et al, 2021; Ruhose & Schwerdt,
2016), we replicated the analyses with various combinations of primary
and secondary school studies in order to maximize the sample size and
the number of included countries. We matched primary and secondary
school studies that were conducted in roughly the same years (e.g.,

4.3.1. Identification strategy
Some countries have a comprehensive secondary school system
while others have a tracked secondary school system. Studies that simply compare the degree of social segregation across countries with
tracked and untracked school systems may be biased because the differences they observe may have existed before students were tracked.

countries with tracked secondary school systems, meaning that the social segregation across schools simply reflects the residential segregation

5.3. Further analyses
We ran alternative specifications to test the robustness of our analyses. We focused on the Dissimilarity Index D in reporting these findings, since the analyses using H led to very similar results. The results of
these specifications are summarized in Table 4.
5.3.1. Intraclass correlation as a proxy of social segregation across schools
To test the robustness of our findings for specifications of the
outcome variables that are not recoded to be binary variables, we
replicated the main analyses with the intraclass correlations (ICCs) of
the number of books at home and parental education variables as
outcome variables. Note that continuous variables are necessary to estimate ICCs; this does not strictly apply in the present case. The ICCs
were estimated using the original ordinal variables of the study cycles in
multilevel null models with schools as clusters. The ICCs reflect the
degree of between-school variation in the number of books at home and
parental education. The results indicated that the ICCs increased more in
early tracking countries than in late tracking ones (see rows (1) in
Table 4). This is consistent with our main findings. Since the number of
books at home and parental education variables were ordinal instead of
continuous and since their response categories differed between the
study cycles, we recommend caution when interpreting these findings.

The estimation of segregation measures is sensitive to the sizes of
student samples in each school. Therefore, we replicated our analyses,
restricting them to schools with at least 15 students and to schools with

Arguably, these tracking decisions are partially independent of previous
academic achievement, although we cannot assess this through our

disadvantaged students benefit from having advantaged schoolmates

tracking increases general achievement levels (Strello et al, 2021;
Hanushek & Wößmann, 2006; Lavrijsen & Nicaise, 2016).

5.3.3. Cohort effects, period effects, and sample inflation
We applied two strategies to combine primary and secondary school
studies: We combined studies that were administered in approximately
the same year (i.e., same-year comparisons) and studies that sampled
approximately the same student cohort in primary and secondary school
(i.e., same-cohort comparisons). The first strategy is often subject to
cohort effects while the second approach may be influenced by period
effects (see Blanchard et al., 1977). Moreover, sample inflation may
result when using the same data multiple times. To investigate whether
these issues had an impact on our analyses, we calculated two different
mean effects. We only included each assessment cycle in one comparison
and only made same-cohort (see rows (4) in Table 4) and respective
same-year (see rows (5) in Table 4) comparisons. Again, the effect of
early between-school tracking was robust across the different specifications and we did not observe large variations in the mean effect
estimates.

6.1. Limitations and future research
Our results contribute evidence on the impact of between-school
tracking on social segregation across schools. We acknowledge, however, that this is just one type of differentiation among others, such as
course-by-course ability tracking within schools (Skopek et al., 2019;
Hillmert & Jacob, 2010). In the present study, we consider both countries with a purely comprehensive system and with a within-school
tracking system as the control group of untracked secondary school
systems. The effects of between-school tracking may have been even
stronger if within-school tracking education systems had been excluded
from the control group of late tracking countries. In the same vein, we
did not differentiate between early tracking countries with more or less
objective track allocation procedures because this information was not
available and the number of tracked systems was small. The effects of
tracking may be higher in early tracking countries, where teachers and
parents have a greater influence on the track allocation decision than in
countries with more objective procedures (e.g., decision based on central exams).
Another set of limitations relate to the sampling structure. Since our
analyses require international comparative data, we selected PISA,
TIMSS, and PIRLS as data sources. However, the sampling strategies in
all three international large-scale assessments are designed for representative student samples, while the samples may be less representative
for schools (e.g., small schools may have been excluded). In addition,
PIRLS and TIMSS follow different sampling strategies than PISA. While
the former two sample one classroom per school in most countries, the
latter samples students from different classrooms. The approach adopted
by TIMSS and PIRLS restricts school heterogeneity (assuming
nonrandom sorting of students between classrooms) but the samples are
more comparable between them than with PISA. Another possible limitation concerns the different school sizes at the primary and secondary
level (see Appendix A.4). In some countries, primary schools are much
smaller than secondary schools, which might affect the proportions and
therefore segregation measures. These three limitations do not invalidate our analyses, since the school samples are representative regardless
of their reduced size and some exclusions. The segregation levels do not
seem to vary greatly between TIMSS Pop. B and PISA and the segregation levels seem to be stable between primary and secondary schools in
countries without early tracking (see Table 2). This suggests that the
data comparisons are adequate.
Another limitation that is associated with sample size issues is that
segregation measures can be subject to bias if researchers compare small
minorities with large majorities. This is especially the case when there
are small minorities within clusters (i.e., schools in the present case),
even if the total sample of individuals and clusters is large (Carrington &
Troske, 1998; Winship, 1977). However, in this study, an average of
31–33% of students were in the minority socioeconomic status categories (for the number of books at home and parental education variables, respectively). Furthermore, we ran additional analyses that
excluded very small schools (with less than 15 or 20 students per
school); these replicated the findings of the main analyses. We therefore
believe that the sample size issue had a limited effect on the segregation
measures and, more importantly, on the effects of interest in the present

5.3.4. Unweighted mean effects
In the main analyses, we weighted the estimation of the mean effects
by the inverse of the standard error (Card, 2012). This implies that for
each survey combination, the more pronounced the tracking effects or
the greater the number of participating countries, the more it contributed to the mean effects. As an alternative, we estimated unweighted
mean effects (see rows (6) in Table 4). The weighted and unweighted

6. Discussion and conclusion
Previous research on the effects of tracking focused almost exclusively on inequalities in students’ academic performance. We extended
this line of research to focus on the effects of between-school tracking on
social segregation across schools to provide a more holistic evaluation of
the effects of early tracking. We believe that integrating students from
various social backgrounds is a valuable good in democratic societies.
Against this background, we consider it problematic if socially advantaged and disadvantaged children have little contact with each other in
school from an early age. In our opinion, the integration of different
social classes is a core task of schools. A school system that is socially
segregated from an early age cannot meet this demand.
Our analyses found strong evidence that between-school tracking
increases social segregation across schools. Previous research applied
purely correlative approaches based on cross-sectional data and found
that tracking was associated with large degrees of social segregation. We
provided more robust evidence for a causal relationship because our
difference-in-differences approach means we did not have effects of
unobserved covariates. Controlling for pre-existing segregation at the
primary school level permitted us to control for other important sources
of social segregation across schools that are independent of tracking,
such as residential segregation and other mechanisms of school choice.
However, it is important to consider that these interpretations rest on
assumptions that we cannot test under our current design. These limitations are discussed in greater detail below.
Proponents of early between-school ability tracking argue that there
is a trade-off between the efficiency and the equality of learning opportunities at school. Previous studies using international comparative
data have challenged this supposed trade-off. Our study complements
this research by providing further evidence on how tracking widens the
social divide between socioeconomically disadvantaged and advantaged
students. Taken together, tracking decisions seem to depend on children’s socioeconomic status and therefore increase school-related social
segregation at secondary school level (see findings of the present study).

Future research on tracking effects on school segregation may
address additional issues that we could not address with the data at
hand. First, future studies may aim to measure the school structure and
school choice mechanisms in the education systems more comprehensively (e.g., within-school tracking, selection based on recommendation
vs. central entrance exams). Second, it may be interesting to disentangle
the primary and secondary effects of socioeconomic status. Segregation
related to primary effects may be more defensible than segregation due
to secondary effects. Likewise, it would be interesting to investigate the
extent to which increases in social segregation across schools have direct
consequences for the achievement gap between disadvantaged and
advantaged students and how much of these increases are a direct
consequence of school track decisions. Finally, previous research has not
directly addressed questions concerning the consequences of earlytracking related social segregation for social inequalities in education
