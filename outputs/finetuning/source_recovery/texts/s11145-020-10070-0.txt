Abstract
This study examined the combined effects of teachers’ instructional practices and
students’ reading-related affective engagement on predicting the high and low levels
of elementary reading literacy from a linguistically and culturally comparative perspective. Data were based on 9748 students from 4 English-speaking and 3 Chinesespeaking education systems participating in the Progress in International Reading
Literacy Study 2016. A mixed theory-based and data-driven approach was adopted.
Four machine learning algorithms, specifically logistic regression, support vector
machine, decision tree, and extreme gradient boosting, were simultaneously used
to classify and predict high- and low-proficiency readers and to identify the most
important factors for the ability separation. The findings showed that for both system
groups, those factors together were sufficiently powerful to discriminate the readers
and that the affective constructs, particularly students’ self-concepts, played a predominant role. The Chinese-speaking systems, compared with their English counterparts, applied more effective pedagogies to nurture sophisticated readers.
Keywords Data mining · Effective reading pedagogies · Elementary reading
literacy · PIRLS 2016 · Reading self-concepts

Introduction
Reading comprehension is universally recognized as a crucial foundation for wellrounded academic achievement in elementary schooling, particularly when children
shift from the word identification and decoding process to understanding the meaning
and reading to learn other subjects (Tse et al., 2016). Progress in International Reading
Literacy Study (PIRLS), launched by the International Association for the Evaluation
of Educational Achievement (IEA) initially in 2001, is an influential large-scale test
conducted globally every 5 years to gauge the reading literacy of elementary fourth
grade students (Mullis & Martin, 2015). The test results and their concomitant background factors about the reader’s nation, home, school and classroom can help scholars
and practitioners gain in-depth insights into children’s reading competence during this
developmental period.
Admittedly, factors contributing to reading attainment have been largely explored
from various data sources (Xiao, Liu, & Hu, 2019). Apart from a learner’s natural
endowments, massive research converges on malleable factors surrounding classroom learning, among which teachers’ pedagogies receive exceptional attention (Park,
Brownell, Bettini, & Benedict, 2017). This is because most learning takes place in the
classroom and teachers’ everyday activities provide formal structured learning experiences, which undoubtedly could facilitate the development of students’ reading abilities
directly (Cheung, Tse, Lam, & Loh, 2009; Mullis & Martin, 2015). Children’s engagement in classroom learning, however, is also consistently powerfully influenced by
their psychological factors, such as their attitudes toward reading, self-confidence and
enjoyment of reading instruction (Tse & Xiao, 2014), which are broadly categorized as
motivation factors or the affective component of learning engagement (Fredricks, Blumenfeld, & Paris, 2004).
Most extant studies have addressed the associations between the abovementioned
classroom learning factors and students’ reading performance in general, with very
few targeting the achievement spectrum (e.g., high, average, low achievers). However,
researchers claimed that the links varied greatly depending on the reader’s achievement level (Petscher & Logan, 2014). Particularly, high and low achievements, as two
bounds of the proficiency distribution, were found to be associated with different affective and contextual factors and in distinct strength (Tse & Xiao, 2014). The high- and
low-achievement inequity warrants special consideration because the two cohorts of
readers, who have demonstrated great disparities in reading competencies at this critical developmental stage, will have not only unequal opportunities for later academic
success but also disparate possibilities of full participation in society (Alivernini, 2013).
Therefore, this study intends to provide more evidence on the extent to which these
factors contribute to the high- and low-ability disparities in elementary fourth graders’
reading literacy through the lens of the latest PIRLS 2016.

Literature review
Effective instructional practices for improving elementary students’ reading
literacy
Considerable variance in reading outcomes has been explained by distinct instructional strategies (Park et al., 2017). Notably, teaching practices vary substantially
among different linguistic groups (Lafontaine, Dupont, & Schillings, 2018), especially typologically remote languages. For instance, the alphabetical coding rules
taught to English-speaking children are unfamiliar to Chinese-speaking children
taught the logographic or ideographic system (Tse, Lam, Lam, Loh, & Westwood, 2007). It is admitted that research on effective pedagogies for elementary
reading literacy has mostly focused on English teaching (Cheung et al., 2009).
However, Chinese education systems (e.g., Hong Kong, Macao, and Chinese Taipei) are often strong performers in international reading tests, particularly Hong
Kong, the top three players in the last three waves of PIRLS. It will be meaningful to explore the success formula of their pedagogies. Thus, relevant studies are
reviewed on the two language scenarios.
Effective elementary English reading literacy instruction
There are several principles that teachers often employ for effective English
reading classes, for example, introducing abundant reading materials (Guthrie,
Schafer, Von Secker, & Alban, 2000), organizing diversified class activities
explicitly teaching a repertoire of strategies (McLaughlin, 2012). These practices
render students active and strategic readers with a deepened understanding of
the interaction between text and language (Pikulski & Chard, 2005). Meanwhile,
regular assessment of learners’ progress is another indispensable component for
teachers to adapt and individualize their teaching to better accommodate students’
needs (Başol & Johanson, 2009).
Effective elementary Chinese reading literacy instruction
The literature offers very little on effective Chinese teaching (Cheung et al.,
2009), except for some reports about Hong Kong mainly using PIRLS data. From
PIRLS 2001 and 2011, Hong Kong teachers were found to shift their focus from
word decoding and definition searching to comprehension strategies (Tse et al.,
2015). Regarding effective pedagogies, reading materials and assessment practices were highly beneficial to fourth graders’ Chinese literacy (Cheung et al.,
2009; Tse et al., 2007). Specific strategies such as making generalizations and
drawing inferences significantly enhanced children’s reading competence (Tse
et al., 2015). It appears that the optimal pedagogies for Chinese and English reading literacy share many commonalities to facilitate students’ higher-level meaning

Instructional practices related to high and low disparities in elementary students’
reading literacy
Two studies, among the scarce explorations of pedagogies related to the high and
low proficiency gaps in reading, warrant mention. With data from 20 countries in
PIRLS 2006, Alivernini (2013) identified an array of multilevel variables that significantly predicted such great discrepancies; however, none of the instructional methods were among them. Very recently, using approximately 200 samples from PIRLS
2016, Xiao, & Hu (2019) investigated whether teachers’ classroom practices alone
could predict high- and low-achieving Canadian readers. The outcome was positive
and some pedagogies about in-class activities and comprehension strategies could
optimally perform this task.

Affective factors associated with elementary students’ reading literacy
Students’ affective engagement in learning, such as their reading attitudes, self-concepts (interchangeable with reading confidence and competency beliefs) and perceptions of reading instruction (Fredricks et al., 2004), has been uniformly and significantly correlated with their reading skills (Hebbecker, Förster, & Souvignier, 2019;
Mullis, Martin, Foy, & Hooper, 2017; Troyer, Kim, Hale, Wantchekon, & Armstrong, 2019; Tse & Xiao, 2014). Generally, more affectively positive students tend
to read more frequently, become more engaged and perseverant in reading tasks, and
thus achieve better reading mastery (McGeown et al., 2015; Loh & Tse, 2009).
Ability‑specific affective factors
Research has demonstrated that children’s reading attitudes and self-concepts crucially exert impacts on children’s reading outcomes contingent on the ability level.
By sampling 9-year-olds in the U.K., Susperreguy, Davis-Kean, Duckworth and
Chen (2017) reported that reading self-concepts could predict all quantile levels
of attainment. Alivernini (2013) also demonstrated the power of self-concepts in
predicting whether a reader would belong to the high- or low-ability group across
20 countries. However, Tse and Xiao (2014) found that reading attitudes were
only associated with the likelihood of a Hong Kong Grade 4 reader being a high
performer.
In contrast, there is far less evidence on the relationships between children’s perceptions of reading instruction and learning achievement. For elementary readers,
only McGeown et al. (2015) found that, unlike reading attitudes and competency
beliefs, enjoyment of reading instruction did not predict variance in reading skills.

Cross‑cultural affective factors
Although a general relationship between affective factors and students’ reading literacy has been established globally, the links may be influenced by different cultures
(Tse & Xiao, 2014). For instance, in Western culture of individualism, learners do
not compare much with others when setting goals, undertaking tasks and evaluating their performance, which causes higher and sometimes even inflated estimations
about their competencies (Farzan, 2018). Empirical research has also documented
students’ very positive learning attitudes yet in weak relationships with scholastic
outcomes (Liou, 2017). In contrast, Confucian Asians value collectivism and make
comparisons in the evaluation process, which results in more realistic self-opinions
(Farzan, 2018). Their learning attitudes are at modest and even low levels but are
more strongly correlated with academic performance (Schütte, 2015). Existing
evidence, however, still seems inconsistent. For example, Guo, Marsh, Parker and
Dicke (2018), examining 15 countries in PIRLS 2011, identified that elementary
school children globally engaged in a similar motivation process for self-concept
formation, which serves as solid support for cross-cultural generalizability. However, exclusive to the Hong Kong scenario, Loh and Tse (2009) did not recognize
clear patterns of relationships between children’s affective variables and their Chinese literacy.
In summary, research evidence on the associations between pedagogies/affect and
children’s reading literacy is inconclusive. More endeavors through state-of-the-art
examinations are needed. Furthermore, although there are still a few studies targeting the high and low bounds of proficiency distribution, they sampled either a linguistically/culturally heterogeneous population (e.g., Alivernini, 2013) or a small
local sample (e.g., Tse & Xiao, 2014). As analyzed above, effective pedagogies and
influential affective factors could vary greatly among distinct linguistic/cultural systems (e.g., English vs Chinese, West vs East). It therefore makes a comparative perspective on the inequity issue with more robust data evidence necessary. Ultimately,
despite the confirmed contributions effective instruction and positive affect make
to reading development, to date, no research has explored the synergistic effects of
these two categories of factors on discriminating between high and low achievers.

Objectives and conceptual framework of the research
Considering the aforementioned research lacuna, this study, based on PIRLS 2016,
intends to examine the combined influences of teachers’ instructional practices and
students’ affective factors on distinguishing the high and low achievers in elementary Grade 4 reading literacy in English-speaking and Chinese-speaking education
systems, which represent two distinct language systems and cultures (Western and
Eastern). It is guided by the following questions:
1. To what extent can the two combined categories of factors discriminate the highand low-level readers in the two groups of linguistic/cultural systems?

2. What are the most influential pedagogical and/or affective factors in discriminating the high- and low-level readers in the two linguistic/cultural system groups?

This investigation is conducted under the PIRLS framework (2016) assessing students’ reading literacy (Mullis & Martin, 2015). PIRLS categorizes student
achievement in international benchmarks: advanced, high, intermediate and low. In
this study, high achievers or high performers refer to students who score at or above
the advanced benchmark level, while low achievers or low performers are below the
intermediate level. High achievers can discriminate and integrate complex information and nuanced story elements across a text, make critical evaluations and show
awareness of language choices, whereas low achievers can only or cannot even
retrieve and regenerate explicitly stated information and make straightforward inferences from the implied ideas (Mullis et al., 2017).

Variables
Two types of variables were employed for students’ reading scores and their influencing factors. Students whose scores were at or above the advanced benchmark of
625 were regarded as high achievers, while those below the intermediate benchmark
of 475 were low achievers.
Regarding the instruction and affect variables, a brief summary of them is presented in Table 3 (detailed descriptions in Appendix 1). Most coded responses were
on a Likert scale indicating a descending degree of agreement or frequency. For
example, ‘1, 2, 3, and 4’ corresponded to ‘every or almost every lesson, approximately half the lessons, some lessons, and never’, respectively. For ease of interpretation, such scoring was reversed.

Statistical models
Recent years have witnessed the emergence of data mining approaches to explore
international large-scale datasets. They are techniques for retrieving useful, relevant
information and detecting previously unseen but underlying patterns from massive
data with no assumptions about the data distributions; that is, they are nonparametric methods requiring parameter tuning in modeling (Larose & Larose, 2014). Such
techniques are not affected by multicollinearity between input variables, which can
be categorical or continuous (Alivernini & Manganelli, 2015). While conventional
statistical approaches rely on theoretical rationales of the relationships between variables and then examine data to test the hypotheses, they can decide how to combine
and use variables to build the most appropriate possible model in a data-driven way.
Researchers, however, suggest a combination of theory-based and data-driven techniques that select, transform and split/integrate input variables informed by theories
or domain knowledge before running the models (Gabriel, Signolet, & Westwell,
2018). The current investigation thus adopted this mixed method to achieve theoretically meaningful and computationally efficient outcomes.
Several studies using large-scale datasets have lent support to the efficiency, accuracy and robustness of data mining models in classification tasks. The algorithms
frequently employed were the support vector machine (SVM) (e.g., Chen, Zhang,
Wei, & Hu, 2019; Gorostiaga & Rojo-Álvarez, 2016; Xiao, & Hu, 2019) and the
decision tree (DT) (e.g., Alivernini, 2013; Alivernini & Manganelli, 2015). In view
of the empirical recognition of the two classifiers, this study utilized both of them
and included the commonly known logistic regression as a baseline model and
another state-of-the-art algorithm, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), to classify high and low achievers. Adopting four statistical tools could firmly validate
the computation results and make comprehensive use of the strengths of each algorithm to serve different research purposes. Logistic regression is a simple data mining approach borrowed from statistics field. When applied in the separation tasks,
it uses an S-shaped curve (logistic function) to fit data by first classifying different
categories of some observations and then predicting the category of the other new

Support vector machine (SVM)
SVM (Cortes & Vapnik, 1995) is commonly used in classification problems (hereafter, SVM specifically refers to the SVM classifier). Its fundamental idea is to find the
decision boundary to optimally separate different classes and maximize the margins,
which are the distances between the boundary and its nearest data points called support vectors. An example of binary classification is intuitively illustrated in Fig. 2.
Once the optimal boundary is found (training phase), the class to which any new
observation belongs, based on the side of the boundary it falls on, can be predicted
(testing phase). SVM uses kernel functions to fit different distributions of data, and
this study exploited the basic linear kernel. Albeit inflexible in classification tasks,
the linear SVM has one unique, impressive property: once the model is ultimately
fitted to the data, a coefficient (weight) is assigned to each input variable, which
resembles logistic regression. The absolute value of the coefficient can be used as
the ranking measure to decide the importance of the variable in the separation task,
and its direction (i.e., positive or negative) can indicate the predicted class (i.e., high
achievers and low achievers in this study). SVM is recognized as being accurate and
robust in classification performance and not susceptible to overfitting, a statistical
issue meaning that the model is too closely fitted to the limited data points and does
not generalize well to unseen data (Wei & Li, 2010). An easy way to diagnose this
issue is to see if the classification accuracy in the training phase is high while that
for the testing phase is not.

Decision tree (DT)
The DT classifier (DT for short) learns a hierarchy of yes/no questions, yielding
a tree of decisions (Müller & Guido, 2016). For the binary classification task,
the algorithm proceeds from the total samples (two classes) and then performs
recursive yes/no partitioning of the samples by responding to a series of ‘if’ statements until only one pure class of samples exists (see Fig. 3 for an example).
At each splitting, an impurity index indicating the homogeneity of one class is
constructed. DT can also estimate each input variable’s relative importance based
on the total reduction in impurity the variable induces, but the value is always
positive and unable to signal which class that variable is indicative of. The importance score for each variable is aggregated to be 1. A crucial downside of this

Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)
One novelty of this study is the use of XGBoost proposed by Chen and Guestrin
(2016). The XGBoost classifier (XGBoost for short) is an ensemble model consisting of a set of decision classification trees. A single DT has to keep splitting if learning irregular patterns to reach a homogeneous class, leading to overfitting and unreliable outcomes. Accordingly, XGBoost employs the ensemble strategy of gradient
boosting by iteratively combining weak base learners into a strong model. Built in a
sequential fashion, each new tree learns from its predecessor to reduce the residual
error such that the overall performance of the model is optimized (an example in
Fig. 4). An input variable’s relative importance can also be retrieved as DT. The
algorithm has been utilized in other fields, and its superior performance is consistently affirmed (Xiao, Wang, Fu, & Wu, 2017; Zhang et al., 2018). To the best of our
knowledge, this study is the first to apply XGBoost to an educational dataset.
Data analysis procedure
The data analysis procedure for the two groups was identical but conducted separately. It involved data preprocessing as well as model development and evaluation to answer the first research question. An optimal subset of input variables for
each group was subsequently selected based on each variable’s relative importance
derived from the optimized classifiers to address the second question. Data manipulation and analyses were implemented using Python 3.6 programming language,
with corresponding packages imported from Scikit-learn (http://sciki​t-learn​.org/
stabl​e/).
Data preprocessing
First, class labelling was performed. Two types of variables, specifically students’
reading scores (output variables) and their background factors (input variables),
were extracted and bound together from the questionnaires. Only high and low
achievers, gauged against their benchmarks, were included in the study. They were
labeled ‘1’ and ‘0’ representing the positive and negative classes, respectively, for
the binary classification. Second, feature engineering was conducted. The input
variables, termed features in machine learning approaches, were subject to feature
engineering. This process is used to prepare the appropriate input dataset for compatibility with the algorithms and contributes to performance optimization through
theoretical frameworks, domain knowledge and statistical techniques (Gorostiaga &
Rojo-Álvarez, 2016; Qiao & Jiao, 2018). The determination of the two categories
of factors in this investigation was grounded in the education concepts, followed
by data wrangling and model-based feature extraction for final analysis. It is worth
mentioning that, unlike traditional statistical models, machine learning approaches

Classification model development and evaluation
Classifier development includes training the classifier with candidate parameters,
finalizing it based on the best parameter(s), and evaluating its generalization performance by testing on other new data (Qiao & Jiao, 2018). Parameter tuning in this
study was integrated with a brute force grid search, which meant exhaustive searching over all parameter combinations.
The whole process is illustrated in Fig. 6. First, the dataset was randomly split
into training and test sets for training and parameter tuning and for final testing/
evaluation, respectively. To have more data learn the model, 20% of all the data
were retained for testing. For the training set, a grid search with cross-validation
(CV) was conducted. In the k-fold CV process, a dataset is equally partitioned
into k folds. The model is trained on k-1 folds and evaluated on the remaining
one. The process loops k times, and the model performance is the average of
the prediction results of the k loops. To make a trade-off between computational
cost and performance, 5-fold CV was employed in this study. The class ‘GridSearchCV’ provided by Scikit-learn efficiently implemented grid search over the
parameter combinations for each classifier and CV simultaneously. Given the
relatively balanced classes of high and low achievers for both groups, the default

a 2-dimensional curve plotting the true positive (correctly predicted high achievers)
rate as a function of the false positive (incorrectly predicted high achievers) rate.
The AUC value is the area under the curve.
All the above metric scores for a perfect classification model would be 1 (all correct predictions) with a randomly constructed model of 0.5. A score that reaches 0.8
is often considered a satisfactory result (Cho & Yoo, 2017; Xia, Broadhurst, Wilson,
& Wishart, 2013).
Important feature selection
As the models with the best parameter(s) were fitted to the data, measures of feature importance could be derived to demonstrate how much a feature had contributed to separating and predicting the two classes of observations: coefficient values
for logistic regression and linear SVM while feature importance scores for DT and
XGBoost. An optimal set of as few features as possible yet with acceptable classification performance (Gorostiaga & Rojo-Álvarez, 2016) was selected as the most
influential features after convergence from different classifiers.

The most influential pedagogical factors for the two system groups
For instructional variables, most of them contributed to high performance; however,
strategies for decoding sounds and words (ATBR10D) and written quizzes/tests on text
comprehension (ATBR13D) predicted low achievement in the English-speaking and
Chinese-speaking systems, respectively. No common denominators underlying effective
reading instruction could be found across the two groups. Reading materials of ‘longer
fiction books with chapters’ (ATBR09AB) and comprehension strategies of ‘make generalizations and draw inferences’ (ATBR12G) were optimal pedagogies adopted by English teachers. Compared with the counterparts, Chinese classrooms employed more practices to nurture high-achieving readers by, for instance, class organization (ATBR08B),
lower-level activities (ATBR10A), comprehension strategies (ATBR12B) and assessment (ATBR13B). Additionally, they emphasized reading assignment (ATBR19B).

Discussion and conclusions
Using a mixed theory-based and data-driven approach, this study explored an array
of malleable variables contributing to the high and low discrepancies in elementary
fourth graders’ reading literacy. It took a universalist and relativist perspective at
the influential patterns between two linguistic/cultural groups, that is, to identify
the similarities and replicability of the findings regardless of languages/cultures, as
well as the uniqueness particular to an individual group (Guo et al., 2018). The findings showed that the classroom learning factors combined to satisfactorily classify
and predict high- and low-achieving readers and that affective engagement played a
more influential role in the separation task than pedagogies did. Nevertheless, subtle
differentiations in factorial patterns were observed between the two groups.
Synergistic effects of affect and instruction factors on academic achievement
The prominence of reading pedagogies and learners’ affective factors has been well
documented. In the classic educational productivity model (Walberg, 1981), a parsimonious suit of nine factor domains that make consistently enormous contributions
to scholastic outcomes is proposed. Five factors, specifically a student’s academic
ability, development, learning motivation, and quantity and quality of instruction, are
assumed to be the most influential (Fraser, Walberg, Welch, & Hattie, 1987). This
could justify the immensely powerful effects of the scrutinized affective domains and
instruction on reading achievement inequity here, although even greater influences
would be expected if combining the other three factors (beyond the scope of PIRLS).
The amalgamation of those factors was substantiated to effectively resolve the separation task. It could be interpreted that, with other significant cognitive and contextual
variables (e.g., intelligence, socioeconomic background, home educational resources)
set aside, whether an elementary fourth grade student is a skilled or struggling reader
can largely be predicted by merely examining his or her responses on those factors.
Such results are crucially beneficial for filling the previous research gap.

Optimal affect factors predicting high and low levels in elementary students’
reading literacy
Across the two groups, greater distinctions were made in high and low achievers’
psychological states than in their reading pedagogies. Children’s self-concepts even
exhibited the predominant role in separation. This may be because if the students
feel competent in reading, they tend to persevere and avail themselves of resources
for assistance with daunting tasks; otherwise, it could be a completely different
picture (Susperreguy et al., 2017). Gradually, a virtuous circle and, conversely, a
vicious circle between children’s self-concepts and reading attainment will be established (Alivernini, 2013). Importantly, Loh and Tse (2009) warned that once children fell prey to negative opinions and low motivation, it was challenging for them
to ever become sophisticated in reading, possibly due to the abovementioned longlasting vicious circle. Competency beliefs, which serve as a booster to motivate students in reading, are more important for those at the lower end of the performance
distribution (Susperreguy et al., 2017). Those could rationalize the stronger link
between self-concepts and low achievement in this study.
Reading attitudes and perceptions of instruction were also associated with reading disparities but to a lesser extent. These affective domains are asserted to be intricately intertwined around the complex, multidimensional ‘self’ (Loh & Tse, 2009),
which renders them show similar influential patterns, including in the prediction of
skilled and unskilled readers. Nevertheless, minor differentiations were still observable between the two types of systems, which may be illustrated from a cultural perspective. Consistent with the literature reviewed at the outset (e.g., Farzan, 2018;
Liou, 2017), children in Western culture were found to have positive views of reading, even when struggling with text meaning, whereas Asian children had much
more realistic or even underestimated opinions of themselves and their teachers.
Optimal instruction factors predicting high and low levels in elementary
students’ reading literacy
The two groups did not share commonalities in the selected optimal instructional
practices. Within the English-speaking systems, decoding sounds and words predicted low achievement. One explanation could be that matching sounds with English-language words is challenging, and even fourth graders still struggle with this
skill (Lafontaine et al., 2018). However, it is taught mainly at primary levels (Grades
1–3), and reading comprehension should be the focus at intermediate levels (Grades
4–5) (Guthrie et al., 2000). Therefore, this practice can be perceived as a remedial
strategy for supporting unskilled fourth grade readers. In contrast, the only important instruction associated with low performance in the Chinese-speaking systems
was written quizzes to assess students’ reading comprehension. Although Chinese
literacy pedagogies emphasize assessing students’ learning progress to help teachers adapt their instruction (Cheung et al., 2009), students might dislike this stressful
form in the middle of a class and then gradually become demotivated to read. For
this teaching purpose, less formal and more efficient oral examinations would be
preferred in elementary classrooms (Başol & Johanson, 2009).

Another group variation lies in the number and variety of teaching practices associated with superior reading performance. Only a few were detected by the English-speaking systems. For example, longer fictions with chapters were often the reading materials
for highly proficient students, most likely because of the appropriate nature of this genre
for children at this developmental stage. Unlike nonfiction books, such books are more
coherent and cohesive in content, thus generating a better thinking flow; having a lower
degree of difficulty and more fascinating plots, they are more likely to intrigue and motivate young children to read (Topping, 2015). Nevertheless, many more activities and
strategies at the surface and higher-order levels were employed in the Chinese-speaking systems, which is consistently encouraged to improve students’ reading competence
(Lafontaine, et al., 2018; McLaughlin, 2012; Guthrie et al., 2000). Furthermore, the priority given to sharpening students’ summary ability could be noted from those practices,
for example, identifying the main ideas, and orally summarizing what was read. Summarization is a complex and demanding skill since it requires students to continuously grapple with meaning and actively monitor their understanding by extracting valuable ideas.
Therefore, there is no doubt that improved summary skills can profoundly facilitate
readers’ comprehension (Stevens, Park, & Vaughn, 2019). Meanwhile, Chinese teachers
made it a common practice to discuss homework in class for prompt feedback on students’ learning progress to consolidate and expand students’ knowledge base (McLaughlin, 2012). It was also a Chinese characteristic that when organizing class, teachers still
led an important role and did not relinquish much responsibility. Instead of encouraging
students’ independent exploration, they cultivated successful readers by creating sameability groups that could enhance learning through differential instruction.
Limitations of this research
This study is not without limitations. First, it aimed to explore a set of factors associated with high and low disparities in reading performance. Future empirical research
is needed if researchers intend to ascertain that these factors are the causes of the
performance gaps. Second, when deciding on the samples for each system, the language of instruction was adopted as the important selection criterion. A further subgrouping of the samples into native and nonnative speakers was not implemented
since no such clear demarcation in students’ background was made by the PIRLS
survey. However, the current sampling method could be sufficient to make linguistic
and cultural comparisons at a macro level to achieve the research objectives.
