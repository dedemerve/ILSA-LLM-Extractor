One of the most reliable predictors of school effectiveness is student engagement, which is not only related to
students’ current achievement but also long-term outcomes. Using linear regression and structural equation
modeling procedures, the present study was aimed at examining the relationship between dropout risk, dimensions of student engagement, and family background. The survey study involved 3251 7th grade Hungarian
students who completed the adapted Student Engagement Instrument and self-reported background questionnaire. According to the results, student performance is mainly related to parents’ educational attainment, but a
moderate or weak correlation with behavioral, academic, and cognitive engagement is also detectable. While
affective engagement shows a weak association with performance, it correlates with other engagement dimensions linked to achievement. This result suggests that support from parents, peers, and teachers may have an
indirect effect on achievement; however, the present study could not confirm this hypothetical model. Based on
our data, parental educational attainment and student engagement account for approximately 41 % of student
performance which draws attention to the complexity of the relationship between student engagement and
performance and highlights the importance of a comprehensive approach to dropout and student engagement in
this context.

Introduction

A comprehensive approach to student engagement

Belonging to a group, whether family, school, or any other community, has been shown to have a positive impact on satisfaction with
life, general wellbeing, psychological and physical health, and cognitive
performance (Allen et al., 2018; Jetten et al., 2017). Besides the family,
school is an important medium for children to experience social relationships and belonging. The school has a vital role in helping children
with different backgrounds and interests participate in school and class
communities (Tarabini et al., 2019).
Ideally, time spent together and social and environmental effects
deepen students’ initial attitude to school and school community into
commitment and investment into community and learning (Fredricks
et al., 2004). Student engagement is a protective factor and promotes
students’ positive educational and social outcomes (Christenson & Pohl,
2020; Fraysier et al., 2020; Green et al., 2012). By contrast, if a student
cannot connect with the school and community permanently, the ultimate consequence may be dropping out (Lyche, 2010).

According to the widely accepted approach of the concept, student
engagement is a multifaceted meta-construct encompassing behavioral,
emotional, and cognitive aspects of attitudes to the school and its
community and interpreting them in their complexity (Salmela-Aro
et al., 2021; Martins et al., 2022). The affective aspect is typically
observed in social relations in the school; the behavioral aspect of
engagement is conspicuous in active engagement in academic, social,
and extracurricular activities, and the cognitive aspect manifests in
learning as an investment and complex academic goals (Fredricks et al.,
2004). Although there seems to be a consensus among researchers that
student engagement includes some affective and behavioral components, approaches differ in the number and content of dimensions
(Salmela-Aro et al., 2021; Martins et al., 2022). The most widespread
student engagement models are two-dimensional (Finn, 1989),
three-dimensional (Fredricks et al., 2004), and four-dimensional
(Appleton et al., 2006; Skinner et al., 2008). The difference between
them is that some researchers distinguish a cognitive dimension in

A four-dimensional conceptualization of student engagement
(Appleton et al., 2006; Reschly & Christenson, 2012) starts out from the
theories of the prevention and intervention of dropout (Appleton, 2012).
In this model, affective engagement addresses a student’s “affiliation/identification with school, including the staff and students that
populate it and the emotions experienced during the tasks of schooling.”
(Appleton, 2012: 726), and its indicators are teacher, peer, and family
support in learning (Appleton et al., 2006, 2008). “Cognitive engagement is defined as investment in the work of learning as well as the
refinement and deployment of strategic thinking” (Appleton, 2012:
726), comprising indicators such as self-regulation, value of learning,
relevance of school-work to future goals, and autonomy (Appleton et al.,
2006, 2008). Behavioral engagement refers to “additional school supportive behaviors that demonstrate an allegiance or adherence to the
school and/or its staff (Appleton, 2012: 726), with indicators including
attendance/absenteeism, deviant behavior form or their absence, and
participation in extracurricular activities (Appleton et al., 2006, 2008).
“Academic engagement is defined by behaviors that have, as their purpose, the high-quality accomplishment of the academic tasks of
schooling” (Appleton, 2012: 726), and expressed by indicators such as
time spent on assignments, doing homework, and active class participation (Appleton et al., 2006, 2008).

Student engagement and dropout risk
Student engagement shows an association not only with students’
current achievement; it is also a predictor of outcomes farther in the
future (e.g. successful completion of secondary studies) (Fraysier et al.,
2020; Rumberger & Rotermund, 2012), and influences students’
educational expectations for higher education (Van den Broeck et al.,
2022). In their meta-analysis, Rumberger and Lim (2008) reviewed the
relationship between dropping out and particular components of student
engagement or factors associated with engagement. They reported on
the findings of research conducted among high school (International
Standard Classification of Education, ISCED 3), middle school (ISCED 2),
and elementary school (ISCED 1) students and their teachers. Among the
results, behavioral engagement appeared to be particularly relevant:
involvement in academic work, attendance, doing homework, effort in
class, and involvement in social aspects of the school including extracurricular activities. Rumberger and Lim (2008) classified these factors
as individual predictors of dropout. They found that absenteeism and
participation in extracurricular activities (particularly in sport in the
case of boys) are more reliable predictors of dropping out in high school
than in middle school. They highlighted misbehavior among individual
behavioral factors, which seemed a stronger dropout predictor in middle
school than academic achievement; the latter was a more reliable predictor in high school (Rumberger & Rotermund, 2012). The
meta-analysis also confirmed the connection between the individual
factors of attitudes, study goals, and self-perception (self-image,
self-esteem, external and internal control) and dropout. These factors
also reflect the cognitive and affective dimensions of engagement
(Appleton et al., 2006, 2008; Reschly, 2020).
Research focusing on the relationship between student engagement
and dropout risk suggests that each dimension of engagement influences
students’ school performance to varying degrees. According to a study
by Archambault et al. (2009) among 11.827 French-Canadian high
school students, while global engagement reliably predicted school
dropout in the short term (ß = 0.15, p < .001), when examining the
dimensions separately, only behavioral engagement was a reliable predictor of dropout (ß = 0.15, p < .001), affective and cognitive engagement not. Wang and Fredricks (2014) also found that behavioral
engagement was the strongest predictor of dropout, based on a longitudinal study conducted among approximately 1.300 adolescents from
the East Coast of the United States. Results show that from Grades 7
through 11, the rates of change in affective and behavioral engagement
were negatively associated with the rate of changes in problem behaviors. They found that lower behavioral (ß = − 0.20, p < .001) and affective engagement (ß = − 0.13, p < .001), and more significant
delinquency (ß = 0.20, p < .001) predicted a greater likelihood of
dropping out.

Dropout risk
Dropping out of school without any qualification has long-term
ramifications not only on the given student’s future and prospects but
also in the broader environment and society. While dropout can be seen
as a status or an event, from the aspect of prevention and educational
intervention, the phenomenon should be understood as a process. This
approach describes dropout as a long and complex process with the
outcome of the student leaving school early with no qualification
(Rumberger & Rotermund, 2012).
Many theoretical and empirical models have been proposed to
explain the predictors of dropping out and the connection of the process
with other outcome factors (Lyche, 2010; De Witte et al., 2013;
González-Rodríguez et al., 2019; Rumberger, 2012). The majority of the
models emphasize the role that contextual factors play in the dropout
process. Rumberger and Lim (2008) based their meta-analysis on
approximately 400 publications and confirmed that no single factor can
completely account for a student’s dropping out: the process is the
combined effect of numerous personal and institutional (family, school,
and community) factors. De Witte and Rogge (2013) monitored the
academic path of an entire cohort in the Netherlands and analyzed the
factors influencing dropout. Five individual and institutional factors
were examined: (1) class and school type, (2) educational attainments,
(3) students’ attitude, (4) parents’ attitude, and (5) socioeconomic status. The results revealed that students’ motivation, aspirations, and
behaviors (estimates are 0.73 to 0.87, p < .001) had the greatest effect
on dropout with parents (estimate of parental education is 0.86, p <
.001, parental interest and support in homework are 1.15 to 1.20, p <
.001) and the characteristics of class (location, peer group quality, size)
also having a non-negligible impact (all estimates, respectively 0.89,
0.95, and 1.08, p < .001). The authors emphasize that the retention of
students who liked school and had a good opinion of their teachers was
greater, and those who paid attention in class were less likely to drop
out. Studies of children groups from preschool or early elementary
school up to the end of high school identified several indicators that can
be predictors of graduation from or dropping out of high school, the
strongest being early academic performance and academic and social
behaviors (Rumberger & Lim, 2008). Lyche (2010) also emphasizes
academic performance as the most vital individual factor predicting
dropout. Performance is also a highly sensitive indicator and can be

Reschly and Christenson (2006, 2012) propose that there is a hierarchy among the dimensions of engagement: affective engagement (i.e.
the attitude to school and school community) and cognitive engagement
(i.e. the attitude to learning and willingness to make an effort to achieve
a goal) have impacts on academic and behavioral engagement, which is
reflected in the indicators of these two dimensions (attendance, participation, grades, test results, etc.). Affective and cognitive engagement
appears to affect educational outcomes through academic and behavioral engagement.
It is well-known that students’ performance at school is largely
determined by demographic and socioeconomic characteristics that we
cannot change as researchers and practicing teachers (Rumberger &
Rotermund, 2012). Therefore, the focus of professional agents such as
teachers, educational psychologists, and researchers is increasingly on
exploring such factors which are not unchangeable predictors of drop
out, but support a school’s decision to plan and provide dropout interventions to students deemed at-risk (Bowers et al., 2012).
As student engagement is not a characteristic feature of the student,
rather it is the student’s current mindset, which is strongly influenced by
environmental impacts (Allen et al., 2018; Tarabini et al., 2019), it is
malleable and responsive to pedagogical intervention (Reschly, 2020).
Piscitello et al. (2022) suggest that a better understanding of the relationship between school engagement and dropout trajectories may lead
to more success in intervening with students who experience demographic and social barriers.

Method
Participants and procedure
The goal of the project constituting the basis of our analysis1 was to
develop and implement continuing teacher education to prevent early
school leaving (ESL) in primary schools (ISCED 1 and 2) in three regions
of Hungary (Paksi et al., 2023). The study project was aimed at exploring
the factors preventing and supporting ESL. Two regions (Central
Hungary and Western Hungary) are economically the most developed,
while the third one (Northern Hungary) is one of the most backward
regions in Hungary. The selection was connected to the project goals as
teachers of these schools participated in a variety of state-funded
in-service training programs; in other words, the schools involved in
the sample were those where the administrative data collected annually
by the educational administration indicated a higher risk of ESL in the
particular region. While the sample is neither nationwide nor representative of Hungarian public education as a whole, it is relevant to our
project, as it targeted the primary schools in each region where the
educationally most vulnerable groups appear.
Participants in the questionnaire-based study included grade 7 students and all of the teachers and school heads of the selected schools.
The reason for selecting seventh-graders was that the national competency assessment in mathematics and reading comprehension takes
place in grades 6 in Hungary. The study also used the assessment data
and the related student and school background questionnaires. The
study was authorized by the Research Ethics Committee of the research
organization, and in the case of students, parental consent was secured.
This paper only uses the data of the study conducted in the fall of the
2020/2021 academic year. The national competency assessment was
canceled due to the COVID-19 pandemic, so those data could not be
used. The survey involved 117 primary schools with 5975 seventh-grade
students in the fall of the 2020/2021 academic year of whom 3251 filled
our electronic questionnaire. The self-reporting survey was administered in groups. Due to missing data and the use of listwise deletion, the
final analytic sample was N = 3230. The mean age of the participants
was 12.8 (SD = 0.65). The gender ratio in the sample was 50–50 %.

The present study
Our research focuses on associations of student engagement and
academic achievement as the strongest predictor of dropout among
Hungarian adolescents.
Two research questions were formulated:

Measurements
We adapted the Student Engagement Instrument2 (SEI; Appleton
et al., 2006, 2008) to measure affective and cognitive engagement.
Validated for 6th through 12th grade, the SEI measures the “internal”
dimensions of student engagement with 35 items. Table 1 shows the
subscales of affective and cognitive engagement and the number and
examples of items regarding each subscale. The students evaluated each
item on a 5-point scale indicating their agreement or disagreement with
the statement.
As part of the adaptation process, the original SEI questionnaire was
initially translated into Hungarian by two translators. Subsequently, the
Hungarian versions were compared, and a unified Hungarian version
was established. Following this, a single translator translated the Hungarian version into English, and we compared it with the original SEI
items. When discrepancies were identified, we refined the Hungarian
translation.

Statistical methods
Research question one concerned the relationship between dropout
risk, students’ engagement, and parents’ levels of education. Pearson
correlation analysis was used to assess the associations between study
variables. We interpret the significant correlation coefficients low from
0.1 to 0.29, moderate from 0.3 to 0.49, and strong from 0.5 (Cohen,
1988). The role of student engagement and parental educational level on
student achievement was examined by linear regression.
Research question two concerned the directly and indirectly effects
of engagement dimensions on achievement. We tested a hypothesized
model of affective and cognitive engagement mediating with behavioral
and academic engagement and the latter influencing academic performance. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted using SPSS
Amos version 27 (Collier, 2020; In’nami & Koizumi, 2013). Two complex models were tested: while affective and cognitive engagement were
involved in the structural model as latent (unobserved) variables, in the
path model the affective and cognitive engagement indexes generated
from the averages of the subscales were applied. Achievement, affective
and cognitive engagement were continuous variables. The other independent variables were involved in the analysis as dichotomous variables. Maximum likelihood estimation was used; missing data were
handled using the setting “Estimate means and intercepts”. The complete database included data for 3230 individuals, but for only 2124
cases, all variables were available. In the models, we worked with a
sample size of 2124 students, and missing data was not imputed. The
statistical power of this sample is high (Bentler & Chou, 1987;
McQuitty, 2004). Model fit was evaluated using the Comparative Fit
Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), and Root Mean Square Error Of
Approximation (RMSEA). CFI and TLI above 0.90 indicate a good model
fit. RMSEA below 0.05 indicates good, up to 0.08 acceptable, and below
0.1 poor fits (Collier, 2020).

Our findings indicate that affective and cognitive engagement in
themselves have little impact on adolescent respondents’ performance;
conversely, with parental educational attainment and behavioral and
academic engagement indicators, they explain approximately 41 % of
academic achievement. Numerous studies of adolescents confirm that
behavioral (including, in a theoretical approach, academic) engagement
is the student engagement dimension that most strongly predicts
dropout (Archambault et al., 2009; Lovelace et al., 2017; Reschly &
Christenson, 2006; Wang & Fredricks, 2014), the strongest predictors
being in particular behavior at school, failure/grade repeat and homework completion. At the same time, behavioral engagement can predict
later dropout or retention long before adolescence, in the early school
years (Alexander et al., 1997). Rotermund’s (2010) analyses of adolescent students’ data reveal that dropout risk is primarily associated with
behavioral engagement and cognitive engagement, which are influenced by affective engagement.
As pointed out in the theoretical overview, in Hungary family status
determines student performance to a large extent. According to the 2018
PISA results, 24 % and 21 % of Hungarian adolescents’ results in the
mathematics and science tests respectively are explained by the family
background (opposed to the respective OECD average of 14 % and 13 %)
(OECD, 2019a). This coincides with our findings, which shows that 21 %
of student achievement is explained by parental educational attainment
alone, with mothers’ level of education having approximately three
times as strong an impact as fathers’. The difference in the impact of
maternal and paternal education is probably due to mothers’ greater
involvement in their children’s school life and school-related decisions
(Epstein, 2011).
Data of Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study
(TIMSS), another international comparative study also confirm that in
most country’s education systems student performance is related to
socioeconomic background, albeit the degree of correlation varies
depending on the characteristics of the social medium and the education
system (homogeneity, centralization, etc.) (Broer et al., 2019). An
analysis based on the TIMSS results comprising 13 countries between
1995 and 2015 found that in countries characterized by significant social inequality the difference in students’ performance with disparate
socioeconomic backgrounds is greater; therefore, family status accounts
for a greater proportion of academic performance.
The second issue we examined was whether affective and cognitive
engagement has an effect on achievement through behavioral and academic engagement. The hypothetical model tested presumed that
changes in behavioral and academic engagement may be preceded by
changes in affective and cognitive engagement, and thus affective and
cognitive engagement as internal dimensions of student engagement
may act as mediators of the more readily observable and measurable
behavioral and academic engagement (Reschly & Christenson, 2006,
2012), and in this way, they are indirectly associated with students’
performance.
Our findings do not support the hypothetical model from any of the
approaches applied: affective and cognitive engagement involved in the
models as latent variables or indexes (as observable variables) showed
poor model fits. In some connections loadings imply that the indicators
do not correlate very strongly with each other. We assume the poor
model fit may be due to the fact that the behavioral and academic
engagement indicators were collected solely by a self-reporting student
questionnaire so that a common method variance could have influenced
the results. This can explain the weak or non-significant correlation of
affective and cognitive engagement with academic and behavioral
engagement indicators.

Limitations and future directions
There are at least three potential limitations concerning the results of
this study. The first limitation concerns the self-reported measurements
8

L. Szabó et al.

International Journal of Educational Research Open 6 (2024) 100328

of student engagement and students’ achievement. As mentioned
earlier, we had no access to personal school records, therefore data
regarding behavioral and academic engagement, and students’
achievement have been collected via student questionnaires, based on
literature (Appleton & Lawrenz, 2011; Hattie, 2009; O’Donnell &
Reschly, 2020; Sticca et al., 2017). In order to increase the validity and
reliability of measurement, in a subsequent study involvement of several
sources (such as parents and teachers) may be considered (Creswell &
Plano-Clark, 2011). This is also justified by the results of the
meta-analysis conducted by Lei et al. (2018), which highlight significant
differences in student-reported and teacher-reported measurements of
student engagement regarding the strength of correlation between academic performance and engagement (rstudent = 0.27, rteacher = 0.48).
Additionally, in their review regarding predictors of dropout, Bowers
et al. (2012) pointed out that there is high variability across the dropout
indicators, therefore they suggest combining indicators in measuring
dropout risk.
A second potential limitation is that this study involved schools
relatively most at-risk of early school leaving in three regions of
Hungary. Consequently, our findings cannot be generalized, as we
focused specifically on schools with a larger proportion of students with
relatively worse socioeconomic background. The PISA report points out
that students in socioeconomically disadvantaged and rural schools are
more likely to report a weaker sense of belonging at school than students
in advantaged urban schools. The 2018 PISA results showed Hungary to
be one of the countries with the biggest gap in favor of advantaged
schools (OECD, 2019b).
Other studies also confirmed the connection of student engagement
with socioeconomic status. For example, Kelly (2008) analysed data
from approximately 2000 American students’ (7th-8th grade), and
found that socio-economically advantaged students appear to participate more in classroom discourse. In her longitudinal study conducted in
Australia Fullarton (2002) reports that 9th grade students of families
with lower socioeconomic status (ß = 0.06, p < .001) and those whose
parents have a lower level of education (ß = 0.08, p < .001) are less
involved in academic, social, and extracurricular activities. Exploring a
randomly selected sub-sample of 10,000 adolescents from a larger
database of the Department of Education’s student information system
in a southern state in the United States, Piscitello et al. (2022) found that
students with higher sociodemographic risk (i.e., African American race,
overage, receiving free lunch, and male gender) or indicators of disengagement (i.e., exclusionary discipline, absences, and failing grades)
exhibited higher rates of dropout in both rural and non-rural settings.
According to the data, indicators of behavioral disengagement were
positively associated with socioeconomic risk (r = 0.27 to 0.84, p <
.001). These findings also confirm that the results of our study conducted with a largely at-risk sample cannot be regarded as representative of Hungarian adolescents. Future studies could repeat the research
on a sample that is representative of socioeconomic background.
The third limitation of the study stems from the timing of the survey.
As mentioned in the relevant section, the survey was conducted during
the COVID-19 pandemic in the fall of 2020. While the closure of Hungarian primary schools had been lifted by then, online teaching had
lasted from the spring until the end of the previous academic year. According to a qualitative study with Hungarian educational stakeholders
(school principals, teachers, students, and parents), disadvantaged regions and children living in poverty had less access to digital education
during school closure than before (Monostori, 2021). Another Hungarian analysis estimated that about 7–12 % of students were left out of
distance learning in the first stage of the lockdown because they had no
digital tools or Internet access (Hermann, 2020); the same rate was 16
%, according to a survey conducted among teachers (Kende et al., 2021).
Additionally, empirical evidence proves that the sense of uncertainty, school closures, and family confinement that comes with the
epidemic, had a significant effect on students’ mental health, wellbeing,
and student engagement worldwide. According to the review of Singh

et al. (2020) and other studies from different countries – for example, the
United States, Greece, and Norway –, younger children and adolescents
experienced significantly more stress and anxiety, reducing opportunities for affective and academic engagement, especially when they
were placed into small groups that they did not like, made in response to
the COVID-19 pandemic (APA, 2020; Giannopoulou et al., 2021; Thorsteinsen et al., 2021, respectively). Consequently, we suppose school
closure and the switch to online learning may have affected school
connectedness, particularly among students with lower socioeconomic
status. After the end of the COVID-19 pandemic, it would be worthwhile
to repeat the examination about student engagement. Maybe a better
response rate could be achieved in future research as in the unusual
circumstances caused by the pandemic.
Due to the characteristics of the sample mentioned earlier and the
circumstances of the data collection – i.e. the COVID-19 pandemic –, our
results cannot be generalized to Hungarian 7th-grade students, but they
point to the importance of research on the topic involving students with
different socioeconomic background, as well. For example, further
investigation is required to explore the relationship between student
engagement and parental support (Martins et al., 2022). As highlighted
by Piscitello et al. (2022), sociocultural influences might have an impact
on both the school climate and policies, potentially influencing the
effectiveness of interventions targeting school dropout. Therefore, a
more comprehensive examination of this system of effects becomes
crucial.
As we mentioned above, the exclusive use of measuring instruments
based on self-report can also represent a limitation of the research.
Future research that incorporates cross-validation between student reports, teacher evaluations, and parent assessments could yield more
precise estimations of the connections between student engagement and
related factors. This, in turn, may provide further insights into the
phenomenon of student engagement.
Conclusion
The main aim of our survey study was to map the relationships between the dimensions of student engagement and their connection with
dropout risk in early adolescence. 7th grade students from Hungary
participated in the measurement through the use of self-reporting
questionnaires. Despite the limitations, the findings contribute to a
growing body of evidence suggesting that behavioral and academic
engagement, as well as cognitive engagement have a great influence on
student performance (Lei et al., 2018; Lovelace et al., 2017). Our results
are consistent with the claim that socioeconomic background affects
significantly student achievement (OECD, 2019b; Piscitello et al., 2022),
in the present study the explanatory power of parental educational level
was stronger than of any dimensions of engagement. Correlations of
affective engagement with other engagement dimensions suggest that
support from parents, peers, and teachers may have an indirect effect on
achievement, although we could not confirm the hypothesized model
regarding the hierarchy of dimensions of engagement (Reschly &
Christenson, 2006, 2012).
As Finn and Zimmer (2012: 98–99) state, “because of its direct
relationship with academic performance and inverse relationship with
negative outcomes, school engagement has been viewed as a protective
factor with respect to educational risk”. As such, student engagement is
the capstone of many prevention and intervention programs today:
while some of the attributes of students (for instance, family status)
cannot be altered, changes in their connectedness to school, its community, and to learning can be shaped (Reschly, 2020) in a way that has
a positive impact on both short and long term academic outcomes as
well as on students’ social success and wellbeing.
The findings of this study draw attention to the complexity of the
relationship between student engagement and performance and confirm
that engagement and performance are also determined by external
factors (e.g. family background). A comprehensive approach and
