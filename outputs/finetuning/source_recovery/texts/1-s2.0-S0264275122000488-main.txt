Despite Chinese students' excellence in international academic assessment, a clear achievement gap exists between urban and rural areas. This study examines factors driving school-level academic performance disparity
between urban and rural areas. Micro-level data from the 2018 Programme for International Student Assessment
survey and a machine learning method are employed. Intermediate learning outcomes and student characteristics (e.g., class size, grade repetition rate, parents' socioeconomic status and emotional support, students'
learning time, and credibility assessment skills) are essential in the difference in academic performance between
the best and worst schools in urban areas. However, in rural areas, school characteristics such as complexity of
admission policies, quality of learning environment, and number of computers are important. These findings
highlight the need for tailored policies in both urban and rural areas.

Several policies have been recommended for reducing the academic
achievement gap between urban and rural areas. This can be reduced by
increased access to educational resources and tangible educational inputs, as well as financial investment (Lounkaew, 2013). Gao et al.

Using PISA 2018 micro-level data for China, this study examines the
factors driving school-level academic performance disparity between
urban and rural areas. Our study contributes to the literature by
adopting the machine learning approach proposed by Wang et al. (2021)
rather than the conventional education production function approach.
The technique used in this study has two advantages. First, we can
assume schooling factors affect academic achievement through two
channels. The first channel is direct impact of schooling inputs on academic performance; the second channel is indirect impact of schooling
inputs on academic achievement through intermediate factors. We make
no assumptions about the functional form of these channels by
employing the machine learning approach. It could be linear, or nonlinear and discontinuous. The first channel examined in this study is
the direct impact of student and school factors on academic achievement. The second channel is that student and school characteristics

Students' academic achievement is determined by students, parents,
schools, and country-level socioeconomic factors, according to a significant body of literature. (Hanushek & Woessmann, 2011; Hu & Wang,
2019; Lee & Lee, 2021). Among 79 countries and regions participating in
the latest Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) in
2018, Chinese secondary school students have the greatest academic
achievement (OECD, 2019). It means, the above factors in China are
more crucial in academic performance compared to other nations.
Chinese students outperform international students, however, their
achievements contain wide disparity. A clear achievement gap between
urban and rural areas can be found. Fig. 1 shows the average 2018 PISA
results in mathematics, science, and reading for both urban and rural
students. Average score of urban schools and rural students differs by 28
points. Rural areas have a higher proportion of underperforming pupils
compared to the urban areas.

countries has suggested several explanations for this issue. Young (1998)
found that rural students have lower academic self-concept compared to
their urban counterparts. Intangible school characteristics are key in

2.2. Methodology
The relationship between schooling factors and student performance
is estimated using a machine learning approach. Shapley flow, a graphbased technique, is used to investigate features that have significant
impact on school achievement (Wang et al., 2021). Shapley flow considers the causal structure of features and identifies key features for
predicting the outcome variable. These features can impact the outcome

non-parametric models such as XGBoost (Chen et al., 2015) to estimate
the causal structure. We randomly split data to form the training and test
sets in an 80:20 ratio for model training and evaluation. In this study,

3. Results and discussion

Using Chinese data from PISA 2018 and the machine learning
methodology proposed by Wang et al. (2021), this study explores the
factors affecting school-level academic performance. We discover that
student and school characteristics, as well as intermediate learning
outcomes, positively impact academic performance. Student and school
characteristics indirectly impacts academic performance through intermediate learning outcomes. The academic performance gap between
best and worst schools in urban areas are mostly affected by intermediate learning outcomes and student characteristics. In rural areas,
however, school characteristics are key in in determining the academic
performance gap between best and worst schools.
These findings highlight the need for tailored policies in both urban
and rural areas. To lower grade repetition, urban schools should support
and invest in their underperforming students. Additional investment in
learning opportunities or extracurricular activities is required to
improve students' intermediate learning outcomes. Family-related policies are required to compensate for the lack of parents' socioeconomic
status and emotional support. Giving specialists' guidance on job choices
is necessary for improving students' expected occupational status.
Rural schools require the similar policies as urban schools, as well as
additional policies. Rural families and schools have less ICT resources
than urban families and schools, which results in lower academic performance. Policies in rural areas should subsidize or offer ICT resources
to homes and schools.
The findings support previous research findings that student and
school characteristics are involved in academic performance, and the
technique used in this study can be applied in various of countries. A
causal structure can be used to examine the performance of a particular
school. Moreover, it can be used to make policies customized to a certain
school. This methodology will help in the micromanagement of policies
proposed in previous studies such as Lounkaew (2013) and Gao et al.
(2018).
This study contains limitations, despite that it employed the most
recent PISA data. Only four eastern Chinese provinces participated in
PISA 2018. The national PISA scores could be lower as only students
from Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang participated in the PISA
2018 assessment. The regional gap may be larger than the reported PISA
scores. According to Angrist, Djankov, Goldberg, & Patrinos (2021),
national PISA scores may be 14–25% lower than those of the regions
participating in the assessment. Although the four provinces may not
fully represent China, information collected from these schools is valuable, and this study may show how factors affecting school performance
in China differ between urban and rural areas.

4. Conclusion

Shapley values are estimated to demonstrate the effects of features
on the academic performance of schools using causal structure
mentioned in Section 2.2. As schools in urban and rural areas might be
substantially different, we evaluate the best and worst schools in each
area.
The Shapley value illustrates how much the feature contributes to
predicted test scores when compared to the benchmark school's performance, which is 581 points (Fig. 2). A factor's contribution to
outcome variables is proportional to its Shapley value. For example, if
Shapley values of two specific features of the school are 10 and 5, then
this school's academic performance is 10 and 5 points higher than that of
the benchmark school. Fig. 2 depicts the top 15 most important causal
relationships.
Compared to the benchmark school, intermediate learning outcomes
and student characteristics positively impact the performance of the best
school in urban areas (Fig. 2.1). Owing to a higher ability to assess
credibility (METASPAM), students at this school can score 41.96 points
higher than students at the benchmark school. Class size (CLSIZE)
positively impacts academic performance compared to the benchmark
school, and it also has an indirect positive effect on academic performance through students' perception of competitiveness at school (PERCOMP) and METASPAM. Parents' economic, social, and cultural status
(ESCS) positively impact school performance compared to the benchmark school. Moreover, ESCS play an essential role in increasing intermediate learning outcomes such as METASPAM and joy of reading
(JOYREAD). School features, on the other hand, have minor impact
compared to other factors. School policies such as ability grouping
(SCHABILICLASS and SCHABILICLASSWITHIN) negatively impact
school performance as compared to the benchmark school.
Fig. 2.2 illustrates compared to the benchmark school, both intermediate learning outcomes and student characteristics negatively
impact the performance of the worst school in urban areas. Students in
this school have poorer intermediate learning outcomes compared to
students in the best school, which negatively impacts academic performance compared to students in the benchmark school. Learning times
(TMINS), parental emotional support (EMOSUPS), and ESCS are all
lower in the worst school compared to the best school and lowering
intermediate learning outcomes. The repetition rate (REPEAT) negatively impacts academic achievement, lowering it by 23.02 points
compared to the benchmark school. Except for CLSIZE, school characteristics do not appear in the top 15 major causal relationships. A student's low expected occupational status (BSMJ) negatively impacts
academic achievement compared to students in the benchmark school.
