Leveraging data from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022, this study examines the
factors influencing secondary school students’ learning achievement in urban and rural areas using unconditional
quantile regression. Subsequently, we investigate how tangible and intangible factors can explain a significant
portion of the urban-rural learning achievement gap using Recentered Influence Function (RIF) Oaxaca-Blinder
decomposition method. Our analysis underscores the significant influence of student and household characteristics on students’ learning achievement in urban and rural settings. However, the influence of these factors
varies across different segments of the learning achievement distribution. The decomposition analysis reveals
that intangible factors, defined as non-physical resources such as school autonomy, leadership, and parental
involvement, account for a substantial share of the urban–rural learning achievement gap. Specifically, these
factors explain approximately 60–70 percent of the urban-rural gap among low-achieving students, compared to
18–28 percent among high-achieving students. These findings highlight the critical role of improving educational
quality, enhancing intangible factors such as school governance, and fostering greater parental engagement to
narrow Cambodia’s urban-rural learning achievement gap.

1. Introduction
Educational quality is a pressing concern in many developing
countries, where rural students often face lower achievement than their
urban peers due to limited resources (Tesema and Braeken, 2018; World
Bank, 2018). These disparities are largely driven by unequal resource
allocation, with urban schools typically benefiting from greater public
and private investment (Echazarra and Radinger, 2019). Identifying the
factors that contribute to these gaps is therefore essential for designing
effective and equitable education policy. Recent empirical studies
further show that the COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated these inequalities, underscoring the urgent need for targeted interventions and
increased public funding for rural schools to strengthen their resource
base and staffing capacity (Shin et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024). Despite
ongoing efforts, however, the urban–rural learning achievement gap
remains a significant challenge in many national education systems.
Cambodia, a developing country in Southeast Asia, exemplifies these
challenges. The Khmer Rouge genocide inflicted significant damage on
the country’s education system, decimating its intellectual capital.

Despite this historical adversity, the government and international donors have made substantial progress in rebuilding its education sector.
The increased school enrollment rates and the restoration of educational
institutions evidence this. However, significant challenges persist,
particularly in terms of education quality. Cambodia ranks among the
lowest in student learning achievement compared to other countries
participating in the 2022 Program for International Student Assessment
(PISA),1 highlighting the urgent need for further improvement. Importantly, this historical disruption continues to shape present disparities,
as limited access to educational resources, such as qualified teachers and
uneven distribution of resources, has widened the learning achievement
gap between urban and rural areas.
Approximately 74.4 percent of Cambodia’s population resides in
rural areas, where significant educational disparities are pronounced.
Large-scale learning assessments and empirical studies have consistently
revealed significant learning gaps between urban and rural students. For
instance, the PISA for Development (PISA-D) report showed that urban
students outperformed their rural peers by 42 points in reading and 51
points in mathematics (MoEYS, 2018). High dropout rates among rural

In response to these challenges, the Ministry of Education, Youth, and

Past empirical studies still have notable limitations regarding the
evidence of the urban-rural students’ learning achievement gap. For
instance, previous studies on the urban-rural student learning achievement gap have focused on mean-based analysis, which may obscure
important nuances. This approach often overlooks the differential influence of factors influencing student performance across their
achievement distribution. For instance, factors that benefit highperforming students may not significantly influence low-performing
students and vice versa. From this perspective, research in developing
countries that examines the urban-rural gap across the distribution of
students’ learning achievements remains scarce, with a few notable
exceptions (Sanfo and Ogawa, 2021; Sanfo, 2023; Lounkaew, 2013).
This gap in the literature underscores the need for empirical work that
goes beyond average influence to better understand and address
urban-rural inequalities across different distributions of students’
learning achievement.
Additionally, Lounkaew (2013) emphasizes the importance of
‘intangible’ factors of education, such as school autonomy, leadership,
parental involvement, and accountability, in addressing the urban-rural
achievement gap. Some studies suggest that these intangible factors may
provide a more significant or equal explanation of the urban-rural gap in
school quality and student learning achievement than tangible factors,
such as financial or physical resources, including teachers or instructional materials (Betts, 1995). These views align with the existing
literature, which suggests that physical resources alone are insufficient
to measure school quality (Hanushek and Woesserman, 2011; Heyneman, 2015). Despite the potential significance of intangible factors, only
a limited number of empirical studies, particularly in Asian developing
countries, have systematically explored their role in explaining disparities in student learning achievement between urban and rural areas.
Building on the above arguments and the identified research gap,
this study poses two research questions: (1) In urban and rural areas,
how do students, household, and school factors influence students’
learning achievement across the distribution of their achievement level?
(2) To what extent can tangible and intangible factors explain the urbanrural learning achievement gap?
This research contributes to the academic discourse and new insight
into education policy by leveraging Cambodia’s large-scale learning
assessment dataset. Specifically, it estimates the determinants of students’ learning achievement across the distribution of their achievement
level, offering insights into the different factors influencing learning
achievement in urban and rural areas. Furthermore, the study quantifies
the relative contributions of tangible and intangible factors to the urbanrural achievement gap in student learning. Notably, this is the first
empirical study to investigate the urban-rural learning achievement gap
in Cambodia, a country grappling with challenges related to education
quality and low student learning achievement.

Despite these empirical challenges, to the best of our knowledge,
only a limited number of studies have examined the distribution of
students’ learning achievements and the role of intangible factors in the
context of the urban-rural achievement gap. For instance, Sanfo and
Ogawa (2021) investigated the urban-rural learning achievement gap
among primary school students in Ethiopia, finding that student and
household characteristics (e.g., labor status and parental education
level) significantly contribute to this disparity. Notably, intangible factors also played a crucial role in explaining the urban-rural learning
achievement gap. Sanfo (2023) extended this analysis to a cross-country
comparison of Benin, Burkina Faso, Togo, and Cameroon, utilizing the
PASEC dataset (Program for the Analysis of CONFEMEN Education
Systems). The study also confirmed the importance of both tangible and
intangible factors in shaping the urban-rural learning gap, with variations in the distribution of students’ learning achievement.
In the context of Asian countries, Lounkaew (2013) examined the
urban-rural disparities in student learning achievement in Thailand.
Analyzing data from the Thai PISA 2019, the study found that the influence of student, family, and school characteristics on achievement
varied across different performance levels. At the mean level, approximately 45–48 percent of the urban-rural achievement gap can be
attributed to intangible factors. Additionally, their decomposition
analysis indicated that the proportion of the achievement gap explained
by intangible factors ranged from 12 to 15 percent for low-performing
students to around 61–69 percent for high-performing students.
In Cambodia, MoEYS has identified the urban-rural achievement gap
as a critical policy concern (MoEYS, 2024). Despite this recognition,
empirical research on this issue remains limited. While Chea and Ogawa
(2020) investigated the influence of both demand-side (student factors)
and supply-side educational factors (school factors) on student learning
achievement in Cambodia, their analysis did not investigate the heterogeneity of student learning achievement distributions and the
intangible factors. Hence, these research gaps call for further empirical
work on Cambodia’s urban-rural learning achievement gap.
2.2. The role of tangible and intangible factors in the urban–rural learning
Research on educational inequality has consistently highlighted the
urban–rural divide as one of the most persistent challenges in developing countries. A large body of evidence suggests that both tangible
and intangible factors influence student achievement and contribute to
the learning disparities between urban and rural areas (e.g., Amini and
Nivorozhkin, 2015; Lee and Borgonovi, 2022; Sanfo and Ogawa, 2021).
Access to adequate resources and infrastructure is a fundamental
determinant of student learning achievement. Studies have shown that
urban schools typically benefit from better facilities, including classrooms, libraries, laboratories, and digital resources, compared to their
rural counterparts, while rural schools often face shortages of basic
educational materials, inadequate infrastructure, and limited financial
investment, which together contribute to lower levels of student performance (e.g., Chea et al., 2022; Hanushek and Woesserman, 2011;
Heyneman and Loxley, 1983).
Beyond physical infrastructure and resources, intangible elements
play a critical role in shaping students’ learning achievements. For
instance, teacher quality is particularly influential, as effective instruction has been shown to improve student achievement significantly
(Chetty et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2024). Other intangible factors include
school autonomy, school leadership, and family involvement. Strong
school management has been associated with improved organizational
practices and student outcomes (Leithwood and Jantzi, 2005; Clark,
2009), while parental engagement has been linked to higher levels of
academic achievement across diverse contexts (Jeynes, 2007). These
intangible dimensions often interact with resource constraints, further
amplifying the disadvantage faced by rural students (Lounkaew, 2013).

One critical consideration lies in the two-level clustered sampling
method’s inherent structure for data collection. In PISA, the first stage of
sampling selects schools, while the second stage draws individual students from these schools. This design introduces the possibility of
correlated error terms among students within the same school, driven by
shared school-level stochastic factors. Consequently, this correlation can
lead to biased estimates of standard deviations, undermining the validity
of statistical inferences. To address this issue, we implement clusteradjusted standard deviation calculations to ensure robust and reliable
statistical inference.

evaluate the contributions of both observed (tangible factors) and unobserved inputs (intangible factors) in explaining the disparities in
student performance between urban and rural students. This technique
allows us to decompose the differences in students’ learning achievement, proxied by PISA scores, between urban and rural groups (Oaxaca,
1973).

4.1. Estimation results of unconditional quintile regression
Fig. 3 presents the coefficient plot from the quintile regression,
illustrating the impact of school location on students’ mathematics and
reading achievement across the entire performance distribution. Overall, the results indicate that attending a rural school negatively influences students’ learning achievement in both mathematics and
reading across each quintile. Notably, this negative influence is more
pronounced in mathematics.
Tables 3 and 4 present the UQR results on factors influencing
mathematics and reading achievement among urban and rural students.
Student-related factors play a significant role in determining PISA scores
in both subjects across urban and rural contexts. In contrast, schoolrelated variables are not as influential as student-related factors in
shaping students’ achievement.
Upon examining the results of each variable in more detail, it is
evident that students’ gender is significantly associated with their
learning achievement. In mathematics, the results indicate that being
female has a statistically negative influence from the 30th to the 90th
quintiles. In urban and rural sub-samples, the negative influence of
being female becomes increasingly pronounced at higher quintiles. In
contrast, the estimation for reading subjects reveals an opposite trend:
female students are more likely to achieve higher scores, particularly at
the 10th and 30th quintiles in rural areas.
Another finding is that early childhood education (ECE) experience
has a positive influence on students’ learning achievement at the 30th,
50th, 70th, and 90th quintiles, with a more substantial influence in
urban areas. The positive coefficient is consistently observed across both
mathematics and reading subjects, although the magnitude varies by
achievement level. In rural areas, the influence of ECE is smaller and less
pronounced, indicating heterogeneous impacts across locations.

Our quantitative analysis demonstrates some notable findings.
Student-related factors significantly determine students’ achievement in
PISA scores for both subjects in urban and rural areas. This finding aligns

Female students are more likely to achieve higher scores, particularly

Pre-school experience positively influences learning achievement,
particularly at higher quintiles and more strongly in urban areas.This
finding is consistent with academic accumulations, which consistently
emphasize the critical role of ECE in enhancing students’ learning
achievement (e.g., Heckman, 2011; Yang, 2024; Miwa et al., 2010).
This consistency is attributed to the higher quality of ECE programs in
urban Cambodia, where better funding often results in more qualified
teachers, superior learning resources, and improved facilities (Rao and
Pearson, 2009). These ECE quality differences likely foster more vital
foundational skills, contributing to higher achievement levels that
become more evident in students with high learning achievement
(Betancur et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023).
Students’ waged labor status has a negatively influence mathematics
and reading achievement, particularly among low-performing students
in the bottom quintile. When students are required to engage in
intense labor, it reduces the time and energy they can dedicate to
studying, thereby hindering their academic performance (Lee et al.,
2021). In subjects like mathematics and reading, where foundational
knowledge and consistent daily practice are crucial, such labor, along
with the resulting reduction in study time due to fatigue, exacerbates the
gap between low-performing students and their higher-performing
peers. In Cambodia, student labor is often associated with subsistence
farming, factory work, or employment in informal sectors. These roles
typically involve significant physical exertion and long hours, leaving
minimal time and energy for students’ academic pursuits.
Our estimation shows that SES negatively influence students’
learning achievement at the 10th and 30th quintiles in rural areas but
becomes positive at higher quintiles. This unexpected finding may be
linked to parental expectations and local future job opportunities. Students with poor academic performance often struggle from an early
stage, such as primary school, leading their parents to have lower expectations for their education. Even when families have adequate resources, these resources may not be allocated toward education for
academically disadvantaged children. This issue is more pronounced in
rural areas, where limited future job opportunities and lower returns on
education further diminish the incentive to invest in children’s learning
(OECD, 2019).
Furthermore, the student’s phone usage has a negative influence on
students’ mathematics and reading test scores. This finding supports
previous work, emphasizing that excessive phone use often consumes
time that could not be spent studying or engaging in educational activities, thereby reducing students’ academic ability (Amez and Baert,
2020; Ma et al., 2023). In Cambodia, the widespread adoption of mobile
phones has paralleled the expansion of internet access, making phone
ownership and usage common among secondary school students.
Consequently, integrating mobile phones into students’ daily lives has
posed significant challenges to maintaining a focused learning environment. Excessive usage often results in distractions, reduced study
time, and sleep deprivation, negatively impacting academic learning
performance.

5.2. The proportion of tangible and intangible factors explaining the gap
in students’ learning achievement between urban and rural areas
Our estimated results suggest that intangible factors play a dominant
role in explaining the urban–rural learning achievement gap among lowachieving students, particularly at the 10th percentile, where they account for more than 70 percent of the disparity. This pattern differs
markedly from the findings of previous studies. For example, Lounkaew
(2013), using a decomposition analysis, reported that intangible factors
explained only about 12–15 percent of the gap among low-performing
students, with their contribution gradually increasing at higher
achievement levels to around 61–69 percent for high-performing students in Thailand. In contrast, our results show the opposite trend: the
explanatory power of intangible factors is strongest at the lower end of
the distribution and tends to decline toward the upper percentiles in
Cambodian context. This discrepancy highlights potential contextual
differences, suggesting that the relative importance of intangible factors
in shaping learning inequalities may vary depending on the educational
context of each country.
Our unexpected OBD results suggest that, in Cambodia, the urbanrural learning achievement gap may be predominantly influenced by
intangible factors such as school management practices, autonomy in
decision-making, and parental participation, especially in lowperforming students. School accountability in urban areas is often
stronger due to higher community expectations and greater oversight by
local authorities than in rural areas (OECD, 2016; Tolo et al., 2020). This
robust accountability fosters an environment where intangible factors,
such as effective leadership and strong stakeholder collaboration, are
pivotal in enhancing learning achievement. In contrast, rural schools
may encounter challenges such as limited community involvement,
often stemming from parents’ under-involvement in education (Othman
and Muijs, 2013). This lack of engagement can hinder efforts to improve
school quality, highlighting the critical role of community attitudes and
parental participation in shaping students’ academic learning achievement. The disparities in school management, accountability structures,
and community involvement between urban and rural areas further
exacerbate the learning achievement gap between students in these
setting. Hence, the persistent dominance of intangible factors at the
lower end of the achievement distribution suggests that improving
school governance and strengthening parental involvement could have
disproportionate benefits for the most disadvantaged students.

This study has some limitations. First, we did not discuss the differences in estimation results between mathematics and reading subjects. While our UQR and OBD analyses revealed few notable differences
in terms of factors influencing students’ learning achievement, some
variables exhibited subject-specific influences. Future research should
discuss these variations among different subjects based on the country
context and the specific characteristics of subjects in depth. Second, due
to data limitations, we were not able to include certain variables that
may influence students’ learning achievement such as private tutoring.
This omission is particularly important, as private tutoring is strongly
associated with learning outcomes in Cambodian secondary education.
Future research should address this limitation by accounting for potential omitted variable bias.

Leveraging nationally representative samples from the Cambodia
PISA 2022 dataset, our study investigated the determinants of students’
learning achievement in urban and rural areas. Furthermore, we estimated the factors (both tangible and intangible) that explain the urbanrural learning achievement gap. Specifically, we applied unconditional
quantile regression (UQR) to estimate the education production function
across students’ learning achievement distributions. Furthermore, the
RIF-Oaxaca–Blinder Decomposition (OBD) examined the proportion of
tangible and intangible factors that explain the urban-rural students’
learning achievement gap across their performance percentile.
Our URQ results indicate that student-level factors significantly influence mathematics and reading PISA test scores, while only a few
school-level factors yield statistically significant results. These influencing factors vary depending on whether the school is urban or rural
and across the distribution of students’ learning achievement. Besides,
our OBD analysis indicates that the proportion of tangible and intangible
factors varies significantly across students’ learning achievement percentiles. Specifically, intangible factors play a more critical role in
explaining the urban-rural learning achievement gap among lowachieving students, whereas tangible factors account for the urbanrural gap among high-achieving students. This finding contrasts with
those of similar past studies. Our results highlight that the lack of
intangible aspects, such as school autonomy and community participation in rural areas, significantly contributes to the geographical gap
among low-achieving students in Cambodia.
This finding highlights essential policy recommendations and implications. First, fostering educational demand-side factors, such as
student and their household, effectively improves learning achievement
for both urban and rural students. Moreover, such support must be
tailored to align with the distribution of students’ achievement levels.

insufficient to guarantee the success of interventions or policies to
reduce the urban-rural learning achievement gap, especially low
