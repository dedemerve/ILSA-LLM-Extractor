Violence in schools is one of the main concerns in Latin American countries and is one of the main
threats for schools to be effective in providing high-quality education. For this work, data from
ICCS 2016 is used to fit decision trees and cluster robust logistic regression models to explore how
some schools immersed in violent environments manage to be resilient to violence. Our results
show that the variables most consistently associated with higher probabilities of a school being
resilient to violence are those related to the students’ attitudes and to a lesser extent, students’
civic knowledge and family background. The paper concludes by discussing the possible policy
and practice implications in the context of each of the countries analysed.

1. Introduction
Data from the International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS1) (Schulz, Ainley, Cox, & Friedman, 2017) show that
social inequalities, poverty and high levels of violence, crime and corruption continue to be serious problems for Latin American youth.
Research over the past years has consistently shown that exposure to violence, as a witness or a victim, is strongly associated with
aggressive, delinquent, and violent behaviour (Garrido & Galvis, 2016). According to Cox, Jaramillo, & Reimers (2006), growing up in
a society with high levels of violence has implications for the development of citizenship competencies and attitudes. Furthermore,
there is a relationship between the support of violence and educational expectations, in that it was found that students who wanted to
obtain a university degree scored lower on the scale of students’ endorsement of the use of violence compared to those who did not
expect to do so. Moreover, there is a strong association between the level of civic knowledge and the use of violence. That is, students
with greater civic knowledge emerged as being significantly less likely to support the use of violence (Schulz, Ainley, Cox, & Friedman,
2017).
Previous research on school violence has identified both risk factors associated with violent behaviour and protecting ones that
contribute to preventing such behaviour. For example, Romero-Abrio, Musitu, Callejas-Jerónimo, Sánchez-Sosa, & Villarreal-González (2018) found that students with low academic performance, attending a school located in urban and socially disadvantaged
areas, as well as living in areas with high social tension have increased probability of presenting violent behaviour. On the other hand,
caring relationships and high expectations are among those identified as protective factors (Williams, Stiffman, & O’Neal, 1998;

exposed to risk-factors but manage not to engage in violent activities can be considered as resilient to violence.
Whilst the phenomenon of individual resiliency to violence has been widely explored in the literature, the same level of inquiry has
not emerged at the organisational level, especially regarding the school environment. Related to the findings described above, some
studies have suggested that activities that involve students in decisions about school, as well as participation in school-based activities
and community networks are important in helping to create a climate of inclusion, respect, and safety (Gutiérrez & Robertson, 2016;
López de Mesa et al., 2013). In fact, in a recent study conducted by the Mayor’s Office of Bogotá (Secretaría de Educación Distrital
2018), it was concluded that a protective factor to mitigate student violence is the development of a sense of belonging of the students
to their educational institutions.
In order to address this organisational level gap, in this paper, we use data from an international study ICCS 2016 to identify the
characteristics of schools resilient to violence in three Latin American countries: Chile, Mexico and Colombia. This is an appropriate
focus, because violence and organised crime (e.g. drug trafficking and kidnapping) are among the most persistent problems across
Latin American countries. Regarding which, the study Global Study on Homicide 2013 shows large disparities in the homicide rates
across regions, but clearly points to the high intensity of homicidal violence in the Americas, with a rate of 13.6 homicides per100,000
population, whereas in Asia it is 2.9, in Europe, 3.0 and in Oceania 3.0 (United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime [UNODC], 2015).
Specifically, in Colombia, México and Chile, and the homicide rates are 26.5, 16.4 and 3.6 per 100,000 people, respectively (UNDOC,
2015).
To frame our analysis, in the following sections we present, first, information about the research context (Latin American Countries
(LAC) – Mexico, Chile, Colombia) followed by a literature review on school violence (factors associated with it and strategies for
addressing it). We then describe the ICCS data and our analytical strategy. Finally, we present our results, discussion, and conclusions
in the context of the three countries investigated.
1.1. Research context: LAC – Mexico, Chile, Colombia
Generally, in LAC, despite there having been considerable improvement in secondary schools in terms of educational outcomes
over the last two decades, low educational quality remains a major problem. Specifically, children from socioeconomically disadvantaged groups as well as those from indigenous and rural backgrounds are less likely to participate in school than their wealthier
urban counterparts. Even when they do attend, they are more likely to receive low quality education, which is the case in classrooms
across LAC. Another substantial concern in Mexico, Chile and Colombia, like other LAC, is violence, which leads to unsafe school
environments and prevents children from attending schools (Fiszbein & Stanton, 2018).
In terms of gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, whilst Colombia’s GDP is approximately $US13,000, Mexico and Chile have
comparatively higher levels of approximately $16,000 and $22,000, respectively. When it comes to Human Development Index (HDI),
Chile is categorised as having a “very high” level, whilst Colombia and Mexico have “high” human development categorisation (Schulz
et al., 2017).
Contextually, in a recent report by Chávez et al. (2021), Colombia ranked the highest for the proportion of parents who believed
that fights with weapons (24 per cent) were likely or highly likely across LCA countries. Mexico has also benefited from “a large share
of initiatives” since community violence has considerable impact there due to the existence of organised criminal groups, which have
targeted schools in order to be able to recruit children and teenager students (p.25). In contrast, Chile has been classified as the safest
country in Latin America, with just 2.74 murders per 100,000 people in 2013 (Chioda, 2017). Taken together the similarities and
differences in these three countries (Colombia, Mexico and Chile), an investigative comparison of Colombia, Mexico and Chile’s school
resilient to violence may present a clearer landscape into educational policies.
1.2. School violence: characteristics and statistics
One of the biggest problems that exist in the study of school violence lies in its conceptualisation, for there is no single definition of
this phenomenon (Ayala-Carrillo, 2015). One of the main reasons is that even within the school environment there are different types
of violence (e.g. verbal, physical, psychological, etc.). Behaviours or actions that can be considered as violent for some, but not so by
others. Furthermore, as values and social norms evolve, this concept becomes increasingly broad and abstract. Despite this, there is
some agreement that school violence can be understood as any behaviour or action exercised with the intention of causing damage to
others, by any member of the educational community (Guerra et al., 2011).
Societies around the world expect schools to prepare children for excellence both in the workplace and socially. Given the great
amount of time that children and young people spend in schools, it is essential that this process takes place in a safe and welcoming
environment (Pinheiro, 2007). However, educational institutions are vulnerable to different types of risks that affect all members of
the educational community and hence the teaching-learning process. The Second Regional Comparative and Explanatory Study
(SERCE), applied in 16 countries in Latin America, found that school climate is one of the most influential variables on student
achievement levels (Latin American Laboratory for Assessment of the Quality of Education [LLECE], 2008).
Konold, Cornell, Sula, & Huang (2017) consider that a positive school climate is reflected in a robust disciplinary structure,
supportive relationships between teachers and students, as well as high educational expectations. Despite continuous efforts to
improve and maintain safe school environments (Conaway, 2014), school violence continues to be a phenomenon that is part of the
daily life of many educational institutions. Regarding which, in Chile, the National Institute of Youth (INJUV, by its acronym in

1.3. Factors associated with school violence
School violence is a complex and multidimensional issue. Many authors have studied the factors associated with it (see, for
example, Ayala-Carrillo, 2015, Neut, 2017, Romero-Abrio et al., 2018, Saavedra, Villalta, & Muñoz, 2015) and although there is no
agreement on a universal list, there is a certain consensus around four major categories in which most of them can be included: school,
social, individual and family factors.
Regarding school factors, variables relating to school climate, quality of peer relationships, student-teacher relationships and
school discipline have been associated with school violence (Buelga, Martínez-Ferrer, & Musitu, 2016). Also, Saavedra et al. (2015)
have pointed out that school failure, poor academic performance, low motivation to undertake homework, school disorganisation and
large schools (in terms of the number of students) are correlated with school violence.
Concerning social factors, it has been observed that the desire to achieve a certain reputation or social recognition is a high-risk
factor that leads many students into violent behaviour (Emler & Reicher, 2005). The attitude towards institutional authority has
also been associated with school violence, with research having shown that violent students tend to present unfavourable attitudes
towards teachers, principals, the police, etc. (Emler, 2008, Emler & Reicher, 2005, Jiménez & Bernal, 2015).
In terms of individual factors, some investigations (e.g. Saavedra et al., 2015, Sanchez-Sosa, Villarreal-Gonzalez, & Musitu, 2013)
have found that the victims of violence often present problems of anxiety, depression and psychological distress. In contrast, the
perpetrators of violence are characterised as presenting maladaptive social behaviour, a tendency misuse their strength and impulsive
behaviour.
Finally, regarding family factors, authors like Olweus (2005) have found that there is greater peer harassment of students whose
parents are separated, belong to non-cohesive families and/or when their parents are overprotective. Ybarra and Mitchell (2004)
suggested that other factors that can influence violent behaviour in students are a crowded household, low socioeconomic status, weak
family relationships, and the presence of weapons in the home. Some other authors have even suggested that there are intergenerational transmission mechanisms of antisocial behaviour (Hunter, Nixon, & Parr, 2010).
From the policy perspective, schools can be seen as an optimal space to foster the protective factors that could counterbalance the
risk factors present in the other categories (i.e. social, individual and family) (Aguilar, Arjona, & Noriega, 2016). Other authors go even
further by stating that it is the school’s responsibility to promote resilience and help protect children from adversity, violence and
failure (Bowes, Maughan, Caspi, Moffitt, and Arseneault, 2010). As Simpson (2008) contended, in order to deal with school violence,
the ideal scenario is to create a resilient school.
1.4. Objectives
The main objective of this paper is to identify patterns associated with the likelihood of schools being resilient to violence using
data from a large-scale dataset containing representative samples of eighth graders in Mexico, Colombia and
Chile. This general objective is broken down into two specific ones, which define in detail the purpose of this study as follows.
(1) To identify the variables that discriminate better between the schools that are resilient to violence and those that are not,
according to their normalised importance.
(2) To identify the set of variables that are more consistently associated with the likelihood of a school to be resilient to violence.
2. Methodology
2.1. Data
We used data from the IEA’s International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS, 2016), which is a large-scale assessment
that evaluates to what extent young people (8th graders) are prepared to undertake their roles as citizens in the participating countries.
It collects information about students’ knowledge of concepts related to civics and citizenship, as well as their beliefs, attitudes, and
behaviours concerning this domain. Additionally, ICCS collects rich contextual data on teacher qualifications and experiences,
teaching practices, school environment and climate as well as home and community support. ICCS 2016 was applied to 94,000 students, 37,000 teachers and 3,800 head teachers across 24 countries in Latin America, Europe and Asia.

2.2. Sample
For this paper, we used data from a subset of schools identified as ‘high-risk’ in Mexico, Colombia and Chile. High-risk schools were
differentiated from their low-risk counterparts by their scores on a scale measuring the principals’ perceptions of crime in the community (C_COMCRI). Because the schools are part of the community they are located in, they tend to be affected by community-based
issues and problems. There is evidence to suggest that the presence of crime within the local community tends to increase the risk of
violence both outside and inside the school (Schulz et al., 2017). Schools with a score on the scale ‘principals’ perceptions of crime in
the community’ higher than the national average were considered as those at high risk of experiencing violence. Only ‘high-risk’
schools were included in the final sample. The participation rates and final sample sizes for these three countries are presented in
Table 1.
The final sample was composed of, in the case of Chile, a total of 85 schools (47.1% comprising 1,169 students) that were nonresilient compared to 52.9% (1,412 students) that were resilient. In Mexico there was a greater disparity in being resilient in that
of the total participating schools, 115 (67% covering 2,312 students) were non-resilient, whilst 38% (1,176 students) were resilient.
Finally, in Colombia, there were a total of 81 schools (58% with 1,849 students) were non-resilient and 42% (1,343 students) were
resilient.
2.3. Measures
This section highlights the dependent variable and independent variables employed for this study.
2.3.1. Dependent variable
The dependent variable was a dichotomous variable indicating whether a school is ‘Resilient to Violence’. A school was considered
to be resilient to violence, if it presented two characteristics: high risk of violence in the community and a low incidence of violence.
According to this definition, from the total sample, 1,412 students in Chile; 1,176 in Mexico; and 1,343 in Colombia attend schools
resilient to violence.
As mentioned before, a school was considered to be in high risk of violence, if it scored higher than the national average on the scale
‘principals’ perceptions of crime in the community’ (C_COMCRI). This scale summarises a set of items asking principals to what extent
(“large”, “moderate”, “small”, “not at all”) the following issues are a source of social tension in the school’s wider community:
organised crime, youth gangs, petty crime, sexual harassment, drug abuse, and alcohol abuse. In turn, a school was considered to have
a low incidence of violence, if it scored lower than the national average on the scale ‘students’ experiences of physical and verbal abuse
at school’ (S_ABUSE). This measure was derived from students’ ratings (“to a large extent”, “to a moderate extent”, “to a small extent”,
“not at all”) regarding the following statements: a student called you by an offensive nickname, a student said things about you to make
others laugh, a student threatened to hurt you, you were physically attacked by another student, a student broke something belonging
to you on purpose, and a student posted offensive pictures or text about you on the Internet.
2.3.2. Independent variables
There are five types of data files corresponding to the five types of questionnaires administered in ICCS 2016. That is, the international, European and Latin American student data files along with the teacher and school data files contain the responses to the
questions asked in the respective questionnaire. Apart from the structure and design variables (e.g. ID, sampling weights, replicate
weights, etc.), each data file contains the responses to each of the questions included in the questionnaires, as well as derived variables
from these single items. In the ICCS 2016 questionnaires, theoretical (unobservable constructs) were measured using sets of individual
(observable) items. In these cases, responses to the individual items were combined to create a derived variable that provided a more
comprehensive picture of the construct of interest than relying on individual item responses.
In our analysis, we used as independent variables all the derived variables included in the school data file, as well as those in the
international and Latin American student files2. The variables that were not used to construct derived variables were included as single
items. Because our analysis is located at the school level (i.e. we are interested in finding school characteristics associated with the
probability of it being resilient to violence), all the student variables were aggregated at the school level by calculating the mean value

2.4. Analytical strategy
We used two main analytical procedures. First, were deployed classification trees to identify the most important predictors of
schools being resilient to violence in Mexico, Colombia and Chile. Then, we employed logistic regression models for each country to
identify the factors that are more consistently associated with specify the best models to predict the same outcome. We used SPSS
(IBM, 2015) for the classification trees and the IEA IDB Analyser (IEA, 2016) to fit the logistic regression models.
After the construction of the dependent variable and the selection of the predictors (see above), in order to identify the variables
that discriminate better between the schools that are resilient to violence and those that are not (objective 1), we first used the data
mining technique known as Classification and Regression Trees (CART). This technique allows for establishing ranking of the predictors according to their normalised importance (Román & Levy, 2003). In this way, the most important independent variables were
selected establishing as an inclusion criterion of a normalised importance of at least 40%.
Once the most important predictors had been selected, the next step was to check the assumptions of non-collinearity of independence of errors and the presence of outliers. Then, in order to identify the set of variables that are more consistently associated with
the likelihood of a school being resilient to violence (objective 2), logistic regression models were fitted for each country in order to
select those variables that established a more consistent association with the probability of a school being resilient or not.
All analyses were carried out taking into account the complexity of the sample and the assessment design of ICCS 2016. We used
sampling weights to take into account any disproportional sampling of subgroups and to adjust for nonresponse, and replicate weights
to account for the nested structure of the data3 (Schulz, Carstens, Losito & Fraillon. 2018).

The technique known as CART allowed us to identify those variables with the highest normalised importance in predicting the
likelihood of a school being resilient to violence in Mexico, Colombia and Chile. One of the main limitations of this data mining
technique is, however, that the structure of the tree will drastically change when a different set of variables is considered. In other
words, a single decision tree is unstable. For instance, a small change in the data can cause the algorithm to select different variables
and cut-off points (López-Martín, Expósito-Casas, Carpintero, & Asensio, 2018; Strobl, Malley, & Tutz, 2009). For this reason, we used
a second analytical technique to complement the results of our analyses (logistic regression models).

4. Discussion and conclusions
This work was aimed at identifying the factors associated with the likelihood of schools being resilient to violence in Mexico,
Colombia and Chile by drawing on the data collected by ICCS 2016. The ultimate objective is to produce empirical evidence to inform

The first specific objective of this paper, addressed using the CART technique, was to identify the variables that best discriminate
between the profile of schools resilient and not resilient to violence across Mexico, Colombia and Chile. In this regard, it can be
concluded that the factors that best differentiate between resilient and not resilient schools are those relating students’ personal or
individual variables. For example, students’ endorsement of the use of violence, students’ support for gender equality and students’
feelings of empathy toward their classmates showed some of the higher levels of normalised importance in the three countries included
in the analysis. These findings are in line with those of previous studies in the field (see, for example, Saavedra et al., 2015;
Sánchez-Sosa et al., 2013).
Another important variable for differentiating between resilient and non-resilient schools is students’ perceptions about the
importance of personal responsibility for citizenship, which has been linked to attitudes towards institutions in more general terms.
Regarding which, Emler (2008); Emler & Reicher (2005); and Jiménez & Bernal (2015) demonstrate that violent students tend to
present unfavourable attitudes towards institutions.
Among the variables referring specifically to schools, the school mean of students’ evaluation of the quality of their interaction at
school was found to be the most important. The quality of the interaction between peers has already been found to be associated with
school violence in a study by Buelga et al. (2016).
Family factors were found to be important for distinguishing between schools resilient and non-resilient to violence only in Mexico,
including variables such as the school mean of highest parental education, the school mean of home literacy resources and the school
mean of highest parental occupational status. These variables relating to the socio-economic status of the family have been previously
reported by Ybarra and Mitchell (2004) as important for explaining processes associated with violence in schools.
Once we had selected the variables that best discriminate between the schools that are resilient to violence and those that are not,
the second specific objective was to identify the most relevant predictors of the probability of a school being resilient to violence. We
did so by fitting logistic regression models for each focal country to predict the probability of a school being resilient using the subset of
variables identified in the first step. The variables identified as the most relevant predictors in this model varied from country to
country.
In the case of Chile, students’ positive attitudes were found to be important predictors of the probability of a school being resilient
to violence. Students’ positive attitudes towards participation in the school and their perceptions of the openness of the classroom for
discussions are associated with an increase in the probability of a school being resilient to violence. Related to this, the Mayor’s Office
of Bogotá (2018) has pointed out that an important factor for mitigating violence in schools has been found in the development of a
sense of belonging to the educational institutions. Higher levels of civic knowledge and the perception of the students about the
importance of personal responsibility for citizenship, also have a positive association with the likelihood of schools being resilient to
violence. These results are supported by the findings of Flores & Lourdes (2014), who have identified the importance of the subject
civic education, in designing a programme to enable students to be political subjects able to transform their environment peacefully.
Another variable that was also found to be salient in the development of resilient schools in Chile is an adequate use of social
networks. This variable is particularly important today since there is evidence that suggests that new technologies are frequently used
to disseminate violent and aggressive expressions (Martínez-Pastor, Catalina-García, & López-de-Ayala-López, 2019). In addition, it
has been observed that schools have a higher probability of being resilient to violence when they have greater availability of resources
in the local community (e.g. public library, cinema, theatre, language schools, museums, etc.). A similar finding was obtained by
Romero-Abrio and colleagues (2018).
In Mexico, as in Chile, the variables related to students’ attitudes are the most consistently associated with higher probability of a
school being resilient. For example, a school tends to have higher chances to be resilient to violence when its students have positive
perceptions about: the quality of their interactions at school, the importance of social movements and participating in legal protests (e.
g. peaceful protests against laws believed to be unjust, or activities aimed at benefiting people in the local community, such as collecting signatures for a petition), and the need for equal rights for all ethnic groups. In support of these findings, other authors, like
Bonilla Castillón and colleagues (2017), have observed that a positive attitude of students towards institutional and social authority
can influence the interactions in a school regulatory system, thereby protecting adolescents from their involvement in violent
behaviour.
A somewhat unexpected result of our analysis is that when students declare higher levels of participation in the school and the
community, their schools tend to have lower probabilities of being resilient to violence. This contradicts the results of authors, such as
Gutiérrez & Robertson, (2016) and López de Mesa and colleagues (2013), who claimed that involving students in school decisions
creates a less violent, more inclusive and safe school climate. This counterintuitive finding could be explained by the causal mechanism
described by Ascorra, López, and Urbina (2016). That is, the authors analysed the quality of school interactions under different
conditions. They concluded that opportunities for supervised participation generate positive interactions. In contrast, school participation under less structured (unsupervised) activities results in lower quality interactions. For schools immersed in a violent social
environment, thus having a high probability of experiencing school violence, the best way forward could be to offer opportunities for
students to participate through well-structured and well-supervised activities.
Another apparently counterintuitive result is that when students report higher levels of endorsement of violence, the school tends
to have a lower probability of being resilient to it. This could be explained by the existence of nation-wide programmes focused on
reducing the levels of school violence in Mexico. For example, the ‘Programa Escuela Segura’ [Safe School Programme] initiated in 2007
has been implemented in more than 50 thousand schools (approximately one-third of the total number of schools), focusing on those
located in the municipalities with a higher incidence of crime (SEP, 2016). So, the pattern showing that schools whose students have
higher levels of endorsement of violence are most likely to be resilient could be explained because these are the same schools that have

Finally, with respect to school resilience to violence in Colombia, there are some similarities with Mexico. For example, the students’ endorsement of violence is also positively associated with the probability of a school being resilient to violence. Similar to
Mexico and because of the history of Colombia’s long internal armed conflict, the Ministry of Education has implemented a number of
programmes aimed at the prevention of violence in schools (Gairín & Barrera, 2014) (e.g. HazPaz [MakePeace], Aprende a Resolver
Conflictos [Learn to Solve Conflicts] (ARCO, by its acronym in Spanish). The explanation for this pattern could be along the same lines
as in Mexico: schools whose students have higher endorsement of violence are more likely to participate in programmes aimed at
preventing school violence and therefore, are more likely to be resilient to it.
Similar to the results in Mexico, the participation of students in the school, the students’ willingness to participate in school activities, the perceptions of the principals about the participation of teachers in school government, and the students’ perception of the
openness of the classroom for discussion are all associated with a decrease in the probability of a school being resilient to violence.
Once again, this result suggests that participatory dynamics per se are not enough to influence the likelihood of a school being resilient
to violence. As Ascorra and colleagues (2016) have advocated, for participation to promote a non-violent school climate to be successful, it has to take place in under a well-structured and well-supervised framework.
On the other hand, feelings of empathy between students and the importance that students place on civic responsibility result in a
positive relationship with the probability of a school being resilient. Different authors have provided evidence of a consistent negative
relationship between empathy and school violence (see, for example, Karcher, 2004; Gini, et al., 2007).
Despite the differences found in our analyses for Chile, Mexico and Colombia, the variables relating to the attitudes and perceptions
of the students reveal more consistent associations to the likelihood of schools being resilient to violence. This finding is reinforced by
the two methods used to analyse the data: the decisions trees and the logistic regression models.
4.1. Implications
Based on these results, we conclude that the most effective interventions to reduce violence in schools should be aimed at
developmental programmes targeting students’ attitudes and perceptions, rather than at the organisational level. From this
perspective, it is important not only to carry out interventions aimed at reducing violent acts, but also, developing interventions with a
focus on preventing violence before it appears in schools. Accordingly, as pointed out by Flores & Lourdes (2014), some

As discussed above, our results relating to the participation of students and teachers in school suggest that the participation has to
take place within a solid and clear structure, with the supervision of staff trained for that purpose. Following Flores & Lourdes (2014),

environment of respect, solidarity, and trust, thereby fostering a culture of respectful and inclusive participation. In turn, this would set
the scene for making the school an example of a democratic and peaceful society.
With regard to policy implications, we believe that the findings of this study give substantial understanding about educational
policies across LAC countries, in this case Chile, Mexico and Colombia. For instance, there are initiatives that run by UNICEF (Chávez
et al., 2021), and World Bank Group (Fiszbein & Stanton, 2018) on stopping violence in Latin America and promoting the future
direction of educational policies to build stronger schools. Their main interest lies in offering safe educatıonal environments for all
students by involving different stakeholders, such as parents, students, community members and school staff to serve this purpose. As
mentioned above, our findings are in line with these kind of initiatiatives. For example, one of the most important predictors of
resilience to violence across the three countries analysed was the perception of the interactions between students at school. Positive
relations among students are the result of combined efforts from all the school community members, including not only students and
teachers, but also, parents and other school staff members. The same can be said about some of the other important predictors
identified here, factors such as empathy, attitudes towards gender equality and diversity in the neighbourhood, all of which are the
result of coordinated efforts from all stakeholders involved in the educational process.
4.2. Limitations and further research
Finally, we must acknowledge that our analyses have an important limitation that is common to most international large-scale
assessments, such as ICCS. Due to the cross-sectional nature of the data, endogeneity caused by reverse causality is present in our
results (see Cordero et al., 2018). In this case, the direct comparison between resilient and non-resilient schools might be biased,
because the treatment could be correlated with unobserved reasons behind the presence of violence in these schools. In order to
disentangle these effects and the mechanisms underlying the patterns that we describe here with this quantitative study, other research
designs are needed (e.g. qualitative case studies and longitudinal studies) so as to produce more detailed information to inform the
design of educational policies and interventions at the school level. Further studies might be concentrated, for example, on the change
in students’ perception of violence in schools over time. First, getting students’ expectation of violence in beginning of the term and
