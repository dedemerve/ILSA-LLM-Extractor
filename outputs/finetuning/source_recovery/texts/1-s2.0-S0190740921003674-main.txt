The impact of the one-child policy on urban children’s development has been widely studied, leaving those from
rural areas unexplored. This study investigated the sibling effects (only-child or not) on adolescents’ academic
achievement in rural China. Altogether 156 pairs of only children and their peers with siblings (Female = 145,
46.5%; aged from 13 to 17; Mage = 14.83) were sampled and matched from rural schools; then they completed
the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA) Math, Reading, and Science tests and parental questionnaires. The descriptive and hierarchical regression results indicated that the adolescents with siblings had
significantly higher academic achievement, parents’ academic expectation, desk ownership, and kindergarten
level than the only children. In addition, kindergarten level, eyesight, and parents’ academic expectations were
significant predictors of rural adolescents’ academic achievement. These findings suggested that: (1) the
educational authorities should build an equitable preschool learning situation; (2) teachers should lead only
children to set and make progress on educational goals; (3) parents should raise the awareness of protecting
children’s eyesight in those remote and rural areas.

1. Introduction
The one-child policy in China (from 1979 to 2016) has been intensively and rigorously implemented in urban China, which has accumulated about one billion of only children during the past four decades (Li,
Yang, & Chen, 2016). This unique policy and its far-reaching impact on
child development have been widely explored, causing a heated debate
about its benefits and drawbacks (Deutsch, 2006; Falbo & Poston, 1993;
Jiao, Ji, & Jing, 1996). Some researchers have found that only children
performed better in academic achievement and had better fitness (Falbo
& Poston, 1993; Jiao, Ji, and Jing, 1996). However, all these studies
have focused on only children in urban China, leaving those from rural
areas unexplored. Furthermore, the number of only children in rural
areas is minimal, as the adults in rural China (39.4% of the total population) tend to have more children than their urban fellows (Poston &
Falbo, 1990). Therefore, very few studies have empirically explored the
impact of singleton status on rural children’s academic development. To
fill this gap, this study endeavors to compare the learning opportunities
and academic achievement between the only children and the non-only

children in rural China, then identify the factors underlying academic
achievement with a large-scale survey, thus paving a way to put forward
some important suggestions for achieving educational equity and
equality in China and other countries.
2. Literature review
2.1. The one-child policy and its impact on child development
The one-child policy was enacted in 1979 by the Chinese government
to curb population growth (Deutsch, 2006). This policy was first
implemented in urban areas and later in rural areas in the late 1980s
(Qian, 2009). Accordingly, in the early 1990s, about 98% of urban
children (ages 3–6) were from one-child families (Jiao, Ji & Jing, 1996).
This policy has effectively slowed down population growth; thus,
China’s total population was 1.3 billion in 2016, merely 0.3 billion more
than in the 1980s. In contrast, if this policy had not been implemented,
the population would have climbed to 1.3 billion by 2000 (Deutsch,
2006). Therefore, this policy has successfully postponed the population

Accordingly, the Chinese government abolished the one-child policy in

In the past decades, the impact of the one-child policy on academic
achievement has been widely explored (Falbo & Poston, 1993; Liu, Lin &
Chen, 2010; Poston & Falbo, 1990). Most researchers jointly found that
the only child performed better in academic achievement, as they
received more educational resources (Falbo & Poston, 1993; Liu, Lin &
Chen, 2010). However, in China, the only child’s advantage in academic
achievement tends to be more pronounced in urban than in rural areas
(Poston & Falbo, 1990). Chinese only children in rural areas often face
significant academic challenges caused by inadequate educational resources and low family capital (Fong, 2007; Poston & Falbo, 1990; Qian,
2009). Affluent families are eager to raise more children in rural areas
because more children typically translate to additional labor resources.
Besides, in rural multi-child families, children could share resources
such as books, and the older children could even tutor the younger ones.
This could have resulted in an even better informal education environment for the rural multi-child families compared with the urban onlychild families, thereby causing no advantage in the academic achievement of the rural only child (Fong, 2007; Poston & Falbo, 1990; Qian,
2009). In this case, the only child development in rural areas has drawn
more and more attention among scholars and parents in China (Xiao &
Feng, 2010a,b): will they have better academic performance than their
counterparts with siblings?
This question, however, is not easy to answer as there were relatively
few only children in rural China. Therefore, Poston and Falbo (1990)
sampled 1465 students from Changchun province to compare only
children’s academic performance and personality traits against those
with siblings. The results indicated that the only child in the rural areas
did not perform better in academic achievement than their peers with
siblings. In addition, more attention from their less-educated parents
and grandparents might not positively impact those only children. These
inconclusive findings have indicated that there might be a different story
about the benefits and disbenefits of being the only child in rural China,
which will be explored in this study.

confirmed that sibling size has slightly impaired children’s academic
performance when the number of siblings is beyond two (Qian, 2009).
The above phenomenon indicated that those with siblings might have
relatively better academic performance, which could be interpreted
using the resource allocation theory that has classified two fundamental
mechanisms. The first type is reinforcement, which means that parents
are likely to invest in the child who shows good potential among their
siblings (Abufhele, Behrman & Bravo, 2017). Accordingly, many studies
have found that parents tended to measure children’s academic ability
and economic potential as the gist of making key decisions, including
which children should receive formal schooling (Akresh, Bagby, de
Walque & Kazianga, 2012; Ayalew, 2005; Quadlin, 2015). And some
parents even change their investments when their children’s academic
ability declines (Quadlin, 2015). The second type is compensation,
which means compensatory parents tend to invest in low-performance
children (Hertwig, Davis & Sulloway, 2002; Quadlin, 2015). Therefore, children who were not good at studying or with poor health were
found to get more attention, time, care, and investments from their
parents (Abufhele, Behrman & Bravo, 2017; Hertwig, Davis & Sulloway,
2002; Quadlin, 2015). And these parents might actively intervene in
their children’s studies to prevent future failures (Quadlin, 2015). By the
above ways, the non-only children might gain more educational resources from family than only children. Moreover, siblings do not
merely dilute parental resources; they are resources for each other
(Blake, 1981). All the above factors could result in even more abundant
education resources, thereby causing no disadvantages in the academic
achievement of bigger family size, which is more common in rural
families.
Nevertheless, the existing researches have some shortages. On the
one side, the existing studies have examined the effect of the only-child
family on academic achievement in urban adolescents; very few studies
have explored the only-child family from rural areas, leaving the onlychild families in rural areas unexplored. On the other side, some
scholars have also found that children’s performance might not necessarily determine parental investment; instead, some parents might
provide maximum resources by their socioeconomic status (SES) and
other factors (Hertwig, Davis & Sulloway, 2002; Perna, 2006; Quadlin,
2015). Therefore, to understand the role of family size in influencing
academic performance more clearly, our study will explore the differences and influencing factors of academic achievement between onlychild families and multi-child families; moreover, the family SES effect
will be discussed and examined in the following section.

2.2. Family size, resource allocation, and academic performance
Researchers have long reflected on the role of family size in influencing the academic performance of children. Dating back to 1960,
Becker formalized the “Quantity-Quality” model, in which he assumed
that family educational investments might be correlated to children’s
quality, and the results confirmed that parents would make a trade-off
between the number and quality of their offspring (Becker, 1960;
Becker & Lewis, 1973; Cáceres-Delpiano, 2006; Kessler, 1991). Since
then, more and more researchers have devoted themselves to family size
and children’s academic performance, but the results had some differences. Many studies have found that family size or the number of siblings could negatively predict children’s academic performance (Blake,
1989; Ma, 2001; Mark, 2006; Tanskanen, Erola & Kallio, 2016), the only
children performed better than those with siblings (Falbo & Poston,
1993; Liu, Lin & Chen, 2010), which means the more siblings, the poorer
academic achievement (Ma, 2001; Mark, 2006). One explanation for this
phenomenon is the resource dilution hypothesis. A small family could
invest a higher level of human capital investment for fewer children,
which results in higher monetary and non-monetary investment in
educational resources for children, especially in urban families (Becker
& Lewis, 1973). Thus, the children from a smaller family size or fewer
siblings could get more educational resources, which further contribute
to higher academic performance (Blake, 1989; Downey, 1995). This
phenomenon is more common in urban families.

2.3. The predictors of academic performance
Many factors can contribute to children’s academic performance,
and the existing studies have confirmed that they could be classified into
individual effects, family effects, and (pre-) school effects (Wang &
Wang, 2006). First, individual factors have an impact on academic
performance. For instance, gender could influence children’s academic
achievement, which might result in gender stereotypes, especially in
math, female students are more likely to be perceived lower ability in
mathematics and reasoning (Cejka & Eagly 1999; Davies et al. 2002),
which could undermine girls’ math achievement (Huguet & Régner
2007, 2009). Moreover, Zhang, Tian, and Li (2012) found that students’
academic performance could be positively predicted by not only gender
but also homework time, singleton status, studying pressure, and selfeducational expectation. Extracurricular tutoring, however, had no
significant influence on students’ academic performance: students who
participated more in extracurricular tutoring might not get good grades.
In contrast, helping students develop good studying habits could
improve a student’s academic performance. Reading is particularly an
important studying habit, which could improve students’ reading literacy and improve other subjects’ performance. Besides, students’ leisure

reading time was a benefit to improve their self-learning ability, learning
efficiency, and comprehensive quality (Cai, Bao & Zuo, 2011). And
leisure reading time was a key predictor of children’s academic performance, especially for reading ability (Cupp, Dyer, Morrison, & Tracz,
1990; Smith, 1990). Recently, scholars have found that equipped nearsighted students with appropriate glasses could have better academic
performance (Glewwe, Park, & Zhao, 2016; Ma et al., 2014; Yi et al.,
2015). The positive correlation between academic performance and
myopia might result from long-term study and homework burden (Qiu
et al., 2017). However, the causal relationship between myopia and
academic achievement is not yet confirmed; this study will explore this.
Second, family factors such as socioeconomic status (SES) and parents’ academic expectation (PAE) were identified the significant contributors to math, reading, and science performance (Li, 2018; Perry &
Mcconey, 2010; Pinquart & Ebeling, 2019; White, 1982). For instance,
Li (2018) found that family SES had a positive effect on academic performance: the higher the family SES, the higher their children’s academic performance. In particular, the parents who were good at
management and highly involved in children’s education could effectively improve their academic performance (Li, 2018). In contrast, the
family SES effect on rural students was primarily reflected in the
household learning environment. Wang (2015) found that low SES was
usually associated with a poor and inappropriate home learning environment in rural China, and some families even had no study desk.
Therefore, to explore the influences of SES and home learning environment, we adopted SES and desk ownership as the indicator of family
factors and controlled their effects in the regression analyses. In addition, a recent study on the ethnic Zhuang adolescents in China (Li et al.,
2020) found that that parental educational expectation (PEE) and selfeducational expectation (SEE) acted as the serial mediators between
the Singleton status and their science literacy. This finding indicated a
significant mediating role of parental expectation and implied that the
Expectancy Value Model (Eccles, 1993) might be cross-cultural and
universal. Accordingly, this study will also examine the impact of
parental expectation on rural adolescents’ academic performance.
Third, school factors also have an impact on children’s academic
performance. Class interaction and emotional interaction between
teachers and students and the existence of motivated peers positively
predicted students’ academic performance (Li, 2018). In addition, students might also be influenced by school SES and resources. School SES
positively predicted the poor students’ academic performance, and poor
students could benefit from classmates with better family backgrounds
(Zhang & Hu, 2019). Adequate school resources were conducive to
students’ development, as students with low family SES could get better
development in schools with sufficient resources. This is because schools
with sufficient resources could have more opportunities to hold activities, which would enhance students’ belonging and identity and make
them more concentrated in studying, thus paying a way to improve
students’ academic performance (Zhang & Hu, 2019). Preschooling
experience has also been confirmed as a key contributor to later math
and reading attainment (Geary, 1994; Starkey, Klein, & Wakeley 2004).
Specifically, in 2003, UNESCO (United Nations Educational, Scientific
and Cultural Organization) reported that students who received preschool education outperformed their peers with no preschooling experience in academic achievement by 26% in Nepal. Jia, Li, and Wang
(2018) found that preschooling experience would improve adolescents’
cognitive competence, academic performance, and social skills in junior
secondary school. Therefore, the preschooling experience has a longterm impact on adolescents’ academic performance.
To sum up, plenty of studies have explored one or two types of factors; very few have focused on individual effects, family effects, and
(pre-) school effects at the same time. Many factors could contribute to
children’s academic performance, so evaluating one or two kinds of
influential factors might be biased. Moreover, few existing studies have
selected rural only children as a sample; the influential factors of rural
only children’s academic development are still unknown. Therefore, it is

necessary to choose rural only children as a sample and comprehensively explore the influential factors of academic achievement.
2.4. The context of this study
Most of the existing studies have examined the effect of singleton
status on academic achievement in urban adolescents; very few studies
have explored only children from rural areas. Along with the national
one-child policy, the “One-Son-Two-Kids” rule has allowed rural couples
to have a second child if the first child was a girl, due to the specific
cultural values of the rural population who were more willing to have a
son in their families (Qian, 2009). Although very few rural families have
chosen to have only one child for affordability (high cost of childcare),
the population of only children in rural areas was still trivial. Consequently, the major difficulty of comparing the sibling effect in rural
China is that it is rare to find a one-child family. Therefore, this study
might be the first to investigate, retrospectively, the long-term effects of
the only child policy on rural students’ academic achievement. However, limited by this retrospective approach, we might not be able to
evaluate all the influential factors thus had to focus on (1) individual
factors (gender, age, singleton status, leisure reading time, and myopia);
(2) family factors such (SES, PAE, desk ownership); and (3) preschool
factors (kindergarten level). In particular, the following research questions guided this study:
(1) Are there any differences in SES, sight, and preschooling experience between only children and non-only children in rural areas
in China?
(2) Are there any differences in academic achievement between only
children and non-only children in rural areas?
(3) What are the contributors to only children and non-only children’s academic performance in rural areas?
3. Method
3.1. Participants
This research was part of a large-scale research project sponsored
and funded by the education authorities of City A in Guangxi province.
This city is in southwestern China and consists of four districts and two
counties. In China, urban areas refer to “municipalities, cities, and towns
established by the state following the administrative system“ (Article 3,
Law of the People’s Republic of China on Urban and Rural Planning
China, 2007). In 2018, the registered population of City A was 4.1537
million, the urban population was 747,500, accounting for 18.0% of the
total population. The rural population was 3.4062 million, accounting
for 82% of the total population. The per capita disposable income of
Chinese rural residents was 14,617 yuan, the rural Engel coefficient was
30.1% in 2018. As for City A, rural residents’ per capita disposable income was 12,816 yuan, and the rural Engel coefficient was 38.45%. The
rural poverty rate in Guangxi Province was 5.7%. The main agricultural
products are corn, wheat, sugarcane, litchi, pineapple, orange, mango,
and tea, and local farmers primarily live on planting. City A has 106
junior secondary schools altogether, and 102 of them are in rural areas.
The parental degrees of the only child and the non-only child groups
were mostly junior secondary school graduates. The one-child family is
not a common family size; it is rare. Therefore, we chose Chinese students as our participants since the one-child policy provided a unique
source of one-child families, which was convenient to explore the sibling
effect in rural children’s academic achievement.
The first author got the contract through the official bidding system
in China and was authorized by the Department of Education to conduct
this project in the 106 schools during 2018. Before data collection, all
the participants were informed and agreed to participate in the study
before completing all the tests and surveys. We used Mean Completer to
analyze missing data and employed IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 to

3.4. Procedure

First, the first author’s University and the Department of Education
of the research site reviewed and approved the study proposal before
data collection. Second, before data collection, consent forms were obtained from the educational directors and the principals of the participating schools. Third, students were briefed about the survey by a
trained graduate researcher and consented to participate in this study.
Fourth, the students from the same class were assembled in the same
room where they completed the paper survey. Finally, the participating
schools conducted the PISA test as a term exam. In particular, this study
was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee, Faculty of Education, Southwest University. The ethics approval date was July 21st,
2018. The IRB approver was Dequan Zhu, Muhua Wang, and Zhendong
Sun. The IRB number was QZZC2018-D3-0008.
3.5. Data analysis
First, a set of ANOVAs and Chi-square tests was conducted to
examine the effect of singleton status on Age, Sight, SES, PAE, KG level,
Reading Time, Desk Ownership, Math, Reading, and Science. Second,
Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to investigate the associations among all the study variables. Third, based on the correlations
of the variables, OLS and 2SLS regression models were analyzed by Stata
SE version 15.0 (StataCorp, 2017). Finally, hierarchical regression
models were analyzed by IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (Hayes,

Second, the average cost of raising an only child might be higher than
that of raising children with siblings (Chen, 2010). The more children,
the less expense on each child, so raising an only child is not as costeffective as raising more children. Yi (2007) found that the cost of
raising three children was 1.6 times that of raising one child, whereas
three children would create three times wealth for society as one child
could do. Chen (2010) indicated that parents of a multi-child family
could use the parenting experience gained from the first child to other
kids, saving on childcare costs. And their children could share books,
and the elder children could tutor the younger ones, reducing the cost
and providing advantages in improving academic achievement (Qian,
2009). Therefore, families with more children could spend more money
on their children’s education by raising them economically. In contrast,
the higher cost of raising an only child and the poverty might have
caused the lower parental investment in education.
Third, this result might also be associated with family resource
allocation. For example, in rural China, a family with more children
usually focuses on all investments on a child who could get the highest
academic achievement (Su & Ding, 2007). Accordingly, their brothers
and sisters might drop out of school after completing the nine-year
compulsory education to make money to support their families (Su &
Ding, 2007). Thus, the most promising child in such a multi-child family
might have more educational resources than the only child with the
same level of family SES in rural areas. This interpretation, however,
needs to be verified through the following studies that adopt qualitative
approaches, such as in-depth case studies in rural China.

4.5. Moderation and MANOVA results
To explore the moderating factors between singleton status and academic achievement, we have conducted PROCESS but found no significant models. Therefore, to verify the interaction effects of gender,
singleton status and income, we conducted MANOVA on PISA Reading,
Math, and Science scores with Singleton status (2) × Gender (2) × Income (8) as the independent variables. The result of Box’s Test of
Equality of Covariance Matrices (p > .05) showed that our sample could
satisfy the null hypothesis as the observed covariance matrices of the
dependent variables were equal across groups. However, the MANOVA
results did not indicate any significant main effects of Gender (Wilks’
Lambda = 0.993) on math (F (1, 310) = 0.134; p > .05), reading (F (1, 310)
= 1.182; p > .05), and science (F (1, 310) = 0.992; p > .05). And the
MANOVA findings did not show significant main effects of Singleton
status (Wilks’ Lambda = 0.999) on math (F (1, 310) = 0.083; p > .05),
reading (F (1, 310) = 0.170; p > .05) and science (F (1, 310) = 0.000027; p
> .05). Nevertheless, the effects of Income (Wilks’ Lambda = 0.900) on
math (F (1, 310) = 2.613; p < .05) and reading (F (1, 310) = 2.075; p < .05)
were found significant.
5. Discussion
This study found significant differences in academic achievement
between the only child and non-only child groups in rural China. In
particular, we were interested in understanding what variables
contributed significantly to the between-group differences in academic
achievement. The predicting and confounding variables at child, family,
and preschool levels have been identified in the hierarchical regression
analyses. This section will discuss the major findings and their implications for policy development.
5.1. Why only children are not always better
This study found that only children had significantly lower academic
achievement than the non-only peers in rural China. In addition, we
found that more only children had myopia than those with siblings who
tended to have a higher level of PAE, desk ownership, and PAE. These
findings jointly indicated that the only children in rural China were
suffering from a relatively disadvantaged situation caused by inadequate educational resources and low SES. This conclusion is in line with
other studies on the rural only children in China (Fong, 2007; Poston &
Falbo, 1990; Qian, 2009). Accordingly, a challenging question has
emerged: Why did the only children in rural China perform worse than
their peers with siblings?
First, this might be caused by the Chinese parental preference of sons
and indulgent parenting, which were commonly observed in rural onlychild families. For instance, in this study, 65.4% of the only children
were boys, whereas 34.6% were girls. This unbalanced boy-to-girl ratio
has also been reported in other studies (Song, 2006; Xiao & Feng, 2010a,

rural children’s educational disadvantage and eventually manifest in

This finding is consistent with the previous studies (Huang & Chen,
2014; Ma et al., 2014; Yi et al., 2015). In China, the ‘demonstration

more resources and financial support, thus having more educational

2014). Therefore, as the indicator of early childhood quality, the
kindergarten ranking level might impact rural children’s academic
performance.
Second, this study also found Parental Academic Expectation (PAE)
as a significant predictor of the total sample, only child and non-only
child groups in math, reading, and science scores. This finding is also
consistent with the existing studies (Duta, 1988; Lin, Fong, & Wang,
2017; Li, Shi, Wu, & Li, 2020). The key role of PAE in academic
achievement could be explained with the Pygmalion effect (Li, Shi, Wu,
& Li, 2020), which means that others’ expectations of a target person
will affect their performance. Similarly, parents of the only children
tended to have a lower expectation of the little emperor’s academic
achievement, thus setting a lower achievement level and eventually
getting a lower academically performing child (Eccles, 1993). Coincidently, a recent study on the ethnic Zhuang adolescents in China (Li
et al., 2020) has also found that that parental educational expectation
played a significant mediating role between the singleton status and
academic performance. These findings have jointly indicated that
parental expectation might be a significant indicator of Chinese adolescents’ academic performance.
Third, this study found that eyesight was a significant predictor of
the total sample’s math, reading, and science. This finding is also
consistent with the previous studies (Glewwe, Park, & Zhao, 2016; Ma
et al., 2014; Yi et al., 2015). In particular, the results showed that only
children were severely shortsighted than their peers with siblings, and
myopia could negatively predict academic performance. This means that
if shortsighted students did not receive optical correction, myopia would
negatively impact students’ academic performance and learning efficiency (Glewwe, Park, & Zhao, 2016; Yi et al., 2015). In rural China, it
should be noted that many nearsighted children did not wear glasses due
to their low affordability caused by a financial problem in their family
(Yi et al., 2015). Besides, in rural areas, vision monitoring and vision
care services were absent, the number of professional doctors was small,
and the equipment quality was poor. Therefore, the rural parents who
were busy with farm work failed to take their children to get correct
glasses in time, becoming another difficulty for the rural children to
solve the myopia problem (Li, 2010). All these problems have jointly
and negatively influenced the academic performance of only children in
rural China.

5.2. The differences in predictors of academic performance for rural
adolescents
First, this study found that gender was the negative predictor for
math achievement of the non-only children sample, meaning girls tend
to have lower math scores. The result was consistent with the previous
studies that girls had poor math performances (McKellar, Marchand,
Diemer, & Malanchuk, 2019; Smeding, 2012). One explanation of the
above phenomenon is gender stereotypes; female students are more
likely to be perceived as relatively weak in mathematics and reasoning
(Cejka & Eagly 1999; Davies et al. 2002), which could undermine girls’
math achievement (Huguet & Régner 2007, 2009). Accordingly, many
studies have found this kind of gender stereotype has the potential to
undermine girls’ and women’s self-perceptions of ability, performance,
and interest in ‘masculine’ discipline, especially in STEM disciplines
(Gunderson, Ramirez, Levine, & Beilock, 2011; McKellar et al., 2019;
Smeding, 2012). The above math gender stereotype also exists in China
(Wang, 2020; Xi et al., 2020). Some research evidence has shown that
the math gender stereotype impaired Chinese girls’ self-confidence and
performance in math (Wang, 2020; Xi et al., 2020). All these findings
have jointly indicated that gender stereotypes might cause the negative
impact of gender on rural non-only children’s math performance.
Second, this study found leisure reading time a significant predictor
of non-only child groups in reading and science scores. Furthermore, this
study found that SES was the positive predictor for math and science
achievement of the only children group but found no significant effect in
the non-only children group. These findings could be addressed by
bioecological theory (Bronfenbrenner, 1989). Family members’ contexts
are embedded within the microsystem, thus placing children in a unique
position to prominently influence their siblings (Bronfenbrenner, 1989;
Wang, Degol, & Amemiya, 2019). Interactions in the microsystem
encompass role models within the child’s daily ecosystem; therefore,
this kind of interaction is considered the most salient process mechanism
for influencing an individual (Wang, Degol, & Amemiya, 2019). Ample
research evidence has shown that siblings could improve children’s
academic achievement by providing educational support and building a
positive learning environment (Bouchey, Shoulberg, Jodl, & Eccles,
2010; Smith, 1993; Wang, Degol, & Amemiya, 2019). On the one side,
for instance, older siblings could teach younger siblings, promoting the
intellectual development of the older sibling (Bouchey, Shoulberg, Jodl,
& Eccles, 2010; Smith, 1993; Wang, Degol, & Amemiya, 2019). However, in the one-child family, as only children lack younger siblings, they
don’t have the opportunity to teach younger siblings, thus creating an
intellectual-development handicap for only children (Smith, 1993).
Teaching between siblings would overcome the disadvantage caused by
the reduced educational monetary and non-monetary investment in
multiple child families (Smith, 1993). Eventually resulted in SES has no
significant impact on rural non-only children’s academic performance.
On the other side, leisure reading could effectively improve non-only
children’s academic achievement, nearly consistent with the previous
studies (Mol & Jolles, 2014; Sauerwein, Theis, & Fischer, 2016; Smith,
1990, 1992). Elucidation of the above result could be having siblings
(especially older siblings) who enjoy leisure reading may provide a
powerful role model for other siblings to identify with and emulate, thus
paving a way to build up a positive learning environment more conductively in multiple child families (Bouchey, Shoulberg, Jodl, & Eccles,
2010). Eventually resulted in leisure reading time has a significant
impact on rural non-only children’s academic performance.

6. Conclusions, limitations, and implications
This study was dedicated to exploring the differences in academic
performance between the only children and their peers with siblings in
rural China. First, we found that the only children in rural areas had
significantly weaker academic performance and worse eyesight than
their peers with siblings. Second, we found that the non-only children
tended to have a higher level of parental academic expectation and desk
ownership, indicating a more advantaged situation than those only
children. Third, academic performance varied over different levels of
Kindergarten level, Parental Achievement Expectation, and Sight. Last
but not least, we found that singleton status could predict math but
could not predict reading and science in rural adolescents.
This study, however, has some limitations. First, this cross-sectional
study did not collect longitudinal data, which might not soundly confirm
the cause-effect relationship between the singleton status and children’s
academic performance. Longitudinal and large-scale studies should be

be enhanced. The local government should provide free monitoring
services and vision care services. The Sanitary bureau should allot more
