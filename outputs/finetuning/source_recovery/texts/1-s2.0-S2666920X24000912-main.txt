A B S T R A C T
The rise of generative artificial intelligence (AI) has sparked debate in education about whether to ban AI tools 
for assessments. This study explores the adoption and impact of AI tools on an undergraduate research proposal 
assignment using a mixed-methods approach. From a sample of 187 students, 69 completed a survey, with 46 
(67%) reporting the use of AI tools. AI-using students were significantly more likely to be higher-performing, 
with a pre-semester average GPA of 5.46 compared to 4.92 for non-users (7-point scale, p = .025). Most stu­
dents used AI assistance for the highest-weighted components of the task, such as the research topic and methods 
section, using AI primarily for generating research ideas and gathering feedback. Regression analysis suggests 
that there was no statistically significant effect of AI use on student performance in the task, with the preferred 
regression specification estimating an effect size of less than 1 mark out of 100. The qualitative analysis identified 
six main themes of AI usage: idea generation, writing assistance, literature search, grammar checking, statistical 
analysis, and overall learning impact. These findings indicate that while AI tools are widely adopted, their impact 
on academic performance is neutral, suggesting a potential for integration into educational practices without 
compromising academic integrity.
1. Introduction
Since the release of OpenAI’s ChatGPT in November 2022, genera­
tive artificial intelligence (AI) has disrupted the education landscape, 
sparking concerns among educators and administrators about academic 
integrity and student learning. Surveys indicate that one in three uni­
versity students has used AI to cheat on assignments, and most report 
being tempted to do so (Sullivan et al., 2023). Concerns over the po­
tential for AI to replace traditional methods of contract cheating, issues 
of equity concerning access to and utilization of AI, and fears regarding 
student disengagement from learning have spurred intensive debates 
(Cotton et al., 2023).
These concerns have prompted some educational institutions to ban 
AI tools, decrying them as a ‘plague on education’ (Sullivan et al., 2023). 
However, enforcing the bans has proved challenging due to ineffective 
AI detection tools (Khalil & Er, 2023). Given the exponential quality 
improvements with each generation of AI models, some scholars have 
predicted the end of essays as an assessment method (Rudolph et al., 
2023; Yeadon et al., 2023). A more optimistic perspective is that, rather 
than replacing student efforts, tools like ChatGPT can have pedagogical 
applications and enhance student learning (Cotton et al., 2023). If 
generative AI integrates into everyday practice as calculators and 
computers have done, educators may prefer to encourage students to 
harness these tools rather than ban them outright. Within this perspec­
tive, Rudolph et al. (2023) argue that the perceived threat to the 
traditional essay presents an opportunity for innovative assessment 
practices.
This study aims to contribute to this ongoing discourse by examining 
the prevalence and impact of AI tool usage in educational assessments, 
shedding light on AI’s implications for the student experience. It em­
ploys quantitative and qualitative analyses of adoption, motivation, and 
performance in an essay-based assessment in which students were given 
free rein to use AI assistance in any manner and to any extent they 
wished. The issues of AI adoption and impact are especially important 
for AI technologies because, despite the growing concerns surrounding 
AI use and academic integrity, little is currently known about the pref­
erences and choices of students. Given the exponential rise in the 
popularity of and access to generative AI tools like ChatGPT, there is a 
pressing need to uncover evidence about how students actually behave 
when given permission to use AI in their assessments, as well as the 
corresponding effects on performance (Playfoot et al., 2023).

technology on performance. Within the first strand of literature, several 
models have been advanced to explain and predict user acceptance of 
educational technology, with the most widespread being the Technology 
Acceptance Model (TAM). Alternative models include: Innovation 
Diffusion Theory (IDT), the earliest model, which focuses on the char­
acteristics of innovations; Motivational Model (MM), which focuses on 
intrinsic and extrinsic psychological motivations in technology adop­
tion; and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology 
(UTAUT), which integrates elements from several previous models. A 
recent review by Grani´c (2022) found that TAM and its subsequent re­
finements (e.g. Benbasat & Barki, 2007; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000) 
remain the most commonly employed among education researchers. The 
various models in this literature have a shared goal of evaluating and 
assessing new information and communication technology (ICT) prod­
ucts and services. Perceptions of usefulness, ease of use, and enjoyment 
of the ICT generally increase student adoption (Grani´c, 2022), as do 
self-efficacy – a student’s confidence in their own study skills and abil­
ities (Hatlevik et al., 2018) – and positive attitudes of peers towards the 
technology (Cheung & Vogel, 2013; Song & Kong, 2017). Less is known 
about which types of students are more likely to adopt ICT in terms of 
demographic characteristics or academic ability. Some characteristics 
have been shown to act as moderators; for example, the perceived use­
fulness of new technology has a stronger effect on adoption for males 
and younger users (Blut et al., 2022), while females and ethnic and/or 
language minorities are more likely to exhibit lower self-efficacy in their 
use of technology (Tømte & Hatlevik, 2011; Volman et al., 2005; Zhou & 
Xu, 2007).
A related body of literature focuses on understanding the effect of 
ICT on student learning, typically measured by academic performance. 
This has been the subject of a long-standing debate among scholars, such 
as the seminal (and opposing) viewpoints put forward by Clark (1983)
and Kozma (1994). Despite a large number of studies, there remains a 
surprising lack of consensus about the link between the educational 
technology use and student learning (Basri et al., 2018; Bulman & 
Fairlie, 2016; Petko et al., 2017). Most quantitative evaluations of ICT 
have not directly tested student learning but have instead relied on ac­
ademic performance, such as data from large-scale international as­
sessments like the Trends in International Mathematics and Science 
Study (De Witte & Rogge, 2014) and the Program for International 
Student Assessment (Skryabin et al., 2015; Spiezia, 2011). There is also 
some evidence that ICT adoption may have a larger effect on perfor­
mance for females and for students with high grade point averages 
(GPAs) prior to adoption (Basri et al., 2018).
Due to the recent availability of freely accessible AI chatbots, only a 
handful of studies to date have looked at student adoption of AI tools. In 
the only study so far to investigate which types of undergraduate stu­
dents adopt (non-permitted) AI tools, Playfoot et al. (2023) examined 
the relationship between degree apathy and the willingness of students 
to use AI tools for assignment writing. Their study revealed that students 
with higher levels of degree apathy were more inclined to misuse AI 
tools, highlighting a significant risk factor for academic misconduct. 
However, there were no differences between AI users and non-users with 
regard to self-efficacy, personality traits such as conscientiousness, 
agreeableness, and narcissism, or actual academic performance. Lai 
et al. (2023) explored the role of intrinsic motivation in the adoption of 
ChatGPT to support active learning for undergraduate students in Hong 
Kong. Their study used a TAM framework, finding that intrinsic moti­
vation was a strong predictor of students’ intentions to use ChatGPT. In 
contrast to TAM’s implications, the perceived ease of use of AI did not 
have a significant impact on student adoption. Finally, Bernabei et al. 
(2023) investigate technology adoption of large language models for 
engineering education. In a qualitative case study of an essay assessment 
in which students were required to use ChatGPT, the authors report that 
the generated submissions were of reasonably high quality, but 
concluded that the use of AI had limited effects on overall student 
performance.
2.1. The purpose of the study
The present study makes several contributions to this small but 
growing body of literature. Firstly, it employs both quantitative and 
qualitative analyses to provide a comprehensive understanding of AI 
adoption, motivation, and experience in an academic setting. Secondly, 
to our knowledge, this study is among the earliest attempts to quanti­
tatively estimate the effect of using AI on student performance in an 
assessment task across a range of regression models and subgroups. 
Understanding whether permitting AI for assessment advantages or 
disadvantages certain types of students, and for what criteria, offers 
valuable insights for educators and policymakers. Thirdly, by investi­
gating the characteristics of students who use AI tools and the extent of 
their usage, this study provides insights into the demographic and aca­
demic factors that influence AI adoption, which is less explored in the 
current literature. Finally, in addition to the quantitative analysis, this 
study conducts a thematic analysis of open-ended student responses to 
gain deeper insights into their experiences and perceptions of using AI 
tools. This qualitative approach adds a rich layer of understanding to the 
quantitative findings and highlights the nuanced and diverse ways in 
which students interact with, benefit from, and perceive the challenges 
of integrating AI tools into their academic work.
Given the exponential rise in the popularity of and access to gener­
ative AI tools like ChatGPT, as well as the continually rapid rate of AI 
technological improvements, questions about which types of students 
adopt these tools, how they are used, and what effect they have on 
measured academic performance are arguably much more relevant than 
specific aspects of the technology itself. Drawing on TAM as a theoretical 
foundation, this study aims to investigate key aspects of student 
engagement with AI tools in academic assessments. A central assump­
tion of TAM is that user adoption hinges on perceived ease of use and 
usefulness (Grani´c & Maranguni´c, 2019; Salloum et al., 2019). The 
model’s adaptability, enhanced through various extensions, allows for a 
nuanced understanding of technology adoption behaviours. In the 
context of AI tools in education, TAM implies varied adoption levels 
among students based on their perceived usefulness of the technology. 
This study, therefore, explores whether students with different academic 
abilities or demographic characteristics demonstrate varying pro­
pensities to adopt AI assistance, as well as the extent of their reliance on 
these tools. In addition to uncovering patterns in AI tool adoption and 
usage, this research explores the academic outcomes of these behav­
iours, contributing to the long-standing debate on the effect of ICT on 
student learning.
To summarise, the current study aims to answer the following 
research questions.
1. What are the characteristics of students who use permitted AI tools in 
essay-based assessment?
2. How and to what extent do students use these tools?
3. Does using AI increase student performance?
3. Method
3.1. Participants
Participants were recruited in May–June 2023 from a second-year 
undergraduate economics class (187 students) at an Australian univer­
sity. The class teaching schedule involved a 2-h lecture and a 1-h tutorial 
each week for 13 weeks. Lectures and tutorials were predominantly 
interactive, with a focus on active learning and ‘flipped’ classroom ac­
tivities. Many of these activities required students to generate, compare 
and critique the output of ChatGPT against their own answers and 
against the lecturer’s answers for various exercises. Throughout the 
semester, students were also shown examples of how AI tools could be 
used for academic research, as well as examples of where AI suggestions 
for research were unreliable or incorrect.

3.2. Experiment procedure
The final assessment item for the class was to write a research pro­
posal, for which students were permitted to use AI tools to assist them. 
The research proposal was worth 40% of the final class grade. Students 
were required to follow a fixed essay structure and not to exceed a limit 
of 1500 words. The marking criteria and respective weightings were: 
Research methods (40%), Presentation (20%), Literature and economic 
concepts (20%), Research topic (10%), and Implications and limitations 
(10%). The full assessment instructions and marking criteria are 
included in Appendix A of the Supplementary Material. Students were 
explicitly told that they could freely use AI tools like ChatGPT for any or 
all components of their research proposal and that they would not be 
marked down or flagged for academic dishonesty in any way if they 
chose to use these tools. In addition to this specific guidance, all students 
were required at the start of the semester to complete a mandatory, 
university-wide integrity module that specified, among other things, 
that lecturers would communicate whether or not AI tools are permitted 
for each assessment item.
3.3. Data collection
Following the marking of the research proposals, students were 
invited to complete a short survey about their use of AI tools in the 
assessment task. The survey was coded in Qualtrics (see Appendix B of 
the Supplementary Material). Participating students were first asked 
whether they used AI tools at all for the task. If yes, they were then asked 
to indicate for which components of the task they had used AI assistance. 
If no, students were asked to indicate their reasons. The survey data 
were then merged with administrative data for each student, including 
demographic variables, their final mark, their marks on the class’s 
previous assessment items, and their academic performance prior to that 
semester (that is, prior to the launch of ChatGPT).
In total, 69 students (out of 187 in the class) completed the survey, a 
response rate of 37%. Survey respondents were higher-performing on 
average than non-respondents, with a mean GPA of 5.28 vs 4.81 on a 7- 
point scale (t = 3.2394,df = 185,p = .001), and were more likely to be 
enrolled full-time (t = 2.1548,df = 185,p = .032). This suggests that it 
is important to include these control variables in the regression analysis 
to correctly interpret the estimated effect of AI use. There were no sig­
nificant differences in terms of age, gender, language, and the type or 
mode of study (Table S1 in the Supplementary Material). Of the re­
spondents, 24 students provided additional, substantive open-ended 
comments, the data for which are qualitatively analysed in section 
4.4. These students had slightly higher GPAs than the remaining survey 
respondents (difference = 0.625, p = .01), but were otherwise statisti­
cally similar in terms of the final assessment mark and demographic 
characteristics.
These differences indicate that the sample of survey respondents may 
not be fully representative of the entire student population and suggest a 
need to control for these characteristics in the regression analysis to 
mitigate potential survey response bias. In the Discussion, this issue is 
discussed in the context of the interpretation of the results, with sug­
gested directions for future research to ensure a comprehensive under­
standing of AI tool usage that generalises to a wider student population.
3.4. Research instruments
To answer the first research question, the grouping variable for two- 
sample t-tests is whether or not the student reported using AI tools for 
their research proposal. Key student characteristics (age, gender, GPA, 
and study variables) were included as outcome variables based on 
availability and relevance from past studies on ICT adoption. Finally, the 
scores from an AI detection tool comprise the outcome variable for an 
auxiliary analysis contributing to a small but growing literature on the 
efficacy of such tools, which Rudolph et al. (2023) review.
To answer the second research question, we examined the extent of 
AI tool usage across for each of the eight components of the research 
essay – namely: Topic, Title, Abstract, Introduction, Literature, Method, 
Conclusion, and References – as well as the total number of AI-assisted 
components per student. Further insights about usage and experience 
are derived from the thematic analysis of open-ended responses (section 
4.4).
To answer the third research question, the primary dependent vari­
able for the regression analysis is the student’s overall assessment mark, 
with secondary dependent variables being the marks for each of five 
assessment criteria. The primary independent variable is AI usage. 
Control variables and additional sub-sample analyses used the same 
student characteristics as above. Additionally, we merged data from a 
previous cohort with the main dataset to use the current cohort as an 
instrument to measure the intention-to-treat effect of AI on perfor­
mance. We also repeated the regressions with both cohorts using student 
scores from earlier assessment items. This robustness analysis helps 
mitigate the potential confounding effect of unobserved factors.
The use of a binary measurement for AI tool usage was chosen for 
several reasons. Firstly, it provides a straightforward way to establish 
baseline adoption rates and exploratory analyses of performance im­
pacts. Secondly, binary measures facilitate comparisons across different 
sections of the research proposal, simplifying the interpretation. Finally, 
adoption of technological tools is frequently assumed to be binary in the 
education literature (Liu et al., 2020; Wakefield et al., 2018). While 
students could use AI tools to varying extents, the binary approach is a 
practical and effective starting point. In section 4.3, the results are 
repeated with a more granular measure of usage based on the number of 
components of the research proposal, and in section 4.4, a thematic 
analysis of open-ended responses provides additional insights into how 
students engage and experience collaboration with AI tools.

4.2. How do students use AI on assessment?
There was considerable variation in how and in what ways students 
used AI to write their proposals. For AI users, the median number of 
components with AI assistance was only 2 out of 8 (Fig. 1). AI was most 
often used for the two most heavily weighted components in the 
marking rubric: generating the research topic and writing the methods 
section. No student relied on AI for all assessment components. The 
maximum usage was for 6 out of 8 components, which was reported by 
two students who both reported that they did not use AI for the Liter­
ature or the References.

4.3. Does AI increase performance?
As a first test of whether AI use increased performance, linear re­
gressions were conducted with the student’s final assessment mark, 
scored on a scale from 0 to 100, as the dependent variable, and the use of 
AI as the main explanatory variable. The unconditional regression model 
revealed that students engaging with AI tools scored, on average, 5 more 
marks than their counterparts, though this difference is not statistically 
significant (Table 2). However, this is unlikely to be a causal effect 
because AI use was not randomised and may be correlated with ability 
due to survey response bias, as noted in section 3. All subsequent models 
therefore add control variables for ability. Model 2 in Table 2 adds the 
student’s GPA prior to the semester, which positively predicts the final 
assessment mark, and Model 3 adds the student’s scores in the course’s 
earlier assessments (tests with a mixture of multiple-choice and calcu­
lation questions), the most recent of which also positively predicts the 
final assessment mark. Note that both sets of ability measures are con­
structed prior to the public release of ChatGPT and are therefore plau­
sibly exogenous control variables. Models 4 and 5 include all available 
demographic control variables and differ only in how they control for 
ability. Model 4 includes both the student’s GPA at the beginning of the 
semester and their test scores for the course’s earlier assessments. 
Because the tests were conducted after the release of ChatGPT 3 (even 
though AI use was not permitted), it is possible that these variables are 
endogenous and therefore do not serve as reliable measures of ability. 
Therefore, Model 5 only includes the student’s GPA as a control for 
ability. In these two models, the main predictor of the student’s mark is 
whether they spoke English at home, which has a positive effect of 
increasing the student’s performance by roughly 11 marks (out of a total 
of 100 marks). There is no significant effect of AI use in any of the 
models on the final research proposal mark, and the coefficients in the 
models with the most controls (4 and 5) estimate the effect to be less 
than 1 mark.
The null effect on performance in the above results makes no 
distinction between students who use AI tools briefly or extensively, 
which may mask a marginal effect of increasing the use of AI. To test 
this, the analysis in Table 2 was repeated after refining the measurement 
of AI adoption. Rather than using a binary indicator of any AI use as the 
main explanatory variable, these analyses used the number of research 
essay components in which a student reported using AI assistance as the 
independent variable. The findings are qualitatively similar to those in 
Table 2: the number of components with AI assistance had no significant 
impact on student marks (Table S2 in the Supplementary Material), and 
the smaller standard errors allow a more precise inference of a null ef­
fect. Among the examined control variables, proficiency in English at 
home persisted as the strongest predictor of academic achievement.
It is possible that, while not increasing the overall mark, AI may 
improve performance on one or more of the separate research essay 
components. To test for this, the same regressions from Table 2 were run 
separately on each of the individual marking criteria. Table 3 shows that 
there are no statistically significant effects of AI use for any criterion, 
and that the estimates are consistently small in magnitude. Taken 
together, the results from the full-sample regressions indicate that there 
is no effect of using AI on the average student performance as measured 
by their assessment marks.
To test whether AI use increased academic performance for partic­
ular groups of students, the regressions were re-run after splitting the 
sample by gender, international/domestic status, English spoken at 
home, economics degree, or low-versus high-GPA (Table S3 in the 
Supplementary Material). Splitting along these demographic variables 
maintains approximately even samples; however, statistical power is 
clearly reduced. The results demonstrate that there are also no signifi­
cant effects of AI use for any of these groups.
The final set of regressions compared the performance of the whole 
class (N = 187) to that of the corresponding 2022 class (N = 203). The 
syllabus, assessments, lecturer and course materials were identical in 
both years, and ChatGPT was only available to the 2023 cohort. This 
allows for estimation of an ‘intention to treat’ effect of AI by using the 
cohort year as an instrument, which greatly increases the statistical 
power. This analysis estimates the overall effect of allowing the use of AI 
on the average student performance, whether the student adopted AI 
tools or not, and makes no assumptions about which sort of students are 
AI users. The drawback of this approach is that there may be unobserved 
factors that changed between years and that affected student perfor­
mance. To address this, we can use a cohort comparison of the scores on 
the other course assessment items (exams) as a benchmark for inter­
preting the estimated cohort effect on the performance in the research 
proposal.
A t-test shows that the 2023 cohort, who had access to ChatGPT, did 
not receive significantly higher marks for their research proposals than 
the 2022 cohort (x2022 = 66.286, x2023 = 65.818, t = 0.275, df = 388,
p = .784). However, this result may be confounded by ability: students 
in 2023 had, on average, lower GPAs prior to the semester than the 2022 
cohort (x2022 = 5.218, x2023 = 4.986, t = 2.472, df = 388, p = .014). 
Consequently, control variables for ability were included in a regression 
analysis (see Supplementary Material). There is no strong evidence that 
access to AI tools increased student performance on the research pro­
posal (Table S4 in the Supplementary Material). To address unobserved 
factors between years, the analysis was repeated for the earlier assess­
ment items to check for cohort differences in the performance on the 
tests that were “ChatGPT-proofed” (that is, the lecturer pre-tested the 
questions to ensure that ChatGPT recommended the incorrect answer 
more often than not). Again, no significant differences in performance 
between the cohorts were found (Table S4), suggesting that unobserved

4.4. Qualitative analysis of open-ended responses
The purpose of the qualitative analysis was to gain a deeper under­
standing of how students used AI tools like ChatGPT in their research 
proposals. While the quantitative data provided insights into the prev­

Overall, the qualitative insights complement the quantitative find­
ings by providing a richer understanding of how students engaged with 
AI tools. While the quantitative data showed no significant difference in 
performance between AI users and non-users, the thematic analysis re­
veals that students predominantly used AI as a supplementary tool 
rather than a replacement for their own efforts, and which might have 
saved them time. This supports the notion that access to current AI tools 
can enhance certain aspects of the learning process for some, but not all, 
students, without undermining academic integrity.
5. Discussion
This study investigated the prevalence, patterns, and implications of 
using AI tools like ChatGPT in the context of an undergraduate research 
proposal assessment. The analysis of the survey data and assessment 
scores provide several findings that contribute to our understanding of 
AI tool adoption in education and to the ongoing debate about AI and 
academic integrity. The results can be summarised in terms of adoption, 
usage, and performance.
Firstly, two-thirds of students chose to use generative AI for their 
assessment when allowed to do so. The high adoption rate suggests that 
students are open to using permitted AI in their assessment tasks, which 
is unsurprising given that many students report using AI for assessment 
even when it is not permitted (Sullivan et al., 2023). High-performing 
students were significantly more likely to utilise AI tools compared to 
low-performing students. This is consistent with the hypothesis that 
students use AI tools as an educational technology rather than as a form 
of cheating; past studies have found that students with high self-efficacy 
are more likely to adopt new educational technologies (Hatlevik et al., 
2018), while lower-achieving students are more likely to employ con­
tract cheating services such as “essay mills” (Sweeney, 2023). Of note is 
that the differences in adoption by achievement level were only evident 
among students who were not enrolled in an economics program. This 
may indicate that high-achieving students are more likely than 
low-achieving students to recognise the benefits of AI tools for tasks 
outside of their usual domain of expertise, while these differences 
disappear for tasks in which students have already acquired 
domain-specific skills that lower the advantages of AI assistance.
Secondly, AI was only partially employed by its adopters, and no 
student used it for more than 6 of the 8 assessment components. Qual­
itative results indicate that students mainly used AI tools as a ‘personal 
tutor’ to help with workshopping ideas and methodological recom­
mendations. This indicates that AI tools may be of some benefit to stu­
dents in the early stages of research proposal writing, especially 
facilitating idea generation and refining the research approach. The 
thematic analysis also suggests that some students use AI to work more 
productively, saving them time on their assessment without affecting 
performance.
Finally, the results from the regression analysis consistently show 
that students who used AI in their research proposal did no better or 
worse than those who chose not to use AI. This is a surprising outcome 
given the ongoing debates concerning the potential threat of AI to aca­
demic integrity and especially the traditional essay format. The equiv­
alence in performance between AI users and non-users is not due to 
differences in ability or other characteristics between the two groups. In 
addition, there were no performance effects on any specific criterion of 
the assessment, for any particular type of student, or from an intention- 
to-treat analysis between cohorts.
6. Conclusion
The findings contribute to the ongoing debate about the role of AI in 
education by providing empirical evidence on student adoption and the 
impact of AI tools in academic assessments. The pattern of results paints 
a hopeful picture for educators and policymakers considering the inte­
gration of AI tools into academic curricula. Most AI users adopted the 
technology to supplement their efforts rather than as a replacement. The 
qualitative analysis of open-ended responses, presented in section 4.4, 
reveals the diverse and predominantly productive ways in which stu­
dents engaged with AI, from idea generation and brainstorming to 
writing assistance and error checking. These self-reported student re­
flections concur with recent qualitative studies that similarly report that 
students generally have a favourable opinion of using AI tools 
(Aleksi´c-Masla´c et al., 2024; Forman et al., 2023; Ngo, 2023; Synekop 
et al., 2024). The students in this study who chose not to use AI per­
formed no better or worse than their peers, and there is no indication 
that specific demographics are disadvantaged by the introduction of the 
technology. There is some evidence that students who used AI found that 
it saved them time, which suggests possible efficiency gains rather than 
performance gains. A possible implication is that AI tool adoption might 
not substantially alter the interpretation of academic outcomes pre­
dicted by traditional assessment models, at least for the current level of 
available technology.
As with any case study, a potential limitation is that the results 
pertain to a single institution and a specific assessment type: a research 
proposal in behavioural economics. However, the generalisability of the 
findings is enhanced insofar as the assessment followed a typical tradi­
tional essay structure common across disciplines (see Supplementary 
Material). This study’s assessment task incorporated key design ele­
ments for the generative-AI era identified by scholars: (a) clear guide­
lines for using AI tools, (b) a fixed essay structure, and (c) worked 
examples of how to use AI, including illustrations of AI’s strengths and 
limitations (Rudolph et al., 2023). For example, while AI’s deficiency in 
academic referencing is widely known, it is less appreciated that AI tools 
are comparatively weak at genuinely original thinking (Cotton et al.,

course involved several active-learning activities in which students 
gained experience in identifying the strengths and weaknesses of 
AI-generated content, as described in section 3.1. It would be valuable 
for future studies to explore the potential moderating factors of different 
teaching and learning environments on the relationships between stu­
dent characteristics, AI use, and performance.
While the results of this study shed light on the prevalence, patterns 
and performance of students using AI tools, it is important to acknowl­
edge that grades do not fully encapsulate student learning. As referenced 
in the Introduction, a large and long-standing literature on the associ­
ation between ICT and student learning, using academic outcomes as a 
measure of learning, has produced mixed results. Interpreting the effect 
of AI technology on student learning through similar outcome measures 
may be problematic given the potential for AI tools to be used to cheat. 
Consequently, directly testing student learning effects through mixed 
methods, such as conducting interviews to explore students’ perceptions 
in greater depth, is a promising avenue for future research. The thematic 
analysis in this study takes a first step in this direction. It reveals that 
students have mixed opinions about the overall usefulness of AI tools, 
generally finding them effective as time-saving devices, but not always 
reliable as content consultants. Survey respondents were higher-ability 
students on average, which suggests caution when generalising the re­
sults of the thematic analysis to a broader student population. In 
particular, students with lower academic performance might have 
different experiences when using AI tools. To mitigate the potential 
impact of survey response bias, future research could aim for higher 
response rates and more representative samples (for instance, by 
requiring responses as part of the assessment task).
Within the theoretical frameworks for adoption of technology, such 
as TAM, future studies could investigate how pre- and post-assessment 
perceived 
usefulness, 
ease 
of 
use, 
and 
even

(Padilla-Mel´endez et al., 2013) are related to whether and to what extent 
students continue to use AI for their studies. Perhaps most relevant to the 
question of AI’s effect on learning are promising results from large 
programs in India (Banerjee et al., 2007; Muralidharan et al., 2019). 
These studies report a large, causal effect on educational gains from 
technological interventions that offer a learning approach tailored to 
each student’s ability. Coupled with the present study’s finding that 
students typically used AI as a “personalised tutor”, a possible implica­
tion is that AI tools may offer significant pedagogical benefits to students 
in under-resourced environments.
Finally, given the ever-accelerating trajectory of AI advancements, a 
natural concern is that the relevance of evidence from today’s tools 
could quickly become outdated. As AI evolves and becomes more so­
phisticated, differences in academic outcomes between students who 
use AI and those who don’t may begin to emerge. However, the message 
from this study’s results about student usage is largely optimistic: most 
students prefer to work with AI tools rather than as a medium to out­
source their work product. These findings suggest that the widespread 
concern among educators about the potential for ‘AI-cheating’ in written 
tasks may not be as warranted as the public debate would lead us to 
believe. Perhaps a more important implication of this study is that, given 
high-performers’ preference for using AI tools, educators should be alert 
to the possibility of widening academic inequalities. A possible policy 
response motivated by this study’s results is that educators could 
encourage students, particularly low-performers, to understand and 
appreciate the synergies of working with AI tools, such as refining the 
student’s original ideas and proposing methodological designs. While 
more research is undoubtedly required to ascertain the long-term effects 
of AI tools on academic performance and integrity, it is essential to 
differentiate between the fear of the unknown and empirically sup­
ported concerns. For now, at least, concerns that AI has killed the college 
essay appear exaggerated.
