Why do East Asian students do so well in mathematics?
A machine learning study
Faming Wang1 · Ronnel B. King2

· Shing On Leung1

Received: 14 October 2021 / Accepted: 25 February 2022 / Published online: 1 April 2022
© Ministry of Science and Technology, Taiwan 2022

Abstract
East Asian students have consistently performed well in mathematics compared to
their international peers. Though many researchers have attempted to explore the
factors that underpin their success, most studies have focused only on a limited set
of variables. Mathematics achievement, however, is a complex phenomenon, determined by multiple factors, and best understood when different variables across levels
of analysis are taken into account. Guided by the socio-ecological theory, the present
study attempts to understand the relative importance of individual, microsystem,
and mesosystem factors in predicting mathematics achievement. We used the Trends
in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) 2019 data provided by
21,340 eighth-grade East Asian students (Chinese Taipei, Hong Kong, Japan, Korea,
and Singapore), 1242 mathematics teachers, and 802 principals. Machine learning
(i.e., random forest regression) was used to analyze the data. Results identified 11
key variables that best predicted East Asian students’ mathematics achievement.
Among these, students’ confidence in mathematics, socioeconomic status, and
school emphasis on academic success were the top three. Theoretical and practical
implications are discussed.
Keywords machine learning · mathematics achievement · East Asia · TIMSS 2019

Why do East Asian students perform so well in mathematics? Many researchers
attributed their success to a shared Confucian heritage culture (e.g., Biggs, 1996;
Ho, 1994; Leung, 2001, 2014; Watkins & Biggs, 1996). The focus on cultural factors has resulted in a spate of research focusing on the “Asian education model” (Li,
2009; Leung, 2001, 2005, 2014; Stevenson et al., 1993; Zhu & Leung, 2011). Other
studies have revealed the role of a diverse set of variables drawn from different
levels of analysis, from personal factors such as gender (Tartre & Fennema, 1995)
and motivation (e.g., perceived competence; Prast et al., 2018) to social contextual
factors such as the role of teachers (e.g., teacher support; Yu & Singh, 2018) and
peers (bullying; Konishi et al., 2010). However, most of them have only focused on a
limited set of focal variables, with few studies simultaneously exploring the importance of multiple predictors across levels of analysis. This has led to a fractionated
understanding of the underpinnings of mathematics achievement. As mathematics
learning is a complex process shaped and reinforced by many different factors, studies that ‘cast a wider net’ might be needed to gain a more holistic perspective and
advance theoretical understanding.
Perhaps, part of the reason that researchers focus on a limited set of variables
is the widespread application of classical statistical approaches (e.g., hierarchical linear modeling [HLM]), which might not be optimal to analyze a large set of
explanatory variables due to the overfitting and multicollinearity problems (Yarkoni
& Westfall, 2017). Recent research has suggested that machine learning approaches
(e.g., random forest) can address the problems encountered by classical statistical
approaches (Breiman, 2001a) and enable researchers to gain a holistic understanding
of the factors influencing mathematics achievement.
Machine learning approaches have been productively applied to numerous topics
under psychology and education (e.g., Martínez-Abad et al., 2020; Shin & Shim,
2021). However, there remains a relative paucity of research using machine learning to explore East Asian students’ mathematics success. Hence, this study aimed
to explore the different factors associated with mathematics achievement using
machine learning and examine the relative importance of different predictors across
levels of analysis. We used the socio-ecological theory (Bronfenbrenner, 1977,
1979, 1989) as a theoretical lens to simultaneously account for the roles of individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors in predicting East Asian students’ mathematics achievement.

Literature review
TIMSS
TIMSS is a worldwide research project designed by the International Association for
the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) and takes place every four years.

The purpose of TIMSS is to monitor trends in mathematics and science achievement
and examine how myriad instructional, curricular, and resource variables influence
students’ achievement. TIMSS 2019 was the most up-to-date international largescale assessment focusing on mathematics achievement. There were 39 participating
economies, with data drawn from 250,000 students, 30,000 teachers, and 8,000 principals for the eighth-grade mathematics assessment. The publicly available, comprehensive, and policy-relevant indicators measured in TIMSS 2019 allow researchers
to explore different factors that impinge on student achievement, which could help
educators make evidence-informed decisions.
Theoretical underpinnings: Socio‑ecological framework
Previous research has emphasized that mathematics achievement is determined by
many different factors (Chen, 2014; Drent et al., 2013). It might be better understood
using Bronfenbrenner’s (1979) socio-ecological theory, which postulates that the
learning process is influenced by multiple levels of the surrounding environment.
Specifically, the socio-ecological theory includes the following layers (Allen
et al., 2018, 2021; Bronfenbrenner, 1977, 1979): (1) the center of the socio-ecological system is the individual, which includes the biological and dispositional characteristics of students; (2) the microsystem includes the immediate surroundings
that a student is embedded in and constitutes the family, peers, and teachers; (3) the
mesosystem represents the school climate, including school resources and policies;
(4) the exosystem and macrosystem pertain to the larger social system and include
political, economic, and cultural features of the society/country.
This study focused on the role of individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors as they are the closest layers to student learning and have the most direct effect
on learning outcomes. We did not include exosystem and macrosystem factors as all
the economies included in this study shared the same cultural heritage.
Due to space constraints, we only give broad brushstrokes in this section. The
definition of each individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factor and some extra
example studies are elucidated in the Supplemental Materials (see Table S1).
Individual factors
Individual factors include demographic variables (e.g., gender and age), motivation
(e.g., liking math, valuing math, and confidence in mathematics), and behaviors (e.g.,
curriculum attendance). For example, in TIMSS, the concepts of liking of, confidence
in, and valuing of mathematics are consistent with key constructs in the expectancyvalue theory, which highlights the positive effect of expectancy for success and task
values in predicting students’ achievement-related outcomes (Eccles & Wigfield,
2020). Liking mathematics (i.e., intrinsic value) refers to the enjoyment that students
obtain when learning mathematics. Valuing mathematics (i.e., utility value) reflects
the belief that mathematics is valuable and useful for achieving plans. Confidence in
mathematics (i.e., self-concept) pertains to students’ beliefs about how well they can
perform well in mathematics learning. Previous studies have indicated that students

with a high level of expectancy-value beliefs tend to exert more effort, engage in
learning more frequently, and spend more time on mathematics, hence achieving a
higher level of mathematics achievement (Lauermann et al., 2017).
Microsystem factors
Microsystem factors pertain to students’ immediate classroom and family social
contexts, including classroom context (e.g., instructional time, instructional content,
and instructional quality) and family’s socioeconomic status (SES). For example,
as Bloom stated, “all learning, whether done in school or elsewhere, requires time”
(Bloom, 1974, p. 682). Instructional time could optimize student academic achievement (Lavy, 2015). For instructional content, Schmidt et al. (2014) concluded that
content-specified topics are the foundation of mathematics learning, and students
who are familiar with these topics would achieve better. Dumay and Dupriez (2007)
discovered that the taught mathematics topics in the class are the determinants of
high achievement. In another strand of research, the positive influence of instructional quality on academic achievement has been demonstrated. For example, IndaCaro et al. (2019) found that instructional clarity (i.e., teacher’s ability to explain
content clearly to the students) could improve students’ behavioral and emotional
engagement in mathematics learning. Infantino and Little (2005) discovered that
fewer disorderly behaviors (e.g., noise and disorder) during mathematics lessons
would lead to better achievement. The TIMSS 2019 results indicated that limitations
to teaching (e.g., students lacking prerequisite knowledge or skills) significantly
affect student achievement; students in classrooms where teachers reported instruction was limited tend to have a lower level of achievement (Mullis et al., 2020).
Mesosystem factors
Mesosystem factors pertain to school-level factors and represent a combination of
variables that reflect school resources (e.g., instruction affected by mathematics
resource shortages and school SES), climate (e.g., students’ sense of school belonging, school discipline problems, safe and orderly school, and student bullying), and
school goals (e.g., school emphasis on academic success). For example, the close
associations between school-level contextual factors and academic achievement
have been discussed in school effectiveness research (Bellei et al., 2020). Sirin
(2005) found that school SES exerts a powerful influence on academic achievement
with a mean effect size of 0.60. Similarly, an increasing number of studies have
demonstrated the crucial role of school climate factors in academic achievement.
Teng (2020) found that an orderly disciplinary climate (i.e., the frequency of disruption, noise, and disorder) optimized student mathematics achievement. Wang et al.
(2012) discovered that a safe and orderly school (i.e., teachers’ perceptions of school
safety and classroom management challenges) was beneficial to student achievement. Furthermore, it was also reported that students who have a higher sense of
school belonging (i.e., student’s sense of being accepted, valued, included toward
school) and were exposed to fewer bullying behaviors tend to have a better academic achievement (Huang, 2020; Korpershoek et al., 2020). Furthermore, school

Although prior research has focused on different factors relevant to mathematics
achievement, most of them have focused only on a limited set of explanatory variables, leading to an incomplete understanding of the phenomenon. The socio-ecological model highlights that to understand student learning, one has to simultaneously
consider variables across different levels of analysis. This study attempts to address
this shortcoming based on the factors measured in TIMSS and simultaneously considers the roles of individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors.
The advantages of using machine learning
The focus on a limited set of variables in previous research may partly be rooted
in most researchers’ preferred methodological approach, which relies on classical
statistical methods. Though valuable and useful, classical statistics also have a few
key limitations. First, classical statistical approaches draw conclusions through null
hypothesis significance testing and rely on the p-value, which is overly influenced
by sampling and measurement errors (Krueger, 2001). Furthermore, classical statistical models are rarely cross-validated with out-of-sample data, which might lead
to low generalizability (i.e., overfitting). Second, many vital predictors of academic
achievement are highly correlated (e.g., self-efficacy and self-confidence; Marsh
et al., 2004); incorporating these variables in one model might lead to problems with
multicollinearity and unreliable parameter estimates.
The problems mentioned above could be addressed by machine learning
approaches (Breiman, 2001a, 2001b). First, machine learning assesses model accuracy without examining null hypothesis significance testing and p-value (Grömping,
2009). Additionally, the inherent cross-validation procedure in machine learning
could assess the generalization of estimated results to out-of-sample data, avoiding
the overfitting problem (Tsamardinos et al., 2018; Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017). Second, machine learning algorithms (e.g., random forest) could handle multicollinearity problems based on permutation procedures, where predictors randomly appear in
each sub-model (Matsuki et al., 2016). Hence, we used a machine learning approach
to answer our research questions in this study.
East Asian education contexts
East Asian students have been very successful in mathematics, and researchers
attributed their success to the Confucian heritage culture, which places a high
value on education, effortful learning, and high standards and aspirations (King
et al., 2018; King & McInerney, 2014; Leung, 2006). However, there are also some
differences in terms of individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors across East
Asian economies, which might lead to different roles of critical factors in predicting
mathematics achievement. For example, the East Asian economies differ in ability
grouping practices which pertains to grouping students into schools or classes based
on their interests and academic performance (Chmielewski, 2014). Singapore and

Hong Kong practice this to a greater extent than Korea, Japan, and Chinese Taipei
(Kaur, 2019; Kelly et al., 2020). According to the survey results of PISA, at least
79% of students in Singapore and Hong Kong attended schools that group students
by ability. By contrast, the corresponding percentages in Korea, Japan, and Chinese
Taipei are as low as 58%, 50%, and 45%, respectively (Organisation for Economic
Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2020).
There also seems to be more social segregation in terms of socioeconomic status
in Hong Kong and Singapore but less so in Korea, Japan, and Chinese Taipei (OECD,
2019). Against this backdrop, the role of various factors might function differently in
predicting students’ achievement across economies in East Asia. Hence, in this study,
we undertake an omnibus analysis that includes all the East Asian cultures in one
model and engage in a supplementary analysis for each of the economies, thereby
allowing us to detect commonalities and differences in the patterns of predictors.
The present study
Guided by the socio-ecological theory, this study aimed to explore how individual,
microsystem, and mesosystem factors predict East Asian students’ mathematics
success. Specifically, we have two purposes. The first was to compare the relative
importance of individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors in contributing to
East Asian students’ mathematics success by including multiple variables measured
in the TIMSS 2019 dataset. The second was to identify the most crucial variables
that best predicted mathematics achievement to inform educational practice. The
conceptual model is displayed in Figure 1.
Machine learning (i.e., random forest regression) was used to achieve the two
purposes. We analyzed data for all the East Asian contexts together in an omnibus
analysis and undertook a supplementary analysis for each region separately.

Methods
Sample
The present study used data from TIMSS 2019. All five East Asian economies,
including Singapore, Chinese Taipei, Korea, Japan, and Hong Kong, were selected.
The sample consisted of 21,340 eighth-grade students (i.e., Chinese Taipei=4915,
Hong Kong=3265, Japan=4446, Korea=3861, and Singapore=4853). The mean
age was 14.33 years old (SD=0.41). The gender ratio was: 49.2% for girls and
50.8% for boys. In addition, 1242 mathematics teachers (i.e., Chinese Taipei=313 ,
Hong Kong=175, Japan=209, Korea=227, and Singapore=306) and 802 principals
(i.e., Chinese Taipei=203, Hong Kong=136, Japan=142, Korea=168, and Singapore=153) were surveyed to obtain relevant class and school factors.
We focused on eighth-graders’ mathematics data for two reasons. First, eighth
grade is near the end of compulsory education. Collecting data from the eighth
grade helps assess what is happening at the end of lower secondary education

Measures
Independent variables
Three criteria guided the selection of independent variables: (1) they should be
available in the TIMSS 2019 student, teacher, and principal questionnaires; (2) they
can be mapped onto Bronfenbrenner’s socio-ecological theory; and (3) they are
significant predictors of achievement in previous studies. Finally, 22 variables were
selected as the independent variables. These variables were derived by TIMSS using
item response theory (IRT) and transformed to a reporting scale with a mean of 10 and
standard deviation of 2 (Yin & Fishbein, 2020). High levels of reliability and validity
were achieved and reported in TIMSS 2019 technical report (Yin & Fishbein, 2020).
We categorized them into individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors. Table S1
in the Supplemental Materials shows the detailed description of each variable.

Dependent variables
Mathematics achievement was used as a proxy for student mathematics ability,
which was measured by items in the domains of number, algebra, geometry, and
data and probability. The scale unit was established with a mean of 500 and a
standard deviation of 100.

Data analysis
Missing data
The proportion of missing data for all variables was small, ranging from 0 to
5.7% (i.e., school composition by socioeconomic background). The missing pattern was missing completely at random. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
multiple imputations were used to handle missing data using SPSS 26.0. This
method can improve the estimate accuracy and has been widely used in PISA
research (e.g., Chiu & Chow, 2010).
Machine learning: Random forest regression
A random forest regression model with 22 independent variables predicting
mathematics achievement was tested with the “randomForest” package in the R
program (Liaw & Wiener, 2018). Due to space constraints, more details about
the random forest approach can be found in the Supplemental Materials. The purpose of the random forest analysis was to rank the relative importance of predictors and select the set of most important variables. Several indices were used
to assess the model accuracy and variable importance (Biau & Scornet, 2016).
First, the coefficient of determination (­R2) and root mean square error (the differences between values predicted by the model and the values observed; RMSE)
were calculated to assess the degree of model fit and model accuracy. A higher
value of ­R2 and a low value of RMSE suggest that the model explains more variance in the dependent variable. Second, the variable importance was assessed
by mean decrease in accuracy (MDA). The variable is more important with the
larger MDA. Last, tenfold cross-validation with five repeats was applied to select
the most crucial set of variables. This procedure evaluates the model performance
by sequentially reducing the number of predictors in the model and calculating
the mean decreased mean square error. The result of the tenfold cross-validation
with five repeats is typically estimated using the out-of-bag mean squared error
(MSE). For critical predictors, the MSE should drop substantially.
Previous studies indicated that single-level random forest produces similar
results to multilevel (or mixed effect) random forest algorithm and is also capable
of handling hierarchical data (Mangino & Finch, 2021). Hence, this study used
the single-level (or fixed effect) random forest algorithm, though we acknowledge

For the whole East Asian samples, we developed a random forest regression model
with all 22 independent variables. Figure 2 displays the relative importance of each

Supplementary analysis: random forest regression for each of the five economies
We repeatedly conducted the random forest regression for each of the participating economies. The top 11 factors within each country were selected with random
forest regression. The rank of these factors is displayed in the last five columns of
Table 3. The results suggested that some factors were important in all economies,
such as students’ confidence in mathematics and school emphasis on academic success. However, some divergences also emerged. For example, the safe and orderly
school was a key factor in Hong Kong and Singapore but not in Chinese Taipei,
Japan, and Singapore.

Discussion
The overall aim of this study was to examine how individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors contribute to East Asian students’ mathematics success in TIMSS
2019. Capitalizing on the advantages of machine learning, the relative importance
of 22 socio-ecological factors was assessed simultaneously. The most crucial 11 factors that facilitated East Asian students’ mathematics were identified. Aside from
the key factors of mathematics achievement for the whole East Asian students, we
also revealed the divergences across East Asian economies.
Relative importance of different predictors
Our findings contributed to previous research by providing a comprehensive picture
about how socio-ecological factors impinge on East Asian students’ mathematics
achievement by examining a wider range of variables. Our study indicated that the
relative importance of variables in contributing to East Asian students’ mathematics
success differed. Compared with previous evidence that “everything seems to work”
(Hattie, 2009, p. 1), this study extends previous research by showing that some predictors might play a more important role than others. Although some studies synthesized meta-analytical research and compared the relative importance of variables in
predicting academic achievement (e.g., Hattie, 2009), these studies drew data from
different datasets and did not specifically focus on the domain of mathematics. The
rigorous analytical approach, nationally representative sample, and high-quality variables measured in TIMSS guaranteed the present findings’ robustness, providing
empirical evidence on which factors are more important for East Asian students’
mathematics success. Moreover, 11 most important variables were identified from
a large number of factors, which could benefit policy-makers, teachers, and other
stakeholders in their quest to identify the most cost-effective intervention targets. In
this section, we only focused on the most important findings of this study, and some
supplementary discussions can be found in the Supplemental Materials.
First, the most important three factors that predict East Asian students’ mathematics achievement stem from individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors,

respectively. The results indicated that mathematics achievement is a joint function of a multiplicity of factors, justifying the need to scrutinize the role of different factors using the lens of a comprehensive socio-ecological perspective.
Specifically, students’ expectancy beliefs (i.e., students confident in mathematics) was found as the most crucial factor in predicting mathematics achievement.
This finding is consistent with previous research (Lee & Stankov, 2018), indicating that belief in one’s capabilities is one of the strongest predictors of achievement (Bandura, 2001). Students who possess a high level of expectancy beliefs
tend to invest more in their studies (e.g., effort, engagement, and persistence),
achieving better than their counterparts (Bong et al., 2012; Yeung et al., 2022).
Among microsystem factors, the paramount factor is student SES which corroborates past studies (e.g., Kim, 2019; King & Trinidad, 2021). Students from
low SES families have been found to be at a considerable disadvantage compared
to their well-off peers (Sirin, 2005). For example, they tend to have a poorer home
learning environment (Galindo & Sonnenschein, 2015) and fewer learning opportunities (e.g., curriculum content exposure; Schmidt et al., 2015). The importance
of SES has implications for policy-makers who may need to enact relevant educational policies that could level the playing field and provide support for the more
disadvantaged students.
Among mesosystem factors, school emphasis on academic success was the most
important factor, which coincides with previous research (Boonen et al., 2014;
Martin et al., 2013). The emphasis on academic success is a typical feature for
East Asian schools, which can be attributed to the examination culture prevalent
in Confucian heritage cultures (Tweed & Lehman, 2002). School emphasis on
academic success pertains to “the extent to which a school is driven by a quest
for academic excellence – a press for academic achievement” (Hoy et al., 2006,
p. 427). In a school with high levels of academic emphasis, teachers set high but
achievable goals for students and believe they can achieve these goals, which
in turn encourage students to pursue academic success (Goddard et al., 2000).
Accordingly, the normative environment on supporting teaching and learning
would be an effectiveness-enhancing factor to facilitate students’ achievement.
Second, individual and microsystem factors seemed to be more important predictors of achievement compared to mesosystem factors. This finding expands previous
research by providing a comprehensive picture about the relative importance of different factors influencing East Asian students’ mathematics achievement. The result is reasonable as individual and microsystem factors are more proximal to student learning
and bear a more direct effect on mathematics achievement than mesosystem (Bronfenbrenner, 1989). However, it is also likely that the relationships among the different variables are more complex than what is presented here as mesosystem factors could also
facilitate microsystem and individual factors (Chiu, 2015; Chiu & Khoo, 2005; King,
2021). Such nuances are beyond the scope of the current study as we only focused on
juxtaposing the relative importance of variables across different levels of analysis.
Third, one of the most important tasks in mathematics education research is to
find the variables not only contribute to mathematics achievement, but also can be
changed through interventions. Among the identified 11 most important factors in
the present study, all of them, except for student SES and school composition by

factors can be used as the basis for education policy reform and accountability
efforts.
Variations among East Asian economies
In addition to the common factors contributing to the whole East Asian students’
mathematics achievement, we also noticed some differences across East Asian
economies.
First, our results suggested that microsystem and mesosystem factors had a more
significant effect on mathematics achievement in Hong Kong and Singapore. This
finding is within expectation. Liu et al. (2006) suggested that the school systems
in Hong Kong and Singapore were more stratified with larger between-class and
between-school variance compared with other East Asian economies. In particular,
Singapore tended to be singled out for its highly competitive and stratified schooling
system though the government is recently experimenting to reduce the stratification
(Wong et al., 2020).
Second, the role of individual factors was larger in Chinese Taipei, Japan, and
Korea. This finding corroborates previous research indicating that the educational
systems in the three economies were more equitable with low disparities regarding the school resource, curriculum content, and teacher quality (Byun et al., 2012;
Yamamoto & Brinton, 2010). Hence, differences in achievement seem to be more
closely related to individuals’ factors.
Implications, limitations, and directions for future research
There are two notable contributions of our study. Substantively, we expand previous
research by opening the black box to show how a wider range of factors contributed
to East Asian students’ mathematics success based on the socio-ecological theory,
thereby advancing theoretical understanding. Compared with previous research
based on a limited number of factors, the simultaneous examination of multiple factors provided a comprehensive picture of the relative importance of different factors.
More importantly, most of the key factors identified are malleable and amenable to
change through educational interventions (e.g., Hecht et al., 2019). The findings on
the key factors of mathematics achievement provide informative evidence for stakeholders to devote intervention resources toward the critical factors that would yield
the largest educational dividends.
Methodologically, our research justified the application of machine learning in
international large-scale assessments which include multiple intercorrelated factors.
The application of machine learning could juxtapose the relative importance of a
large number of predictors without facing problems that bedevil classical statistics,
such as multicollinearity and overfitting.
However, some key limitations of the present study should be mentioned. One
limitation of this study is related to the sample. We analyzed the secondary data
obtained from TIMSS 2019; however, Mainland China, the biggest East Asian

economy, did not participate in TIMSS 2019. Including samples from Mainland
China would make the findings more generalizable. Another limitation is related to
the cross-sectional design of TIMSS, which prevents us from making causal conclusions. In future studies, we encourage researchers to carry out experimental or longitudinal research to further investigate the causal effects of key predictors on mathematics achievement. The last limitation is that we only identified the key factors of
mathematics achievement without examining the theoretical mechanisms that link
these factors. Hence, future studies may deepen our understanding by exploring the
key theoretical mechanisms on how key factors influence mathematics achievement.

Conclusion
Developing a holistic understanding of the relative importance of key factors significantly advances our theoretical understanding of a phenomenon that is only superficially understood. Rooted in socio-ecological theory, we ranked the relative importance of 22 individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors that contribute to East
Asian students’ mathematics success. Notably, we identified 11 key factors that were
most important, the top three of which were: students’ confidence in mathematics,
family socioeconomic status, and school emphasis on academic success. This study
provides evidence-based information for researchers, practitioners, and policy-makers who are interested in understanding and optimizing mathematics achievement.
