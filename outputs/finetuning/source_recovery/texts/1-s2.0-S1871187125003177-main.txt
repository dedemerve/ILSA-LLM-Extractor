This study investigates the key factors and underlying mechanisms influencing creative thinking
among East Asian secondary school students using PISA 2022 data. Using a rigorous machine
learning approach, we compared five predictive models and identified LightGBM as the most
accurate in forecasting creative thinking. We subsequently employed SHAP (SHapley Additive
exPlanations), an interpretable machine learning technique, to quantify feature contributions and
decompose individual predictions into additive effects, enabling both global and local interpretability. The results revealed three key findings: 1) Academic performance (reading, science,
and math performance), ESCS (family economic, social and cultural status), and gender were the
five most important predictors; 2) While reading and science performance exhibited a nearly
linear positive relationship with creative thinking, ESCS, math performance, empathy and selfefficacy in digital competencies exhibit threshold effects—their positive influence was strongest
within optimal value ranges. By contrast, creative family climate and expected educational
attainment showed negative relationship with creative thinking; 3) SHAP-based interaction analyses identified the strongest interactions between reading performance and ESCS, science
performance and gender, and science performance and ESCS. These findings enhance the theoretical understanding of the multidimensional determinants of creative thinking in East Asian
educational contexts and provide researchers and practitioners with empirically grounded insights for designing targeted interventions.

Creativity has been widely recognized as a fundamental 21st-century competency and an essential element of contemporary education (Chen et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2021). This is evidenced by its prominent inclusion in the 5Cs framework developed by the
Partnership for 21st Century Learning (P21), where creativity constitutes a core dimension for future-ready individuals (Gan et al.,
2020). Importantly, creativity development depends not merely on fostering intuitive and inspirational processes, but more crucially
on cultivating core cognitive capacities, particularly creative thinking (Dietrich, 2004). Therefore, educational policy for creativity
enhancement should prioritize the systematic development of creative thinking skills.
Since 2000, the OECD’s Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) has served as a benchmark for evaluating global
education systems through standardized assessments of 15-year-olds’ competencies. Conducted triennially, PISA administers tests in

1. Literature review
1.1. The definition of creative thinking in PISA
PISA defines creative thinking as “the competence to engage productively in the generation, evaluation and improvement of ideas
that can result in original and effective solutions, advances in knowledge and impactful expressions of imagination (OECD, 2019)”.
This conceptualization aligns with the cognitive processes and outcomes characteristic of “little-c” creativity (Kaufman & Beghetto,
2009). That is, the form of creative thinking that adolescents aged 15 can realistically demonstrate in everyday educational contexts,
such as those creative actions in which the nonexpert may participate each day.
The competency model of creative thinking proposed by PISA underscores three core facets of creative thinking: (1) generate

1.2. Existing theoretical framework of influential factors for creative thinking
The construction of a comprehensive theoretical model of critical influential factors for creative thinking is rather complicated
since the development of creativity involves multiple dimensions and dynamic interactions. A robust body of evidence has consistently
established a strong positive relationship between individual cognitive abilities (particularly intelligence and domain-specific academic performance) and creative thinking (e.g., Kim, 2020; Yang & Zhao, 2021). These factors constitute a fundamental cognitive
foundation for creative thinking, as they provide the necessary knowledge structures and mental flexibility for generating and evaluating novel ideas.
Grounded in the seminal 4P framework (i.e., “person,” “process,” “place,” and “product”) from early creativity psychology, Davis
(1986) conceptualized creative thinking as an individualized problem-solving system wherein novel ideas or solutions trigger
cognitive restructuring. Specifically, individuals actively modify their perceptual frameworks while deploying both conscious and
unconscious cognitive strategies such as novel associations, reconceptualization, and perspective-taking to facilitate innovative outcomes. This theoretical position underscores that domain-specific knowledge, social-emotional competency, and creative self-efficacy
constitute fundamental individual determinants of creative thinking. Importantly, environmental catalysts further modulate this
process, as evidenced by social-cognitive triggers and structural enablers (Dodds et al., 2002). Notably, empirical work by Wu et al.
(2014) highlights the predominant role of school and familial systems in shaping these interactive dynamics, suggesting that creativity
development is ultimately embedded within socio-ecological systems.
In the PISA2022 Creative Thinking Framework (OECD, 2019), it is emphasized that successful student engagement in creative work
and the development of creative thinking depend on both internal and external resources (shown in Fig. 1). Students’ internal resources, external factors, and their creative achievement and progress are strongly interconnected. Specifically, internal resources
encompass the knowledge, skills, and attitudes that facilitate creative thinking, such as domain-specific knowledge and experience,
cognitive ability, and openness to new ideas. External factors, on the other hand, include cultural norms and expectations, school and
home climate, and educational approaches. These external factors shape the development and refinement of students’ internal resources, as well as their level of creative engagement.
As discussed above, the existing theoretical framework of influential factors for creative thinking converges on a multi-systemic
understanding of creative thinking development, integrating individual, social, and environmental factors through complementary
theoretical lenses. It is crucial to note, however, that the empirical strength of these relationships varies significantly across these
factors. While the links between individual cognitive or academic factors and creative thinking are well-established and robust (Akpur,
2023; Gajda et al., 2017), the evidence for some environmental factors is often more limited, context-dependent, and demonstrates
weaker effect sizes (Acar et al., 2023). This study includes a range of predictors from the well-established to the exploratory to
empirically test their relative importance and complex interactions within the context of East Asia.
1.3. Empirical studies concerning influential factors on creative thinking
1.3.1. Individual factors influencing creative thinking
Previous empirical research on influential factors affecting creative thinking has traditionally examined individual and environmental factors in isolation. Regarding individual factors, studies have systematically investigated various determinants, including
gender differences (Hong et al., 2013; Matud et al., 2007), academic achievement (Yang & Zhao, 2021), frequency of physical activity
(Romance et al., 2023), social-emotional characteristics (Saleem et al., 2024), and attitudes towards creative thinking (Puozzo &
Audrin, 2021).
A strong consensus in the literature confirms that general cognitive ability and academic performance are among the most powerful
predictors of creative thinking (Akpur, 2023). For example, Yang and Zhao’s (2021) study established a significant positive correlation
between students’ creative thinking abilities and academic performance, with this relationship being especially pronounced among
academically disadvantaged students. This relationship is thought to be mediated by the fact that higher academic achievement
provides a richer knowledge base for forming novel associations and solving problems creatively (Kim, 2020).
Social-emotional characteristics are defined as temperamental, cognitive, and behavioral traits that shape emotional processing
and social interactions (Eisenberg et al., 2015). The PISA 2022 Creative Thinking framework (OECD, 2019) highlights seven core
dimensions of these characteristics: curiosity, persistence, assertiveness, empathy, cooperative tendency, stress resistance, and
emotional control, and emphasizes they play pivotal roles in the development of creative thinking. Empirical evidence substantiates
these theoretical connections; notably, Saleem et al. (2024) investigated with 484 Toronto preschoolers, examining the relationship
between social-emotional competence and creative thinking, demonstrating that stronger social-emotional skills are associated with
higher creative thinking. This finding suggests that foundational social-emotional skills may serve as important precursors for creative
thinking during critical early educational stages.
As defined by the PISA 2022 Creative Thinking framework (OECD, 2019), attitudes towards creative thinking refer to students’
engagement with cognitive processes, activities, and tasks that support or contribute to one’s capacity to engage in creative thinking.
The framework identifies four key dimensions of these attitudes: creative self-efficacy, openness to intellect, openness to art, and
imagination coupled with adventurousness. Empirical research has substantiated the significance of these attitudinal factors; for

1.3.2. Environmental factors influencing creative thinking
Research on environmental influences on creativity has predominantly examined family and school contexts, exploring factors such
as family socioeconomic status (SES) (Acar et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2024), digital activities in the classroom (Fakaruddin et al., 2024;
Wang et al., 2024), as well as family and school creative climate (Hoffmann et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b). Empirical evidence
consistently demonstrates the significant role of these contextual factors. For instance, Castillo-Vergara et al. (2018) conducted a
survey assessing creative thinking among 1062 fifth-grade students from 24 Chilean educational institutions, revealing that higher
family SES correlated with enhanced creative thinking. Further supporting this relationship, Acar et al. (2023) examined the relationship between SES and creativity through a comprehensive meta-analysis of 117 empirical studies, and found a small but significant
positive correlation between creativity and SES.
The environmental creative climate has also been demonstrated to influence students’ creative thinking. For instance, Hoffmann
et al. (2025) conducted a large-scale study involving 5274 students across 31 high schools in Mexico, revealing that students who
perceived their school as fostering a positive creative climate were more likely to believe in the value of creativity and subsequently
engage in more creative behaviors. Similarly, Li et al., 2025b examined 610 Chinese secondary students and demonstrated that family
creative climate had a fully mediated positive effect on creative scientific problem finding through intrinsic motivation. Together,
these findings underscore the critical role of both school and family creative climate in shaping adolescents’ creative thinking, while
also highlighting the dynamic interplay between external creative climates and students’ personal factors.
In addition to these established factors, there is growing though less consistent scholarly interest in the role of digital technology
use and creativity development, particularly given the global shift towards digitalization in education. This has led to the emergence of
digital creativity as a distinct research field (Zhang et al., 2022), where information and communication technologies (ICT) serve as
both platforms and agents for creative processes. Notably, the empirical evidence linking digital technology use directly to creative
thinking is emergent and often demonstrates weaker effects compared to more established factors like academic performance or SES
(Acar et al., 2023). Early, small-scale investigations (e.g., Wheeler & Bromfield, 2002) suggested a potential relationship. More
recently, experimental studies (e.g., Wang & Li, 2024) with specific interventions have shown that digital environments can provide
opportunities to enhance certain aspects of creative thinking, such as idea fluency and flexibility. However, the generalizable and
scalable impact of digital technology use on creative thinking remains an open question, with findings often being highly
context-dependent.
1.4. The present study
1.4.1. Rationale for predictive variable selection in the present study
Grounded in the aforementioned theoretical and empirical literature, the selection of predictive variables for this study was guided
by a dual principle of comprehensiveness and hierarchical prioritization. Comprehensiveness is reflected in the effort to include
variables that cover the two broad categories of factors influencing students’ creative thinking, as outlined in the PISA 2022 Creative
Thinking Framework: internal resources and external factors, while also aiming to represent the secondary dimensions under each of
these categories as fully as the available data permitted. Variables were excluded if they were not measured in the PISA 2022
assessment module or if they exhibited an excessive proportion of missing values (>10 %).
Hierarchical prioritization was implemented as follows: First, we included well-established predictors with strong empirical
support, such as core academic performance (reading, math, and science) and family economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS). This
approach enables a robust comparison of feature importance and ensures that the analysis accounts for the most influential known
determinants of creative thinking. Second, we incorporated factors of emerging or contextually specific interest where empirical
evidence remains mixed or is particularly relevant to the East Asian educational context. Examples include expected educational
attainment, digital technology use and self-efficacy, digital environment, and environmental creative climate. These variables were
included not based on an assumption of their dominant influence, but rather to empirically evaluate their relative importance against
established factors and to investigate their potential interaction effects within a large-scale, real-world dataset.
1.4.2. Research gaps and study aims
In summary, while existing studies have successfully identified various discrete factors which range from personal traits to environmental conditions, they still reveal three critical limitations that constrain both theoretical advancement and practical application.
Firstly, a concerning pattern emerges wherein most factors are typically investigated in isolation through small-scale studies, with
particularly glaring gaps in large-sample research within East Asian educational settings. This is particularly problematic given East
Asia’s unique examination-oriented culture (Li, 2012), where intense academic pressure may differentially impact creative development compared to Western contexts, making region-specific investigations imperative for understanding contextual influences on
creative thinking. Secondly, although recent work has begun exploring multifactorial models of creative thinking (Hoffmann et al.,
2025), the field still lacks robust methodologies to quantify the relative weights of each factor (i.e., the feature importance), severely
limiting the capacity for evidence-based policy prioritization. Thirdly, while conventional analytical approaches such as structural
equation modeling (SEM) can be extended to model certain interactions and non-linearities (Kelava & Brandt, 2009), it often requires
strong a priori theoretical assumptions about which specific effects to test, and the model specification process can become

computationally complex when exploring a high-dimensional set of potential predictors from large-scale assessments like PISA.
Machine learning is considered an appropriate methodology for analyzing large-scale data (i.e., the number of possible predictors
as well as data points). More importantly, interpretable machine learning (IML) approaches offer a powerful, data-driven framework to
automatically screen and model complex relationships without pre-specified parametric assumptions (Freiesleben et al., 2024). These
methods can not only extract a measure of variable importance (also called feature importance), which quantifies how useful different
predictors are in forecasting the outcome, but also flexibly uncover intricate patterns, including nonlinear effects and multi-way interactions (Lavelle-Hill et al., 2025). Therefore, the present study employed an IML approach primarily for its data-driven capability to
automatically screen and model complex, non-linear relationships and higher-order interactions without requiring pre-specified model
forms. This is particularly advantageous for the exploratory analysis of high-dimensional data from large-scale assessments like PISA,
where the number of potential predictors and interactions is vast. Based on the above consideration, the present study aims to address
the following three questions:
RQ1: What are the key determinants of creative thinking among East Asian middle school students, and what is their relative
importance?
RQ2: How do the relationships between these key factors and creative thinking vary in terms of directionality and heterogeneity?
RQ3: How do interactions between these critical factors shape creative thinking in the East Asian context?
2. Method
2.1. Research framework
All analyses were conducted in Python 3.10, utilizing key libraries including scikit-learn, xgboost, lightgbm, and shap for model
implementation, tuning, and interpretation. This study employs a rigorous interpretable machine learning pipeline to systematically
analyze determinants of creative thinking, as illustrated in Fig. 2. After extracting data for East Asian students from the PISA 2022
dataset, predictor variables were systematically selected across individual and environmental dimensions based on the existing
theoretical framework and empirical research. Variables with missing data rates exceeding 10 % were excluded to ensure robustness.
A nested resampling procedure was implemented to enhance the validity and stability of the results (Bischl et al., 2012; Jankowsky
et al., 2024). The entire dataset was repeatedly partitioned 100 times into 80 % training and 20 % testing subsets, each with a unique
random seed (integers from 1 to 100). For each iteration, all downstream procedures—including missing value imputation, data
transformation, hyperparameter tuning via cross-validation, model fitting and evaluation—were performed independently within the
training set to prevent data leakage. Five machine learning models were trained and evaluated: Elastic Net Regression (ENR), Random
Forest Regression (RFR), Support Vector Regression (SVR), XGBoost, and LightGBM. ENR was incorporated to serve as an interpretable
baseline model for comparison against the more complex, non-linear models. Three tree-based models (i.e., RFR, XGBoost, LightGBM)
were selected especially due to their capacity to capture nonlinear and interactive effects. Model performance was assessed using three
metrics: root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and R², with results aggregated across all iterations to ensure

The creative thinking score for each student was represented by 10 plausible values (PVs) generated through the PISA 2022′s Item
Response Theory (IRT) scaling procedure. As shown in OECD technical guidelines (OECD, 2024b), these PVs are drawn from the
posterior ability distribution using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) estimation to properly account for measurement error in the
assessment. In alignment with PISA’s international analytical standards, the 10 PVs were combined using Rubin’s rules (Rubin, 1978)
for multiple imputations. Specifically, by calculating the mean across PVs for point estimates while appropriately pooling their variances to reflect the imputation uncertainty. This approach yields a single composite creative thinking score for each student that
incorporates both the probabilistic nature of IRT scaling and the complex survey design of PISA, while maintaining the assessment’s
measurement properties throughout our analytical pipeline.
2.2.2. Selection of predictive variables
Building on the analytical framework of factors influencing creative thinking, this study incorporated a total of 30 predictive
variables encompassing both individual and environmental factors (see Table 1 for details). The individual-level variables, drawn from
the PISA 2022 Student Main Survey Questionnaire, Student ICT Questionnaire, and achievement tests in mathematics, reading, and
science, comprised 19 indicators across five dimensions: basic demographic information, academic performance, socio-emotional
characteristics, attitude towards creative thinking, and digital technology use and self-efficacy. The calculation methods for mathematics, reading, and science performance scores were consistent with those for creative thinking, incorporating 10 PVs in weighted
computations. For environmental factors, variables characterizing home and school environments were selected from the same
questionnaires (all student-reported), including five family environment indicators and six school environment indicators.
2.3. Data sources and preprocessing
The data for this study were derived from the PISA 2022 assessment and cover five East Asian economies: Hong Kong (China),
Macau (China), Chinese Taipei, South Korea, and Singapore. Japan was excluded as it did not participate in the creative thinking
assessment module. The final sample comprises 29,208 students, of whom 48.62 % are female. This includes 5907 students from Hong
Kong (China), 6454 from South Korea, 4384 from Macau (China), 6606 from Singapore, and 5857 from Chinese Taipei.
To ensure the robustness and generalizability of our machine learning models, we adopted a nested resampling procedure. The
entire dataset was randomly split into 80 % training and 20 % testing sets, a process repeated 100 times, each with a unique random
seed. For every iteration, all data preprocessing steps were fit exclusively on the training set and then applied to the testing set to
prevent data leakage. Specifically, this involved: (1) data transformation, wherein categorical variables like gender were numerically
encoded (1=female, 2=male) using label encoding, while continuous variables were standardized (Z-score normalization); (2) missing
value imputation using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm (KNNImputer from the scikit-learn library). This preprocessing
pipeline addressed missing data, categorical representation, and scale harmonization while preserving the dataset’s statistical properties for the following machine learning applications.

2.4.1. Elastic net regression
Elastic net regression, introduced by Zou and Hastie (2005), is a regularized linear regression method that combines the L1 (Lasso)
and L2 (Ridge) penalties to overcome the limitations of each individual approach. This hybrid technique is particularly effective in
scenarios with high-dimensional data or highly correlated predictors, as it promotes both sparsity and grouping stability. By balancing
the trade-off between variable selection and regularization strength through a mixing parameter, elastic net achieves improved
predictive performance and model interpretability. In this study, the maximum number of iterations for the elastic net regression
algorithm is set to 10,000 to ensure adequate convergence of the optimization process.
2.4.2. Support vector regression
Support vector regression (SVR), an extension of Support Vector Machine (SVM) for regression tasks, was formalized by Smola and
Schölkopf (2004). It employs Structural Risk Minimization (SRM) and the Kernel Trick to build an ε-insensitive tube in
high-dimensional feature space, effectively handling high-dimensional, and non-linear problems while ensuring global optimality
under convex optimization. In addition, even with a small data sample, the SVR may provide high generalization results.
2.4.3. Random forest regression
Random Forest is an ensemble learning method that builds multiple decision trees for prediction, where each tree is trained on an
independently bootstrapped subset of data and can handle both regression and classification tasks (Breiman, 2001). In random forest
regression, individual trees output numerical values, while in classification they output class labels. The algorithm employs bootstrap
sampling (with replacement) to create diverse training subsets for each tree, and typically uses random feature selection for node
splitting. Model robustness is improved through aggregation (e.g., voting or averaging).
2.4.4. XGBoost
XGBoost, developed by Chen and Guestrin (2016), is an advanced gradient boosting framework that enhances predictive performance by iteratively constructing decision trees to minimize residual errors. The algorithm incorporates several innovative features: it
utilizes a regularized objective function with L1/L2 penalty terms to prevent overfitting, implements dynamic sample weighting
through gradient and Hessian-based optimization to focus on challenging observations, and employs an efficient approximate quantile
algorithm for rapid split point identification during tree construction. This combination of methodological innovations makes XGBoost
particularly effective for handling complex predictive modeling tasks across various domains.
2.4.5. LightGBM
LightGBM (Light Gradient Boosting Machine), developed by Microsoft (Ke et al., 2017), is an advanced gradient boosting
framework designed for efficient and scalable machine learning. As an improvement over traditional gradient boosting methods,
LightGBM introduces two key innovations: Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) and Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB), which
significantly enhance computational efficiency while maintaining high accuracy. The algorithm employs a leaf-wise tree growth
strategy (as opposed to level-wise growth) to achieve better convergence with lower loss, making it particularly effective for handling
large-scale datasets.

2.5. Leave-One-Group-Out cross-validation
To mitigate the risk of overoptimistic performance estimates due to our data’s grouped structure where students are nested within
five distinct economies, we employed Leave-One-Group-Out Cross-Validation (LOGO-CV). LOGO-CV is a resampling technique
designed to assess the generalization performance of predictive models in the presence of grouped data structures. Its validation
procedure involves iteratively holding out all samples from one distinct group as the test set, while using the remaining groups for
model training (Pedregosa et al., 2011). In our procedure, each of the five economies (Hong Kong-China, South Korea, Macau-China,
Singapore, and Chinese Taipei) is iteratively held out as the test set, while the model is trained on the remaining four. By enforcing a
strict group-level separation between training and test sets, LOGO-CV yields a conservative and robust estimate of model generalizability, ensuring our findings are not unduly influenced by economy-level confounding effects.
2.6. SHAP method
While machine learning models such as XGBoost exhibit strong predictive capabilities in regression tasks, their inherent opacity as
“black box” systems complicates the interpretation of decision-making processes. In response to this challenge, the SHapley Additive
exPlanations (SHAP) method was created. It is a cutting-edge explanatory approach that utilizes the cooperative game theory and
localized explanations principles, aiming to explicitly demonstrate the influence of individual features on the model’s predictions

explanatory model for each prediction instance, and calculates the “SHAP value” for each feature. The SHAP value corresponding to
each feature can be understood as the importance of individual input features in contributing to the model’s predicted value (Xu et al.,
2025). In Python, the SHAP package offers a convenient and efficient tool to compute and visualize Shapley values for model
explainability (Chen et al., 2025b). For a given prediction f, the SHAP value for feature i is defined as:

4. Discussion
4.1. Empirical contributions
Leveraging East Asian student data from the 2022 PISA survey, this study applied the SHAP interpretable machine learning
framework as a novel tool to systematically analyze the key factors and underlying mechanisms influencing creative thinking. The
analysis operates at three levels: global feature importance (mean |SHAP|), local feature directionality and heterogeneity (individual
SHAP distributions), and bivariate feature interactions (SHAP interaction values). Our approach advances beyond traditional methods
by 1) identifying the key factors influencing creative thinking through SHAP values, 2) capturing nonlinear relationships via
dependence plots, and 3) revealing interaction effects through SHAP interaction values and interaction dependence plots. The findings
offer preliminary insights and potential avenues for educational policymakers aimed at developing targeted intervention strategies for
cultivating students’ creative thinking.
In terms of feature importance and directionality, the results indicate that academic performance (reading, science, and math
performance), family ESCS, and gender are the top five factors predicting creative thinking. First, foundational academic performance
is positively correlated with creative thinking. This finding aligns with previous research (Yang & Zhao, 2021; Kim, 2020), highlighting
the role of academic performance as a prerequisite for developing creative thinking. Notably, among the three domains of academic
performance, reading performance emerges as the strongest predictor. This result is different from OECD’s analysis of all countries,
who found that math performance was the most predictive variable for creative thinking (OECD, 2024a). The superior reading performance may be attributed to the unique design of the PISA 2022 creative thinking assessment, emphasizing students’ ability to
comprehend and articulate written responses to open-ended problems (OECD, 2019). This assessment approach likely amplifies the
influence of reading ability. Second, ESCS significantly and positively predicts creative thinking, corroborating prior studies on the
impact of family environment (Lu et al., 2024; Jankowska et al., 2024). This can be explained by theories of resource and capital
16

Thinking Skills and Creativity 60 (2026) 102069

H. Chen et al.

accumulation: higher ESCS families not only provide more material resources (e.g., learning tools and extracurricular materials) but
also cultivate an environment conducive to innovative thinking through exposure to arts, critical discussions, and cultural capital
transmission (Jankowska et al., 2024).
Third, gender differences reveal that female students outperform males in creative thinking, a trend consistent across all countries
in the PISA 2022 data (OECD, 2024a). However, as critically discussed by Barbot and Kaufman (2025), the magnitude and consistency
of this gender gap are unprecedented in the creativity literature and raise fundamental questions about the construct validity of the
PISA assessment itself. Therefore, while our SHAP analysis identifies gender as one of the top predictors, this disparity may not solely
reflect true differences in creative thinking. It could be significantly amplified by the assessment’s heavy reliance on verbal and visual
domains where the gap was most pronounced (OECD, 2024a), potentially favoring communication styles more commonly associated
with females. Furthermore, the observed gap may be partly attributable to developmental differences in 15-year-old adolescence.
Neurocognitive research suggests that girls at age 15 often show advanced maturation in prefrontal brain regions and connecting white
matter tracts supporting executive functions, such as cognitive flexibility and self-regulation (Lenroot & Giedd, 2006), which are
critical for performing well on complex, open-ended assessments like the PISA creative thinking test. This suggests that creativity
training and education should consider gender-specific interventions, but more importantly, future assessments must strive for a more
balanced and multidimensional measurement of creative thinking to avoid methodological confounding.
Furthermore, it was found that self-efficacy in digital competencies, expected educational attainment, empathy, creative activities
at school, and family creative climate were among the sixth to tenth most influential predictors. Self-efficacy in digital competencies,
defined as a belief in one’s ability to use digital tools effectively, showed a positive association with creative thinking, likely by
enabling students to explore, iterate, and express ideas in digital environments (Santoso et al., 2019). Empathy also exhibited a modest
yet consistent positive contribution to creative thinking, and this positive link was consistent with prior research (Tie et al., 2024).
Heightened empathy enables individuals to perceive problems from multiple viewpoints, enhancing cognitive flexibility and fostering
more innovative and creative solutions (Walther et al., 2017). Notably, the study found a negative relationship between students’
expected educational attainment and creative thinking. This result may reflect the highly competitive educational environment in East
Asia, where excessively high educational expectations are often linked to overreliance on rigid test-taking strategies, increased risk
aversion, and a shift from intrinsic motivation to extrinsic achievement goals—factors known to suppress creative thinking (Ke &
Liang, 2023; Muench et al., 2022).
Notably, family creative climate and school creative activities showed counterintuitive negative associations with creative
thinking. This pattern mirrors the surprising finding in the PISA report of a negative correlation between participation in creative
activities and creative thinking (OECD, 2024a). A plausible explanation for these paradoxical findings is that the mere frequency of
creative participation is insufficient for enhancing creativity outcomes; instead, the development of creativity primarily requires
in-depth engagement and high-quality activities (Barbot & Kaufman, 2025). That is, overparticipation without depth, or sporadic
participation across many domains, may potentially hinder creativity development because such engagement often lacks consistency
and intrinsic motivation. In this context, our interaction analysis, which revealed the interaction between family creative climate and
empathy on creative thinking, directly addresses the need for more nuanced investigation into these complex relationships.
Regarding feature heterogeneity, reading and science performance demonstrate a nearly linear positive correlation with creative
thinking. In contrast, ESCS, math performance, empathy, and self-efficacy in digital competencies exhibit threshold effects, where their
positive impact on creative thinking is most pronounced when values fall within a specific range. This finding aligns with the
moderation principle of creative thinking cultivation (Castillo-Vergara et al., 2018), which suggests that the influence of family socioeconomic and cultural factors, as well as individual psychological traits, on creative thinking is not uniformly positive but rather
operates within an optimal range.
Regarding feature interactions, the results reveal that creative thinking emerges from complex interactions between personal and
contextual factors, rather than from isolated predictors. Three key patterns demonstrate this interdependence: First, we observed a
compensatory interaction between ESCS and academic performance. In low-ESCS settings within East Asian societies, higher academic
performance correlated with reduced creative thinking, likely due to intensified conformity pressures and examination-oriented imperatives in resource-limited environments (Ke & Liang, 2023). Conversely, in high-ESCS settings, socioeconomic resources buffer
these pressures, enabling academic achievement to enhance creative thinking by providing access to diverse learning experiences,
psychological security, and opportunities for knowledge exploration beyond standardized curricula. Second, a notable crossover
interaction emerged between gender and science performance. Females exhibited a creativity advantage among low science achievers,
while males demonstrated an advantage among high achievers. This finding challenges blanket assumptions about gender differences
in creativity, highlighting that such effects are conditional on domain-specific competence.
Finally, the interaction analysis resolved the counterintuitive negative main effect of family creative climate by revealing its
dependence on student empathy. In low creative family environments, lower empathy appeared beneficial, possibly because less
empathetic students are less affected by familial discouragement or lack of engagement. However, in high creative climates, higher
empathy markedly enhanced creative output, which is because an enriching environment allows empathetic children to leverage their
emotional sensitivity for imaginative and divergent thinking (Li & Li, 2025). This indicates that the family creative climate functions
not as a direct determinant, but as a moderator that either suppresses or activates a student’s innate empathic potential. Interventions
focusing on family climate should therefore account for individual differences in empathy to be effective. It is important to note that
the measure of family creative climate is based on student self-report. This introduces the possibility that the interaction effect is partly
shaped by students’ subjective perceptions, as those with higher empathy might be more attuned to and report a more positive familial
climate. Future research would benefit from incorporating multi-informant reports (e.g., from parents) to disentangle these effects.

4.2. Implications and future directions
While our findings are derived from correlational data and cannot directly prescribe policy, the multi-faceted evidence of global
importance, local heterogeneity, and bivariate interactions offers valuable heuristic insights and highlights critical avenues for future
research and potential intervention. The complex, interdependent nature of the predictors suggests that initiatives aimed at fostering
creative thinking may be most effective when they move beyond one-size-fits-all approaches.
First, the consistent prominence of reading performance, which surpassed even math and science performance as the top predictor,
underscores its foundational role. This implies that efforts to enhance creative thinking should strongly prioritize reading ability,
particularly the kind that supports comprehension and expression in open-ended scenarios. Educational initiatives might experiment
with integrating advanced reading modules that emphasize interpretative flexibility, analogical reasoning, and narrative creativity
across subjects, not just in language arts.
Second, the prevalence of threshold effects (e.g., in ESCS, math performance, empathy, and digital self-efficacy) suggests that more
is not always better. Resources and interventions may yield greater returns if they are calibrated toward achieving sufficient levels in
these traits rather than maximizing them. Additionally, the negative association of expected education attainment and creative
thinking serves as a critical warning: Policymakers and parents should be cautious of excessive academic expectations, which may
trade off short-term achievement for long-term innovative capacity.
Third, and most significantly, the interactions reveal that effectiveness depends critically on targeting specific subgroups. Our
findings suggest that: 1) Support for high-achieving students in low-ESCS schools should aim to reduce conformity pressure and
provide creative outlets insulated from high-stakes testing; 2) Educational strategies should consider gender not as a fixed predictor of
creative thinking, but as a factor that interacts strongly with mastery in specific domains, such as the science domain; 3) Familyoriented programs might move beyond generic climate advice. Instead, they could explore providing parents with strategies
tailored to their child’s empathy level.
5. Conclusion
This study utilizes PISA 2022 data from East Asian students and employs the SHAP interpretable machine learning framework to
systematically examine the key determinants of middle school students’ creative thinking and their underlying mechanisms. Although
some of the key determinants identified in this study conform to those presented in the PISA results document, their nonlinear and
interdependent roles in the East Asian context offer a refined map for designing smarter interventions. The main findings reveal that:
(1) In terms of feature importance, academic performance (reading, science, and math performance), family ESCS, and gender emerge
as the five most influential predictors; (2) Regarding feature directionality and heterogeneity, reading and science performance show
nearly linear positive relationship with creative thinking, while ESCS, math performance, empathy and self-efficacy in digital competencies exhibit threshold effects—their positive influence peaks when values fall within optimal ranges. By contrast, creative family
climate and expected educational attainment show negative relationship with creative thinking; (3) Regarding feature interactions, the
three most substantial interaction effects were identified between reading performance and ESCS; between science performance and
gender; and between science performance and ESCS. Specifically, in low-ESCS settings, higher reading and science performance
correlated with reduced creative thinking, while in high-ESCS settings, this relationship was reversed. Furthermore, females exhibited
a creativity advantage among low science achievers, while males demonstrated an advantage among high science achievers.
Notably, this study has at least the following three limitations: First, while this study focused on East Asia, the absence of
comparative data from other cultural regions (e.g., Western countries) limits the generalizability of findings. Future research could
leverage the global PISA database to examine how cultural values (e.g., individualism vs. collectivism) moderate creative thinking
development. Second, while this study revealed the interaction effects between the key predictors, translating these insights into action
requires causal verification. Future longitudinal and experimental studies should prioritize investigating the mechanisms behind these
interactions. For example, through randomized trials offering differentiated creativity support based on students’ academic,
emotional, and socioeconomic profiles. Our study serves to generate hypotheses and pinpoint specific subgroups and mechanisms that
merit prioritized investigation. Third, the robustness check using LOGO-CV confirmed that LightGBM’s superior performance in
standard validation was partly artificial, inflated by its sensitivity to economy-specific characteristics. Consequently, while the SHAP
analysis from LightGBM effectively reveals complex relationships within these East Asian economies, the relative importance of
predictors and their interactions might not be fully generalizable to entirely different cultural contexts. This underscores that our
findings are most robust for understanding the mechanisms within similar educational systems, and researchers should be cautious
when extrapolating them.
