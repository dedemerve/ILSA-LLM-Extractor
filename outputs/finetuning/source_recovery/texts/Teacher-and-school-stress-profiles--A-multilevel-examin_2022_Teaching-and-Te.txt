We examined three common sources of teacher stress (workload, student behavior, expectation stress) to
identify teacher and school proﬁles. Data from the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS)
2018 were used (n ¼ 3117 teachers from 255 Australian schools). Latent proﬁle analysis (LPA) revealed
ﬁve teacher proﬁles: Low-Burden (17%), Mixed-Burden-Workload (15%), Mixed-Burden-Behavior (19%),
Average-Burden (41%), and High-Burden (18%). Multilevel LPA identiﬁed three school proﬁles: Lower
Pressure Climate (Workload) (17%), Lower Pressure Climate (Behavior) (23%), and Higher Pressure
Climate (59%). The proﬁles were associated differently with background characteristics and work-related
outcomes (strain, occupational commitment). Findings provide directions for efforts to support teachers.

1. Introduction
Stress is known to be a major concern for teachers. Research
over the past several decades has identiﬁed that teachers experience above average levels of stress, and that stress is associated
with greater burnout and lower retention among teachers (e.g.,
Gallup, 2014; Pogere et al., 2019). However, stress has been deﬁned
and interpreted in a variety of ways in the literature. Some researchers have focused on sources of stress (i.e., stressors; Klassen
& Chiu, 2011), whereas others have focused on stress as the experience of negative outcomes (e.g., Bottiani et al., 2019). In the present study, we examined both forms of stress to obtain a more
complete picture of teachers' stress-related experiences at

are appraised negatively by teachers to be stressful (e.g., workloads

for teachers’ well-being (strain) and retention in the profession
(occupational commitment).

knowledge about the role of stressors in teachers’ work (e.g.,
showing that stressors predict greater burnout; Pogere et al., 2019),
it typically focuses on the sample-wide average associations between stressors and predictors (which does not reveal if associations vary for different types of teachers), and has not considered
the interplay between different sources of stress. This gap is
important to address because it is possible that there are distinct
combinations of stressors experienced by different types of teachers (e.g., high on one stressor and low on the others, and vice
versa)dand that different combinations are more (or less) detrimental for teachers. Knowing the common stress proﬁles among
teachers is important for offering targeted support in schools and
for guiding policy to better support teachers across the profession.
In order to identify proﬁles, person-centered research is the
appropriate analytic approach and thus was used in the current
study.
Another important gap in the literature concerns multilevel
examinations, which allow investigations of factors at both the
individual- and organization-level. The vast bulk of prior research
on stressors (or strains) has examined this factor only at the
teacher-level (e.g., Collie et al., 2012). However, stressors can also be
a school-wide phenomenon (O'Brennan et al., 2017), and it is also
possible that there are different school stress proﬁles (i.e., different
types of schools identiﬁable by the composition of teacher stress
proﬁles). Identifying the role of stress at the teacher- and schoollevel is essential for determining whether efforts to support individuals are the same or different from those targeted to the
school-level.
The primary purpose of the present study was, thus, to extend
prior research on teacher stress by conducting a multilevel personcentered analysis of three commonly experienced sources of stress
among teachers: workload stress, student behavior stress, and
expectation stress. In particular, we sought to identify stress proﬁles based on these factors at both the teacher- and school-level,
along with predictors (i.e., teacher and school characteristics) and
outcomes (i.e., strain and occupational commitment) of the proﬁles
at both levels. Fig. 1 demonstrates the model under examination.
Data from Australian teachers and schools that participated in the
Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018 were used
in the present study.

Two conceptual frameworks provide understanding that guides
our study. The ﬁrst framework is the transactional theory of stress
(Lazarus, 1966; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), which establishes that
potential stressors are appraised by the individual based on their
perceptions of whether they have the resources to effectively
navigate the stressors. As noted above, sources of stress are environmental factors and conditions that have been appraised as
exceeding the individual's ability to cope. In that sense, potential
stressors are not inherently imbued with a particular meaning.
Instead, it is because the individual has appraised the stressor as
detrimental that determines its negative impact on their wellbeing (see also Hascher et al., 2021 for a theoretical application
among teachers). In the present study, we focused on teachers'
negative appraisals of three stressors (e.g., having too much
workload).
The second framework that is relevant to the present study is
job demands-resources (JD-R) theory (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017).
One major component of JD-R theory that is relevant to the present
study is the role of job demands, which are posited to undermine
employee functioning at work (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). Job
demands require an investment of effort in order to overcome. As
such, job demands are often harmful for employee functioning
because they make it harder for employees to achieve their work
goals (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). Sources of stress are one such job
demand. Because the individual has appraised these job-related
factors as being stressful and demanding, they are associated
with impaired well-being and functioning at work (Skaalvik &
Skaalvik, 2018). The present study provided the opportunity to
consider three sources of stress in relation to teacher- and schoollevel outcomes.

to teachers' perceptions of having to do too much lesson preparation, instruction, or marking work in the time available to them

present study, which specify that there are distinct stressors relevant to different contexts and occupations (e.g., particular jobs will

Taken together, prior research provides understanding about
the detrimental role of stressors among teachers using variablecentered approaches. However, this work has typically involved
examining only one or two sources of stress (e.g., Collie et al., 2012;
MacNell, Driscoll, & Hunt, 2015; Pogere et al., 2019), or a higherorder stressor factor that does not differentiate between the sources (Ouellette et al., 2018). Prior research has also focused on how
stressors are linked with outcomes on average, rather than
considering how the stressors might co-occur for different subpopulations. Because teachers can experience multiple stressors at
the same time (Hascher et al., 2021), it is now important that prior
work is extended to reveal the interplay between highly salient
experiences among teachers. The present study, thus, advanced
understanding by considering all three sources simultaneously and
from a person-centered approach.

1.2.2. Summary
In sum, very few studies have considered teacher stress proﬁles
and these have focused on strain. Apparently, no research to date
has considered multiple sources of stress in identifying teacher
proﬁles using person-centered approaches. Further, the extent to
which schools can be identiﬁed based on the prevalence of different
teacher stress proﬁles remains unexamined. We propose it is
important to address these gaps in order to ascertain the major
stress proﬁles evident among teachers and schools, and to better
inform policy and practice hoping to support healthy and effective
teachers and schools.
In the present study, it was hypothesized that there may be
some teachers who report low levels across all three sources of
stress. Likewise, there may be teachers who report high levels
across all three sources. At the same time, it was anticipated that
there may also be heterogeneous proﬁles made up of different
levels of the stress factors. These hypotheses rest on conceptual
support from JD-R theory showing that employees can vary in their
perceptions of different job demands (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017),
and other research that has identiﬁed teacher and school proﬁles
based on different levels of other job demands (Collie, Malmberg
et al., 2020). The hypotheses also rest on empirical evidence
showing that the different sources of stress are only moderately
correlated (e.g., Klassen, 2010). These moderate correlations suggest that teachers who are high in one source of stress are not
necessarily high in the others. For example, there may be some
teachers who experience high workload and expectation stress, but
low levels of student behavior stressdsuch as in academicallyfocused schools (Luthar et al., 2020). Conversely, there may be
some teachers who experience high student behavior and expectation stress, but low levels of workload stressdpotentially in under resourced schools (Schmidt & Jones-Fosu, 2019). The present
study set out to ascertain the extent to which these different
teacher proﬁles were evident.

1.2.1. Variable- and person-centered approaches to examining
stress
Variable- and person-centered approaches provide important
complementary understanding of phenomena (Morin et al., 2016).
Variable-centered analytic approaches (e.g., regression) rest on the
assumption of population homogeneity and typically provide understanding about associations among factors at a sample-wide
level (e.g., the extent to which a stressor is associated with outcomes on average; Morin et al., 2016). Variable-centered research
provides important understanding about the outcomes that are
uniquely associated with different stressorsdand is thus relevant
for pinpointing factors to target in broad intervention efforts. As
noted earlier, the vast bulk of research on teacher stress has been
variable-centered in nature, but little is known about the joint roles
of different sources of stress, such as whether particular combinations of stressors are more or less detrimental than other combinations. This research gap is ideally examined by way of personcentered analytic approaches. Person-centered analyses (e.g., latent
proﬁle analysis) are able to identify distinct proﬁles of individuals
who fare similarly across a range of factors, and the extent to which
particular combinations are better aligned with important outcomes (Morin et al., 2016). Person-centered analyses provide an
alternative way of understanding sources of stress among teachers,
and can reveal knowledge of how intervention efforts can be targeted to the speciﬁc needs of different proﬁles.

1.3. School-level examinations
Examinations at the school-level yield information about factors
that can be a target for whole-school efforts. Although the vast
majority of research involving teachers has been conducted at the
teacher-level, this is beginning to change as studies are increasingly
highlighting the importance of context (e.g., Ainsworth & Oldﬁeld,
2019) to explain adaptive or maladaptive teacher outcomes.
Emerging variable-centered research also reveals the importance of
considering factors at the school-level. For example, school-average
student behavior stress is associated with lower school-average
occupational commitment and greater school-average burnout
(Collie, 2021; O'Brennan et al., 2017). This means it is not only the
individual that should be considered in relation to stressors, but
also working conditions and the broader school climate.

2. Study overview
The present study examined three common sources of stress
among teachers: workload stress, student behavior stress, and
expectation stress using data from TALIS 2018. In phase one, latent
proﬁle analysis was employed to identify teacher proﬁles. The
extent to which the teacher proﬁles were associated with predictors (gender, teaching experience) and outcomes (strain, occupational commitment) was then examined. In phase two, we used
multilevel latent proﬁle analysis to identify school proﬁles based on
the relative prevalence of the teacher proﬁles. Similar to phase one,
we also examined the extent to which the school proﬁles were
associated with predictors (school size, proportion of low-SES
students) and outcomes (school-average strain and occupational
commitment). Fig. 1 shows the models under examination.

1.4. Proﬁles and their links with teacher and school outcomes
Teacher and school proﬁles have been shown to be associated
with different levels of work-related outcomes (Collie, Malmberg
et al., 2020). In the current study, we examined two outcomes at
both the teacher- and school-level: strain and occupational
commitment. Strain refers to the negative psychological or physiological outcomes that result from stressors (e.g., feeling highly
stressed, reduced mental health; Bliese et al., 2017). Occupational
commitment refers to teachers’ emotional or psychological attachment to their profession (Meyer et al., 1993).
As noted above, prior variable-centered research has shown that
sources of stress are implicated in teachers’ work-related outcomes
(e.g., greater burnout; greater intentions to quit; Klassen & Chiu,
2011; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2017a, 2018). At the school-level, researchers have shown that student behavior stress is associated
with lower occupational commitment (Collie, 2021). However, the
extent to which these ﬁndings occur when considering multiple
sources of stress simultaneously via proﬁles remains to be examined. The present study, thus, provided the opportunity to extend
prior research by examining whether different teacher and school
stress proﬁles are associated with different levels of the outcomes
at the teacher- and school-level. Based on prior variable-centered
research, it was anticipated that proﬁles generally lower in the
three sources of stress would have the lowest strain and highest
occupational commitment (Klassen & Chiu, 2011). At the same
time, we anticipated there might be some nuance to this depending
on the speciﬁc combination of factors within each proﬁle.

3. Methods
3.1. Sample and procedures
The phase one sample included 3117 teachers from 225 schools
located in Australia who participated in TALIS 2018.1 Every ﬁve
years, the OECD runs TALIS, which involves data collection from a
nationally-representative sample of teachers and schools using a
two-stage stratiﬁed cluster sampling design (see OECD, 2020 for
full details). In the present study, participating teachers were 63%
female and around half (51%) of the sample were aged between 30
and 49 years. Participants had an average teaching experience of 15
(SD ¼ 12) years, most (84%) were working full-time, and almost all
(97%) had a bachelor's degree or higher. Participants taught at
ISCED Level 2 (lower secondary). Two-thirds (66%) of the participating schools were publicly managed, and most had fewer than
30% of students from low-SES backgrounds (73%). Most schools
(58%) had a male principal who had, on average, 8 (SD ¼ 6) years of
principal experience. The schools were located in villages (4%;
3000 people), towns (29%; 3001e100,000 people), and cities
(67%; >100,000 people). There were on average 15 (SD ¼ 3)
teachers per school.
In order to provide evidence of the representativeness of this
sample, it is helpful to turn to the Australian Teacher Workforce
Data (2021) report, which provides details about the population
of Australian secondary school teachers (based on data from three
states/territories of Australia: New South Wales, Northern Territory,
and South Australia). Secondary school teachers are 67% female and
47% are aged between 30 and 49 years. Most (64%) teachers work
full time, and 100% hold a bachelor's degree or higher. Most (65%)
teachers worked in publicly managed schools and almost twothirds (65%) work in cities. As these parameters show, the sample
collected in TALIS 2018 is very similar to the broader population of
Australian teachers.

of stress have also been associated with teaching experience (Collie,
2021), suggesting they may change over the career. At the schoollevel, we examined school size and proportion of students from
low socio-economic backgrounds. Prior research has suggested that
school size and socio-economic status (SES) can be linked with

3.2.1. Sources of stress
Sources of stress were assessed with the “Work Stress” scale in
TALIS 2018. The question stem for these items is “Thinking about
your job at this school, to what extent are the following sources of
stress in your work?” Workload stress was assessed with three items
(“Having too much lesson preparation,” “Having too many lessons
to teach,” and “Having too much marking.”). Student behavior stress
was assessed with two items (“Maintaining classroom discipline”
and “Being intimidated or verbally abused by students”). Expectation stress was assessed with three items (“Being held responsible
for students' achievement,” “Keeping up with changing requirement from <local, municipality/regional, state, or national/
federal> authorities,” and “Addressing parent or guardian concerns”). Reliability was estimated with McDonald's omega (u) and
was .76 for workload stress, 0.75 for student behavior stress, and
0.62 for expectation stress.2

3.4. Preliminary analyses
Preliminary conﬁrmatory factor analyses (CFA) were conducted
to ascertain the psychometric properties of the measures. Separate
models were estimated for the proﬁle indicators (the sources of
stress) and the outcomes (strain, occupational commitment). The
measurement model for the proﬁle indicator variables was estimated at the teacher-level. This is because the indicators are only
estimated at the teacher-level, and then the teacher proﬁles are
used to estimate school-level proﬁles. In contrast, the measurement model for the outcomes was multilevel because the outcomes
were modelled at both the teacher- and school-level. For the
multilevel analysis, isomorphism (equality) of the factor loadings
was tested by comparing a model where factor loadings were freely
estimated at both levels with a constrained model. Changes in ﬁt
of  0.015 for RMSEA and  0.01 in CFI and TLI were used to
establish invariance (Chen, 2007; Cheung & Rensvold, 2002). The
estimates of reliability (omega) and intraclass correlation coefﬁcients (ICC) reported in the “Measures” sections were calculated
from these measurement models. Factor scores were saved from
the measurement models and used in main analyses, together with
manifest scores reﬂecting the teacher and school characteristics.

3.2.2. Teacher and school background characteristics
For teacher background characteristics, teacher gender was
coded 0 for females and 1 for males. Teaching experience was
measured in years. For school background characteristics, school
size was assessed as 1 (under 250 students), 2 (250e400 students),
3 (500e749 students), 4 (750e999 students), or 5 (>1000 students). Proportion of students from low socio-economic status
(SES) backgrounds was coded as 1 (0%), 2 (1e10%), 3 (11e30%), 4
(31e60%), or 5 (more than 60%).
3.2.3. Teachers and school outcomes
Strain was assessed with 3 items from the TALIS 2018 “Experiences” scale (“I experience stress in my work,” “My job negatively
impacts my mental health,” and “My job negatively impacts my
physical health”). Reliability was adequate at the teacher-level
(u ¼ 0.84) and school-level (u ¼ 0.95). The intraclass correlation
(ICC), which shows the proportion of variance at the school-level,
was 8%. Although this proportion is somewhat modest, it is sufﬁcient to support the need for multilevel analyses (Bliese et al.,
2018).
Occupational commitment was assessed with 4 items (“The advantages of being a teacher clearly outweigh the disadvantages,” “If
I could decide again, I would still choose to work as a teacher,” “I
regret that I decided to become a teacher” [reverse scored], and “I
wonder whether it would have been better to choose another
profession” [reverse scored]). Reliability was adequate at the
teacher-level (u ¼ 0.84) and school-level (u ¼ 0.98). The ICC was
7%.

3.5. Phase one: single-level latent proﬁle analyses
Using single-level latent proﬁle analysis (LPA), teacher burden
proﬁles were estimated based on the means and variances of the
proﬁle indicators at Level 1 (L1). We estimated solutions including
1 to 8 proﬁles with 10,000 random sets of start values, 1000 iterations, and 500 ﬁnal stage optimizations. We checked that the best
log-likelihood value was properly replicated for all models. Several
indices of ﬁt were consulted to ascertain the adequacy of the solutions, along with elbow plots (e.g., Morin & Litalien, 2019). More
precisely, we used the Akaike information criterion (AIC), the
consistent-AIC (CAIC), the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and
the sample-size-adjusted-BIC (SSA-BIC). For these indices, a lower
value indicates better ﬁt. For the elbow plots, the point at which the
decline in the CAIC, BIC, and SSA-BIC noticeably ﬂattens is used to
guide model selection (Morin et al., 2016). The p-value from the
adjusted LoeMendeleRubin Likelihood Ratio Test is also reported
(pLMR), where a signiﬁcant p-value provides support for a solution
over a solution with one less proﬁle. Alongside these indices, we
also considered parsimony, conceptual relevance, and statistical
adequacy to determine the optimal solution.
Once the optimal solution was identiﬁed, models were run to
test the teacher background characteristics (gender, teaching
experience) as predictors of proﬁle membership. Associations with
predictors were assessed using a multinomial logistic link function

3.6. Phase two: multilevel latent proﬁle analysis
Using multilevel LPA, school proﬁles were estimated based on
the distinct proportion of teacher proﬁles. As such, the school
proﬁles were identiﬁed based on the relative frequency of the
various teacher burden proﬁles, rather than based on the means
and variance as in phase one. To maintain the stability of the
teacher proﬁles identiﬁed in phase one, the manual 3-step
approach was used (Litalien et al., 2019; see also; Collie,
Malmberg et al., 2020). Solutions with 1e8 school proﬁles were
estimated using at least 10,000 random start values, 1000 iterations, and 500 ﬁnal stage optimizations. Model selection relied on
the identical criteria speciﬁed under phase one (with the exception
of the pLMR value, which is not available for multilevel models).
After determining the optimal solution, the same analyses outlined
in phase one involving predictors and outcomes were rundthis
time at the school-level. Namely, the school background characteristics (school size, proportion of low-SES students) were tested
as predictors of proﬁle membership. In addition, between-proﬁle
mean differences on the outcomes (school-average strain, occupational commitment, and self-efﬁcacy) were examined with and
without controls for school background characteristics (school size,
proportion of students from low SES backgrounds).

5. Discussion
The aim of the present study was to examine the extent to which
there are major proﬁles of teachers for whom three common
sources of stress at work co-occur in distinct ways, and to see
whether different proﬁles of schools can also be identiﬁed based on
the prevalence of different teacher proﬁles within schools. In phase
one, latent proﬁle analysis (LPA) revealed ﬁve teacher proﬁles: the
Low-Burden, Mixed-Burden-Workload, Mixed-Burden-Behavior,
Average-Burden, and High-Burden proﬁles. Results demonstrated
that male teachers and more experienced teachers were more
likely to be members of the less burdened proﬁles. In addition, the
Low-Burden proﬁle and the two Mixed-Burden proﬁles typically
displayed the lowest strain and highest occupational commitment.
In phase two, three school proﬁles were identiﬁed: the Lower
Pressure Climate (Workload), the Lower Pressure Climate
(Behavior), and the Higher Pressure Climate. Larger schools were
more likely to correspond to the Lower Pressure Climate (Workload) proﬁle than to the Lower Pressure Climate (Behavior) proﬁle.
In addition, the two Lower Pressure Climate proﬁles typically displayed the lowest school-average levels of strain and the highest
school-average occupational commitment. Key ﬁndings are discussed below.
5.1. Findings of note from the teacher-level results
Five teacher proﬁles were identiﬁed in analyses. Teachers in the
Low-Burden proﬁle represented less than 1 in 10 teachers in the
sampledan understandably small proportion given that teaching is
well-recognized as a stressful profession (Gallup, 2014). The two
Mixed-Burden proﬁles reﬂected different patterns of stress: one
higher in workload stress, the other higher in student behavior
stress. Together, the two Mixed-Burden proﬁles represented 3 in 10
teachers. The Average-Burden proﬁle was the largest, representing
4 in 10 teachers in the sample. This suggests that many teachers are
faring reasonably well, but still experiencing regular sources of

the profession, this is typically accompanied by leadership roles
that can have large workloads.

5.2. Findings of note from the school-level results
Three school proﬁles were identiﬁed. Two were named Lower
Pressure Climate proﬁlesdone more reﬂective of workload stress
and the other more reﬂective of student behavior stress. More
precisely, the Lower Pressure Climate (Workload) proﬁle comprised
a large proportion of the Mixed-Burden-Workload teacher proﬁle.
In contrast, the Lower Pressure Climate (Behavior) proﬁle
comprised a large proportion of the Mixed-Burden-Behavior
teacher proﬁle. Together, these two school proﬁles, which each
represented about 2 in 10 schools in our sample, replicate the
divergence between workload stress and student behavior stress
that was seen at the teacher-level. This ﬁnding is notable because it

In terms of different practices that can be used to address the
needs of each proﬁle, our ﬁndings suggest these practices should be
implemented by school leaders to target both individuals as well as
the school as a whole. Starting with workload stress, assigning
supportive mentors, developing professional learning communities
where lesson preparation and marking moderation can be shared,
providing a reduced workload for early career teachers, and task
management strategy development can be helpful at the
individual-level (Carroll et al., 2020; Pogodzinski, 2015). At the
same time, workload is likely to be highly dependent upon schoolwide policies and practices. As such, school leaders may want to
consider efforts to reduce workload across the school, such as
reducing requirements for compliance and accountability work
where feasible (Putwain & von der Embse, 2019). For student
behavior stress, helping teachers to build positive teacher-student
relationships is one approach that has shown promise (Pianta
et al., 2012). At the school-level, developing school-wide supports
for helping teachers to manage disruptive behavior and building a
positive school climate are vitaldincluding creation of shared
language and norms (Weissberg et al., 2015). In this vein, trauma
informed school wide approaches (e.g., Howard, 2018) are
becoming increasingly prevalent in Australian schools. Finally, for
expectation stress, school leaders that demonstrate their trust in
teachers as professionals can help to reduce stress among individual teachers and the whole school (Ford et al., 2019). Likewise,
efforts to develop positive school-home partnerships are important
to ensure teachers, school leaders, and parents are aligned in their
goals for students (Carroll et al., 2020).

shows that in addition to individual differences in stress levels (e.g.,
some teachers may struggle more than others to manage disruptive
student behavior), there are also school-level differences in stress
levels (e.g., in some schools, teachers collectively struggle with
disruptive student behavior). In other words, the context matters
for teacher stressdit is not enough to ask teachers to manage their
own stress. There must also be school-level support. This ﬁnding
thus highlights the importance of efforts that not only consider the
individual, but also the school as a whole (discussed in implications
below). The third school proﬁle, the Higher Pressure Climate, was
characterized by substantial proportions of the Average- and HighBurden teacher proﬁles. The size of this third proﬁle is a concern
given that it represented 6 in 10 schools; however, this proportion
does align with broader recognition of the escalating job demands
faced by teachers in recent years (Parliament of Australia, 2019).
Once again, the existence of this proﬁle underscores the importance of whole school efforts to support teachers.
In terms of predictors, larger schools were more likely to
correspond to the Lower Pressure Climate (Workload) proﬁle than
to the Lower Pressure Climate (Behavior) proﬁle. This is an interesting ﬁnding given that larger schools can often come with an
economy of scale, which can help to reduce workload given there
are more resources and staff members. At the same time, researchers have shown that larger schools often have less teacher
input in decision-making (Collie, 2021), which could impact
workload stress, for example, if teachers are being assigned extra
work without consultation. Turning to the outcomes, the ﬁndings
align with JD-R theory (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017) and recent
research extending that to the school-level (Collie, Malmberg et al.,
2020). The Lower Pressure Climate (Behavior) proﬁle displayed the
lowest school-average strain and highest occupational commitment. This was followed by the Lower Pressure Climate (Workload)
proﬁle and then the Higher Pressure Climate (however, the significant difference in strain between the two Lower Pressure Climate
proﬁles disappeared after controls for school size and SES were
included). The poorer outcomes for the Higher Pressure Climate
proﬁle is unsurprising given that this proﬁle had large proportions
of the Average- and High-Burden proﬁles. In sum, although prior
variable-centered research has shown that school-level stressors
are linked with school outcomes (e.g., Collie, 2021), the present
study extends that by revealing three school proﬁles that are
differently associated with the outcomesdand thus the ﬁndings
reveal the proﬁles of schools where stressors are and are not linked
with poorer school-level outcomes. Future research that considers
other school-level outcomes, such as teacher engagement and
instructional support will be important to further elucidate the role
of these school proﬁles.

5.4. Limitations and future directions
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the
results of our study. First, the present study was cross-sectional and
so we could not ascertain the extent to which teachers and schools
move in and out of the different proﬁles. Future research that examines trajectories of stress, and predictors and outcomes over
time, will be important. Second, by using the TALIS 2018 data, we
had access to a sample that is considered nationally-representative
and that has a large enough sample size at the school-level to
enable multilevel modeling. Notwithstanding these strengths, use
of secondary data does come with some constraints, such as the
available variables. Although the selection of variables in the present study was determined based on theory, it will be important to
extend the examination to other outcomes and other sources of
stress to broaden knowledge of stress proﬁles. In addition, replication is an important future step to ascertain the extent to which
the proﬁles identiﬁed in the present study are evident in other
samples of teachersdand in other countries. Finally, qualitative
research is important for delving further into some of the reasons
behind the ﬁndings revealed in our study and particularly in understanding the nuanced interplay between combinations of
teacher- and school-level stressors. Such research will help to build
a more complete picture of teacher stress.

5.3. Implications for practice
The different proﬁles provide direction for guiding efforts to
support teachers and schools. Teachers in the Mixed-BurdenWorkload proﬁle would likely beneﬁt from efforts to reduce workload pressure, whereas teachers in the Mixed-Burden-Behavior
proﬁle may need extra support to manage challenging student
behavior. Teachers in the Average- and High-Burden proﬁles would
likely beneﬁt from efforts to address all sources of stress, which may
include broader stress reduction interventions (see for example
Taylor et al., 2021). At the school level, similar targeting is relevant
for the school proﬁles: a larger focus on reducing workload stress for
the Lower Pressure Climate (Workload) proﬁle appears warranted,
whereas a larger focus on strategies and support for managing
student behavior stress for the Lower Pressure Climate (Behavior)
proﬁle is likely important. For the Higher Pressure Climate, attention
to all sources of stress appears necessary.

6. Conclusion
The present study examined three common sources of stress in
teachers’ work and identiﬁed teacher and school proﬁles. The results identiﬁed stress proﬁles that reﬂect distinct subpopulations of
teachers and schools. The ﬁndings also showed that the three
