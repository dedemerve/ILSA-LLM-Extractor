A B S T R A C T
This study explores teachers' expectancies for success, subjective values, and four dimensions of the perceived 
costs of integrating professional development into their teaching practice to support students' self-regulated 
learning (SRL). Teachers from 10 Estonian schools participated in a professional development program and 
evaluated their motivation and teaching practices at the beginning and end of the program. Drawing on situated 
expectancy-value theory and using person-oriented analysis, we identified two distinct motivational profiles at 
both time points. The profiles with higher motivation tended to report a higher extent in some of the SRL-related 
teaching practices. The findings highlight the need for a longitudinal approach to studying teachers’ motivation 
in professional development.
1. Introduction
An important goal of contemporary education is developing students' 
ability to manage their own learning, with self-regulated learning as a 
key concept. Self-regulated learning (SRL) may be defined as a 
constructive process where learners set goals and attempt to plan, 
monitor, control, and reflect on their cognition, motivation, behavior, 
and environment (Pintrich, 2000). Both the school context and teachers' 
individual characteristics are important for supporting students' SRL (De 
Smul et al., 2019), as is SRL-related in-service professional development 
(PD) for teachers (Hirt et al., 2025; Panadero, 2017; Vosniadou et al., 
2024). There are positive examples of interventions that foster students' 
SRL, however, interventions conducted by researchers tend to exert a 
higher effect on student outcomes than interventions performed by 
teachers (Dignath et al., 2008). Teachers' lower motivation and expec­
tations of success have been suggested as possible reasons for the limited 
effectiveness of SRL-related PD (Kikas et al., 2022; Kramarski and 
Heaysman, 2021). Other recent findings have indicated that some 
teachers might not know or value the importance of SRL promotion 
(Vosniadou et al., 2024). Therefore, there have been calls to investigate 
relationships between teachers' SRL practices and SRL-related beliefs 
and self-efficacy (Heirweg et al., 2022; Vosniadou et al., 2024), and to 
study teachers' competences in supporting students' SRL in the context of 
PD (Hirt et al., 2025). The present study focuses on teachers as agents in 
fostering their students’ SRL (Karlen et al., 2020), with motivation as an 
important link between PD and teaching practices (Osman & Warner, 
2020).
More specifically, teachers' perceived costs might be a factor in un­
derstanding their relatively low SRL promotion in practice (Hirt et al., 
2025), as teachers might refrain from SRL instruction due to time and 
effort that is needed for implementation (Cleary et al., 2022; Sins et al., 
2024). In teachers' day-to-day work, the perceived costs of SRL in­
struction and PD integration might be experienced in multiple ways. The 
implementation of new teaching practices might require too much en­
ergy (i.e., task effort cost), the attendance of PD sessions and application 
of new techniques and strategies might be perceived as a reason for 
missing out on other important things (i.e., loss of valued alternatives 
cost), the learning situation and implementation could be perceived as 
stressful (i.e., emotional cost), or the other commitments of teachers, 
such as lesson planning, communication or administrative tasks (i.e., 
outside effort cost) might be perceived as hindrances for SRL promotion 
and PD integration. A recent study highlighted the need to explore the 
multiple dimensions of perceived costs, with an emphasis on outside 
effort cost as a possible barrier for integrating SRL-related PD with 
teaching practice (Norma et al., 2026). International educational data 
also highlights the possible significance of perceived costs in the context 
of professional learning. According to the latest results from the OECD 
Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS; OECD, 2025), “lack

The aforementioned research gap has practical implications for PD 
implementation, as different dimensions of cost might need addressing 
in specific ways. For example, it would be important to differentiate 
whether the high perceived cost of PD implementation is due to 
administrative tasks (i.e., outside effort cost) or anxiety that occurs 
during the learning situation (i.e., emotional cost), the former of which 
might need addressing on an organizational level while the latter might 
be better targeted by PD facilitators. Furthermore, previous studies of 
teacher motivation in SRL-related PD have mostly relied on variable- 
centered approaches. However, as teachers are not a homogeneous 
group, person-oriented analysis, such as Latent Profile Analysis (LPA), 
could provide a deeper understanding of how teachers' motivational 
profiles differ and how these differences relate to their self-reported 
teaching practices. This could inform targeted interventions to support 
teachers' motivation in fostering students' SRL. While we are aware of 
one cross-sectional analysis of teachers’ expectancy-value-cost profiles 
and SRL promotion (Hirt et al., 2022), the authors of that study high­
lighted the need for a longitudinal approach. Regarding subjective task 
values (STVs) and perceived costs, a longitudinal analysis might be 
especially insightful, because unlike competence-related beliefs that 
tend to be associated with present performance, subjective values and 
perceived costs might be more strongly related to long-term interest, 
task choices and sustained behavior (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020).
Drawing on Situated Expectancy-Value Theory (SEVT; Eccles & 
Wigfield, 2020), this study explores the interplay between teachers' 
motivation and SRL-related professional development, with a focus on 
the underexplored dimensions of perceived costs, employing 
person-oriented and longitudinal analysis. While SEVT explains how 
cultural and social characteristics and processes contribute to the more 
proximal motivational determinants, the present study focuses on 
teachers' individual expectancies, values and costs, treating the 
contextual factors as a conceptual background, rather than examining 
them empirically in the current analyses. Three research questions guide 
the study: To what extent do teachers' self-reported SRL-related teaching 
practices change during the PD program? How are teachers' expec­
tancies, STVs, and different dimensions of perceived cost related to 
teachers' self-reported SRL-related teaching practices at the beginning 
and the end of the PD program? What kind of motivational profiles can 
be identified based on teachers’ motivational beliefs for integrating 
SRL-related PD into their teaching practice, both at the beginning and 
the end of the PD program?
2. Literature review
2.1. Situated Expectancy-Value Theory
There are multiple theories of human motivation that are relevant in 
educational contexts, such as Self-Determination Theory (SDT; Ryan & 
Deci, 2020), Social Cognitive Theory (SCT; Schunk & DiBenedetto, 
2020) and Situated Expectancy-Value Theory (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). 
While SDT is often used to study teachers' general work-related moti­
vation, prior SRL-related research has tended to use SCT to conceptu­
alize teacher motivation, likely because many influential SRL 
frameworks are also rooted in SCT (Panadero, 2017). Recent years have 
shown the emergence of SEVT as a commonly used motivational theory 
in the context of SRL and teacher PD, as it is compatible with the concept 
of self-efficacy from SCT (Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2020), while allowing 
for a differentiated view of task-related subjective values and cost. As the 
present study seeks to explore teachers’ costs in the context of 
SRL-related PD, we selected SEVT as the primary lens for the study.
SEVT posits that “individuals' expectancies for success and subjective 
task values are the most proximal psychological determinants of task 
and activity choice, performance, and engagement in the chosen activ­
ities” (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020, p. 3). Expectancies for success are in­
dividuals’ beliefs about how well they will perform a given task. The 
subjective value of a task is a function of three components: intrinsic 
value, utility value, and attainment value (Eccles et al., 1983). Intrinsic 
value refers to the enjoyment of performing the task, utility value refers 
to the perceived usefulness of completing the task, and attainment value 
is conceptualized as the relative identity-based importance of engaging 
with the task (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Perceived cost has also been 
grouped with the three aforementioned value components, but studies 
in the last decade have shown that it often emerges as a distinct factor of 
proximal determinants of performance and outcomes (e.g., Barron & 
Hulleman, 2015).
Eccles et al. (1983) initially proposed three dimensions that influ­
ence an individual's perceived cost of success or failure: effort, loss of 
valued alternatives, and the psychological cost of failure. For decades, 
perceived cost was neglected by researchers or included as a 
one-dimensional construct, but in recent years there has been growing 
interest in exploring potential measures, dimensions, and effects of 
perceived cost (Eccles & Wigfield, 2024). One of the most influential 
conceptualizations of the dimensions of cost was proposed by Flake 
et al., who defined cost as “negative appraisals of what is invested, 
required or given up to engage in a task” (Flake et al., 2015, p. 237). 
They described four distinct but related dimensions of cost: task effort 
cost (e.g., “This class requires too much effort”), outside effort cost (e.g., 
“I have so many other responsibilities that I am unable to put in the 
effort that is necessary for this class”), loss of valued alternatives cost (e. 
g., “I have to sacrifice too much to be in this class”), and emotional cost 
(e.g., “This class is too stressful”; Flake et al., 2015, p. 237).
As individuals' motivation is situated, their perceptions of cost 
depend on the present situation. Recent research has found that students' 
expected or anticipated costs for a task are related to achievement and 
outcomes in a distinct way compared to the immediate or experienced 
negative appraisals of engaging with that task (Beymer, Flake, & 
Schmidt, 2023). Therefore, it is important to measure perceived costs in 
multiple moments of time and be clear whether respondents are 
prompted to answer about their anticipated or experienced costs. In line 
with calls for a more situated approach to studying motivational pro­
cesses, Kim et al. (2023) explored the relationship between students’ 
general and momentary cost perceptions. They found that some di­
mensions of general cost perceptions (i.e., outside effort cost and 
emotional cost) were significantly associated with their momentary 
equivalents while for other dimensions (i.e., task effort cost and loss of 
valued alternatives) such associations were not found (Kim et al., 2023). 
Additionally, for most cost dimensions, the relationship between general 
and momentary cost perceptions was moderated by the use of motiva­
tional regulation strategies, but this was not the case for outside effort 
cost (Kim et al., 2023). These findings highlight the importance of 
adopting a multidimensional approach to studying perceived cost.
2.2. Teachers’ expectancies, values, and perceived costs
Although SEVT was initially developed from research focusing on 
students' motivation, it has also been used to study teacher motivation, 
both as a learner and a teacher. In line with earlier findings, rooted in 
Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura, 1977), that teachers' self-efficacy and 
beliefs about the importance of SRL were related to SRL promotion 
(Dignath-van Ewijk, 2016), Karlen et al. (2023) found that teachers' 
self-efficacy and the perceived intrinsic value of promoting students' SRL 
were positively associated with their self-reported promotion and their

students' perceived promotion of metacognition. Another recent study 
found that teachers' self-efficacy in promoting SRL indirectly predicted 
students' self-efficacy, utility, and task value for SRL via teachers' and 
students' perceived SRL promotion (Jud et al., 2024). This implies that to 
influence student outcomes, teachers' motivation to promote students’ 
SRL must result in SRL-promoting teaching practices. Furthermore, 
Cleary et al. (2022) highlighted that self-efficacy for SRL-promotion is 
situated, finding that teachers reported lower efficacy for promoting SRL 
with students perceived as academically at risk, compared to high 
achieving students. As the concept of self-efficacy is both theoretically 
and empirically similar to expectancies for success, and the definition of 
the latter was based on the former (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020), we argue 
that adopting SEVT to study teacher motivation in PD allows for a more 
nuanced view of subjective task values and costs, while maintaining the 
strong predictive power of self-efficacy.
A more detailed analysis of teachers' perceived value and cost di­
mensions showed that in addition to teachers' expectancies for success 
and the perceived intrinsic value of promoting SRL, there was a signif­
icant association between utility and attainment values and SRL pro­
motion, although no clear relationship was found for teachers' perceived 
cost and promotion of SRL (Jud et al., 2023). Using a person-oriented 
approach, Hirt et al. (2022) found three distinct latent profiles with 
significant differences regarding teachers’ expectancies, intrinsic, util­
ity, and attainment values, and opportunity costs regarding the pro­
motion of SRL. In terms of their self-reported promotion of SRL, the least 
motivated group of teachers (lower expectancies and values and higher 
costs) reported significantly less SRL promotion, showing the explana­
tory power of the person-oriented approach in studying teacher moti­
vation. However, the study was cross-sectional, and the authors 
emphasized the need for a longitudinal approach.
While the aforementioned studies focused on teachers' motivation to 
promote students' SRL, motivation is also an important determinant of 
successful PD, and SEVT has been used to explain variations in changes 
in teachers' practice (e.g., Bostr¨om & Palm, 2020). Heirweg et al. (2022)
highlight the particular challenge of teacher motivation in the context of 
school-wide PD programs, where teachers' subjective value of SRL and 
motivation might be more diverse, compared to PD programs that are 
organized for individual teachers. To measure teachers’ motivation to 
integrate professional development into practice Osman and Warner 
(2020) developed the EVC-PD scale that consists of three distinct but 
interrelated factors, where expectancy and value are positively related 
but have a negative relationship with cost. However, the EVC-PD scale 
treats cost as a unidimensional construct with none of the items referring 
to outside effort cost.
Qualitative analysis in a recent study revealed that emotional cost 
was rarely mentioned by teachers, whereas outside effort cost (e.g., the 
effort required for routine or specific unrelated tasks and the strain 
experienced during demanding periods) was frequently mentioned, 
emphasizing the importance of a multidimensional perspective when 
studying teachers' perceptions of cost in professional development 
(Norma et al., 2026). A similar conclusion was reached by Alp et al. 
(2023), who also complemented the use of Osman and Warner's (2020)
EVC-PD scale with qualitative analysis, finding that outside effort cost 
played an important role in teachers' perceived cost of PD. External 
barriers, such as time constraints or parental resistance were also found 
by Cleary et al. (2022) in their qualitative analysis of teachers who 
exhibited weaker SRL-knowledge and skills after participating in PD.
Recent intervention research further illustrates how different cost 
perceptions manifest in actual teaching behavior. In a study of a PD 
program focused on explicit SRL instruction, Sins et al. (2024) found 
that while participating teachers significantly increased their explicit 
strategy instruction compared to a control group, the overall occurrence 
remained low (~3.5% of observed instances), with 7 of the 18 teachers 
manifesting no explicit instruction after having received extensive 
training. Their qualitative interviews revealed that while teachers 
seemed to value the PD, a common challenge was to actually implement 
it in practice, noting that it required effort to turn it into a routine (Sins 
et al., 2024). This suggests that high subjective value might not be suf­
ficient to guarantee implementation if the perceived costs of integrating 
PD remain high. These qualitative insights are complemented by lon­
gitudinal evidence from a multi-day SRL PD program by Hirt et al. 
(2025), who found that while the intervention improved teachers’ 
intrinsic value for promoting SRL compared to a control group, oppor­
tunity costs and task effort costs could not be reduced despite imple­
mentation ideas and learning materials provided by the program. The 
authors noted that persisting high effort costs could possibly explain the 
lack of intervention effect for self-reported promotion of SRL and 
metacognition (Hirt et al., 2025).
Although the present study does not empirically analyze the 
contextual influences of teachers' motivation and SRL-promotion, it is 
important to note that cultural, organizational and policy-level factors 
interact with teachers' motivational constructs. For example, Bostr¨om 
and Palm (2020) found that competing policy demands, such as pre­
paring students for national tests and implementing a new curriculum, 
constrained teachers' perceived opportunities to try new activities in 
their classrooms, exemplifying outside effort cost external to the de­
mands of the PD program itself affecting teachers' integration of PD. This 
pattern was also present in teachers' self-reported lower willingness to 
prioritize the implementation of PD practices during periods with high 
workload, compared to their general motivation for the changes. The 
authors suggest that the mandatory grading of lower-secondary students 
might have played a role in why year-7 teachers perceived the imple­
mentation of a new curriculum more demanding than year-4 teachers, as 
well as highlighting school- and class-level factors, such as perceived 
support from principals or large class sizes. They hypothesized that 
principals' support might be perceived as an indicator of the attainment 
value of PD-related changes, while problematic class situations might 
relate to lower expectancies for success. In the specific context of SRL 
implementation, De Smul et al. (2019) found that school-level SRL-re­
lated vision was associated with teachers' beliefs about the importance 
of SRL for students, reflecting teachers' attainment and utility values, 
while reflective dialogue among colleagues was related to teachers’ 
self-efficacy for promoting SRL, corresponding closely to expectancies 
for success.
To summarize, SEVT has been used to study teachers' SRL promotion 
and integration of PD, but there are still some less-explored aspects 
which deserve attention in order to understand and support teachers in 
SRL-related PD. Specifically, prior research has focused less on 
perceived cost as a potential motivational determinant, with some 
studies examining cost as a unidimensional construct, instead of a 
multidimensional one. Moreover, most of the studies on this topic have 
utilized variable-oriented approaches that could be extended with 
person-oriented analysis to identify possible different motivational 
profiles among teachers. Furthermore, most of the studies that have 
explored the relations between teachers’ motivation and SRL promotion 
have been cross-sectional, and could be extended with findings from 
longitudinal analysis.
The present study aimed to explore the relationship between teach­
ers’ motivation to integrate the knowledge and skills acquired during 
the PD program, and their SRL-related teaching practices at the begin­
ning and end of the PD program, emphasizing the underexplored four 
dimensions of perceived costs. We used person-oriented analysis to 
identify distinct motivational profiles and their possible differences in 
teaching practices. The following three research questions guided the 
study (see Fig. 1):
(RQ1): To what extent do teachers' self-reported SRL-related teach­
ing practices change during the PD program? In line with a previous 
study (Norma et al., 2026), we hypothesize that teachers’ self-reported 
SRL-related teaching practices will significantly increase from the 
beginning to the end of the PD program (Hypothesis 1).
(RQ2): How are teachers' expectancies, STVs, and different di­
mensions of perceived cost related to teachers' self-reported SRL-related

teaching practices at the beginning and the end of the PD program? 
According to SEVT (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020), expectancies and values 
are proximal psychological determinants of activity choice, performance 
and engagement; and empirical evidence in the context of 
SRL-promotion confirms that these beliefs are associated with 
self-reported practices (e.g., Jud et al., 2023; Karlen et al., 2023). 
Therefore, we hypothesize that teachers' initial and eventual expec­
tancies and STVs will be positively associated with their SRL-related 
practices at the beginning and the end of the PD program (Hypothesis 
2). As perceived costs are defined as negative appraisals related to 
engaging with a task (Flake et al., 2015), with effort (Sins et al., 2024), 
opportunity costs (Hirt et al., 2025), and external barriers (Cleary et al., 
2022) as possible hindrances to SRL implementation, we hypothesize 
that teachers' initial and eventual perceived costs (task effort, outside 
effort, loss of valued alternatives, and emotional cost) will be negatively 
associated with their SRL-related practices at the beginning and the end 
of the PD program (Hypothesis 3). Finally, based on large-scale inter­
national data (OECD, 2025) as well as qualitative analyses of teachers’ 
integration of PD (Alp et al., 2023; Norma et al., 2026), we hypothesize 
that teachers will report significantly higher levels of task effort cost and 
outside effort cost compared to emotional cost or loss of valued alter­
natives (Hypothesis 4).
(RQ3): What kind of motivational profiles can be identified based on 
teachers’ motivational beliefs (expectancies for success, STVs, perceived 
costs) for integrating SRL-related PD into their teaching practice, and 
how do they relate to teaching practices? Drawing on previous person- 
oriented research that suggests teachers are not a homogeneous group, 
with more motivated teachers reporting higher SRL-promotion (Hirt 
et al., 2022), we expect that distinct motivational profiles will emerge 
with significantly different expectancies, STVs and perceived cost. 
Specifically, we hypothesize that the teachers who belong to profiles 
with higher expectancies, STVs and lower perceived costs at the begin­
ning of the PD program, will report a greater extent of using SRL-related 
teaching practices, both at the beginning and end of the program (Hy­
pothesis 5); while teachers who belong to profiles with higher expec­
tancies, STVs and lower perceived costs at the end of the PD program, 
will report a greater extent of using SRL-related teaching practices at the 
end of the program (Hypothesis 6).
3. Methods
3.1. Research design and context
This study employed a longitudinal survey linked to a year-long PD 
program, with data collected across four waves (see Table 1). The timing 
of the data collection was designed to balance the aims of the research 
and participant load, with self-reported teaching practices collected at 
the first (T1) and the last (T4) seminars, to capture changes over the 
whole duration of the program. The initial measurement of teachers' 
motivation (T2) occurred at the end of the third session, allowing time 
for classroom implementation and ensuring that we measured teachers’ 
experienced costs, rather than merely anticipated costs of integrating 
PD. The second measurement point for motivation (T3) was at the end of 
the 10th session, when the teachers received the last practical assign­
ment, to assess motivation during the active implementation phase.
The study was conducted during the 2023/24 academic year, when 
teachers from 10 Estonian schools participated in a PD program that was 
focused on supporting students' self-regulated learning. The Estonian 
education system is known for its relatively high results in Programme 
for International Student Assessment (PISA), and the high autonomy of 
teachers and school leaders, but recent years have shown an increase in 
discussions about teacher workload and stress (Eisenschmidt et al., 
2023). According to TALIS 2024 (OECD, 2025), Estonian lower sec­
ondary teachers tend to report less participation in (at least) monthly 
collaborative professional learning in their school (15.4% of teachers), 
compared to the OECD average (21.1%); and Estonian teachers are also 
slightly less likely to feel that professional development activities in the 
last 12 months had a positive impact on their teaching practices (51.2% 
of teachers), compared to the OECD average (55.2%). In a similar 
pattern to international results, Estonian lower secondary teachers cite 
the lack of time due to other commitments and responsibilities as the 
main barrier to their participation in professional learning. Notably, the 
share of Estonian teachers who report experiencing a lot of stress in their 
work (28.5%) is higher than the OECD average (19.3%); however, the 
share of teachers who agree that too much administrative work is a 
source of stress (39.1%) is slightly lower than average (51.7%). Finally, 
the shares of teachers in Estonia who agree that there are too many 
change initiatives (30.5%) and the lack of necessary resources needed to 
implement change initiatives (28.9%) are comparable to the OECD av­
erages (31.1% and 30.7% respectively). These results further highlight 
the need to focus on different dimensions of teachers’ perceived costs,

The PD program was jointly organized by the educational program 
Teach for Estonia and Tallinn University with the aim of supporting 
teachers in fostering their students’ self-regulated learning. The program 
consisted of three 180-min joint seminars for all school teams in the 
beginning, middle and end of the program, where teachers from 10 
schools participated together, and eight 90-min monthly learning com­
munity sessions in each school, with individual tasks for integrating PD 
with practice between each meeting. Both the seminars and the monthly 
sessions at schools were facilitated by instructors from Teach for Estonia. 
The content of the program was broadly based on the model of SRL 
proposed by Pintrich (2000). Table A1 in the appendix presents the key 
topics and theoretical groundings of the seminars and sessions.
3.2. Participating schools and teachers
The study utilized a convenience sample of 171 teachers nested 
within 10 Estonian schools. Participants were recruited in a two-stage 
process. First, schools were recruited voluntarily by the organizers of 
the Teach for Estonia program, paying a participation fee to cover 
program expenses (see Table 2 for school types). Second, within 
participating schools, school leaders determined teacher selection 
(mandatory or voluntary), forming learning communities of approxi­
mately 15 teachers (ranging from 13 to 23).
Response rates fluctuated across the four measurement points due to 
the nature of longitudinal data collection during seminars, where 
teachers were absent due to illness, other obligations or decided not to 
participate in data collection at a given time. Response rates ranged from 
67.8% at T3 (N = 116) to 80.7% at T2 (N = 138). Detailed participation 
numbers by school and wave are presented in Table 2.
The participants were informed that the data was being collected for 
research purposes and were given the option to withdraw their partic­
ipation. Demographic data was collected at the first measurement point 
with 136 teachers reporting their work experience, main grade levels 
and main subjects taught (see Table 3). We relied on participant- 
generated pseudonyms to combine longitudinal data. Slight in­
consistencies in pseudonym use altered the number of comparable cases 
at different measurement points.

3.3. Measures
Teachers' self-reported SRL-related teaching practices were collected 
with a 27-item questionnaire that was originally designed for teachers' 
self-assessment of their support for students’ learning-to-learn compe­
tence as a part of a larger Estonian project for development and 
assessment of general competencies (Kikas et al., 2018), and has been 
used in teacher education studies in Estonia (Norma et al., 2026). Items 
were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Does not corre­
spond to my activities at all”) to 5 (“Corresponds to my activities very 
well”) with none of the items reverse-coded. Composite scores were used 
by calculating the mean of the items in each factor.
Exploratory factor analysis in a previous study (N = 255) revealed a 
5-factor structure (χ2 = 316, df = 226, p < .001, TLI = .95, RMSEA =
.04). Given the pre-existing validation of the scale on a larger sample, 
and the relative complexity (27 items) of the scale relative to the present 
sample (N = 136), we relied on internal consistency and inter-factor 
correlations (ranging from r = .38 to .67), to confirm the validity of 
the scale in the present study. While developed for the local context, the 
instrument's factor structure aligns with the theoretical distinction be­
tween direct SRL-related strategy instruction and indirect SRL promo­
tion that was proposed in a review by Dignath and Veenman (2021). 
Two of the factors can be categorized as direct strategy instruction: 
Promoting Metacognition (PM, 11 items, e.g., “I let students create a 
plan on how to implement longer projects and monitor if the students act 
according to the plan.“, α = .87, ω = .87), which targets the different 
phases of self-regulation (Pintrich, 2000); and Teaching Cognitive 
Strategies (CS, 5 items, e.g., “We practice with students creating oral or 
written summaries, diagrams, drawings, mind maps, etc. about the 
learned material.“, α = .79, ω = .79), capturing the teaching of effective 
learning strategies (Fiorella & Mayer, 2015; Weinstein et al., 2018). 
Three remaining factors align with the indirect promotion of SRL 
through the creation of powerful learning environments: Providing 
Emotional Support (ES, 5 items, e.g., “I create an environment where 
students dare to express their opinions, ask questions, and voice their 
doubts.“, α = .71, ω = .72, Pianta et al., 2008); Scaffolding (SC, 3 items, 
e.g., “I provide supportive hints when a student is stuck with a task.“, α 
= .59, ω = .63; Van de Pol et al., 2010); and Prompting Concrete Ex­
amples (CE, 3 items, e.g., “I ask students to provide examples from their 
lives and previously learned material related to the topic being stud­
ied.“, α = .76, ω = .76; Assor et al., 2002). While the McDonald's Omega 
estimates for four of the five subscales were above the tentative guide­
line of ω ≥.65, the estimate for the Scaffolding subscale was slightly 
below that level (Kalkbrenner, 2023). As Kalkbrenner (2023) notes, 
these are not absolute thresholds; consequently, the subscale was 
retained to preserve content validity given the exploratory nature of the 
study and low item count.
To assess teachers' expectancy and perceived value of integrating PD 
into practice, we modified an adapted and translated version of Osman 
and Warner's (2020) EVC-PD scale that has been used in an earlier study 
in the Estonian context (Norma et al., 2026). The items were rated on a 
6-point Likert scale (1 = Disagree completely, 6 = Agree completely), 
and composite scores were calculated as the mean of items. Sample 
items include: Expectancy (3 items, e.g., “I know that I can effectively 
put into practice the things presented in this training”, α = .78, ω = .79); 
and Subjective Task Value (3 items, e.g., “Participating in this training 
will help me in my job.“, α = .76, ω = .77). McDonald's Omega estimates 
were within the acceptable levels of ≥.65 to .80 for both subscales 
(Kalkbrenner, 2023).
To measure the dimensions of teachers' perceived experienced cost of 
integrating PD, we adapted and translated 12 items from the four-factor 
cost scale developed by Flake et al. (2015), that has been previously 
adapted to teacher work motivation (Beymer et al., 2023) and teacher 
SRL-promotion (Hirt et al., 2022) contexts. As with the expectancy and 
value subscales, the items were rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 =
Disagree completely, 6 = Agree completely), with higher ratings 
indicating higher perceived costs, and composite scores were calculated 
as the mean of items. Sample items, back-translated from the adapted 
version, include: Task Effort (3 items, e.g., “I have to contribute too 
much energy to apply what was learned in this training”, α = .90, ω =
.90); Emotional Cost (3 items, e.g., “Applying what was learned in this 
training makes me too anxious”, α = .82, ω = .83); Outside Effort (3 
items, e.g., “Due to my other responsibilities, I do not have enough time 
to apply what was learned in this professional development program”, α 
= .88, ω = .89); and Loss of Valued Alternatives Cost (3 items, e.g., 
“Applying what was learned in this training causes me to miss too many 
other things that are important to me”, α = .87, ω = .87). McDonald's 
Omega estimates for all subscales exceeded the guideline of ≥.80, 
indicating strong internal consistency (Kalkbrenner, 2023).
For the adapted and expanded EVC-PD scale, we performed confir­
matory factor analysis to confirm the expected factor structure of ex­
pectancies, STVs, and the four dimensions of costs. The analysis 
indicated a decent model fit (CFI = .93, TLI = .92, SRMR = .05; Schu­
macker and Lomax, 2010), although it should be noted that RMSEA 
(.09) was slightly above the common .08 threshold and might indicate a 
mediocre fit (MacCallum et al., 1996). Full fit statistics, factor loadings, 
and composite reliability are reported in Appendix Table A2.
3.4. Data collection and analysis
The study employed a longitudinal design with data collected at four 
measurement points. The data was collected between August 2023 and 
June 2024 (see Table 1) using Google Forms and analyzed with Jamovi 
version 2.3.28.0 (The jamovi project, 2022). As the data collection took 
place during the seminars and learning community sessions, we decided 
to minimize participant burden with self-assessment, in order to provide 
teachers with more opportunities for discussion and active participation, 
with only two measurement points for teaching practices (T1 and T4) 
and motivation (T2 and T3) respectively.
Missing data were handled at both the item and wave levels. At the 
item level there were only two missing values in the entire dataset and 
composite scores were calculated as the mean of the available items. At 
the wave level, we employed Full Information Maximum Likelihood 
(FIML) for wave-specific modeling (e.g., CFA, LPA), while pairwise 
deletion was used for longitudinal analyses and variable-centered 
comparisons (e.g., paired sample t-tests). We examined potential attri­
tion bias by using independent samples t-test and Chi-square tests. As 
detailed in Section 4.1, these analyses revealed no differences in de­
mographics, values, or costs, although retained teachers reported 
slightly higher initial expectancies. Data distribution was assessed using 
skewness (ranging from −1.03 to 1.27) and kurtosis (ranging from −.69 
to 1.76), both of which fell within the acceptable limits of ±2 for 
parametric analyses (George & Mallery, 2019). The nested structure of 
the data (teachers within 10 schools) was analyzed by calculating 
Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICCs; see Section 4.1). However, we 
did not employ multilevel modeling in our analysis because the number 
of schools (N = 10) is substantially lower than the recommended min­
imum of 30 to 50 groups required for accurate estimation (Hox et al., 
2010). We used Welch's one-way ANOVA to examine potential con­
founding effects of teacher demographics and school context. Longitu­
dinal measurement invariance was tested for the motivational 
constructs using the lavaan package with Robust Maximum Likelihood 
(MLR) estimation. Discriminant validity for the cost scales was assessed 
using the Heterotrait-Monotrait ratio (HTMT).
The primary analyses were aligned with the research questions and 
hypotheses, with statistical significance set at p < .05. To address RQ1 
and test the predicted increase in SRL-related teaching practices (Hy­
pothesis 1), we conducted paired samples t-tests comparing practices at 
T1 and T4. Regarding RQ2, we calculated Pearson correlation co­
efficients to examine the hypothesized positive associations between 
teaching expectancies, values, and teaching practices (Hypothesis 2), 
and the predicted negative associations between teachers' perceived

costs and teaching practices (Hypothesis 3). Furthermore, we utilized 
the Friedman test with Durbin-Conover pairwise comparisons, in order 
to test for differences in teachers' cost perceptions, hypothesizing that 
teachers report higher levels of task effort and outside effort cost, 
compared to other cost dimensions (Hypothesis 4). To address RQ3, 
Latent Profile Analysis was conducted to identify distinct motivational 
profiles among participating teachers. To determine the number of 
profiles, we preferred the model with higher entropy and lower sample

profile resulted in a very small number of cases (Spurk et al., 2020). 
Finally, we used Welch's one-way ANOVA to analyze differences in 
self-reported teaching practices for different motivational profiles (Hy­

4. Results
4.1. Preliminary analysis
To examine attrition bias, we compared teachers who evaluated their 
practices both at the beginning and end of the program (N = 87) to 
teachers who did it only at the beginning of the program (N = 49). In­
dependent samples t-tests and Chi-square tests revealed no significant 
differences in initial teaching practices, task values, costs, or de­
mographics (school, experience, grade). The only significant difference 
emerged for initial expectancies, t (97) = 2.22, p = .029, with teachers 
who participated in both measurement points reporting higher initial 
expectancies. These results suggest that while teachers with lower ex­
pectancies early in the program were slightly more likely to withdraw, 
the retained sample remained representative of the initial sample in 
terms of teaching practices, task value, all types of costs and de­
mographic characteristics.
We examined confounding effects using Welch's one-way ANOVA. 
While significant initial differences emerged by school affiliation for 
expectancies F (9, 49.4) = 2.94, p = .007, STVs, F (9, 39.2) = 3.51, p =
.002, and scaffolding, F (9, 45.2) = 4.41, p = .001, these differences 
were not sustained. By the end of the program, no significant school- 
level differences were found for motivational constructs and teaching 
practices. Furthermore, although ICCs for initial expectancies (ICC =
.15), task values (ICC = .18), scaffolding (ICC = .10) and emotional 
support (ICC = .07) were substantial (see Table A3), clustering effects 
for initial cost variables, most of the initial teaching practices and all of 
the eventual motivational constructs and practices were low (ICC ≤.05).
A similar pattern was present for demographic variables. Differences 
in initial prompting of concrete examples F (6, 21.7) = 3.89, p = .009, 
and initial provision of emotional support, F (6, 23.4) = 3.25, p = .018, 
were found based on teaching experience, with less experienced teach­
ers reporting lower levels. Grade level was associated with differences in 
initial emotional support F (5, 25.0) = 3.03, p = .029, with lower reports 
from teachers who mainly taught in grades 7 to 9. However, no signif­
icant differences were found for any other variables at the beginning of 
the PD program, and, notably, no significant differences were found at 
the end of the program. Given the inconsistent initial differences and 
their absence in the eventual results, we decided not to include these 
background variables or school clusters in our analysis.
To ensure consistent measurement of the six motivational constructs 
over time, we tested for longitudinal measurement invariance using 
robust maximum likelihood estimation. A configural invariance model 
demonstrated acceptable fit, χ2 (510) = 820.21, CFI = .92, RMSEA =
.07. Subsequent comparisons supported metric invariance (Δχ2 (12) =
14.6, p = .26) and full scalar invariance (Δχ2 (12) = 10.8, p = .54, ΔCFI 
= .00). The results support comparison of motivational constructs over 
time.
For the adapted perceived cost scale, discriminant validity was 
assessed using the Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio. While three 
construct pairs met the threshold of .90 for conceptually related con­
structs (Henseler et al., 2015), three ratios exceeded this value, 
indicating high overlap. Specifically, HTMT values were .91 for outside 
effort and alternative cost, .95 for task effort and emotional cost, and .97 
for task effort and loss of valued alternatives. Despite this statistical 
overlap, we retained the four-factor cost structure. Although a high 
HTMT value indicates a lack of empirical uniqueness in the specific 
sample, as Henseler et al. (2015, p. 131) note, it “does not necessarily 
imply that the underlying concepts are identical, especially when 
follow-up research provides continued support for differing relation­
ships with the antecedent and the resultant concepts.” Our decision 
aligns with the original scale validation by Flake et al. (2015), who re­
ported latent correlations as high as .93 and .95 between these factors, 
yet maintained the multidimensional structure because it received 
strong structural support and demonstrated differential predictive val­
idity regarding student outcomes. Similar patterns of high in­
tercorrelations with differences in qualitative associations were found in 
research on teachers' general work-related motivation (Beymer et al., 
2023). For example, high outside effort cost was uniquely associated 
with challenges related to paperwork, while high emotional cost was 
associated with teachers' emotional burden related to their students’ 
struggles. Furthermore, qualitative results in teacher PD contexts (Alp 
et al., 2023; Norma et al., 2026) confirm that teachers distinguish be­
tween different cost dimensions, with specific emphasis on outside 
effort, supporting the practical necessity of modeling them separately.
4.2. Teachers’ motivation and SRL-related teaching practices
The first research question focused on the extent to which teachers' 
self-reported SRL-related teaching practices changed during the PD 
program. Descriptive statistics for teachers’ self-reported practices at 
measurement time 1 and 4 are presented in Table 4.
A paired samples t-test showed a significant increase in all five fac­
tors of teachers’ SRL-related teaching practices, indicating a positive 
change over time and supporting Hypothesis 1 (see Table 5).
The second research question explored how teachers' expectancies, 
STVs, and different dimensions of perceived cost were related to 
teachers’ self-reported SRL-related teaching practices at beginning and 
end of the program.
Our second hypothesis was that there would be positive associations 
between teachers’ expectancies for success, STVs, and self-reported 
promotion of SRL. Expectancies for success and subjective task values 
at the beginning of the program were both significantly positively 
related to two categories of initial SRL-related practices: promoting 
metacognition and providing emotional support, but the correlations 
were not significant for the three other categories: teaching cognitive 
strategies, scaffolding and prompting concrete examples (Table 6). 
Regarding practices reported at the end of the program, we found sig­
nificant correlations with initial expectancies and three types of prac­
tices 
(promoting 
metacognition, 
providing 
emotional 
support, 
scaffolding). Three associations were also present between initial STVs 
and eventual practices (promoting metacognition, providing emotional 
support, prompting concrete examples). Overall, for teacher motivation 
at the beginning of the program, we found more instances of significant 
correlations with practices at the end of the program, compared to 
practices at the beginning of the program.
Expectancies for success at the second measurement point were 
significantly related to four of the five factors of SRL-related teaching 
practices at the end of the PD program, while STVs at the second mea­
surement point were positively associated with only one type of prac­
tices at the end of the program (Table 7). There were fewer significant 
relationships between motivation at the end of the program and teach­
ing practices at the beginning of the program, compared to practices at 
the end of the program. As we found some significant positive relations 
and no negative relations between teachers’ expectancies, STVs, and 
teaching practices, Hypothesis 2 was partially supported.
Regarding the four dimensions of perceived cost, we expected to find 
negative associations with teachers’ self-reported SRL-related teaching

Regarding the relative intensity of perceived costs, Friedman tests

program. Given that task effort cost and outside effort cost exceeded 
other cost dimensions at both time points, Hypothesis 4 was fully 
supported.

profiles can be identified based on teachers’ motivational beliefs for

beginning and the end of the PD program. Based on model fit indices

extremely small membership (Spurk et al., 2020), we chose the model

values ranging from 40.67 to 206.99. Therefore, we refer to them as the 
Initially less motivated profile (29% of teachers) and the Initially more 
motivated profile (71% of teachers). The profiles are presented in Fig. 2.

initially less motivated profile tended to report lower expectancies and

We predicted (Hypothesis 6) that teachers who belong to profiles 
with higher expectancies, STVs and lower perceived costs at the end of 
the PD program, will report a greater extent of using SRL-related 
teaching practices at the end of the program. Welch's ANOVA indi­
cated that the eventually less motivated profile did not differ significantly 
from the eventually more motivated profile in any of the factors of self- 
reported practices at the beginning of the PD program. However, there 
were significant differences in two of the factors of their self-reported 
practices at the end of the program, with teachers in the eventually less 
motivated profile reporting significantly less promotion of metacognition 
(p = .040) and provision of emotional support (p = .014). Therefore, 
Hypothesis 6 was partially supported.
Regarding the stability of the group classification, we analyzed 
profile memberships of teachers in Time 2 and Time 3 (N = 88) and the 
analysis revealed that 78.4% of teachers remained in a similar type of 
profile at both time points, specifically 50.0% of teachers were classified 
into more motivated profiles at both time points and 28.4% of teachers 
belonged to the less motivated profiles at both measurement points. 21.6 
% of teachers did not remain in a conceptually similar profile, with 
17.0% of teachers belonging to the initially more motivated group and 
eventually less motivated group, while 4.5% of teachers belonged to the 
initially less motivated group and eventually more motivated group.
5. Discussion
This study explored the relationship between teachers' motivation to 
integrate the knowledge and skills acquired during the PD program, and 
their SRL-related teaching practices at the beginning and end of the PD 
program. Drawing on SEVT, we focused on teachers’ expectancies for 
success, STVs, and four dimensions of cost. Using a person-oriented 
analysis (LPA), we distinguished two motivational profiles, both at the 
beginning and the end of the PD program. The groups differed in their 
motivational dimensions and some of their self-reported SRL-related 
teaching practices.
First, comparing teachers' self-reported SRL-related teaching prac­
tices at the beginning and the end of the PD program (RQ1), we found a 
significant increase in all five factors of teachers' teaching practices, 
confirming Hypothesis 1. This result is important because it aligns with 
previous findings that participation in an SRL-related PD program may 
be accompanied by increases in teachers' self-reported practices (Norma

et al., 2026). However, these results should be interpreted with caution 
as our study did not involve a control group. While our study relied 
solely on teachers' self-reported data and did not include students' 
perspective, other researchers have shown that teacher-reported SRL 
promotion is related to student-reported SRL promotion (Karlen et al., 
2023) and students' SRL motivation (Jud et al., 2024). While provision 
of emotional support and scaffolding were the two highest-rated cate­
gories of practices at both measurement points, we saw the greatest 
increase in teachers' self-reported promotion of metacognition. There 
are two likely explanations for that. First of all, promotion of metacog­
nition was notably the lowest category of practices at the beginning of 
the program. Secondly, together with cognitive strategies, it was one of 
the two topics that were emphasized the most in our PD program. Based 
on the largest increase in teachers’ self-reported promotion of meta­
cognition, and the feedback we got from participants, we would highly 
advise future researchers and SRL-related PD designers to focus on the 
distinctions between direct and indirect SRL promotion, as well as 
explicit and implicit strategy instruction (Dignath & Veenman, 2021), as 
this was a common and recurring theme in our PD program and the 
participating teachers often found the framework instructive.
Regarding the relationship between teachers' SRL-related teaching 
practices and motivational dimensions (RQ2), we obtained mixed re­
sults. Significant positive associations were found between expectancies, 
values and some of the practices, partially supporting Hypothesis 2, and 
in alignment with prior empirical findings on teachers' SRL-promotion 
and motivational beliefs (Jud et al., 2023; Karlen et al., 2023). Hy­
pothesis 3 was also partially supported, as we found some significant 
negative relations and no significant positive associations between costs 
and practices. Specifically, we found teachers' initial motivational di­
mensions to be more related to their SRL-promoting practices at the end 
of the program, compared to the practices at the beginning of the pro­
gram. This was especially prevalent for teachers' task values and task 
effort costs, highlighting the importance of addressing these aspects of 
teacher motivation at the beginning of the PD program. These results are 
in line with previous findings in SEVT research that have shown ex­
pectancies for success to be more predictive of performance and STVs to 
be related with task choices, long-term interest, and sustained behavior 
(e.g., Eccles & Wigfield, 2020; Flake et al., 2015; Osman & Warner, 
2020; Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). For teachers' motivational dimensions 
at the end of the program, we found expectancies for success to be more 
related to SRL-promoting practices at the end of the program, while 
eventual STVs and perceived costs were less related to eventual prac­
tices. In other words, in the context of PD and SRL promotion, while task 
values and costs seem to be particularly important to initiate teachers' 
choice and engagement in new practices, expectancies for success at 
integrating what they have learned could be more associated with their 
practices at a given moment. This is compatible with some prior results 
in teacher PD or SRL-promotion. For example, in a longitudinal study, 
Bostr¨om and Palm (2020) found that teachers who ended up imple­
menting more of the PD-related activities differed in terms of their utility 
values at the end of the PD, with non-significant differences in their 
expectancies and costs. However, a cross-sectional study by Jud et al. 
(2023) found that teachers' expectancies showed stronger relationships 
with promotion of SRL, compared to values and costs, with authors 
suggesting that this could be explained by values relating to one's 
choices, compared to expectancies being associated with outcomes. 
These results emphasize the importance of longitudinal research on 
teacher motivation to support SRL because a cross-sectional analysis 
might not reveal strong relations between STVs or costs and teaching 
practices. For researchers, this implies that interventions focusing on 
increasing STVs or alleviating costs might require longer durations to 
reveal positive associations with performance. Considering the rela­
tionship between motivational dimensions and teaching practices, PD 
programs should actively implement interventions with the potential to 
increase teachers' expectancies for success and STVs, and decrease 
different types of costs of integrating PD into their practice. For 
expectancies, the PD design could include concrete and manageable 
tasks where teachers are likely to experience success in integrating PD, 
in addition to positive feedback and opportunities for teachers to reflect 
on how their current strengths and practices enable them to develop and 
integrate new knowledge and skills. For utility and attainment value, PD 
should highlight and provide opportunities for teachers to reflect how 
the program could improve their students' learning and how it relates to 
their core values and principles as teachers. Regarding intrinsic value, 
PD could offer active and collaborative activities, autonomy and choice 
for teachers on how they integrate PD with their classroom practice.
This study was unique in its quantitative focus on four distinct factors 
of teachers' costs of integrating PD. Our analysis revealed significant 
differences in the extent of perceived cost dimensions, extending the 
prior qualitative findings in SRL-related PD research that has suggested 
external barriers, time constraints and effort as possible challenges to 
teachers' SRL-PD implementation (Cleary et al., 2022; Sins et al., 2024). 
Supporting Hypothesis 4, teachers' estimations of their task effort cost 
and outside effort cost were significantly higher than their estimations of 
emotional cost and loss of valued alternative cost at both the beginning 
and end of the PD program. The differences in the mean values of some 
dimensions of costs highlight the importance of taking a more differ­
entiated approach to studying teachers' perceptions of costs (e.g., 
Beymer et al., 2023), with outside effort cost being the least explored 
relevant dimension (Alp et al., 2023). The concept of outside effort costs 
as demands related to teachers' responsibilities outside of PD might 
explain why teachers struggle with supporting students' SRL, even if 
provided with training and support by PD programs. Specifically, Hirt 
et al. (2025) found that teachers' cost perceptions were not reduced 
despite the provision of various implementation ideas and teaching 
materials. While Hirt et al. (2025) did not explicitly measure outside 
effort cost, it could be an explanation for the persistence of teachers' cost 
perceptions, given the intercorrelatedness of cost dimensions. In one of 
the few studies on teacher motivation explicitly differentiating between 
teachers' task effort costs and outside effort costs, Alp et al. (2023) found 
that the demands related to the existing national curriculum, or the lack 
of required time for effective implementation were often mentioned as 
barriers in teachers' reflections at the end of the PD program. Similarly, 
Cleary et al. (2022) found that teachers who struggled most with SRL 
promotion after PD often attributed their difficulties to external barriers, 
such as lack of time and parental influences. While task effort cost could 
be addressed by teachers' motivational regulation strategies or provision 
of ready-to-use adaptable materials, and the creation of a psycholog­
ically safe environment is crucial for minimizing emotional costs, 
outside effort cost might not be easily alleviated on a personal level. 
When teachers have so many other responsibilities that they are unable 
to put in the effort to integrate PD with their practice, changes at 
organizational or political level might be necessary. In addition to 
supportive, evidence-based PD, teachers require additional time, re­
sources, and school-level organizational support to integrate what they 
have learned into their teaching practices. School leaders could reduce 
outside effort cost by allocating specific non-teaching working hours for 
PD and by avoiding scheduling it near the commonly more demanding 
periods of a school year (e.g., near the end of the learning period where 
grading workloads are higher). Furthermore, school leaders could adjust 
the workloads of teachers who are participating in PD and be more 
explicit about competing tasks that should be paused or deprioritized 
during PD. Otherwise, if non-PD-related responsibilities end up hin­
dering teachers' efforts, PD is less likely to positively affect teachers' 
practices and students’ SRL.
However, not all causes of costs can be fully eliminated on an 
organizational or PD program level, and sometimes it might be useful to 
help teachers re-interpret costs as worthwhile, or, on a more systemic 
level, turn to policy-level advocacy for resources that match the needs of 
change initiatives, as 30.7% of OECD teachers cite the lack of resources 
for implementation. Policy-level interventions might include protected 
time allocation for teachers' professional development, capping contact

hours during intensive PD periods, and the reduction of non- 
instructional administrative tasks that have been found to be associ­
ated with teachers’ high outside effort cost (Beymer et al., 2023), and 
are a source of stress for 51.7% of OECD (2025) teachers. Previous 
research has found that schools with successful SRL implementation are 
characterised by a shared SRL vision, focused PD, teacher reflection and 
collective responsibility, highlighting the need for a school-wide 
approach to SRL-related PD (De Smul et al., 2020; Sins et al., 2024). 
Although teacher learning is an important part of school improvement, 
consideration and cultivation of other aspects of school culture are also 
necessary for successful changes in practices (Eisenschmidt & Vanari, 
2025). Furthermore, findings suggest that school leadership and col­
lective efficacy may shape the extent to which school cultures become 
improvement-oriented. A high improvement-oriented culture was 
associated with greater teacher job satisfaction, perceived impact, and 
enriched teaching practices, whereas less improvement-oriented cul­
tures were related to lower job satisfaction, perceived impact, and more 
traditional teaching practices, underscoring the dynamic role of school 
culture in supporting teacher motivation and SRL promotion 
(Eisenschmidt & Oppi, 2025).
We also explored what kinds of motivational profiles could be 
identified based on teachers’ motivational beliefs for integrating SRL- 
related PD into their teaching practice (RQ3). At both the beginning 
and end of the PD program, we found two motivational profiles that 
differed in terms of expectancies, STVs, and the four dimensions of 
perceived costs, highlighting that teachers should not be treated as a 
homogeneous group in terms of their motivation (Hirt et al., 2022). For 
all profiles, higher expectancies were associated with higher STVs and 
lower costs, while lower expectancies were associated with lower STVs 
and higher costs. This is in line with general tendencies in SEVT and 
earlier studies of teacher motivation that have found motivational di­
mensions to be distinct but related (e.g., Osman & Warner, 2020; Hirt 
et al., 2022).
Regarding the motivational profiles at the beginning of the program, 
our Hypothesis 5, that teachers who belong to the initially more moti­
vated profile will report a greater extent of using SRL-related practices, 
was partially supported. We found that teachers in the initially more 
motivated profile reported higher average use of two SRL-related teaching 
practices (PM, ES) at the beginning of the PD program, compared to 
teachers in the initially less motivated profile. This finding is consistent 
with prior results indicating that teachers belonging to more motivated 
profiles tend to engage in a higher extent of SRL-promoting practices 
(Hirt et al., 2022). Importantly, the difference in reported practices for 
the two profiles was greater at the end of the program – membership of 
the initially more motivated group of teachers was associated with even­
tually higher average self-reported SRL promotion in four out of the five 
factors of teaching practices (PM, ES, SC, CE). The fact that initial 
motivational profiles revealed greater differences in self-reported 
teaching practices at the end of the program suggests that the motiva­
tion to implement PD in practice might be associated with deeper 
learning that takes place between PD sessions when teachers engage and 
experiment with new ideas and techniques in daily work-related activ­
ities (Osman & Warner, 2020). This again highlights the importance of 
longitudinal data collection to reveal the connections between teacher 
motivation and teaching practices. For practitioners and PD designers 
this indicates that short motivational scales like the EVC-PD by Osman 
and Warner (2020), or the cost subscale we adapted from Flake et al. 
(2015) could be useful tools to identify teachers or groups who might 
need targeted support for some of the motivational dimensions.
When we studied teachers in the two motivational profiles at the end 
of the program, we found that teachers in the eventually more motivated 
profile did not differ from eventually less motivated teachers in their initial 
SRL-related practices; however, there were significant differences at the 
end of the program for two categories of practices (PM, ES), partially 
supporting our Hypothesis 6. These results, as well as correlational re­
sults at two time points, indicate that although motivation and practices 
are reciprocally related (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020), it is likely that initial 
motivation has a stronger longitudinal association to future practices, 
compared to the association of prior performance with future motiva­
tion. According to Eccles and Wigfield (2020), it is not simply prior 
achievement or performance but the interpretation of prior experience 
that influences the more proximal determinants of task and activity 
choice and engagement. This indicates that just as students can be hel­
ped in re-interpreting their experience in a way that enhances their 
motivation (Rosenzweig et al., 2022), researchers and practitioners 
could explore distinct ways for supporting teachers in their in­
terpretations of experience in the context of PD.
5.1. Limitations and future directions
The primary limitation of this study is its sole reliance on teachers' 
self-reported quantitative data at four measurement points. While our 
decision to only collect data near the beginning and end of the program 
was made to reduce burden on teachers, a more frequent data collection 
would allow for the exploration of the extent of fluctuation in motiva­
tion and its relation to specific program tasks and teaching practices. To 
enhance the validity of the self-reported data and overall results, future 
studies should complement self-reports with additional forms of data 
collection (e.g., classroom observations, qualitative teacher interviews) 
to explore the interplay between teacher motivation and classroom 
practice, as well as contextual factors. It would also be valuable to add 
students’ perspectives to the analysis to account for their perception of 
SRL support from their teachers. Furthermore, although we tested for 
initial differences across demographic variables, their exclusion from 
the main analyses limits the interpretation of the results of how specific 
teacher characteristics interact with motivation. Future analyses should 
systematically control for and discuss the potential influence of factors 
such as teaching experience, grade level, and subject area on teacher 
motivation for PD integration and teaching practices.
The second limitation is the specific context and relatively small 
sample size of the present study and the lack of a control group. The 
absence of a control group limits our ability to make strong causal claims 
regarding the effect of the PD program on teaching practices and 
motivation. The single-country, small-sample context may limit the 
generalizability of the findings regarding the relations between moti­
vation and teaching practices to other teacher populations. Although the 
Estonian system shares many similarities with other international con­
texts in terms of the barriers to teachers’ professional learning, and the 
perceived lack of necessary resources to implement change initiatives, 
Estonian teachers report higher than average work-related stress and 
comparatively low participation in collaborative learning in schools 
(OECD, 2025), which might influence how teachers perceive the costs of 
implementing PD. Therefore, cross-national or multi-site replications 
are encouraged to test for stability and transferability of the results. 
Nevertheless, SEVT as the main theoretical framework has previously 
been used to study teacher motivation in different contexts, and the 
main findings of the study align with the theory.
The third limitation is our treatment of motivation as primarily an 
individual construct, without an analysis of cultural and social processes 
that contribute to the more proximal processes of expectancies, values 
and costs. For example, high teacher workloads in the Estonian context 
could directly contribute to teachers' perceptions of outside effort costs, 
while shared school-level vision for promoting SRL could have a positive 
effect on subjective task values, and high collective efficacy could sup­
port teachers’ individual expectancies of success. Future research should 
therefore incorporate school-level and system-level variables, to explore 
the effects of organizational, social, cultural or political factors on 
teacher motivation, as well as analysis about how PD design features (e. 
g., duration, materials) contribute to the more proximal motivational 
components.
Finally, a methodological limitation concerns the discriminant val­
idity of the perceived cost subscales. Our analysis revealed high

statistical overlap between certain cost dimensions, particularly be­
tween task effort cost, emotional cost, and loss of valued alternatives, 
with Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratios exceeding the threshold of 
.90. This suggests that the measurement items may have not captured 
the conceptual distinctions between these specific cost dimensions for 
the teachers in our sample. However, we retained the multidimensional 
structure because it aligns with the original scale validation (Flake et al., 
2015) and provides crucial theoretical and conceptual granularity. As 
recent findings indicate, task effort and loss of valued alternatives cost 
might be more sensitive to the specific task, and might fluctuate more 
from situation to situation, while emotional cost and outside effort cost 
could be more stable and related to the longer-term contextual factors 
(Kim et al., 2023). As our measuring of teachers' costs was relatively 
broad and not explicit about specific PD-related tasks, teachers could 
have interpreted them in less differentiated ways. Nevertheless, we 
maintain that it could be useful for researchers and practitioners to 
explore the possible barriers for integrating PD on multiple contextual 
and temporal levels, distinguishing between long-term factors and 
task-specific perceptions, as well as more stable and situative personal 
characteristics. Future research should aim for a more frequent and 
situated approach to measuring teachers’ cost perceptions via experi­
ence sampling or diary studies, as well as refine these measurement 
instruments to enhance their empirical distinctiveness, perhaps by 
developing items that more explicitly contrast the nuances of emotional 
load versus resource depletion, or considering merging some of the cost 
dimensions for model parsimony. Additionally, further mixed-method 
approaches could help validate whether these dimensions operate 
distinctly in practice, even when they are highly correlated in quanti­
tative data.
In addition to the usual appeal for more studies with greater sample 
sizes in new contexts that focus on multiple dimensions of costs and a 
person-centered analysis, we see three interesting avenues for further 
research. First, our study used SEVT to analyze teacher motivation, but 
we did not draw explicitly on the motivational intervention literature (e. 
g., Rosenzweig et al., 2022) to actively support, target, and study 
teachers' expectancies, values, and costs, which is something that PD 
designers and researchers could leverage. Second, it would be inter­
esting and valuable to explore and support teachers' own motivational 
regulation (e.g., Wolters, 2003) in the context of professional develop­
ment to understand how teachers could increase and maintain their 
motivation to integrate what they have learned during PD. Finally, 
future research should extend beyond the frequency or extent of specific 
SRL-promoting practices and assess how SRL promotion is linked to 
teachers' motivating styles (Aelterman et al., 2019; Vermote et al., 
2020). As SRL relies on student autonomy, it could be insightful to study 
whether 
teachers 
implement 
SRL-promoting 
practices 
in 
an 
autonomy-supportive and structuring way, or if high perceived costs 
push them toward controlling or chaotic motivating styles (Katz & Mo`e, 
2024), and the effect these dynamics have on students’ learning.
6. Conclusion
This study provides empirical evidence for the longitudinal 
relationship between teachers' motivational dimensions and self- 
reported teaching practices in the context of an SRL-related PD pro­
gram. By employing person-oriented analysis, we found distinct moti­
vational profiles that also differed in their promotion of SRL. However, 
the absence of a control group limits the ability to make causal claims 
about the impact of the PD program. Theoretically, this study highlights 
the necessity of treating teachers' perceived cost as a multidimensional 
construct, with the importance of outside effort cost as a potential bar­
rier to SRL-related PD integration. Although the current study did not 
incorporate school-level and system-level variables, it is evident that 
future research of SRL-related PD would benefit from a more systemic 
approach, as the treatment of teacher motivation as an isolated indi­
vidual trait or a simple reaction to PD design features might not reveal 
the actual barriers to PD integration. For researchers and practitioners, 
the results provide further evidence for the potential of drawing on SEVT 
to support and understand teachers’ motivation in the context of SRL 
promotion and effective professional development.
