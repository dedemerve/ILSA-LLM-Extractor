Abstract
This study applied explainable machine learning (ML) to identify school-level
conditions that predict organisational innovativeness using OECD TALIS 2018
principal data from 47 education systems. Six machine learning algorithms were
compared, and XGBoost achieved the best predictive performance. SHAP (SHapley
Additive exPlanations) was then applied to interpret the model and rank the most
influential factors. The results show that organisational innovativeness is shaped
primarily by relational and cultural conditions rather than by isolated structural inputs. School climate emerged as the strongest predictor, particularly the encouragement of staff-initiated projects, mutual support, shared responsibility, participatory
decision-making, and cooperation with the local community. Shortage of time with
students, by contrast, reduced predicted innovativeness. Principals’ job satisfaction
was the second most influential factor. Leadership practices that promote teacher
cooperation for pedagogical development showed a modest positive association.
Diversity beliefs among teachers and access to mentoring programmes contributed
small but generally positive signals. Overall, the findings suggest that organisational
innovativeness in schools depends mainly on a supportive and participatory school
climate, principals’ job satisfaction, instructional leadership practice, diversity belief and structured mentoring.
Keywords Machine learning · Organisational innovativeness · TALIS · School
climate · Leadership · Mentoring · Job satisfaction

Introduction
The pace of educational transformation has accelerated significantly over the past
two decades. Modern educational institutions, originally designed to meet the standardized demands of the Industrial Era, are now confronted with diverse challenges

driven by digital transformation and the rise of artificial intelligence (Arar et al.,
2024). These technological disruptions, combined with rapidly evolving social, economic, and environmental conditions, necessitate that contemporary schools adopt
innovative management practices and adapt continuously to uphold core educational
aims, including learning, equity, student well-being, and social justice (Dedering
& Pietsch, 2025; Germain, 2022). Consequently, adaptability and innovation have
become central themes in educational research (Levin, 2015; Pietsch et al., 2024).
Given that innovation is increasingly recognized as a critical driver of school
improvement and stakeholder outcomes (Alzouebi et al., 2025; Guzman & JaillierCastrillon, 2021), further research is warranted to identify specific pathways for fostering innovative school environments.
Schools function as complex social systems where innovation is considered a vital
source of organizational vitality (Fidan & Balcı, 2017). Unlike corporate models
that prioritize efficiency and profitability (Ahlstrom, 2010), educational innovation
is fundamentally distinct in its drivers and characteristics. While the OECD (2016)
notes that educational innovations vary globally, recent scholarship emphasizes that
they are predominantly relational, cultural, and political rather than merely technical or structural (Akın, 2016; Erdoğan et al., 2024; Riveras-León & Tomàs-Folch,
2020). This relational foundation is situated within what Anderson (1996) describes
as a framework of “cultural politics” that serves as both the source and the determinant of innovation. According to this perspective, educational innovations arise
from cultural politics, and their viability depends on how effectively these political dynamics are navigated. However, even when cultural dynamics are successfully
managed, Slavin (1989) cautions that innovations in educational organizations typically follow a trajectory of initial excitement, broad adoption, disappointment, and
eventual abandonment—a recurring cycle that he characterizes as “the classic swing
of the pendulum”. Taken together, these dynamics underscore the need for theoretical frameworks that conceptualize educational innovation as culturally embedded
and shaped by intersecting relational, political, and transnational forces that extend
beyond organizational boundaries.
Organisational innovativeness
Innovativeness is broadly defined as employees’ perceptions of their organization’s
openness to change and new ideas (Patterson et al., 2005), which represent essential
preconditions for translating novel ideas into applied practice. Blömeke et al. (2021)
identified four distinct operational definitions of innovativeness at the individual and
organisational levels: (1) individual attitudes, (2) psychological climate characteristics, (3) organisational climates, and (4) organisational leadership characteristics.
At the individual level, innovativeness refers to either the individual attitude that
expresses a personal tendency among organization members to evaluate and implement new ideas or the psychological climate characteristic, which involves members’ evaluations of their own organization but where responses are analysed at the
individual level. At the organisational level, innovativeness is addressed as either
organisational climate characteristics, which are analysed by aggregating individual
responses to the organisational level, or as an organisational leadership character-

istics. In this context, innovativeness can be said to be both an individual and an
organisational variable. Our study adopts the organisational level.
Organisational innovativeness has been conceptualized as a process involving the
identification of new knowledge sources, the adaptation of ideas generated from these
sources, and the capacity to respond to internal and external knowledge flows (Birasnav et al., 2023). In schools, organisational innovativeness refers to the extent to
which the institution provides a supportive climate and infrastructure for the renewal
of pedagogical practices, administrative processes, and learning environments (Hargreaves & Shirley, 2009; OECD, 2019). The OECD (2019) identifies innovative educational organizations within the scope of the school’s ability to recognize needs for
change, respond quickly to changes, adopt new ideas, and provide support for developing innovations. From this perspective, which complements this study’s definition
of organizational innovativeness, school innovativeness is not merely a count of new
ideas but also a climate-level construct that reflects the organizational capacity to
identify needs and generate, adopt, and sustain new practices.
Within this framework, innovativeness is understood both as a mechanism for
institutional self-renewal and as an outcome of ongoing improvement processes (Pan
et al., 2024). Schools that are highly innovative demonstrate the ability to implement novel approaches that generate meaningful improvements across all aspects of
the organization, including teaching, administration, and stakeholder collaboration
(Pihie & Bagheri, 2013). However, as Vincent-Lancrin (2023) highlights, organizational innovativeness does not emerge spontaneously; instead, it requires deliberate leadership orientations that promote innovation, structures that create access to
diverse knowledge sources, teams dedicated to managing innovation processes, and
strategic investments in resources that sustain innovation over time.
Despite the growing relevance of the topic, empirical research exploring how
organisational innovativeness can be implemented and strengthened in school settings remains limited, particularly compared with the extensive literature in the business field (Blömeke et al., 2021; Newaz, 2022; Smith et al., 2008). Nevertheless,
a range of contextual, cultural, and structural factors, including leadership, organisational culture, national policies, and power distance, have been shown to shape
schools’ innovative capacity (Lu & Campbell, 2021; Masry-Herzallah & Da’as, 2021;
McCharen et al., 2011; Vieluf et al., 2012). In an increasingly globalized educational
landscape, shared strategic approaches adopted across education systems may enable
schools to increase their organizational innovativeness (Dedering & Pietsch, 2025).
Reflecting this growing interest, the OECD’s Teaching and Learning International
Survey (TALIS) includes a specific set of indicators designed to measure organisational innovativeness at the school level (Ainley & Carstens, 2018). Consequently,
identifying potential universal dimensions of an innovative school climate via largescale international datasets has become an important line of inquiry, which this study
addresses through machine learning techniques applied to large-scale datasets from
TALIS 2018.

Factors affecting organisational innovativeness
Many studies have examined the factors affecting organisational innovativeness in
different sectors, ranging from schools to business organisations. Smith et al. (2008)
conducted a systematic literature review of more than 100 studies and identified nine
core factors that determine an organization’s capacity to manage innovation: management style and leadership, resources, organisational structure, corporate strategy,
technology, knowledge management, employees, and the innovation process. A more
recent review by Newaz (2022) expanded this view, grouping 102 distinct factors
into ten thematic clusters (pp. 183–190): culture, human resource management, top
management orientation, the external environment, organisational proficiencies,
leadership, knowledge management, market pressure and competition, technology
adaptation, and research and development. To illustrate one of these factors, Newaz
(2022) highlighted how national culture, internal norms, and practices exercise a crucial influence on organizational performance and innovation.
Although Smith’s and Newaz’s findings arise primarily from corporate and business contexts, their identified drivers—including leadership behaviours, knowledge
processes, and resource management—parallel factors are observed in educational
settings such as school leadership, teacher collaboration, professional development,
and participatory decision-making (Blömeke et al., 2021; Ham & Lee, 2024; Hsieh et
al., 2024; Pan et al., 2024; Svenja et al., 2012). Similarly, within the Turkish national
context, Kaya (2021) demonstrated that school-level characteristics—including size,
resource adequacy, school climate, leadership, job satisfaction, and staffing levels—
significantly influence organizational innovativeness.
In addition to these descriptive findings, several theoretical perspectives can help
explain how such factors influence organisational innovativeness in schools. A central framework for understanding how working conditions shape teachers’ well-being
and performance is the job demands–resources (JD-R) model developed by Bakker
and Demerouti (2007). Rather than focusing on innovation per se, the model distinguishes job demands and job resources and explains how they jointly affect employees’ strain, engagement, and performance. In understanding the implications of this
model for school innovativeness, it should be noted that the term “job resources”
refers to school conditions, such as leadership or school climate, that, on the one
hand, counterbalance potential impediments to teachers’ classroom performance and
health (e.g., lack of time, infrastructure needs) and, on the other hand, enhance their
work engagement and well-being (Bakker, 2011). Conversely, the constantly changing and uncertain demands of the teaching profession, combined with the emotional
labour required in teacher–student and teacher–parent interactions, can lead to the
depletion of mental and emotional resources (Uslukaya et al., 2025). In this context,
the leadership of school principals and other job resources can play a significant role
not only in establishing and maintaining an innovation-conducive school climate but
also in buffering the negative effects of high job demands on teachers’ well-being and
sustaining the organisational capacity for innovation.
Another significant theoretical lens for understanding school innovativeness is
social capital theory, which focuses on how social relationships, trust and networks
within schools shape teachers’ work and school climates (e.g., Geijsel, 2001; Pan et

al., 2024). Studies based on this theory have defined school innovativeness through
teachers’ perceptions of organisational openness to innovations and new ideas (Pan
et al., 2024). The majority of studies examining the factors that affect innovativeness
in schools emphasize the determining role of the school climate and social bonds. For
example, Kirsten and Du Preez (2010) suggest strengthening teamwork, and Moolenaar et al. (2011) propose that teachers’ participation in social networks can enable
risk-taking and support innovative behaviours. Furthermore, collaboration is one of
the fundamental prerequisites for school innovativeness; it facilitates the implementation of school reforms and lays the groundwork for applying new ideas (Bryk and
Schneider 2002; Kirsten and Du Preez 2010; Moolenaar et al. 2011; Westheimer
2008).
From both the JD-R and social capital perspectives, leadership emerges as a pivotal enabler in creating and sustaining an innovation culture. Vincent-Lancrin (2023)
argues that developing a pro-innovation culture hinges on leaders who motivate and
empower their staff to experiment while ensuring access to the knowledge and skills
needed for innovation. Empirical studies further underscore this: transformational,
entrepreneurial, and participative leadership styles have been shown to directly foster
organisational innovativeness (Birasnav et al., 2023; Çoban & Atasoy, 2020; Hsieh et
al., 2024; Pihie et al., 2014). Consistent with these findings, research on modern leadership theories (e.g., distributed leadership, transformational leadership, instructional
leadership, and school leadership) consistently reveals that leadership is a crucial factor in the development of innovative behaviours in schools (Harris, 2008; Leithwood
& Jantzi, 2005; Marks & Printy, 2003). These studies indicate that leadership fosters
teacher job satisfaction, commitment, and a climate based on trust, thereby enabling
their innovative practices.
In summary, the literature converges on a multifaceted set of drivers, ranging from
structural and cultural conditions to leadership and external pressures that jointly
determine an organization’s innovation capacity. However, given the multivariable
nature of these influences, there is a need for more research to answer the open question of which elements most powerfully shape a school’s innovativeness. Unpacking
the relative importance of these factors in schools is essential for designing targeted
policies and practices that nurture a sustainable innovation climate, a need that has
become even more urgent as artificial intelligence (AI) technologies further accelerate change and as large-scale data such as TALIS 2018 enable more sophisticated,
data-driven analyses of organisational innovativeness.

Purpose of the study
In light of this context, the present study aims to examine the factors influencing
school leaders’ perceptions of institutional openness to innovation and change, as
measured by TALIS 2018 items. Drawing on ISCED Level 2 data from the OECD’s
TALIS 2018, the study utilizes internationally comparable responses from school
principals to identify key factors associated with organizational innovativeness. To
elucidate patterns and relationships that are latent within the data, an ML analytical approach is employed. Different ML algorithms are benchmarked for predictive

power, and the best-performing model is then examined in greater depth. The findings are interpreted in light of the literature, with particular emphasis on factors that
carry significant implications for educational policy and school development.

Method
Given that organisational innovativeness stems from the interplay of multiple factors operating simultaneously, traditional univariate and bivariate approaches may
be insufficient for identifying which conditions exert the strongest influence when
considered together. Thus, ML-based modelling approaches have emerged as powerful tools for uncovering multidimensional and potentially nonlinear relationships,
enabling the identification of established predictors as well as the discovery of previously unrecognized yet potentially influential factors (Christensen et al., 2025; Masci
et al., 2018; Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David, 2014). This methodological development
provides considerable potential for uncovering optimal conditions for educational
organizations and settings (Hilbert et al., 2021).
In addition to highly interpretable approaches such as linear regression, we examine nonlinear ML models, including decision trees and support vector regression
(SVR), as well as advanced ensemble learning techniques such as random forest,
XGBoost, and LightGBM. These models are benchmarked in terms of both predictive
accuracy and interpretability for predicting the organizational innovativeness dimension. Following these comparisons, the final analysis was conducted via XGBoost,
which demonstrated superior performance in this study. To ensure transparency and
explainability, we augment the analysis via SHAP (SHapley Additive Explanations),
a method widely adopted in the field of explainable AI (XAI) (Lundberg & Lee,
2017). This approach not only yields a highly accurate prediction model but also
quantifies the contribution of each feature to the model’s output (Molnar, 2020).
Data source and variables
The TALIS variables encompass a range of managerial dimensions—such as leadership practices, decision-making processes, the school climate, teacher development,
and resource management—measured through corresponding items and scales, as
documented in the TALIS 2018 technical report (OECD, 2019). In the present study,
all variables were estimated via a single pooled model utilizing the complete international dataset derived from the TALIS 2018 cycle. While this dataset includes sampling weights at the teacher and school levels to ensure national representativeness
within each jurisdiction, it does not provide country-level weights designed to balance nations against one another (OECD, 2019). Consequently, separate countryspecific models were not constructed; instead, all observations meeting the inclusion
criteria contributed equally to the analysis.
In this study, we used data from school principals in 47 TALIS 2018 countries and
economies (n = 9,247 schools), drawing on a dataset comprising 303 school-level
variables. As one of these variables, organisational innovativeness in TALIS 2018
is captured by the T3PORGIN scale, which combines four principal questionnaire

13

Journal of Educational Change

items (TC3G28A–D) measured on a four-point Likert scale (1 = “Strongly disagree”
to 4 = “Strongly agree”). This scale indicates a school’s capacity to identify and
pursue new ideas, adapt to emerging changes, and provide support for innovation,
thereby reflecting the extent to which its culture, structures, and processes encourage
and enable innovative practices.
The data were collected from school principals working at ISCED level 2 (lower
secondary education), which corresponds to the stage between primary education
and upper secondary/high school education. After excluding participants who had
missing values on T3PORGIN, we retained 8,792 participants, as 455 observations
were missing for the dependent variable. From the initial 303 variables, we applied
the exclusion criteria described in the relevant sections (see Table 1), resulting in a
final set of 221 variables included in the analysis (all dataset, related analysis logs,
and paths were attached to this paper).
Variable selection was guided by a manipulability criterion, focusing on schooland practice-level variables that are potentially amenable to policy or organisational
intervention (e.g., leadership practices, professional collaboration, and the school
climate). The primary aim was not to model macro-level structural determinants of
organisational innovativeness but to identify actionable levers within schools.

Missing-data–rate–based filtering
In the first step, we calculate the proportion of missing values for each variable and
exclude those with more than 12% missing data, as documented in Christensen et al.
(2025). This threshold balances the need to maintain the sample size with the requirement for data quality. The literature recommends careful evaluation of variables with
5–20% missingness (Little & Rubin, 2019), since imputing variables with very high
absence rates can introduce noise and reduce reliability (Schafer & Graham, 2002).
Applying this criterion removed 48 variables from the dataset.
High-correlation–based elimination
In the second step, we examined pairwise Pearson correlations among the remaining variables to guard against multicollinearity. Any pair with r > 0.60 was flagged
(Dormann et al., 2013). From each highly correlated pair, we removed the variable
with the higher missing-data rate, thereby preserving the feature with more complete
information. This dual criterion minimized information loss while addressing redundancy, resulting in the exclusion of 34 additional variables.
The removal of items was documented with justifications to ensure methodological transparency. After both filters were applied, 221 variables remained for analysis. These features represent a broad spectrum of managerial dimensions, such as
leadership practices, decision-making processes, teacher support strategies, resource
management, and school-climate measures, each of which are theoretically linked to
organisational innovativeness in the education literature.
Missing data analysis and imputation
When inspecting the TALIS 2018 dataset, missing responses emerged as a significant
concern. In OECD-administered surveys, items left unanswered for systematic or
random reasons were coded as − 99. During preprocessing, these − 99 codes were
converted to NaN so that the analyses could properly recognize them as missing.
To determine the appropriate imputation strategy, the missing data mechanism
was evaluated by examining the relationships between missing values and other variables, as well as the patterns of missing data. Specifically, t-tests were conducted for
continuous variables, and chi-square tests were used for categorical variables. The
analyses indicated that the missingness was largely missing completely at random
(MCAR). Given this finding, missing values in continuous variables were handled
via the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) method, whereas missing values in categorical
variables were imputed via the model.
The KNN approach was selected because it estimates each missing value by identifying observations that are closest in Euclidean distance and averaging their values
for the corresponding variable (Troyanskaya et al., 2001). This method preserves
the underlying structural relationships in the data, producing statistically meaningful
estimates. Notably, this approach was also successfully used by Christensen et al.
(2025) for imputing missing TALIS data when exploring school factors related to
professional learning communities.

For implementation, the imputation procedure was carried out in Python via
the KNNImputer function from the scikit-learn library. The following parameters
were specified in this process: n_neighbors (k) = 5, weights = “uniform”, and metric
= “nan_euclidean”. The “nan_euclidean” metric computes the Euclidean distance
between two observations only on variables that are present in both; in this way,
observations with missing values can still be included in the distance calculations and
in the imputation process.
The choice of k = 5 was based on established guidelines. In KNN algorithms, the
number of neighbours (k) is typically chosen between 3 and 15 in the literature,
and the optimal value depends on the dataset (Jerez et al., 2010; Troyanskaya et al.,
2001). In the present study, we used k = 5, which is a computationally efficient and
highly generalisable choice for medium-sized datasets (Beretta & Santaniello, 2016).
This value is also the default parameter in the scikit-learn library. To ensure that
neighbourhood calculations were not affected by differences in scale, all variables
were standardized via StandardScaler.
The imputation of missing values was applied only to the independent (predictor)
variables. The dependent variable T3PORGIN was not included in the imputation
process. Only observations with complete data on the dependent variable were used
in the modelling process.
After imputation, the dataset was carefully examined; the distributions, means,
and standard deviations of the variables were compared with their original (premissingness) values. These checks indicated that KNN imputation did not distort
the data structure and produced realistic, representative values. This approach aligns
with established best practices. The literature suggests that KNN imputation works
particularly well when the proportion of missing data is moderate and clear patterns
of similarity exist among variables (Jerez et al., 2010). By standardizing variables
and addressing outliers prior to imputation, potential risks were minimized. As a
result, the KNN-imputed dataset effectively resolved the missing-data problem and
provided a clean foundation for subsequent modelling.
Data normalization
Machine learning algorithms, especially distance-based methods (e.g., KNN, SVR)
and those that rely on parameter weight optimization (e.g., LightGBM), are sensitive to the scale of the input features (Han et al., 2012). When numerical variables
at different scales are combined, features with larger ranges can unduly dominate
the model. To avoid this situation, all predictor variables are standardized via the
StandardScaler function in the scikit-learn library. This operation recenters the data
to zero mean and rescales it to unit variance, effectively approximating a standard
normal distribution. Such standardization is critical for algorithms such as SVR and
KNN imputation, which compute distances in feature space (Fei et al., 2021).
In our workflow, after removing the target variable T3PORGIN, we standardized only the predictor features. First, we fitted the StandardScaler on all numerical
inputs; then, we used the trained scaler to transform these features. The target variable remained at its original scale since regression models require unscaled target
values to produce accurate predictions.

Data splitting and evaluation strategy
After all the preprocessing steps (including missing data imputation and data normalization), the dataset was partitioned into training and test subsets such that the
reliability of the model development process and the impartiality of the performance
evaluation were ensured. The dataset was split into 80% training (n = 7033) and 20%
test (n = 1759) datasets. The training set was used for model learning and hyperparameter optimization, whereas the test set was kept completely isolated as a heldout test set and used only for final performance evaluation. To optimize the model
hyperparameters and prevent overfitting, fivefold cross-validation (5-fold CV) was
conducted on the training set. This procedure guarantees that the test data are not
involved in the model selection process. To preserve the distribution of the dependent
variable across the training and test sets, stratification based on quartiles (Q1, Q2, and
Q3) was applied. The success of this stratification was verified by the similarity of the
quartile values in the training and test sets (see Table 2).
These results indicate that the training and held-out test sets have similar distributions of the dependent variable, thereby ensuring that the model evaluation is fair and
representative. The data-splitting procedure was made reproducible by setting the
parameter random_state = 42.

TALIS 2018 dataset, we evaluated a diverse set of general-purpose machine learning
algorithms adapted for regression tasks—ranging from classic statistical models to
state-of-the-art ensemble learners.

Factors affecting model performance: hyperparameters
Machine learning algorithms depend on hyperparameters—settings specified by the
user before training—that critically shape learning behavior but are not learned from
the data itself. Proper hyperparameter selection helps a model avoid overfitting (too
complex) and underfitting (too simple), directly influencing predictive accuracy and
generalizability (Weerts et al., 2020). In this study, we perform hyperparameter optimization in three steps:
1. Define candidate grids for each algorithm.
2. Evaluate each combination via 5-fold cross-validation on the training data, ensuring that every fold serves once as a validation.
3. Select the best hyperparameters based on the highest R² averaged across folds.
This systematic tuning process ensures that each algorithm operates at its optimal
performance, balancing bias and variance to achieve reliable predictions of organisational innovativeness. The resulting optimal configurations for each model are summarized in Table 3, alongside the search ranges evaluated.

Discussion
Prior research indicates that the conditions shaping school innovativeness vary across
contexts, including school size; school climate; leadership, staffing, and resource
adequacy; and school location and school type (Birasnav et al., 2023; Kaya, 2021;
Yalçın et al., 2023), among other organisational and contextual factors. In this study,
using TALIS 2018 principal data and an explainable machine-learning approach,
we found that the strongest predictors of principals’ perceived organisational innovativeness cluster around the school climate, followed by principal job satisfaction
and instructional leadership practices, with diversity-related beliefs and mentoring
access contributing smaller yet generally positive signals. Collectively, these results
provide actionable guidance for educational leaders and policymakers by highlighting the organisational conditions most strongly associated with innovative capacity in schools—particularly participatory and collaborative climates, supportive
work environments for principals, and leadership routines that reinforce continuous
improvement.

Journal of Educational Change

School climate
School climate items emerge as the most influential predictor set in the study, aligning with prior TALIS-based evidence from Türkiye, which indicates that school
climate positively predicts organisational innovativeness (Kaya, 2021). The highestranked climate indicators consistently reflect teacher initiative and collective efficacy. Schools where staff are encouraged to lead new initiatives and where a mutually
supportive, collaborative culture is reported show the strongest positive contributions to predicted innovativeness. A second set of climate items highlights shared
responsibility and participatory decision-making, including opportunities for both
staff and students to participate in school decisions, as well as an explicit culture of
shared responsibility for school issues. The prominence of cooperation with the local
community further highlights the importance of external orientation, suggesting that
innovativeness is linked not only to internal collaboration but also to schools’ capacity to mobilize relationships and resources beyond the organization. In addition to
these enabling conditions, the model identifies a clear capacity constraint: greater
hindrance due to the shortage or inadequacy of time with students contributes negatively to predicted innovativeness.
Taken together, the SHAP patterns suggest that the model primarily “recognizes”
innovative schools as those combining empowered staff initiatives, collaborative and
participatory cultures, community connectedness, coherent implementation capacity, and strong relational trust—while time scarcity functions as a practical inhibitor. These findings are consistent with scholarship linking school innovativeness
to collaborative organizational climates and social capital (e.g., Blömeke et al.,
2021; Birasnav et al., 2023; Dedering & Pietsch, 2025; Pan et al., 2024) and with
broader work showing that trust, collaboration, supportive management, openness
to innovation, understanding of curricular goals, and high expectations for student
achievement tend to cooccur with innovation-oriented organizational functioning
in education (e.g., Chou et al., 2018; Çoban & Atasoy, 2020; Ersozlu et al., 2022;
Hidayat & Patras, 2024; Hu et al., 2024; Huang & Wang, 2021; Kundu & Roy, 2023;
Moolenaar et al., 2010, 2011; Polatcan et al., 2024; Vermeulen et al., 2020; Zhou &
Ismail, 2025).
Job satisfaction
Our item-level results indicate that principals’ job satisfaction—captured by (a) willingness to recommend the school, (b) enjoyment of their work, (c) perceived collegial support and (d) perceived overall feelings of satisfaction at work—shows a clear
positive association with organisational innovativeness. Interpreted substantively,
the model “credits” innovativeness to leadership contexts in which principals feel
attached to their school, experience their work as meaningful and efficacious, and
perceive strong relational support from colleagues. This pattern aligns with TALISbased evidence from Türkiye, which shows that school administrators’ job satisfaction significantly predicts organisational innovativeness (Kaya, 2021). It is also
consistent with education-sector studies that link satisfaction and supportive climates
to innovation-related outcomes, albeit often from a teacher-focused perspective (e.g.,

13

Journal of Educational Change

Atasoy & Özden, 2022; Hu et al., 2024). Beyond schooling, correctional-sector evidence similarly reports positive associations between perceptions of organisational
innovation and job satisfaction (Lambert & Hogan, 2010).
Theoretically, the prominence of job-satisfaction items becomes more intelligible when situated within the emotional and psychosocial realities of school leadership and the job demands–resources (JD–R) tradition. Principals’ work is widely
characterized by sustained accountability pressures, frequent conflict management,
and intensive relational labour. Maxwell and Riley (2017), for example, show that
principals report substantially greater emotional demands and burnout (and lower
well-being) than the general population does, even while job satisfaction can remain
comparatively high. Longitudinal JD–R evidence likewise indicates that job demands
are linked to greater burnout and lower job satisfaction over time, whereas resources
such as resilience show protective associations (Marsh et al., 2023). Complementary
findings from Canadian principals further suggest that work intensification is consequential for job satisfaction, whereas support and high-quality professional relationships operate as key buffers (Wang et al., 2018).
Within this framing, principals’ job satisfaction can be interpreted as an integrative indicator of whether leaders can successfully manage emotional labour while
mobilizing psychological and social resources. Evidence from the broader well-being
literature links job satisfaction to lower burnout-related outcomes (as shown metaanalytically among teachers) (Yorulmaz et al., 2017) and connects positive psychological resources (e.g., hope, efficacy, resilience, optimism) to job satisfaction and
well-being (Avey et al., 2011; Kurt & Demirbolat, 2019). In turn, psychological capital and job satisfaction have been associated with innovative behaviour in organisational research, providing a plausible mechanism for why satisfaction-related items
exhibit strong positive contributions in our model (Alshebami, 2021). Taken together,
these strands suggest a coherent interpretation: principals who report higher job satisfaction are more likely to retain the affective energy, resilience, and relational capacity required to sustain experimentation, support teachers’ risk-taking, and persist
through implementation challenges—core conditions for school-level organisational
innovation.
Leadership
Our results indicate that school leadership practices—such as leaders’ specific
actions to support cooperation for the development of new practices—are associated with organisational innovativeness. This finding is consistent with evidence
that leadership can foster innovation-oriented behaviours in schools. For example,
via multilevel analysis of Taiwan TALIS 2018 data, Hsieh et al. (2024) reported
that distributed leadership had a significant direct effect on teacher innovativeness,
whereas the effect of instructional leadership was mainly indirect and was mediated
through teacher self-efficacy. Related work also explores how leaders can enable systemic change in increasingly complex environments (Arar et al., 2024), and a recent
systematic review of social justice leadership argues that culturally responsive and
equity-oriented approaches move beyond rigid, hierarchical models by foregrounding inclusivity, care, and critical consciousness in educational settings (Chaaban et

13

Journal of Educational Change

al., 2025). Accordingly, a positive relationship between principal inclusive leadership
and teachers’ innovative behaviour has been found in recent research, with psychological empowerment and the school innovation climate serving as mediators (Bao,
2024).
More specifically, among the leadership approaches discussed in the organisational
innovativeness literature, our results align most closely with instructional leadership.
Behaviors such as staffing a school with high-quality teachers, providing appropriate
support, observing teaching practices, and fostering teacher growth and collaboration are widely treated as core responsibilities of instructional leaders (Hallinger,
2005; Horng & Loeb, 2010). Similarly, Kilag and Sasan (2023) asserted that instructional leaders promote teacher development by modelling effective practices, providing feedback, and facilitating collaborative learning opportunities—suggesting that
instructional leadership extends beyond monitoring to position teacher collaboration
as a routine for building professional capacity. By fostering a climate of cooperation, leaders help clarify expectations and strengthen the collective implementation
of new practices by the followers. Consistent with this interpretation, Sullivan et al.
(2012), in their study of leadership for collaboration in public management, identified
leadership behaviours focused on supporting professional collaboration as vital for
organisational effectiveness.
Schooling in diverse environments
The diversity-belief item (TC3G40D) asks principals to estimate how many teachers in their school would agree that children and young people should learn that
people of different cultures have a lot in common. Across TALIS countries, this
belief is reported as the most widely endorsed cultural-diversity orientation among
teachers, with very high levels of principal-reported agreement (OECD, 2019). Conceptually, this belief aligns with culturally responsive school leadership (CRSL), a
framework that emphasizes leaders’ roles in shaping school environments and curricula to affirm minority identities, promote inclusion and equity, and build common ground across diverse student populations (Khalifa, 2020; Khalifa et al., 2016).
CRSL positions principals as critical agents who foster inclusive climates through
critically self-reflective practice, culturally sustaining pedagogy, and meaningful
community engagement—capacities that may simultaneously enable organizational
innovativeness by expanding the range of perspectives and practices available within
the school. Thus, as Yamashiro et al. (2022) argue, leadership preparation programs
must address the mutually reinforcing goals of increasing the development of leaders
from underrepresented populations and cultivating culturally responsive leadership
dispositions for effective schooling in diverse environments. Our finding that this
diversity-belief variable predicts organisational innovativeness suggests that culturally responsive orientations may serve not only equity goals but also broader organisational capacity for change and adaptation.
Empirically, scholars suggest that schools embracing cultural diversity may foster more transformational and innovative organisational structures (Cardona et al.,
2025; Culha & Yücel, 2024) and that inclusive, emotionally supportive environments
can enhance innovative behaviour (Çelik et al., 2024). Consistent with diversity-

as-resource arguments, groups that value diversity may produce stronger outcomes
by widening perspectives and improving problem solving (Homan et al., 2007),
and diversifying the educator workforce has a strong positive influence on students
(Cheung & Gong, 2022).
Moreover, our SHAP results indicate that this item contributes only weakly and
variably to predicting innovativeness compared with the dominant climate and jobsatisfaction predictors. Thus, while the direction is broadly compatible with CRSL
and diversity-as-resource perspectives, the empirical signal here is best interpreted as
small within the study.
Induction and mentoring
Our results suggest that providing induction and mentoring support to a larger proportion of teachers enables schools to become more innovative in organizational terms.
Schools with structured mentoring opportunities for all teachers tend to exhibit more
innovative practices. The importance of supporting new teachers through structured
mentoring has been well documented in the literature (Ingersoll & Strong, 2011;
Smith & Ingersoll, 2004). However, our finding that schools providing mentoring
to all teachers show greater innovativeness suggests broader benefits. This aligns
with Bressman et al.‘s (2018) argument that experienced teachers, like novices, need
opportunities for reflection, goal setting, and professional growth. Similarly, in the
business sector, Moore and Wang (2017) reported that mentoring top leaders promotes organizational innovativeness by fostering psychological safety—an environment where not only leaders but also all organizational members are encouraged to
explore new ideas and speak up. Mentoring that encompasses all career stages may
contribute to a learning organization culture that supports continuous improvement
and innovation in schools.
Limitations
The findings adopt an exploratory, predictive perspective by identifying factors
associated with organisational innovativeness; accordingly, the study does not
claim causal effects. The scope is limited to variables available in TALIS 2018 and
to participating education systems, which provides rare cross-system coverage but
also constrains contextual sensitivity. The machine learning models capture statistical regularities that hold on average in the pooled international sample; however,
they cannot explain how the meaning and salience of “innovation” may vary across
systems with distinct histories, policies, and sociocultural norms. A key limitation
concerns cross-national measurement invariance of the dependent variable: because
only configural invariance was supported (and stronger forms such as metric/scalar
invariance were not established), the identified associations should be interpreted as
pooled-sample patterns rather than effects that operate identically across countries.
Under these conditions, the use of a pooled prediction model raises the possibility that the algorithm may partially capture country-specific measurement artefacts
or response styles, in addition to substantive relationships between predictors and
the outcome. Consequently, predictors identified as important in the pooled model

13

Journal of Educational Change

should not be interpreted as evidence of invariant effects operating uniformly across
countries. Rather, these findings indicate variables that are salient within the pooled
international sample, with the explicit recognition that the nature, strength, and functional form of these associations may vary across national contexts. Future research
should replicate these analyses via country-specific models or multigroup approaches
under stronger measurement invariance conditions to assess the robustness and contextual specificity of the identified predictors. In addition, all the measures rely on
principals’ self-reports, increasing the risk of single-source bias and common method
variance, which may inflate associations. Finally, prioritizing malleable school process variables necessarily deemphasizes structural contextual factors (e.g., socioeconomic composition) that shape inequality and may help identify systematically
underresourced schools; future work could integrate such indicators as controls,
moderators, or complementary risk markers and examine country-specific models to
assess contextual variation.

Conclusion
This study used TALIS 2018 principal data from 47 education systems to examine which school-level conditions most strongly predict principals’ perceptions of
organisational innovativeness. Using an explainable machine learning workflow, we
compared several predictive algorithms, selected XGBoost as the best-performing
approach, and then applied SHAP to interpret the direction and relative contribution of the most influential predictors. Overall, the results suggest that organisational
innovativeness is shaped less by single structural inputs and more by an interlocking
set of cultural and relational conditions within schools.
The school climate emerged as the dominant predictor cluster: predicted innovativeness was greater in schools where staff are encouraged to initiate new practices,
collaboration and mutual support are strong, responsibility is shared, decision-making
is participatory, and the school maintains an outward orientation through cooperation
with the local community. In contrast, practical capacity constraints—particularly
perceived shortages of time for working with students—were associated with lower
predicted innovativeness, which is consistent with the idea that implementation bandwidth is a key condition for sustaining change. Principals’ job satisfaction constituted a robust secondary predictor. Greater predicted innovativeness was associated
with principals reporting stronger attachment to their school, greater enjoyment and
overall satisfaction at work, and more supportive collegial relations, suggesting that
leader well-being and relational support may function as organisational resources
for persistence and follow-through during improvement efforts. Leadership practices,
especially actions to support teacher cooperation in developing new practices, also
showed a small but positive association, aligning with an instructional-leadership
interpretation that emphasizes leaders’ supporting professional learning and collaboration. Two additional predictors—principals’ inclusive diversity beliefs and
access to mentoring—showed smaller yet generally positive contributions. Although
weaker and, for diverse beliefs, more variable, these patterns suggest that inclusive

13

Journal of Educational Change

orientations and mentoring infrastructures may modestly strengthen the relational
and learning conditions that support innovation.
Methodologically, the study demonstrates the value of combining predictive
benchmarking with explainable AI to map the relative importance and direction of
correlates of school innovativeness in large-scale datasets. This finding indicates
that efforts to strengthen innovativeness may benefit from cultivating participatory,
trust-based climates; protecting time and implementation capacity; supporting principal well-being; and institutionalizing collaborative leadership practices and mentoring routines. Future research should extend these findings with longitudinal and
mixed-method designs to clarify mechanisms and strengthen causal inference and to
examine how patterns vary across national and organisational contexts. Finally, these
results should be interpreted as pooled-sample predictive regularities that highlight
potential leverage points within schools, not as universal effects that operate identically across countries; structural and contextual constraints remain central considerations for policy design.
