Abstract 
The standard methodology for many large-scale assessments in education involves 
regressing latent variables on numerous contextual variables to estimate proficiency 
distributions. To reduce the number of contextual variables used in the regression 
and improve estimation, we propose and evaluate principal component analysis 
on the covariance matrix as a data reduction method for the contextual variables. 
This adjustment, compared to the conventional use of a correlation matrix, weights 
variables with respect to sample size. In a simulation study involving low test reliability 
for a subset of the sample, we found that PCA-covariance substantially reduces esti-
mation bias and mean squared error. We demonstrate how a large-scale assessment 
can transition to using PCA-covariance without impacting primary trend inferences 
using data from the 2022 National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP). Our 
findings demonstrate that PCA-covariance accommodates a broader reporting scope 
with improved estimation.
Introduction
Large-scale assessments in education such as PISA, PIAAC, NAEP, TIMSS, PIRLS and 
NEPS follow similar design principles and analysis methodology (Jewsbury, 2023). These 
assessments estimate parameters of interest such as means for policy-relevant groups on 
item response theory (IRT) latent proficiency variables using plausible values (Mislevy, 
1991; Mislevy et al., 1992). Plausible values are multiple imputations for the unobserved 
values on the latent proficiency variables, drawn from conditional proficiency distribu-
tions from a latent regression model serving as the imputation model. Plausible values 
can be treated similarly to observed values for latent proficiency and enable second-
ary users to analyze large-scale assessment data with standard complete-data statistical 
analysis methods such as t-tests and ANOVA (Jewsbury et al., 2025).
The construction of the latent regression models to serve as the imputation model for 
plausible values involves a major trade-off. On one hand, to support proficiency mean 
estimation for a large number of groups, inclusion of a large number of predictors to rep-
resent the groups is required. If the proficiency mean for a group is estimated based on 
the plausible values, but an indicator variable for that group is not included or explained

population mean (Mislevy, 1991).
Notably, biases due to non-congeniality may be mitigated to the extent that the group 
indicator variable is explained by the predictors of the latent regression, even if the 
group indicator variable is not included in the model directly (Marsman et  al., 2016; 
Mislevy, 1991). Consequently, inclusion of more predictors in the latent regression may 
not only prevent congeniality-related biases for the included variables but also reduce 
congeniality-related biases on variables not directly included, as the proportion of vari-
ance explained in excluded variables may be expected to increase as the number of pre-
dictors increase (von Davier, 2013). For these reasons, including as many predictors as 
possible in the latent regression model is often recommended (Schofield et al., 2015; von 
Davier, 2013; von Davier et al., 2019). This follows recommendations in the broader mul-
tiple imputation literature towards imputation models that are as large as possible (Col-
lins et al., 2001; Little & Rubin, 2019; Murray, 2018; Rubin, 1996; Schafer, 2003).
On the other hand, inclusion of excessive predictors in the latent regression has long 
been understood to introduce estimation instability (von Davier et al., 2019). A common 
strategy to reduce the number of predictors is the use of principal component analysis 
(PCA) as a data reduction technique. The resulting principal components rather than 
the original contextual variables are used as predictors in the latent regression, poten-
tially with some high-priority primary variables also included as predictors (Jewsbury, 
2023). The number of principal components selected is typically chosen as the minimum 
number of components required to explain a pre-set amount of variance in the contex-
tual variables such as 80% or 90% (NCES, 2024f). The number may be limited to not 
exceed a pre-set number of components relative to the sample size, such as the sample 
size divided by 15 or 20 in the case of PISA (OECD, 2024). Due to such limits, the pro-
portion of variance in the contextual variables explained by the selected principal com-
ponents has been decreasing as the number of contextual variables has increased over 
time in NAEP and PISA (NCES, 2024a; OECD, 2024).1
In many large-scale assessments, most contextual variables are categorical, such as 
demographics or responses on a discrete rating scale such as a Likert scale. In practice, 
these categorical contextual variables are converted to contrast-coded dummy variables 
(von Davier et al., 2007), Jewsbury et al., 2024a),2 Principal component analysis is then 
applied to the correlation matrix of the contrast-coded dummy variables (PCA-COR).
Jewsbury et al. (in press) showed that estimation bias and error on means and vari-
ances may be substantial with IRT-latent regression models with many predictors, par-
ticularly when the test reliability is low for even just a subset of the test takers. Notably, 
their simulation involved the standard large-scale assessment methodology, that is, 
PCA-COR applied to the correlation matrix of dichotomous variables. They identified

that the estimation instability was due to the accumulation of bias and error across the 
large number of estimated regression coefficients impacting the estimated means and 
variances. Importantly, this suggests that estimation instability occurs not due to the 
number of regression coefficients per se, but also on how well those regression coeffi-
cients are estimated. Consequently, estimation could be improved by carefully selecting 
the set of predictors such that all the regression coefficients can be well-estimated in the 
data, but PCA-COR has no direct mechanism to ensure this.
A relatively modest change to the standard large-scale assessment methodology is 
to apply PCA to the covariance matrix of the contrast-coded dummy variables (PCA-
COV), rather than the correlation matrix. When PCA is applied to the covariance 
matrix, higher variance contextual variables are prioritized in the data reduction while 
less variance is explained in the low variance contextual variables. As we show in this 
paper, estimating the effects for low variance dichotomous variables may be associated 
with greater estimation bias and error, so de-prioritizing these variables may prevent the 
inclusion of poorly estimated regression coefficients, and hence allow many predictors 
to be included in the IRT-latent regression without compromising estimation stability.
As many large-scale assessments highly value score comparability across time, some-
times referred to as trends, the evaluation of methodological modifications can intro-
duce a trade-off. A methodology that may improve estimation quality of comparisons 
within a particular data collection may introduce methodological confounds when com-
paring results from that data collection and prior results based on the earlier methodol-
ogy. PCA-COV has the potential benefit as a relatively modest change in that it may be 
less likely to introduce methodological confounds on score comparisons over time.
A strategy incorporated by some large-scale assessments such as PISA involves includ-
ing a small number of primary variables directly as predictors, bypassing PCA (OECD, 
2024). This strategy ensures that  high-priority variables are fully represented in the 
latent regression, guaranteeing congeniality for latent mean estimation conditional on 
these variables. We describe this strategy as a hybrid approach, where a small number 
of primary variables are directly included while all other variables are subject to data 
reduction via PCA. As high priority but low variance variables can be under-represented 
by PCA-COV, hybrid approaches could be used to ensure all high priority variables are 
represented.
Present paper
In the first section, we describe the theory to motivate the potential use of PCA-COV 
for data reduction in large-scale assessments. We provide a technical description to 
introduce notation and describe how estimation error on the IRT-latent regression coef-
ficients translates to estimation error on mean estimates with the plausible values. We 
show that bias and variance of regression coefficient estimators in simplified IRT-latent 
regression models are functions of the variance of the (dichotomous) predictor. We then 
provide a short description of PCA and describe how PCA-COV weights or prioritizes 
variables by variance.
In the second section, we describe a simulation that compares PCA-COR, PCA-COV, 
and their hybrid counterparts using a simulated assessment design that can produce large 
estimation bias and error (Jewsbury et al., in press). The key research question is whether

of predictors, congeniality-related biases may be introduced. An alternate approach is to 
only specify regression coefficients that are well estimated with minimal bias and error, 
potentially allowing for large numbers of predictors without compromising estimation 
stability. The question then becomes how we can be principled in the selection of predic-
tors for the latent regression that prevents the specification of poorly estimated regres-
sion coefficients.

Simulation study
Data generation
General: In order to ensure a realistic population model data structure, the contextual 
information and proficiency values were not generated but instead drawn from a real 
large-scale assessment dataset, from the 2019 NAEP mathematics grade 8 assessment. 
The dataset, comprising 147,400 cases, was treated as a population to draw random 
samples (with replacement) of size 1000 within each simulation. These smaller samples 
are analogous to the state samples in U.S. large-scale assessments or country samples in 
international assessments (Jewsbury, 2023).5
The contextual information from the real data collection was used as the contextual 
information in the simulation, xi . The first plausible value for the NAEP mathematics 
composite scale, scaled to have a mean of zero and a variance of 1, was used as the true 
latent proficiency of each simulee θi in both the item response generation as well as to 
calculate the true values in the bias and mean squared error (MSE) calculations. Latent 
proficiency was univariate. Item responses were generated according to the specifica-
tions given below with the simdata function in the mirt 1.30 R package (Chalmers, 
2012).
Contextual information: Contextual variables in the NAEP dataset included variables 
collected directly from states, as well as questionnaires provided to the student, to their 
teachers, and to their school administrators. Two-way interactions between the major 
reporting variables were also included as contextual variables.6 All variables were con-
trast-coded dichotomous variables. The contrast-coded dichotomous variables included 
a contrast indicating missing for each contextual variable. Following the standard NAEP 
procedure, the SWEEP operator was applied to remove variables with zero counts or 
very small variances (Goodnight, 1979).
In total after applying SWEEP there were 1584 total contrast-coded dichotomous vari-
ables, including 133 major reporting variables, 268 two-way interactions between major 
reporting variables, 428 student questionnaire variables, 419 teacher questionnaire vari-
ables, 305 school questionnaire variables, and 31 variables from special questionnaires 
for students with disabilities and English language learners.7

The item parameter distributions were chosen to roughly correspond to real mathe-

Item responses: While there were 40 items in the pool, data for each simulee was gener-
ated with a smaller number of observed item responses to simulate the matrix sampling 
designs common in large-scale assessments (Jewsbury, 2023), and to represent naturally 
occurring missing data. Specifically, each simulee had a 10% chance to have only one observed 
item response, 10% chance to have two observed item responses, 10% chance to have three 
observed item responses, and 70% chance to have 8 observed item responses. Responses were 
selected to be missing completely at random (MCAR; Jewsbury et al., 2024b).
The data generating model for the item responses was selected to correspond to an 
assessment design shown to be inducive of estimation biases when the number of pre-
dictors is large relative to the sample size (see Jewsbury et al., in press).9 The same item 
response sets were used in each simulation condition.

Model and plausible value estimation
Model specification: For every simulated assessment, seven IRT-latent regression mod-
els were estimated with increasingly more predictors in addition to an intercept term in 
increments of 50 up to 300. Specifically, a PCA was conducted based on the data reduc-
tion method, and the principal components were ordered from largest to smallest eigen-
value. For the Hybrid conditions, the dummy variables for the main reporting groups 
were included separately and ordered first. The IRT-latent regression models included 
an intercept term in addition to regression parameters for the first P variables, where 
P ∈{0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300} . Note that when P = 0 , only the intercept term was 
included. For reference, the number of predictors used in the official 2019 NAEP mathe-
matics grade 8 analysis ranged from 108–305 across the state-level analyses with a mean 
of 242 (NCES, 2024e).
Model estimation: The item parameters were fixed to the generating values rather 
than estimated. There were two reasons for this. First, to reduce confounds associated 
with item parameter estimation. Second, to resemble common operational practice 
where item parameters are precisely estimated in larger datasets comprising multiple 
countries, states, or even time periods while the the latent regression parameters are 
estimated in much smaller subsets of the total data such as individual states (NCES, 
2024a; OECD, 2024).
The IRT-latent regression models were estimated with the mirt function in the mirt 
R package (Chalmers, 2012). The estimation was marginal-maximum likelihood via an 
EM algorithm (Bock & Aitkin, 1981; Mislevy, 1984, 1985), using 51 equally spaced quad-
rature points between -5 and 5. The maximum number of EM cycles was increased to 
5000.
Plausible values generation: PVs were drawn from approximated posterior distribu-
tions conditional on the fixed maximum-likelihood estimates of the regression parame-
ters. The PVs were drawn conditional on fixed regression parameters to avoid additional 
confounds associated with sampling from the regression parameters (for analysis and 
discussion, see Jewsbury et al., in press).

The bias on the means is shown in the top left panel of Fig.  2. All methods are

observed: negative biases for the higher performing White and Asian groups and 
positive for the lower performing Black, Hispanic, AI/AN and NH/PI groups. These

not represented as predictors in the latent regression (Mislevy, 1991). As expected, 
these biases are reduced as P increases. Notably, PCA-COV still shows shrinkage-to-
the-population-mean bias for the smaller groups (Asian, and especially AI/AN and

small groups. PCA-COV-Hybrid that directly includes the main reporting groups as 
predictors including Asian, AI/AN, and NH/PI, shows the best performance in terms 
of bias on the means.
Based on the simulations and discussions in Jewsbury et al., (in press), negative biases 
on the means especially for low proficiency groups are expected as P increases under 
the simulated assessment design conditions. All methods show the bias tending in the 
negative direction especially for the lower performing groups, Black, Hispanic, AI/AN 
and NH/PI. However, the magnitude of the negative biases is larger for PCA-COR-based 
methods than for PCA-COV-based methods. Non-negligible biases are observed with 
both PCA-COR methods even for P = 50 for Black, Hispanic, AI/AN and NH/PI.

the PCA-COV methods consistently outperform the PCA-COR methods. Interest-

despite the expectation that PCA-COV might under-represent small groups com-
pared to PCA-COR. PCA-COV-Hybrid slightly underperforms PCA-COV in terms of 
MSE, which is more notable for the smaller groups.

variances can be very large, especially with the PCA-COR methods. The performance 
of the two PCA-COV methods is similar, and both methods notably outperform both 
PCA-COR methods. Interestingly, again, the greatest outperformance of the PCA-

As noted previously, the bias and MSE of means and variances reflect the cumulative

of the contextual variables in the latent regression without introducing excessive bias 
or error on the main reporting outcomes. This behavior aligns with theoretical expec-

the data reduction (see Eq. 8), PCA-COV methods avoid specifying poorly estimated 
regression coefficients associated with low-variance dichotomous variables (see Eq.  6

Real data comparison in NAEP
A major challenge in introducing methodological innovations in large-scale assessments 
that involve score comparisons over time (trends) is the potential to introduce method-
related confounds to these score comparisons. In this section, we provide a real data 
example showing that primary trend inferences may be unaffected and therefore uncon-
founded by a transition to PCA-COV, even with a larger latent regression that accounts 
for more variance in the contextual variables.
This study summarizes findings from a comprehensive empirical investigation con-
ducted for the 2022 NAEP reading and mathematics assessments that provided support 
for the transition of NAEP to a modified data reduction method in 2022 (NCES, 2024f).
Data collection
The data was collected as part of the NAEP assessments in 2022. Details concerning the 
data collection in NAEP are available in other places (e.g., Jewsbury et al., 2020; NCES, 
2024c).
Model and plausible value estimation
This real data comparison follows the operational NAEP procedures. For precise infor-
mation on the operational procedures used by NAEP, the reader is directed to extensive 
documentation in official sources (e.g., NCES, 2024a). Only the key methodology details 
and differences between the alternate methods are summarized below.
Following standard operational NAEP procedures (Jewsbury, 2023), the IRT-latent 
regression was estimated in two steps. First, estimates of the item parameters were 
obtained by fitting an IRT model to the item response data. Second, estimates of the 
regression parameters were obtained by fitting an IRT-latent regression to the item 
response and contextual information data, with the item parameters fixed to the esti-
mates from the first step.
Two IRT-latent regression models were fit, using either the previous data reduction 
method to select the predictors used in the regression (COR) or the newly proposed 
method based on PCA-covariance using a hybrid procedure (COV-H). To minimize 

Page 15 of 40
Jewsbury and Johnson ﻿Large-scale Assessments in Education           (2025) 13:30 
	
confusion, we distinguish between the two procedures by referring to them as COR and 
COV-H in the real data comparison. This nomenclature is intended to differentiate these 
procedures from their counterparts, PCA-COR and PCA-COV-Hybrid, from the earlier 
simulation study.
Apart from the data reduction method, the IRT-latent regression models followed 
standard procedures from previous NAEP assessments (see Jewsbury, 2023) which 
involves applying a data reduction method and fitting a separate IRT-latent regression 
model for each state sample separately, generating PVs for all students within each state, 
and combining the PVs across all states into a single dataset (for more detail, see NCES, 
2024a).
For both data reduction procedures COR and COV-H, the first two steps to obtain the 
predictor variables for the latent regression were the same. First, all contextual infor-
mation was converted to contrast-coded dummy variables. Second, a SWEEP operator 
(Goodnight, 1979) was applied to remove variables with near perfect multicollineari-
ties and variables with near zero variances. After these two steps, the two procedures 
diverged.
For the data reduction procedure COR, principal component analysis was fit to the 
correlation matrix of the remaining contrast-coded dummy variables. In previous NAEP 
assessments since 2017, the minimum number of principal components were selected 
that explain 80% of the variance in the dummy variables. However, because the 2022 
NAEP assessments had smaller sample size and more contextual variables than the pre-
vious assessments, this rule would have resulted in latent regressions with very low sam-
ple size to number of predictor ratios for many states. Therefore, if explaining 80% of the 
variance reduced this ratio below 8, the ratio was capped at 8. This occurred for approxi-
mately 50% of the states at grade 8, where 75% to 80% of the variance in the dummy 
variables was explained. The IRT-latent regression was fit using the selected principal 
components as the predictors.
For the data reduction procedure COV-H, a hybrid procedure similar to PCA-COV-
Hybrid in the simulation study was applied. First, variables for the five main constructs 
that NAEP is congressionally-mandated to measure, gender, race/ethnicity, English lan-
guage learner status, disability status, and socioeconomic status measured by free and 
reduced price lunch eligibility, were selected to be used as direct predictors in the IRT-
latent regression model. The remaining, non-main variables were regressed on the vari-
ables for the five main constructs. Principal component analysis was fit to the covariance 
matrix of the residuals from this regression. The minimum number of principal com-
ponents to explain 90% of the variance in the residuals were selected. The IRT-latent 
regression was fit using the dummy variables for the five main constructs in combination 
with the selected principal components as predictors.
In the COV-H procedure, the principal components were selected to explain 90% of 
the residuals following the observation in the simulation study that more predictors may 
be used with PCA-covariance. 90% was the original setting used in NAEP until it was 
reduced to 80% in 2017 (NCES, 2024f). Note that 90% variance explained in the residu-
als of the non-main variables means that more than 90% of the variance in the non-main 
variables is explained by the predictors in the IRT-latent regression, as a proportion of 
the variance is already explained by the main variables that were directly included as 

Page 16 of 40
Jewsbury and Johnson ﻿Large-scale Assessments in Education           (2025) 13:30 
predictors. Furthermore, 100% of the variance in the main variables is explained by the 
predictors. Consequently, substantially more variance in the contextual information 
was explained by the predictors for the COV-H procedure than in the COR procedure. 
While the COR procedure explained 75–80% of the variance in the contextual variables 
within each state, the COV-H procedure explained approximately 92% of the variance 
within each state.

Discussion
To summarize, we showed analytically and empirically that IRT-latent regression 
coefficient estimation bias and error, respectively, is inversely associated with the 
variance of dichotomous predictors under simplified IRT models. Notably, many 
regression coefficients may be estimated with very little error, whereas regression 
coefficients for low-variance dichotomous predictors can involve substantial estima-
tion error (see Fig. 1). These results motivated the consideration of PCA-COV, which 
weights variables by variance (Eq. 8). The simulation shows that PCA-COV greatly 
improves estimation of means and variances with large latent regression models in an 
assessment design involving low test reliability. Finally, the real data comparison pro-
vides an example of how a real large-scale assessment may transition to PCA-COV 
without introducing methodological confounds on primary trend comparisons, while 
also increasing the amount of variance explained in the contextual variables. The sim-
ulation and real data results taken together suggest that PCA-COV can improve esti-
mation robustness to potential future increases in the number of contextual variables 
or decreases in test reliability or sample size, while not impacting primary trend infer-
ences that are currently well-estimated.
We also evaluated the use of hybrid models, where a small number of primary vari-
ables were directly included as predictors in the regression while the remaining vari-
ables were subject to PCA. Hybrid models may be more important with PCA-COV, 
as low variance primary variables may be poorly explained, resulting in congenial-
ity-related biases (Meng, 1994; Mislevy, 1991). Indeed, the simulation results (Fig. 2) 
showed that a larger number of predictors was required to reduce mean bias for small 
groups with PCA-COV vs PCA-COR (specifically, Asian, AI/AN, and NH/PI). The 
least mean bias on these groups was obtained with PCA-COV-Hybrid, verifying the 
utility of combining hybrid approaches with PCA-COV when mean bias for small pri-
mary variables is prioritized. Interestingly, however, the greatest outperformance of 
the PCA-COV based approaches versus the PCA-COR based approaches in terms of 
mean MSE, variance bias, and variance MSE, was observed for smaller groups.
A major advantage of PCA-COV compared to other potential improvements to 
improve estimation robustness in large-scale assessments is that PCA-COV may be 
less likely to introduce new undesired behaviors. Approaches that use the cognitive 
item responses such as LASSO or partial least squares regression may increase over-
fitting due to double-dipping (Kriegeskorte et al., 2009). Direct use of the test reliability 
in the data reduction may also introduce issues associated with circularity. Changes to 
the latent regression model or plausible value generation directly may be more likely to 
introduce methodological confounds. Thus, PCA-COV being a relatively minor meth-
odological change may be an advantage for large-scale assessments that prioritize score 
comparability over time and want to avoid introducing method-related confounds on 
trends, while also meaningfully improving estimation robustness.
Much of the discussion in this paper is premised on the assumption that most or all of 
the contextual variables subject to PCA are dichotomous with equivalent scoring (e.g., 
taking the values of zero and one), which is typically the case in large-scale assessments 

Page 19 of 40
Jewsbury and Johnson ﻿Large-scale Assessments in Education           (2025) 13:30 
	
where the contextual variables are contrast-coded (Jewsbury, 2023). When PCA-COV 
is used, the scale of the variables impacts the prioritization of the variables in the data 
reduction, so care should be taken to avoid unintended behaviors in the data reduc-
tion. For example, continuous variables could be scaled to have a variance of .25 to have 
the maximum weight for a dummy-coded variable (i.e., a dummy-coded variable with a 
mean of .5). If continuous variables are not scaled, these variables may be unintention-
ally prioritized or de-prioritized in the data reduction relative to their variances.
Two related research questions were evaluated with the same simulation design as the 
primary research question concerning PCA-correlation versus PCA-covariance. First, in 
Appendix C we consider whether excluding test takers with few item responses from the 
regression parameter estimation, while still generating plausible values for these test tak-
ers conditional on their item responses, improves estimators of parameters of interest. 
This is an approach taken by certain large-scale assessments. However, the simulation 
results summarized in Appendix A suggests that this procedure increases estimator bias 
and variance. This is consistent with theoretical results (Jewsbury et al., in press) that 
fewer item responses used to estimate regression parameters will increase, not decrease, 
bias.
Second, in Appendix D we consider applying a weight factor equal to the square root 
of the number of observed items to every case when applying PCA-covariance. This 
effectively weights variables based on the aggregated number of item responses rather 
than simply sample size with PCA-covariance, which may produce better results accord-
ing to analytical results (Jewsbury et al., in press). While simulation results do show an 
improvement particularly in the bias and MSE for variances, the differences with and 
without the weight factor are small. As the differences were small, the weight factor was 
not applied in NAEP due to the additional complexity of the approach and the potential 
to introduce unexpected behaviors.
In summary, PCA-COV was shown via simulation to substantially improve estimation 
of proficiency means and variances when test information is low for a subset of the sam-
ple and when the number of predictors is large. Analytical results are provided to moti-
vate the use of PCA-COV and provide insight on how the method enables improved 
estimation. A real data example is provided to show how a large-scale assessment may 
transition to PCA-COV without introducing methodological confounds on score com-
parisons over time, while improving estimation robustness and increasing the variance 
explained in the contextual variables.

Appendix C: Exclusion of test takers with few item responses in the regression 
parameter estimation
The simulation results from Jewsbury et  al. (in press) suggest that the estimators 
examined in this paper are not robust to large P with respect to the sample size N 
in assessment designs where at least a minority of test takers had low test informa-
tion given their ability and the number of items responded to. One potential solution 
would be to simply exclude item responses from the data from test takers with too 
few item responses (e.g., less than 4–6) when estimating the regression parameters, 
but still generating PVs for those test takers conditional on their item responses.
However, the analytical results in Appendix A suggest that this strategy would be 
counter-productive. In the simple IRT-latent regression model, bias on regression 
parameters increases as the number of item responses used in the estimation decrease. 
Consequently, deleting item responses from the dataset is expected to increase, not 
decrease, estimator bias.
We evaluated whether deleting item responses for test takers with few observed 
item responses in both simulation designs. In both simulation designs, deleting item 
responses increased estimator bias.

equivalent condition but where test takers with fewer than 4 item responses are excluded 
from the regression parameter estimation (PCA-COR-E), using the simulation design 
from the main text. The assessment design conditions are the same as shown in the main 
text: the difficult 3PL item condition, 8 items per test taker, and the Missing condition. 
While some item responses were excluded from the regression parameter estimation, all 
item responses were used to generate the PVs. The below tables show a clear pattern that

Appendix D: Applying a weight factor associated with the number of item 
responses in PCA‑COV
The difference between PCA-covariance and PCA-correlation may be described as 
that PCA-covariance weights variables based on sample size, to de-prioritize small 
groups in the latent regression that may introduce larger bias and variance into the 
results. However, the analytical results in Appendix A suggest that the number of 
item responses is more important than the sample size per se, so if the number of item 
responses across test takers varies, considering only sample size may not be ideal.
To effectively weight variables based on item responses rather than sample size, 
we can apply a weight factor to each case equal to the square root of the number of 
observed item responses. This weight factor is only used to select the principal com-
ponents and not to estimate the regression parameters, or parameters of interest such 
as means.
The two tables below compare PCA-COV involving no weight factor to PCA-COV-J 
where each case is weighted by the number of observed item responses. Across all com-
parisons, the results are fairly similar. There appears to be only a minor improvement 
when the case weight is applied, which is more evident in the bias and MSE of variances.
