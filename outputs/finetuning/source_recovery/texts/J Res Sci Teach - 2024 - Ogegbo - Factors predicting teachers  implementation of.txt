Abstract
Inquiry-Based Teaching Practice (IBTP) is an essential
component of science education, and promoting its
implementation is at the heart of various reform efforts.
Even though science teachers regard IBTP as an essential pedagogical method, they rarely use it for various
reasons. This study utilizes Bronfenbrenner's ecological
framework to examine potential factors at various
levels of the educational ecosystem that predict the
implementation of inquiry-based teaching practices
among Grade 9 science teachers in South Africa. To this
end, quantitative data from 537 educators who participated in the Trends in International Mathematics and
Science Study (TIMSS) 2019 national assessment were
utilized. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics,
Pearson correlation, and hierarchical multiple regression analysis. Results revealed that four variables at different socioeconomic levels were perceived to strongly
predict teachers' implementation of inquiry teaching

1 | INTRODUCTION
For many years, inquiry-based learning has been a strongly advocated teaching approach in science education curriculum worldwide, including South Africa (Department of Basic Education,
DBE, 2013; National Research Council, 2000; Trna et al., 2012). Inquiry-based teaching is a pedagogical strategy in which teachers provide students with opportunities to ask scientifically oriented questions; design investigations; collect, analyze, and interpret data; formulate models,
hypotheses, and explanations based on scientific evidence; generate predictions; communicate
and use arguments to defend explanations (Crawford, 2014; Jimenez-Liso et al., 2021; Lederman
et al., 2014; Pedaste et al., 2015). Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of inquiry
as an appropriate educational strategy for fostering the development of necessary knowledge
and skills and solid student motivation in science. For instance, research has shown that using
an inquiry-based approach enables students to construct knowledge of science concepts and the
nature of science, increases students' interest, engagement, and academic achievement in science, and transforms learning processes and outcomes to meet future educational skills and
competencies. (Crawford, 2014; National Research Council, 2000; Trna et al., 2012). Inquiry
teaching practices have also been found to benefit students' conceptual learning, understanding
of science culture and scientific practice, and retention and application of higher-order thinking
skills (Kuzhabekova, 2015). Furthermore, the use of inquiry-based teaching has been shown to
positively impact students' learning of science, with an effect size of 0.5. (Furtak et al., 2012). In
other words, students who participate in science-related activities through inquiry tend to
understand science content better than those who receive traditional teacher lectures. Research
also correlates teachers' frequent utilization of inquiry-based approaches with students' performance in science examinations (Jerrim et al., 2022).
Despite the prominent role that inquiry plays in the teaching and learning of science
(Furtak et al., 2012; Kuzhabekova, 2015; Trna et al., 2012), studies have consistently shown that
inquiry-based approaches are rarely implemented in the typical science classroom, and teachers
are often reluctant to effectively adopt these approaches due to a range of factors, some of
which are beyond their control (Ramnarain & Hlatswayo, 2018; Crawford, 2014;
Kuzhabekova, 2015). Many of these factors were identified based on qualitative case studies
involving a small number of participants from different regions. As a result, the generalizability
of their findings to a larger population is limited. Nevertheless, a comprehensive review of these

the need for adopting an ecological approach. This implies that there is a need to gain a better
understanding of possible predictors of teachers' implementation of inquiry teaching practices
and how to support them in effectively implementing inquiry teaching practices in the classroom. In South Africa, like every other country, the Department of Basic Education (DBE) stipulates that students must acquire and apply scientific knowledge and inquiry skills in ways that
are meaningful to their own lives (DBE, 2011). This reflects the importance that the
South African government places on the utilization of inquiry-based teaching and learning in
the classroom. Despite the South African curriculum's emphasis on the use of inquiry-based
teaching, students from South Africa continue to underperform in science subjects as evidenced
by outcomes in national assessments such as the final National Senior Certificate Examinations
and international assessments like the Trend in International Mathematics and Science Study
(TIMMS). For instance, the 2021 National Senior Certificate diagnostic report highlights that
students in the country struggle with applying inquiry skills such as identifying variables, drawing conclusions, interpreting results, and creating models and hypotheses (DBE, 2021). Since
South Africa began participating in TIMSS in 1995, it has consistently been ranked at the bottom or second to last among all participating countries. The persistent decline in South African
students' performance in TIMSS assessments since 1995 has raised significant concerns. This
issue not only affects a substantial number of school children but also has implications for the
country's future in terms of the availability of competent students who can pursue scientific
careers (Oosthuizen, 2021). This therefore indicates that the objective of integrating inquirybased approaches into the South African curriculum is still far from being accomplished.
Though the TIMSS assessment consistently shows that teachers report a high implementation
of inquiry-based practices, this contradicts the low performance of the majority of students in
the TIMSS assessment and the actual classroom practice of most South African teachers (Ramnarain & Hlatswayo, 2018; Oosthuizen, 2021). One possible explanation for the continuous poor
performance of South African students in national and international assessments may be the
reluctance of teachers to effectively implement inquiry-based practices in the classroom (Ramnarain & Hlatswayo, 2018). Thus, to effectively support teachers in enhancing how students
approach and solve scientific inquiry problems, a better understanding of factors that influence
teachers' implementation of inquiry-based teaching practices is needed.
Teachers are believed to be at the center of successful curriculum implementation. However, they face a complex and constantly changing educational system influenced by various
factors, which shape their development and guide them in a specific direction (Knowles
et al., 1999). As a result, this affects how teachers teach, not only in different countries but also
within a country as well. Examining these factors through Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Framework provides a comprehensive understanding of the complex interactions between teachers
and their environments. This framework will offer valuable insights for policymakers, helping
them negotiate potential factors and understand the broader context in which teachers work,
including the direct and indirect influences this context has on their instructional practices,
especially the implementation of inquiry-based teaching practices.

tend to influence teachers’ personal growth and development in designing and implementing

may influence their teaching practice (Bronfenbrenner, 1979). These contexts are organized
structures comprising several nested systems ranging from direct, face-to-face settings to more
distant and wider social settings like classrooms and cultural context (Bronfenbrenner, 1993).
Since the ecological system theory examines human behavior within contextual and
interdependent structures, the effect of context on teachers’ inquiry teaching practices is
emphasized. This implies that the surrounding environment of a teacher can negatively or positively affect his or her adoption of inquiry practices in science classrooms. The theory is also
useful for understanding how educational or social structures affect individual teachers’ adoption of inquiry teaching approaches.
The ecological systems theory also clarifies how individuals' interactions with social structures affect their experiences, opportunities, and personal identities (Bronfenbrenner, 1994).
Within the context of this study, the model considers and integrates factors unique to the
teacher and the teacher's work environment/community. Studies have identified numerous
interconnected social systems that influence individual teachers’ implementation of inquiry
teaching approaches, such as teacher's characteristics (Kuzhabekova, 2015; Teig et al., 2019),
teacher's work environment (Akuma & Callaghan, 2019; Salinas et al., 2022), and education
policies and legislations (Allen et al., 2016). The ecological model prioritizes the individual
teachers and recognizes the interconnected systems that affect them. The model could be useful
in understanding the adoption of inquiry teaching approaches in science classrooms, particularly in classroom settings where teachers need to make individual decisions about adopting
inquiry practices. In light of the above discussion, the ecological systems theory (see Figure 1)
provides a framework for understanding various environmental factors that might influence
teachers’ adoption of inquiry-based teaching practices. Specifically, the social-ecological theory
categorizes the teachers’ environment into the following layers: the individual (intrapersonal
characteristics), microsystem (immediate environment of an individual), mesosystem (interactions between multiple microsystems in an individual's life), exosystem (external environment
that indirectly affects an individual), and macrosystem (broader context that shapes and influences an individual) (Bronfenbrenner, 1979).

2.3 | Microsystem
The microsystem is described as the “pattern of activities, interpersonal relations, and roles
experienced by the developing person in a given face-to-face setting with particular physical
and material characteristics” that allow or hinder direct interaction with activities in the person's immediate environment (Bronfenbrenner, 1979, p. 22). According to Bronfenbrenner,
interactions in the immediate environment are not limited to persons but also include objects,
symbols, physical structures, and psychological processes a person engages in to construct an
understanding of experience in their immediate settings. The microsystem in this study refers
to the people, objects, and other elements within the school that teachers interact with directly
and, in turn, directly impact their instructional practice. It includes students, colleagues, and
subject matter experts who help shape the teacher's instructional practice (inquiry teaching
approaches), the teacher-student relationship, teachers' ability to manage the classroom and
classroom-related issues such as instructional time and teachers' workload are also explored at
this level. These microsystem elements directly impact a teacher's frequent use of inquiry teaching practices and are an important component of the overall instructional environment in
which the teacher operates.
The classroom contains students with varying physiological and psychological characteristics that include how a classroom is organized, the type of activities and interactions encouraged or discouraged in the classroom, and the calmness or chaotic nature of the classroom,
which are likely to influence how teachers implement inquiry teaching practices. Several

(inquiry approaches). Teachers are often confronted with challenges in their instructional practices, especially when addressing and adapting to circumstances such as students' financial
troubles, special needs, disruptive conduct, and lack of enthusiasm (Gholam, 2019;
Pongsophon & Herman, 2017). Classroom achievement is another important component within
this system as it directly impacts teachers' implementation of inquiry-based teaching practices.
Research has shown that frequent use of inquiry practices enhances student achievement
(Jerrim et al., 2022). Conversely, teachers might be more inclined to use inquiry-based teaching
in higher-achieving classrooms than in lower ones. Another significant factor that has a direct
impact on teachers' performance and instructional practices is the workload associated with
teachers' instruction, which includes tasks like curriculum organization, lesson note preparation, encouraging student engagement in curriculum instructions, conducting continuous
assessments, managing the classroom, and any other routine duties assigned by the principal
(Baroudi & Rodjan Helder, 2019; Correia & Harrison, 2020). In some situations, teachers may
only see workload as a barrier to their instructional practice once the demands are too timeconsuming, causing them to perceive the task as overly demanding. Moreover, teachers' workloads tend to increase as class sizes grow, which can have a negative effect on their motivation,
commitment, performance, and effort levels when employing inquiry teaching practices in the
classroom (Salinas et al., 2022). The impact of class size on a teacher's decision to utilize
inquiry-based methods is also of great importance. It is believed that teachers with larger class
sizes may face difficulties in implementing science investigations due to the challenges associated with managing a greater number of students, ensuring safety, and providing individualized
attention during hands-on activities. The size of the class can directly influence the teacher's
ability to effectively carry out inquiry-based teaching strategies.

teachers face several challenges in the classroom, which lowers their commitment and job satisfaction and impedes the adoption of inquiry-based teaching practices (Pongsophon & Herman, 2017). Issues concerning teachers' job satisfaction are arising in science education studies
because job satisfaction has been identified as an important dimension of teacher quality in
large-scale studies (Martin et al., 2017; von Davier, 2020). According to Song and Mustafa
(2015), the level of job satisfaction among teachers is typically influenced by various factors,
including teacher collaboration. This teacher collaboration is often linked with professional
growth at the school level and a sense of belonging to the work community and has been found
to have a substantial and direct association with the performance and instructional practice of
science teachers (Song & Mustafa, 2015).
Furthermore, Pongsophon and Herman (2017) explained how collaboration among teachers
has also been shown to increase teacher engagement, job satisfaction, frequent implementation
of inquiry approaches, and self-confidence in teaching inquiry. Teachers' collaborative activities
involve many interactions inherent to implementing inquiry-based teaching practices. Teachers'
collaborative activities are shared through accountability and collective responsibility to provide
a vehicle for continuous improvement of teacher practices. They could include participation in
extra-curricular activities, curriculum and assessment development, interaction with students'
yearly trajectories, school events involving other colleagues, and cooperation with school
administrators and/or subject advisors (Correia & Harrison, 2020; Metz et al., 2007).
Studies have also indicated that school assessments appear to limit teachers' ability to implement inquiry-based practices because they are still aligned with national exam standards that
assess students' knowledge and understanding of a concept in the traditional way (Baroudi &
Rodjan Helder, 2019; Correia & Harrison, 2020). Moreover, teachers find it challenging to
implement inquiry in their classrooms due to school emphasis placed on students' success,
types of assessments, and lesson planning (Correia & Harrison, 2020; Kang & Keinonen, 2016;
Nicol, 2021).

2.5 | Exosystem
The exosystem encompasses external settings or contexts in which the teacher is not a direct
participant but factors at this level still indirectly influence teachers' implementation of inquiry
teaching practices (Bronfenbrenner, 1994). This includes organizational structures or events
that the teacher is not directly involved in but still influences his/her instructional practice.
Examples could include school leadership structures, school location, school culture, values
and ideology, subject matter assessments, professional development, authority and collaborative
patterns (leaders, staff, students, parents, community), funding sources, resource allocation,
school rituals, school policies and legislations, teacher evaluation criteria and work plans, curriculum design, and support of the district authorities and the student cohort (Anderson
et al., 2014; Correia & Harrison, 2020; Peng, 2012).
Studies show that organizational factors at the exosystem level, such as funding situations
(e.g., salary cuts), can change and impede teachers' abilities to provide thoughtful instruction or
engage their classes in science activities. Though budgets are set aside for educational reforms,
schools in underprivileged settings need more financial support and, as a result, struggle with a
lack of teaching materials or poor resources (Akuma & Callaghan, 2019; Baroudi & Rodjan Helder, 2019). Issues related to school autonomy, lack of professional or moral support from superiors (jurisdiction), external professional development, standardized testing, shortage of
classroom assistants, and alignment of the curriculum at the school level have also been identified as significant factors within teachers' social settings that indirectly influence their implementation of inquiry teaching practices (Baroudi & Rodjan Helder, 2019; Kang &
Keinonen, 2016; Tang et al., 2020).
Another dimension of the exosystem involves school climate also known as school culture,
which shapes how teachers respond to changes in educational policy, feel about themselves and
interest in their work, all of which influence the quality and effectiveness of teachers' instructional practice (Hatziconstantis & Kolympari, 2021). The National School Climate Centre
(2021) described school climate as a social construct that reflects norms, goals, values, interpersonal relationships, teaching and learning practices, and organizational structures of the school
based on patterns of students, parents, and school personnel's experience of school life. In light
of this, several factors hindering the implementation of inquiry teaching practice have been recognized at the exosystem level. For instance, Fitzgerald et al. (2019) reported that Australian
teachers perceived their ability to use inquiry-based instruction in high school science classes to
be hampered by increasingly stringent occupational health and safety regulations within the
school context. Similarly, Mohammed and Amponsah (2021) reported that educational administrators in many contexts do not promote and support inquiry-based science teaching in schools
due to their uninformed or partially informed conceptions of scientific inquiry. Consequently
hindering the necessary support and training that teachers require to implement inquiry-based
instruction.

TIMSS data, this study examines if a school's socioeconomic status at the macrosystem level
impacts the implementation of inquiry teaching practices.

2.7 | The present study
The TIMSS is a comprehensive and international large-scale assessment that has become
increasingly relevant for understanding educational structure, curricula, and pedagogy across
countries and is conducted with Grade 4 and Grade 8 students every 4 years (Hopfenbeck
et al., 2018; Martin et al., 2017). South Africa is one of the countries that had the special dispensation of having Grade 9 students (rather than Grade 8 students) participating in the TIMSS
assessments. In trying to find ways to raise the very low outcomes in science, it is important to
gain insights into teachers' implementation of inquiry teaching practices in South Africa. The
TIMSS dataset provides valuable insights into various contextual factors associated with
the teaching and learning of science and mathematics at both teacher and student levels. In
addition, the TIMSS data provide unique opportunities to generalize findings to a wide population and to analyze the determinants of teachers' practices in science subjects (Pongsophon &
Herman, 2017; Teig et al., 2019). Though the study did not assess the actual implementation, it
examines perceived factors predicting teachers' implementation of inquiry teaching practices in

3 | METHODOLOG Y
This study employed a quantitative approach to carry out a secondary analysis of the TIMSS
2019 data for South Africa using both descriptive and inferential statistics. The use of quantitative analysis is valuable in managing and comprehending a large dataset with numerous data
points simultaneously (Creswell & Creswell, 2017). This study acknowledges the nested structure of the TIMSS data and includes measures related to environmental factors that can impact
teachers' adoption of inquiry practices based on Bronfenbrenner's ecological system theory, as
shown in Figure 1. A multivariate (correlation) analysis was conducted to identify possible relationships between teachers' use of inquiry-based teaching approaches and factors affecting their
adoption of inquiry teaching practices at the intrapersonal, microsystem, mesosystem,
exosystem, and macrosystem levels. Examining these correlations facilitated the understanding
of the relationships between the variables recorded in the database and determining their significance through statistical analysis (Patten & Newhart, 2018). In order to determine the possible
variables that might influence teachers' implementation of inquiry-based teaching practices, a
hierarchical multiple regression model was used (Teig et al., 2019).

3.1 | Sample
The 2019 TIMSS study utilized a two-stage stratified cluster design in choosing participants
within a country (LaRoche et al., 2020). This sampling design is used to select sampled schools,
and then intact classrooms of students with their respective teachers are selected randomly
within the participating schools. To ensure accuracy, sampling weights were used to select science teacher data and plausible science values, as established by recommended procedures
(LaRoche et al., 2020; Martin et al., 2017). Hence, the sample data included in this study consisted of 537 Grade 9 educators who were part of a nationally representative sample of
South African students that participated in TIMSS 2019. These educators are nested in
520 schools. To eliminate sample biases in our investigation, the teacher sampling weight was
employed to construct most statistics (Martin et al., 2017). In contrast to other TIMSS countries,
the sample for the study was drawn from Grade 9 instead of Grade 8. This decision was
influenced by the poor performance of South Africa, Botswana, and Honduras in the TIMSS
2003 assessment for Grade 8 students, as well as the challenges associated with imputations
and estimations. As a result, the International Study Centre recommended that these countries
should conduct the TIMSS survey at the Grade 9 level (Reddy & Hannan, 2021).

3.2 | Source of data
Data for this study were gathered through school and teacher contextual questionnaires distributed to principals and eighth-grade science educators from a nationally representative sample
of South African students (excluding benchmarking participants) that took part in the 2019
TIMSS assessment. The data used in this study included students’ achievement, teachers' demographic information, and teachers' and principals' perceptions about the teaching and learning
environment. While it can be challenging to acquire insights into the complex nature of
teachers' instructional practice using self-report surveys, the TIMSS questionnaire thoroughly
explored numerous factors such as beliefs, perceptions, attitudes, and practices to provide a
more in-depth understanding of teachers' instructional practice. As a result, the constructs used
in this study were selected mainly by leveraging corresponding constructs already present in the
available school and teacher contextual questionnaire data, as suggested by theoretical representations of the Ecological system model and reviewed literature. However, single-item measures
were used in situations when no multi-items were available to validate specific elements of the
essential constructs. Hence, validity of the single-item scale was established using content validity, while that of multi-items was established using convergent and discriminant validity. As a
result, the current study examined the correlations between numerous parameters at various
ecological levels. It specifically looked at the impact of macrosystem factors (e.g., the school's
socioeconomic status), exosystem factors (e.g., school safety, school climate and culture, assessment strategy and teacher professional development), mesosystem factors (e.g., job satisfaction,
resource allocation, class size, and school-based collaboration to plan instruction), microsystem
factors (e.g., teacher's workload, students' needs and characteristics, or language barrier) and
teachers' intrapersonal characteristics on the implementation of inquiry teaching practice.

3.3 | Measures of variables selected for this study
The TIMSS 2019 measures used were constructed using the IRT (Item Response Theory) scaling
methods (von Davier, 2020). “IRT models show the relationship between the ability or trait
(symbolized θ) measured by the instrument and an item response” (DeMars, 2010, p. 3). TIMSS
2019 assessment used the three-parameter logistic (3PL) IRT model for multiple-choice items, a
2PL IRT model for constructed-response items worth one score point, and the generalized partial
credit model for constructed-response items worth up to 2 score points (von Davier, 2020). The
generalized partial credit model was used to evaluate binary and polytomous ordinal items
(worth up to 2 points). To assess the data sets used in this study, we compared the definition of
constructs described in the literature review with the information in the TIMSS questionnaire
item. We selected TIMSS constructs and items corresponding to the required measure in the
Bronfenbrenner ecology theory and performed the appropriate changes for this study. New constructs were developed, and existing TIMSS constructs selected were checked using factor analysis to discover their underlying factor structure. They were then renamed to correspond with our
theoretical framework, codes were assigned, and appropriate measurement scales were chosen.

3.9 | Methods of analyses
The TIMSS 2019 teacher and school data file was first merged using the IDB analyzer (https://
www.iea.nl/data-tools/tools) to ensure correct links between teachers, schools, and their students. The merged data were then translated into the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences
(SPSS) for further analysis after being coded and verified for accuracy. Since this study aims to
investigate factors within the different levels of ecological systems that might influence teachers'
implementation of inquiry teaching practices, a hierarchical multiple regression analysis was
used to examine relations between variables measured at different levels of the multilevel data
structure. This type of analysis considers the nesting of individual observations within several
groups and recognizes the correlation between observations within each group. Some multidimensional constructs were incorporated into the model by using the average scores of the
individual items that make up the multidimensional construct to create a composite score in
the data analysis since each of the factors under these multidimensional constructs contributes
equally to the overall construct.
Due to the interaction between students' performance and other factors on teachers' instructional (inquiry) practices as suggested in the literature, this study controlled for classroom
achievement in the main analysis to minimize the likelihood of potential confounding effects.
To take account of measurement errors in classroom achievement, a range of five plausible
values of scores in science tests was provided for each student (for a detailed description, see
Martin and Mullis 2012). In this study, the proficiency score for overall science achievement
drawn from the five plausible values obtained through the IRT methodology was used in the
analysis. As a result, five regression models were computed and fitted with the sampling weight
of science teachers:
• Model 1: Predicting teacher's implementation of inquiry teaching practices from teachers'
intrapersonal characteristics and students' achievement (control variable).
• Model 2: Predicting teacher's implementation of inquiry teaching practices from
microsystem-level factors.
• Model 3: Predicting teacher's implementation of inquiry teaching practices from micro- and
mesosystem-level factors.
• Model 4: Predicting teacher's implementation of inquiry teaching practices from micro-,
meso-, and exosystem-level factors.
• Model 5: Predicting teacher's implementation of inquiry teaching practices from micro-,
meso-, exo- and macrosystem-level factors.
To investigate the presence of missing data across all variables, the Little's MCAR (missing
completely at random) test was used. The test resulted in a chi-square value of 25146.518 with
559 degrees of freedom and a significance level of.000, which is less than the p-value of 0.05.
This implies that the pattern of missing data is not completely random (MNAR), which compromises the generalizability of the findings. However, because the frequency of missing data was
determined to be minimal across all variables, the listwise deletion method was employed to

5 | DISCUSSION
This study extends previous works on factors that predict teachers’ implementation of inquirybased teaching practices in South African science classrooms. Most previous research has
focused on small number of participants within selected schools or regions. However, this is
one of the few studies that explores factors predicting teachers’ implementation of inquiry
teaching practices in South Africa from an ecological perspective using large assessment scales
like TIMSS. The multiple regression analysis of TIMSS 2019 data from Grade 9 science teachers
and their principals revealed that various intrapersonal, mesosystem, and exosystem factors are
perceived to affect the implementation of inquiry teaching practices in South African science
classrooms.

5.1 | Factors at the interpersonal level (control variables)
Although the findings of this study suggest that intrapersonal factors (teachers' background
information) examined do not possess a substantial predictive impact on their implementation
of inquiry teaching practices, the collective influence of factors at this level contributed approximately 1.2% to teachers' implementation of inquiry-based teaching practices. Nevertheless, it
was discovered that teachers' major areas of study had the most contribution from the interpersonal level to the overall analyses. Teachers' major area of study was found to have a moderate
negative effect on the level of education completed by participating teachers (r = 0.303) and a
small negative effect on teacher's perceptions of the significance of assessment strategy
(r = 0.120) suggesting that teachers with a specialized area of study (majors in science and science education) tend to have a moderately lower level of education completed and slightly

teachers' formal education experiences play at the interpersonal level in implementing inquiry
teaching approaches. It is assumed that teachers who have been exposed to inquiry-based learning more thoroughly during their formal education are more inclined to frequently use this
approach in their lessons (Letina, 2019). Hence, exposing future teachers to inquiry-based activities during their initial teacher education program can help them implement this strategy systematically and successfully in their classrooms.
Among the control variables, results showed a lack of significant association between
learners' academic achievement in the TIMSS 2019 and teachers' implementation of inquirybased teaching practices. However, it is worth noting that teaching students with inquiry-based
practices can help them create deep and transferable learning, strengthening their engagement
with science activities and enhancing their learning of scientific concepts, which in turn can
positively impact their overall academic achievement (Gholam, 2019; Jerrim et al., 2022;
Kuzhabekova, 2015).

5.2 | Factors at the microsystem level
The results obtained from the microsystem level indicate that none of the microsystems directly
involved in teachers' interactions have an impact on their adoption of inquiry teaching
practices. The findings reported by the teachers surveyed did not align with previous research
studies that identified factors within the microsystem level as obstacles to implementing
inquiry-based practices. Studies have revealed that teachers consider language difficulties as factors hindering the implementation of inquiry-based teaching practices (Baroudi & Rodjan Helder, 2019; Gholam, 2019; Peng, 2012). Additionally, teachers often express concerns about
having limited time to incorporate inquiry methods into their classrooms due to various administrative responsibilities, and the need to prepare and assess instructional plans (Baroudi &
Rodjan Helder, 2019; Correia & Harrison, 2020), thus increasing their overall workload. However, the representation of Grade 9 South African teachers who participated in the TIMSS 2019
national survey did not perceive workload as a hindrance to their implementation of inquirybased teaching practices. In addition to factors at the microsystem level, it is interesting to know
that the results of this study show that class size is not a significant predictor of teachers' implementation of inquiry teaching practices, corroborating the findings of Tang et al. (2020).

5.3 | Factors at the mesosystem level
Findings at the mesosystem level identified teachers' job satisfaction as a strong predictor of
teachers' implementation of inquiry practices in South Africa. This is consistent with previous
research that showed a relationship between teachers' job satisfaction and classroom inquiry
implementation (Pongsophon & Herman, 2017). Studies have claimed that teachers satisfied
with their jobs tend to provide students with higher-quality instruction and more learning support, whereas teachers unhappy in their jobs tend to feel less connected to the profession
(Kiran & Sungur, 2018; Song & Mustafa, 2015). Nevertheless, the level of teachers' job satisfaction tends to decrease as they face several issues related to microsystem factors and other
exosystem factors such as school culture and climate, pressure, low salary, autonomy, and
career growth, all of which affect their instructional quality in terms of implementing inquiry

adopting inquiry teaching practices (Kiran & Sungur, 2018). Thus, improving teachers' job satisfaction through creating a collegial and positive work environment, school safety, assessment
practices, and participating in authentic professional development activities may improve
teachers' ability to implement inquiry teaching practices.
In addition to teachers' job satisfaction, lack of instructional resources was also identified as
essential in predicting teachers' adoption of inquiry-based practices. This is consistent with previous research which found that the availability of resources for science instruction has a positive impact on the adoption and implementation of inquiry-based approaches in schools
(Ramnarain & Hlatswayo, 2018; Baroudi & Rodjan Helder, 2019; Kang & Keinonen, 2016). This
implies that teachers are more likely to implement inquiry-based teaching practices when their
schools have the necessary equipment and materials. However, some teachers may face challenges in conducting inquiry-based activities in their classrooms due to limited resources, laboratory equipment, chemicals, and limited budget to purchase equipment. This lack of resources
can prevent teachers from adopting and incorporating inquiry-based teaching practices in their
classrooms.
Furthermore, the results of the study revealed that teachers' perceptions of the significance
of various assessment strategies appear to be the most predictive factor influencing teachers'
implementation of inquiry teaching practices in South Africa at the mesosystem level. The frequency and types of assessments used by teachers are believed to be influenced by factors such
as class size, the individual teacher, and the specific objective behind assessing students' learning progress. In science classrooms, teachers often create assessment strategies that primarily
consist of tests, quizzes, and multiple-choice or short-answer questions that ask students to
identify or recall facts, concepts, or terminology instead of in-depth reasoning and application
of underlying concepts or to solve problems and explain phenomena (Harlen, 2013; NRC, 2000;
Quigley et al., 2011). However, it is important to note that relying solely on these conventional
and high-stakes assessment strategies can limit teachers' ability to measure the goals of inquirybased science education (Correia & Harrison, 2020). In light of this, research argues that using
performance assessments can allow teachers to implement and assess inquiry teaching practices
in their classrooms (Quigley et al., 2011).

5.4 | Factors at the exosystem level
Findings of this study reveal that teachers’ participation in past professional development on
inquiry skills was found as a significant exosystem-level factor that could influence the implementation of inquiry teaching practices in South Africa. This finding aligns with existing
research (Baroudi & Rodjan Helder, 2019; Kang & Keinonen, 2016). For instance, in a study
that described barriers influencing the implementation of inquiry-based pedagogies in Australian schools, Fitzgerald et al. (2019) found that a typical professional development program for
science teachers is mainly lecture-driven, with little authentic experience in how to incorporate
ideas from the professional development in their classrooms. This finding is broadly consistent
with other studies that have found professional development programs for science teachers to
be inadequate, detached, or inapplicable to real problems in the classroom (Baroudi & Rodjan
Helder, 2019; Lieberman & Pointer Mace, 2008). Moreover, research claims that what teachers
study in teacher education programs sometimes does not apply to what happens in a real

According to research, establishing professional development programs that allow teachers to

teachers' ability to implement inquiry teaching practices is interconnected with the kind of professional development activities they experience.

5.5 | Factors at the macrosystem level
Another interesting finding is that schools' socioeconomic composition at the macrosystem
level does not influence teachers' implementation of inquiry-teaching practices, supporting the
claims that economic and educational development at the macrosystem level does not prevent
science teachers from implementing inquiry-teaching (Tang et al., 2020). Nevertheless, it is possible that the relevance given to teachers' implementation of inquiry teaching practices at the
macrosystem level may be influenced by the quality of support they receive at the microsystem,
mesosystem, and exosystem levels.
Based on the findings of this study, teachers who participated in the nationally representative sample of the TIMSS 2019 survey reported how several environmental factors are perceived
to substantially affect the effective implementation of inquiry-based teaching practices in the
South African educational system.

5.6 | Limitation
Using a comprehensive database such as TIMSS has both advantages and disadvantages. While
the TIMSS study contains a large quantity of data for analysis, it is crucial to note that this study
is based on secondary data analysis, which has particular concerns and limitations. One limitation is that the TIMSS data is not from a randomized experiment, so it cannot be used to
describe causal relationships. In addition, some of the constructs analyzed are single items with
possible psychometric drawbacks. However, Fuchs and Diamantopoulos (2009) contend that
single-item measures are appropriate with unidimensional, clearly defined, and limited in-scope
constructs. Another limitation of the study is its dependence on teachers' self-reports on the frequency with which they implement different inquiry activities and various contextual factors
predicting their implementation, which are potentially susceptible to social desirability bias and
lack corroborating evidence (Fisher, 1993). However, research contends that self-report is essential to assess the psychological factors that drive human learning, such as motivation and strategy utilization (Pekrun, 2020).
Furthermore, rather than measuring actual frequency, the TIMSS scale assesses teachers'
views about the frequency of inquiry activities. As a result, future research should focus on
measuring the quality of inquiry activities conducted in classrooms rather than just the quantity. To accomplish this, adding students' self-reports on the frequent implementation of science
experiments might be beneficial for triangulation. Furthermore, since this study only examined
the perceptions of teachers who responded to the South African science teacher TIMSS

teachers/schools, educational system, or country.

6 | C ON C L U S I ON
Teachers make essential decisions about implementing instructional practices based on several
factors affecting them. The ecological system theory provides a framework for demonstrating
the importance of considering contextual factors that might originate from different levels of
the educational ecosystem and can possibly influence the implementation of inquiry-based
teaching approaches in South Africa and across different countries. The factors identified in this
study were based on the perceptions of Grade 9 South African science educators teaching students from a nationally representative sample that took part in TIMSS 2019. The results of this
study showed that teachers perceive that the adoption of inquiry teaching practices in
South African science classrooms might be influenced by various factors at different levels.
According to the findings, the implementation of inquiry teaching practices results from a
confluence of distal and proximal factors in the teachers' classrooms rather than a single factor.
Among several ecological factors explored, the study findings suggest that teachers' job satisfaction, resource shortage, and assessment strategy at the mesosystem level, as well as participation in inquiry-based professional development at the exosystem level, are strong predictors of
inquiry teaching in South Africa. Therefore, paying attention to how government policies and
support programs are offered at the mesosystem and exosystem level is critical for increasing
teachers' confidence, self-efficacy, and ability to develop assessment strategies in implementing
inquiry-based instruction, which could translate into their overall job satisfaction. Moreover,
establishing well-designed professional development programs emphasizing inquiry-based
learning can enhance teachers' capacity to implement inquiry teaching practices in the classroom or other school settings.
