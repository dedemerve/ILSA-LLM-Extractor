By using the PISA 2018 data across the United States, Singapore, and Finland, this study examined whether students’ frequency of reading on digital devices and their frequency of and attitude
toward using digital devices were related to digital reading achievement. The results showed that
student attitude toward the use of digital devices was positively associated with digital reading
achievement. This relationship was stronger for male students in the United States and Finland.
Students’ frequency of using digital devices was negatively associated with digital reading
achievement, while students’ frequency of reading on digital devices was positively associated
with digital reading achievement. The association between frequency of reading on digital devices and digital reading achievement was stronger for male students in Singapore.

1. Introduction
Information and Communication Technology (ICT) has been gaining its importance in classrooms in the past decades as technology
evolves rapidly. Researchers have shed light on how the use of ICT in education could potentially benefit both teaching and learning (e.
g., Biagi & Loi, 2013; Hu, Gong, Lai, & Leung, 2018). When examining the use of ICT, one fundamental and crucial aspect that cannot
be neglected is digital reading. Considering how much content is presented on digital screens as opposed to paper materials in the
context of ICT use, it makes considerable sense to try to better comprehend if and how students’ learning will be impacted by the
introduction of digital reading. This is particularly more important now than ever as more and more schools are forced to go online
with the COVID-19 pandemic, and digital reading and learning become inevitable on a large scale. In addition, a deeper understanding
of the focal issue will yield valuable practical affordance to students, practitioners, and policymakers, which will not only make
teaching and learning in a digitalized context more effective, but at the same time help narrow digital divide (defined as a difference in
the ability to access and use technology among different groups of people. See Norris, 2001 for example) and bring digital equity to all
students (Morse, 2004).

Thus far, research on the topic of digital reading converges on the comparison of reading performance between different reading
mediums (digital vs. paper), with the majority consent that digital reading is not comparable to traditional reading for both young and
adult readers (e.g., Mangen, Walgermo, & Brønnick, 2013). As an example, Støle and colleagues (2020) explored if there were any
differences in reading performance between digital reading and traditional reading among children. Adopting a within-subject design,
the researchers recruited 1,139 students (10 years old and younger) to participate in two reading comprehension tests, one on
computers and the other one on paper. The test results showed that students performed significantly better on paper. The researchers
further suggested that the differences in reading performance could be attributed to reading habits as well as gender. Similarly, Chen
and collaborators (2014) focused on older readers and examined college students’ reading performance on different reading mediums.
The researchers compared 90 college students’ reading comprehension on screen and on paper. The focal students were asked to read
four texts on different reading mediums and answer some reading comprehension questions. A one-way ANOVA on the test results
showed that students performed significantly better on paper than on computers.
To shed light on the possible causes for differences between digital reading and traditional reading, some researchers asserted that,
from either a conceptual or an empirical perspective, it would be valuable to look at students’ attitude and habits of digital reading in
order to understand digital reading in-depth (e.g., Chou, 2012; Myrberg & Wiberg, 2015). Based on the premise, a few studies revealed
that students’ attitude toward digital reading and digital devices, as well as their digital device use, may be correlated with students’
academic achievements. For example, Lebens and colleagues (2009) examined the attitude of students from different socio-economic
backgrounds toward technology. It was found that even though students from lower socio-economic backgrounds agreed on the
importance of technology, they “[felt] cautious about actually using the computer” (p. 263). This anxiety could negatively affect
students’ academic achievement, considering that “attitudes toward a certain subject [were] linked to achievement and therefore
[influenced] later educational and occupational prospects” (p. 265). In a different study, Steffens (2014) examined the relationship
between students’ digital reading scores and their use of ICT using the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) data
from three European education systems. It was found that German and Dutch students with a high frequency of ICT use tended to have
higher PISA test scores, whereas Finnish students with a low frequency of ICT use had higher PISA test scores. As the researcher pointed
out, the findings were not “clear-cut” (p. 553) and more studies with a closer look at the issue would be needed.
Despite the efforts made by researchers in the field, the findings on digital readings have been inconclusive (Clinton, 2019; Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman, & Salmerón, 2018; Kong, Seo, & Zhai, 2018) and the understanding of digital reading is limited. Meanwhile,
more empirical evidence is needed to comprehend the relationship between students’ academic achievement and their attitude toward
and their use of ICT (Myrberg & Wiberg, 2015). Moreover, different countries embrace different education systems and different
cultures (e.g., individualism-collectivism; Masculinity-femininity, see Rasinen, 2003, Sarbaini et al., 2019, and Uotinen, 1998 for
example). Studies have argued that these differences could subsequently affect students’ perception and use of technology. As an
example, Sarbaini and colleagues (2019) focused on one aspect of Hofstede’s Culture Model and discovered that culture (e.g.,
individualism-collectivism) could impact the adoption of e-learning technology. In addition, it is argued that cross-national studies
have their advantages in interpreting how policies and practices might affect student learning in different settings (Caro et al., 2016).
Inspired by previous studies, the present study looked at relevant PISA 2018 data across three countries, the United States, Singapore,
and Finland, and aimed to answer the following research questions:
(1) Are students’ frequency of and attitude toward use of digital devices, and their frequency of reading on digital devices related to
their digital reading achievement across three education systems (the United States, Singapore, and Finland)?
(2) Do the relationships between the frequency of and attitude toward use of digital devices, their frequency of reading on digital
devices, and digital reading achievement remain the same across male and female students in the United States, Singapore, and
Finland?
The decision to include gender as a moderator for the present study was inspired by existing literature (e.g., Harju-Luukkainen
et al., 2016). Studies have been investigating the role of gender vis-à-vis digital reading and digital device use from different perspectives such as usage (Drabowicz, 2014), self-efficacy (Tømte & Hatlevik, 2011), and motivation (McGeown, Goodwin, Henderson,
& Wright, 2012). Some studies claimed that gender played a role in reading comprehension (e.g., Abdorahimzadeh, 2014; Logan &
Johnston, 2010) while others claimed gender did not account for differences in reading performance (e.g., Brantmeier, 2003).
Meanwhile, researchers have not reached a consensus about the role of gender in people’s attitudes toward ICT (Faqih & Jaradat,
2015). By including gender as a moderator for the present study, this study hoped to further explore the role of gender in the relationships among interested variables.
Similar to the present study, two recent studies also examined how German and Dutch students’ digital reading achievements were
associated with their use and attitude of digital devices by looking at the PISA data (PISA 2015: Gubbels, Swart, & Groen, 2020; PISA
2012: Naumann & Sälzer, 2017). An earlier study (Steffens, 2014) also investigated the same issue across three European education
systems. While the present study resembles the three studies mentioned here and examines relations among similar interested variables, it adds to the field of digital reading in mainly three ways: 1) Previous studies largely focused on European education systems
while the present study focuses on Finland, the United States and Singapore, presenting three education systems concurrently. At the
same time, previous literacy studies have suggested possible differences among countries and regions in terms of reading habits,
reading strategies, etc (e.g., Wu & Peng, 2017). By juxtaposing three different education systems on three different continents (North
America, Europe, and Asia), the present study further examines if there are differences among the focal education systems in terms of
what might affect students’ digital reading achievement. In the present study, Finland, the United States, and Singapore are selected
because their students performed well in the PISA 2018 reading tests while these countries represent three distinctive education

2. Methods
2.1. Participants and Data Collection
The students from the United States (4,838 students from 215 schools), Singapore (6,676 students from 167 schools), and Finland
(5,649 students from 214 schools) participating in PISA 2018 were included in this study. PISA survey was conducted every three years
since 2000 with the purpose of “[assessing] students’ knowledge and skills in reading, mathematics and science” (OECD, 2019, p. 3)
and providing stakeholders with the collected information to help them make informed decisions. PISA adopted a stratified systematic
sampling method by allowing sampling probabilities to be proportional to the number of 15-year-old students in the school on the basis
of school grade enrollments. PISA also had a two-stage sampling process where (1) samples of schools were selected first and (2)
samples of students within the schools sampled (OECD, 2015). PISA 2018 was its seventh round and it focused on reading in a digital
context (OECD, 2019). Reading literacy in PISA 2018 was defined as “understanding, using, evaluating, reflecting on and engaging
with texts in order to achieve one’s goals, to develop one’s knowledge and potential, and to participate in society” (OECD, 2019, p. 3).
2.2. Measures
2.2.1. Dependent variable
Student digital reading achievement was used as a dependent variable. In order to maintain the accuracy of measurement (OECD,
2015), ten plausible values for reading achievement provided in PISA 2018 data were used for main analysis.
2.2.2. Focal independent variables
Student frequency of and attitude toward digital use and frequency of reading on digital devices were used as focal independent
variables. Frequency of digital use included using digital devices for entertainment (12 items), for schoolwork at home (12 items), and
for schoolwork at school (10 items). Attitude toward using digital devices encompassed interest (6 items), competency (5 items) and
autonomy (5 items) in the use of digital devices. We used the two standardized composite variables provided by the PISA 2018 data
sets. Frequency of reading on digital devices was measured on a five-point Likert scale by the following six items: The frequency of
getting involved in reading emails/online news, chatting online, online information searches to learn about a particular topic,
participating in online group discussions, and searching for practical information online. Internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s
alpha [α]) for frequency of and attitude toward digital use and frequency of reading on digital devices was .75, .75, and .74,
respectively. We used the composite variables for analysis.
2.2.3. Covariates
Student-level covariates included student gender, economic, social and cultural status (ESCS), age when a student first used digital
devices, home language use, immigrant status, and availability of digital devices at home. School-level covariates used included school
resource availability in digital devices and school policies/programs related to the use of digital devices. School resource availability in
digital devices was measured by eleven items using a 4-point Likert scale (α = .91) and school policies/programs related to the use of
digital devices was assessed by eight items on a 4-point Likert scale (α = .74). Composite variables for each of them were created. A
dummy variable denoting female students was generated. A dummy variable representing home language use was created, with the
code of 1 when their home language was corresponding to the test language and that of 0 otherwise. Two dummy variables representing second- and third-generation immigrant status were created, with natives used as a reference group. All other variables
including school-level variables used were continuous. Both student- and school-level covariates were used for the purpose of 1)
computing intraclass correlations and 2) controlling both student- and school-level characteristics that would seemingly be associated
with focal independent variables and outcome variable, thus setting the stage for better estimation of the primary associations of
interest in this study.
2.2.4. Moderator
Student gender was also used as a moderator. Three gender interaction terms were generated with three continuous focal independent variables before they were put into the models: (1) frequency of and (2) attitudes toward the use of digital devices and (3)
frequency of reading on digital devices.
2.3. Analytic plan
The distributions and outliers of the variables and multicollinearity among the variables were checked using descriptive statistics,

correlations, and variance inflation factors (VIFs). A series of multi-level linear models with and without gender interactions were
conducted separately for three education systems. Multi-level models were used since the students were nested within schools,
meaning that the students in the same schools tended to share common characteristics; henceforth, they should not be treated
independently and the cluster effects should be considered (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). Both student- and school-level weights and
clusters were implemented to account for design effects shown in the PISA data. The Stata survey (svy) commands (Stata 15: StataCorp, 2017) was used for statistical analysis and statistical significance was set at the .05 level.
3. Results
3.1. Student and School Characteristics
The number of students used for final analysis was 3,645, 6,090, and 4,352 in the United States, Singapore, and Finland respectively, due to the presence of missing data in the variables used. As shown in Table 1, 50.9% of them were male students, 81.3% were
native, 8.2% second-generation immigrant students, and 8.3% first-generation. The average digital reading achievement was 525.50
(SD = 108.22). The three focal independent variables and the continuous student- and school-level covariates such as age of first digital
device use, availability of digital devices at home were standardized. On average, a higher proportion of digital device availability at
home was shown. Likewise, both school digital resource availability and school policies/programs related to digital device use were
skewed to the left, indicating that schools on average have higher proportions of school digital resource availability and school
policies/programs related to digital device use.
Table 2 shows the weighted correlations among the variables used in this study. Results indicated that digital reading achievement
was positively correlated with female students, student attitude toward the use of digital devices, frequency of reading on digital
devices, and school policies related to digital devices (r’s ranging from .04 to .16, p < .01), but it was negatively correlated with
frequency of using digital devices (r = -.06, p < .01), age of first digital use (r = -.14, p < .01), and school digital resource availability
(r’s ranging from -.14 to -.06, p < .01). Results also indicated that student ESCS was positively correlated with three focal independent
variables (r’s ranging from .15 to .16, p < .01) and ICT availability at home was also positively correlated with three focal independent
variables (r’s in the range of .10 to .18, p < .01). Yet student home language use (using the test language at home) was negatively
correlated with frequency of reading on digital devices (r = -.03, p < .01) and frequency of using digital devices (r = -.02, p < .01),
while it was positively correlated with student attitudes towards the use of digital devices (r = .02, p < .01). The correlations among
three focal independent variables ranged from .29 to .40 (p < .01). The results of multicollinearity tests (‘collin’ command in Stata)
indicated that the variation inflation factors (VIFs) remained lower than 10 (ranging from 1.04 to 1.43, with a 1.15 as the mean VIF),
meaning that we did not have a multicollinearity issue in our data.

Three separate multi-level models with random effects were conducted to examine whether frequency of and attitude toward the
use of digital devices and frequency of reading on digital devices were related to student digital reading achievement across three
education systems. Results found that frequency of the use of digital devices was significantly associated with decreased digital reading
achievement (B = -25.23, SE = 5.65, p < .001; B = -21.83, SE = 2.33, p < .001; and B = -27.42, SE = 3.53, p < .001 for the United
States, Singapore, and Finland respectively), when controlling for student demographic characteristics and school digital resource
availability and policies/programs related to the use of digital devices (Table 3). However, results indicated that student attitude
toward the use of digital devices was significantly associated with increased digital reading achievement (B = 20.22, SE = 4.47, p <
.001; B = 9.83, SE = 2.49, p < .001; and B = 20.09, SE = 2.84, p < .001 for the United States, Singapore, and Finland respectively). In a
similar vein, it was found that students’ frequency of reading on digital devices was significantly and positively associated with digital
reading achievement (B = 8.72, SE = 4.29, p < .001; B = 12.80, SE = 2.92, p < .001; and B = 22.41, SE = 3.17, p < .001 for the United
States, Singapore, and Finland respectively).
In terms of three gender interactions examined for each education system, there were two significant negative gender interactions
with students’ attitude toward the use of digital devices: one in the United States (B = -10.19, SE = 5.19, p < .05) and the other one in
Finland (B = -11.09, SE = 4.14, p < .01). This means that the relationship between student attitude toward the use of digital devices
and digital reading achievement was weaker for female students than for male students in the United States and Finland (See Fig. 1). In
other words, the already positive relationship between student attitude toward the use of digital devices and digital reading
achievement was stronger for male students in the United States and Finland. In contrast, there were no significant gender interactions
in the same relationship in Singapore. However, there was a significant negative gender interaction with frequency of reading on
digital devices in Singapore (B = -12.74, SE = 4.66, p < .01), meaning that the positive significant association between student frequency of reading on digital devices and reading achievement became attenuated among female students (in contrast, the association
was stronger for male students) in Singapore (See Fig. 2). As for the relationships between frequency of using digital devices and digital
reading achievement, there were no gender interactions in all three education systems, signifying that the relationships remained the
same across male and female students in the United States, Singapore, and Finland.
4. Discussion and Conclusion
The present study showed that student attitude toward the use of digital devices was positively associated with students’ digital
reading achievement in all three education systems. This finding echoed the findings from previous studies (e.g., Gubbels et al., 2020).
As researchers pointed out, a positive attitude toward a subject meant students were more motivated to learn this subject, and was
often connected with high achievement (Lebens, Graff, & Mayer, 2009).
The present study also showed that the frequency of using digital devices was negatively associated with students’ digital reading
achievement. This finding, to some extent, echoed previous studies’ (e.g., Gubbels et al., 2020) findings that excessive digital device
use was negatively related to digital reading performance. Yet, it was still not clear as to why excessive digital device use might
negatively impact students’ digital reading achievement, especially considering that the attitude and the use of digital devices might be
positively associated with each other (Al-Zaidiyeen, Mei, & Fook, 2010). Some researchers (Gilleece & Eivers, 2018) found that
low-performance students tended to spend more time on digital devices looking for information in order to complete homework, which

Meanwhile, the present study found that students’ frequency of reading on digital devices was positively associated with students’
digital reading achievement. This was in contrast to the finding that the frequency of using digital devices in general was negatively
associated with students’ digital reading achievement. As Steffens (2014) pointed out, it was crucial to distinguish different types of
ICT use and adopt a differentiated approach to examine the relationship between ICT use and students’ academic achievement.
Steffens based his argument on the premise that “computer, for instance, [did] not have the same meaning as using a video games
console” and treating all ICT use as a whole would fail to showcase the nuances in interpreting the relationship between ICT use and
students’ academic achievement. In the present study, using digital devices specifically for the purpose of reading was distinctive from
using digital devices in general, which included uses of digital devices for non-reading purposes (i.e., playing video games, watching
online videos, messaging). The contrast in findings of the present study substantiated Steffen’s contemplation and suggested that using
digital devices specifically for the purpose of reading, as opposed to using digital devices in general or using digital devices for leisure
purposes (Gubbels et al., 2020), might actually benefit students’ digital reading achievement. It also yielded affordance for future PISA
questionnaire design in the way that more differentiated items regarding students’ frequency of reading on digital devices, and maybe
also students’ attitude toward reading on digital devices, could and should be included to help researchers and other stakeholders to
gain a more insightful look into this issue.
When examining the role of gender, it was indicated through the analysis that the relationship between student attitude toward the
use of digital devices and students’ digital reading achievement was stronger for male students than female students in the United
States and Finland. At the same time, the association between frequency of reading on digital devices and digital reading achievement
was stronger for male students than female students in Singapore. These findings could be explained by claims that gender would
significantly affect students’ digital reading achievement (Gubbels et al., 2020; Naumann & Sälzer, 2017) and female students were
believed to have higher intrinsic reading motivation (McGeown et al., 2012), which might in turn make their digital reading performance less affected by their attitude toward digital devices and frequency of reading on digital devices.
It was not clear why the gender effects only appeared in certain countries for certain relationships. It remained unknown why the
gender effects on the association between student attitude toward the use of digital devices and students’ digital reading achievement
were only shown in the United States and Finland and was stronger for male students, and the association between frequency of
reading on digital devices and digital reading achievement only appeared in Singapore and was stronger for male students.
According to the Global Gender Gap Report 2020 (World Economic Forum, 2020), Finland ranked high at third place while the
United States and Singapore ranked 53th and 54th respectively in the Global Gender Gap Index 2020 Rankings. This meant that gender

gap appeared to be a much less prominent issue in Finland than in the United States or Singapore. Similarly, based on Hofstede’s
Culture Model, Finland was considered a more feminine country than the United States and Singapore. This meant that Finland values
equality (which included gender equality) more than the other two countries here. In that sense, one would expect that Finland was the
outlier country when investigating the gender effects. However, the findings in this study about gender effects were in the mix.
Studies have argued that culture also played a role in gender-related issues (e.g., Bagozzi et al., 1999). Hofstede’s Culture Model
provided insights into more perspectives than just gender. For example, it also showed that Singapore scored lower than the United
States and Finland in the category of uncertainty avoidance, which indicated that Singaporeans were more tolerant of innovative ideas
such as technology or digital reading. Could the (lack of) gender effects in Singapore in this study be (partially) affected by the low
tendency of Singaporeans to avoid uncertainty? Moreover, Singapore was unique and different from the other two western countries in
many other perspectives and it embraced both the western concepts and Asian cultures (Ang & Stratton, 1995). Could other cultural
dimensions also play a role here?
Based on these premises and Steffen’s (2014) argument that cultural capital could be “a key factor in explaining the relationship
between ICT use and PISA achievement” (p. 561), it might be meaningful for future studies to examine the role of gender in the
relationship between students’ attitude toward the use of digital devices, frequency of reading on digital devices, and digital reading
achievement more closely for the focal education systems from a cultural perspective. For starters, researchers could investigate the
different dimensions in the Hofstede’s Culture Model (i.e., power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance, long
term orientation, indulgence) and their relationship with the gender effects.
The findings of the present study yielded some practical implications. In order to improve students’ digital reading achievement,
teachers and parents could make sure that students have a positive attitude toward ICT through approaches such as familiarizing
students with different types of technology with the purpose of eliminating technophobia (Panda & Mishra, 2007). At the same time,
teachers and parents could encourage students to spend time reading on digital devices while carefully monitoring students’ screen
time for other purposes.
In addition, teachers and parents should particularly be aware of the possible impacts on digital reading performance brought by
students’ gender and cultural differences and tailor strategies when necessary to meet students’ needs. While not directly supported by
findings of this study, teachers and parents could try to help students’ digital reading performance from a gender and/or cultural
perspective. For example, based on the assumption in Hofstede’s Cultural Model, Singapore scored higher in the category of power
distance and was much more collectivistic than the other two countries. This meant Singaporeans were more likely to respect authorities’ opinions and follow rules. Thus, teachers and parents in Singapore could make use of this premise and set good examples for
students to read on screen. It was possible that the influence from parents and teachers could override other factors affecting students’
acceptance and performance of digital reading. On the other hand, the same strategy might not work in the United States and Finland
because these two countries were considered as individualistic by Hofstede’s Cultural Model. In this sense, instead of simply asking
students to read on screen, parents and teachers could encourage students to explore digital reading and find the most appropriate
digital reading strategies themselves while being fully aware of the role that gender might play in the process. Again, these proposed
strategies and the roles of gender and culture need to be further empirically investigated by future studies.
Although it may not be feasible to provide all students with abundant digital resources currently, the present study suggests that it is
still possible to help with students’ digital reading achievement and to realize digital equity through smaller steps.
5. Limitations and Directions for Future Studies
As mentioned in the previous section, looking at the differences (e.g., gender/cultural differences) between different countries
more systematically might provide a more insightful interpretation to the relationships in inquiry. Future studies could consider
embedding and investigating relevant items in terms of the distinctions among education systems in order to provide a deeper understanding of digital reading. At the same time, more education systems could be included with additional country-level variables (i.
e., educational stratification) under the multi-level framework to magnify and highlight these distinctions. By including more countryspecific variables, it is also made possible for future studies to further examine the explanatory mechanisms in the relationships among
the focal variables in the present study.
Additionally, although we wanted to include two other school-level variables in the models (e.g., school sector [public vs. private]
and school location), not all three education systems had observations (school sector) and Singapore had only one level in school
location (large city). Thus, the two seemingly important school-level variables could not be included in the models and analyses of the
present study. Future studies that looked at different countries or different versions of the PISA surveys could consider adding these
variables if they were available.
Besides, as an exploratory study which aims to show an overarching picture, the present study does not differentiate between
reading for academic purposes and reading for entertainment purposes. Considering the distinctive nature of these two types of
reading, future studies could home in on the reading purposes and investigate whether and how reading purposes play a role in
students’ digital reading experience and performance. As an example, future studies could examine if students’ frequency of reading
for academic purposes and reading for entertainment purposes have different effects on students’ attitude toward digital reading, and if
so, how this difference further contributes to students’ academic achievement.
Lastly, although not a focus of this study, it was discovered that there was a negative association between school digital resource
availability and student digital reading achievement in the United States and Singapore. Future studies could investigate more to find
why there was this negative association and why this association only existed in the United States and Singapore, but not in Finland.
