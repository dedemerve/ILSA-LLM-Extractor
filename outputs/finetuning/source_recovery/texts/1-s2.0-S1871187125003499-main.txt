The effects of teaching initiative, ICT application, and teaching
collaboration on teachers’ creative thinking: Latent profile and
multiple logistic regression analysis
Hongde Wu , Guannan Wu , Runbing Yang , Mengqi Lin , Weizhong Zhang *
College of Education, Zhejiang Normal University, Jinhua Zhejiang, China

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Creative thinking

Teaching collaboration
Self-determination theory

Creative thinking is a core ability for teachers to adapt to educational reform, yet it is shaped by
multiple factors, especially amid widespread information and communications technology (ICT)
use. Guided by self-determination theory (SDT), this study drew on the Teacher Questionnaire of
the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022 data from 4069 teachers in
Hong Kong and Macao Special Administrative Region, China, and used Mplus 8.3 for latent profile
analysis (LPA) and multivariate logistic regression. Results showed teachers’ creative thinking
can be divided into five classes: low, medium-low, medium, medium-high, and high. Taking the
low class as a reference, teaching initiative and teaching collaboration positively predicted medium, medium-high, and high classes, while ICT application positively predicted medium-low,
medium, medium-high, and high classes, with the three factors’ effects strengthening as class
level rose. Additionally, demographic variables differentially predicted classes: gender and age
were negative predictors, while region and subject were positive predictors. These findings
advance understanding of factors influencing teachers’ creative thinking in contemporary education and inform efforts to foster this ability.

1. Introduction
As a key element for enhancing individuals’ thinking abilities and innovative capabilities, creative thinking has increasingly
become a focal point of educational research (Barbot & Kaufman, 2025; OECD, 2023). It not only exerts significant impacts on
teachers’ own professional development (Hadar & Tirosh, 2019; Kautz et al., 2014) but also profoundly influences the cultivation of
students’ creative thinking and abilities (e.g., teachers with excellent creative thinking can design more engaging and innovative
learning activities, foster students’ critical thinking and problem-solving skills, and create a classroom atmosphere that encourages
exploration and innovation) (Kupers et al., 2018; Runco & Jaeger, 2012). With the increasing diversification of current teaching modes
(e.g., project-based learning, flipped classroom, and inquiry-based instruction) (Adhami & Taghizadeh, 2022), and the rapid development and application of artificial intelligence (AI) technology (e.g., generative AI tools such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek, as well as
intelligent tutoring systems that support personalized learning) (Lin et al., 2023; Pham & Sampson, 2022), teachers need to possess

sufficient creative thinking to adapt to diverse teaching strategies, integrate new technologies, or meet the needs of varied students,

However, while existing studies have explored the impact of creative thinking on teachers’ professional development (Adey, 2006;

and characteristics of teachers’ creative thinking? (2) What are the effects of teachers’ teaching initiative, ICT application and teaching
collaboration, and other demographic variables on their own creative thinking classes, and what specific predictive functions do these
factors have?
2. Literature review
2.1. Theoretical framework
SDT, proposed by Deci and Ryan (1980), is a well-established motivational theory widely validated in psychology and education,
particularly in explaining how social environmental factors foster intrinsic motivation and further drive complex cognitive abilities,
including creative thinking (Gagné & Deci, 2005; Sheldon, 1995). Unlike general motivational frameworks that focus on external
incentives, SDT emphasizes that the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs, namely autonomy, competence, and relatedness,
serves as the core driver for individuals to engage in sustained, high-quality creative behaviors (Ryan & Deci, 2000). For teachers, this
theoretical logic is highly applicable: when their psychological needs are met, they are more likely to break free from rigid teaching
routines, take risks in pedagogical innovation, and generate novel ideas that align with student development needs, all of which are key
manifestations of teachers’ creative thinking.
Specifically, autonomy refers to the degree of voluntary control teachers perceive in teaching decision-making (e.g., independently
designing curriculum modules, selecting inquiry-based teaching methods), rather than passive implementation of standardized requirements (Ryan & Deci, 2000). This aligns with the “initiative” required for creative thinking, as autonomous teachers are more
willing to invest time in exploring unconventional strategies (Haapaniemi et al., 2020). Competence denotes teachers’ confidence in
mastering and applying professional skills to achieve teaching goals (e.g., using digital tools to design personalized learning tasks),
which is not limited to technical proficiency but also includes the ability to flexibly integrate skills into creative practice (Cerasoli et al.,
2016; Chiu et al., 2024). For teachers, competence in ICT application or innovative pedagogies provides the foundational capacity to
translate creative ideas into actionable lessons. Relatedness goes beyond simple collaborative behavior, as it refers to the emotional
connection and mutual support teachers feel in social interactions (e.g., sharing teaching dilemmas with colleagues, co-constructing
teaching strategies), which reduces the anxiety of “innovating alone” and creates a safe context for creative exploration (Akbari et al.,
2015; Butz & Stupnisky, 2017).
2.2. Teachers’ creative thinking
Creative thinking is broadly defined as the ability to generate, evaluate, and refine original, useful ideas to solve complex problems
(Runco & Jaeger, 2012; Stein, 1953). For teachers, however, this construct is not a generalized cognitive skill but a context-specific
competence deeply embedded in educational practice, and its core function is to support students’ creative development and adapt
to dynamic curriculum reforms (Hong & Milgram, 2010; OECD, 2023). The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
2022 (OECD, 2023) further operationalizes teachers’ creative thinking into three interdependent dimensions, which directly guide the
variable selection in this study: openness to creativity (teachers’ ratings of their agreement with statements about their openness to
creative activities), creative values (teachers’ ratings of their agreement with statements about their values regarding creativity), and
teachers’ use of creative pedagogies (teachers’ ratings of how much importance they place on using creative pedagogies in class).
This operationalization is of crucial importance because it distinguishes teachers’ creative thinking from general creativity. For
instance, teachers’ creative thinking is not merely about generating new ideas, but rather about ensuring these ideas are pedagogically
effective and student-centered (Anderson et al., 2022). Existing studies have shown that teachers with strong creative thinking are
more likely to adapt to curriculum reforms (Cheng, 2010), integrate emerging technologies into instruction (Henriksen et al., 2021;
Yilmaz, 2021), and engage in the evidence-based refinement of teaching practices (Huang et al., 2022). Furthermore, they are more
likely to design engaging inquiry-based activities (Yonwong et al., 2024), foster students’ critical thinking and problem-solving skills
(Calavia et al., 2021), and create classroom environments that encourage exploratory learning (Anderson et al., 2022; Ayyildiz &
Yilmaz, 2021). It can be said that it is mainly summarized into three interrelated characteristics: openness (breaking free from
traditional teaching paradigms to accept diverse perspectives) (Barbot & Kaufman, 2025; Kaufman, 2013), creativity (designing novel

Despite the growing recognition of the significance of teachers’ creative thinking in educational contexts (Pesout & Nietfeld, 2021;
Shermukhammadov, 2022), existing research has largely overlooked the in-depth exploration of its classification and characteristics.
Most studies either apply generalized creativity frameworks (e.g., Torrance’s creativity constructs) to educational research without
contextualizing them in pedagogical scenarios (Almeida et al., 2008; Kim, 2006), or merely confirm the existence of teachers’ creative
thinking and treat it as a continuous variable, overlooking the existence of distinct subgroups (Kettler et al., 2018; Mullet et al., 2016).
Therefore, this study addresses these gaps by focusing on the latent characteristics of teachers’ creative thinking and using the
context-specific operationalization of PISA 2022 to ensure ecological validity.
2.3. Teaching initiative, ICT application, and teaching collaboration
To explore the antecedents of teachers’ creative thinking through the lens of SDT, we selected three key variables, namely teaching
initiative, ICT application, and teaching collaboration, based on two criteria: (1) their direct alignment with SDT’s three basic needs,
and (2) their operationalization in the Teacher Questionnaire for PISA 2022 (OECD, 2024), which ensures consistency with the study’s
data source and enhances empirical rigor.
2.3.1. Teaching initiative and autonomy
Teaching initiative is the concrete manifestation of SDT’s autonomy need in teaching practice, rather than a vague “proactive
attitude” (Ryan & Deci, 2000). It refers to teachers’ awareness and behaviors of proactively initiating and designing innovative
teaching activities (e.g., curricula, research projects) in their teaching practice to promote teaching improvement, meet students’
learning needs, and facilitate their own teaching development. (Brew & Cahir, 2013; Krainer et al., 2018). Studies have shown that
educational governance models that support teacher autonomy significantly enhance teachers’ willingness to break away from
traditional teaching frameworks and try innovative pedagogies (Lennert da Silva & Mølstad, 2020; Salokangas et al., 2019). Additionally, teachers with high teaching initiative possess stronger professional identity and intrinsic motivation, because the satisfaction
of autonomy needs activates their internal drive to explore new teaching concepts, thereby enabling them to exhibit greater intrinsic
motivation to innovate (Haapaniemi et al., 2020; Kengatharan, 2020). The PISA 2022 variable AUTONOMY, which measures
“teachers’ ratings of how much control they have over various decisions at their school (e.g., “Determining course content” and
“Disciplining students”)” (OECD, 2024), directly captures this dimension of autonomy, justifying its selection as the operational indicator of teaching initiative.
2.3.2. ICT application and competence
ICT application refers to the integration of information and communication technology tools (e.g., e-learning platforms, multimedia resources, and virtual collaboration tools) into teaching, learning, and teacher professional development processes (Haldorai
et al., 2020). It is a potential indicator of SDT’s competence need (rather than a narrow focus on “technical skill use”), as competence in
SDT is tied to purposeful application of skills in meaningful contexts (Cerasoli et al., 2016). The United Nations Educational, Scientific
and Cultural Organization (UNESCO, 2018) classifies teachers’ ICT application into three levels: knowledge acquisition, deepening,
and creation, and points out that ICT application at the “knowledge creation” level (e.g., using ICT to design personalized creative
tasks) is most closely associated with innovative teaching. Studies have shown that Polish teachers who frequently use ICT tools for
innovative purposes (rather than merely replacing traditional teaching) demonstrate higher levels of creative pedagogical practices, as
proficient ICT use enhances their sense of teaching efficacy (Szyszka et al., 2022). Furthermore, Dutch teachers who use ICT to foster
students’ collaborative and problem-solving skills (key components of creative thinking) also exhibit higher levels of creative thinking
themselves (Nidup, 2022). The PISA 2022 variable TCICTUSE specifically measures “teachers’ frequency ratings of how often they
used specific ICT applications while teaching (e.g., “Digital learning games” and “Data logging and monitoring tools”)” (OECD, 2024).
This measure can serve as a reference for SDT’s focus on “competence for innovation”. Although it does not clearly reflect the
“knowledge creation” level of ICT use emphasized by UNESCO and prior studies, teachers are more likely to feel competent in technical
application and gain more confidence to explore novel pedagogical forms when such tool use is oriented toward creative teaching goals
(Wu et al., 2022; Yilmaz, 2021).
2.3.3. Teaching collaboration and relatedness
Teaching collaboration fully embodies SDT’s relatedness need, which includes both instrumental collaboration (resource sharing)
and emotional relatedness (mutual support) (Akbari et al., 2015; Vangrieken et al., 2015). It refers to teachers’ interactions with
colleagues to co-construct teaching practices (e.g., collaborative lesson planning for creative writing, peer observation and feedback on
inquiry-based lessons) and share emotional experiences (e.g., discussing challenges in implementing creative pedagogies, or celebrating successful innovation attempts) (Sawyer & Rimm-Kaufman, 2007; Vangrieken et al., 2015). This goes beyond simple “information exchange”: relatedness need satisfaction occurs when teachers feel accepted, understood, and supported by their peers, a
critical condition for creative thinking, as innovation often involves uncertainty and risk. Studies have found that teaching collaboration provides a “scaffold” for creative thinking: through knowledge sharing and viewpoint collision, it exposes teachers to diverse
teaching concepts (e.g., learning innovative pedagogies from colleagues) and refines their own creative thinking (Cojorn & Sonsupap,
2024; Vangrieken et al., 2015). Furthermore, research indicates that collaborative teachers have higher job satisfaction and are more
willing to try creative teaching strategies. This is because the sense of belonging derived from collaboration reduces the anxiety of

PISA 2022 variable EXCHT, which measures “teachers’ frequency ratings of how often they participate in teaching-related co-operation (e.g., Exchange teaching materials with colleagues” and “Attend team conferences”)” (OECD, 2024), directly captures this dual
dimension of relatedness.
In summary, while previous studies have examined teaching initiative, ICT application, and teaching collaboration in isolation, few
have systematically linked them to SDT’s three needs to explain teachers’ creative thinking. This study addresses this gap by framing
the three variables as SDT-based antecedents, leveraging PISA 2022′s context-specific operationalizations to ensure theoretical consistency and empirical validity. This framework not only explains how each variable influences teachers’ creative thinking but also
reveals their interactive effects, laying the foundation for subsequent latent profile and regression analyses.
3. Methodology
3.1. Sample selection
The data for this study was sourced from the Teacher Questionnaire for PISA 2022 (International Option) released by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), which adopted a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design (OECD,
2023): the first stage samples schools proportionally to school size, and the second stage samples teachers within selected schools. To
ensure the representativeness of the sample, this study applied the teacher-level sampling weight (WTTEACH) provided in the PISA
2022 dataset, this weight adjusts for non-response rates and unequal sampling probabilities across regions/schools, a key requirement
for addressing PISA’s complex sampling framework.
Then, teachers from Hong Kong and Macao Special Administrative Regions of China (region codes: 344 and 446) were selected for
analysis primarily for the following reasons: As Special Administrative Regions of China, these two regions share core educational
characteristics (e.g., bilingual instruction, alignment with international assessment standards) and a similar overall context (both
operating under China’s unified framework). Additionally, incorporating “region” as a control variable allows us to avoid the masking
of heterogeneity, a setup that is not feasible in monocultural or entirely distinct national contexts.
Finally, the initial total sample size is 4251 people and only divided into mathematics teachers and general teachers according to
the type of subject. After excluding the control variables with missing values in the study and the three key variables involved in the
core analysis (creative thinking) that are all missing samples, a total of 4069 samples were obtained, and descriptive statistical analysis
was conducted on the variables involved in the research institute (see Table 1). Among them, there were 2165 people (53.2 %) in Hong
Kong Special Administrative Region, 1904 people (46.8 %) in Macao Special Administrative Region, 2156 females (53.0 %), 1913
males (47.0 %), 911 mathematics teachers (22.4 %), and a total average age of 39.74 years (standard deviation=9.93).
3.2. Data selection and processing
First, in line with PISA data standards, this study selected items calibrated by Item Response Theory (IRT) and calculated Weighted
Likelihood Estimation (WLE; mean=0, standard deviation=1; a higher WLE score stands for higher ability or tendency) scores for all
key variables. Notably, these WLE scores integrate two components: psychometric calibration (adjusting for item difficulty and
discrimination) to ensure scale reliability, and PISA’s WTTEACH to correct for sampling bias, differing from simple scale scoring and
aligning with PISA’s complex design requirements (Ahmed et al., 2005; OECD, 2023). Second, based on the PISA 2022 Assessment and
Analytical Framework and the three core needs of SDT, combined with the data in Table 1: Gender, Region, and Subject are all binary
variables, and there are no missing values for Region and Subject. In order to preserve data information to the maximum extent, reduce
estimation bias, and enhance the rigor of the research, the Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) method was directly used to
handle missing values (Missing=99) in Mplus (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2010). Finally, the scales for the latent variables used in this
study will be briefly described in this section. More details can be found in the Technical Report (OECD, 2024) and the Assessment and
Analytical Framework (OECD, 2023) of PISA 2022.

3.2.2. Teaching initiative
The Teaching initiative Scale mainly described teachers’ ratings of how much control they have over various decisions at their school
(e.g., “Determining course content”, “Disciplining students”) in question TC246 were scaled into the index of “Teachers’ work autonomy”. Each of the seven items included in this scale had four response options (“No control”, “Some control”, “A lot of control”,

and English versions of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region, and 0.81 and 0.78 in the Chinese and English versions of the
Macao Special Administrative Region, respectively.
3.2.3. ICT application
The ICT application Scale mainly described teachers’ frequency ratings of how often they used specific ICT applications while
teaching (e.g., “Digital learning games”, “Data logging and monitoring tools”) in question TC169 were scaled into the index of
“Teachers’ use of specific ICT applications”. Note that this scale was linked to the TCICTUSE scale in PISA 2018. Each of the 14 items
included in this scale had four response options (“Never”, “In some lessons”, “In most lessons”, “In every or almost every lesson”)
(OECD, 2024). The internal consistency of the test is well, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.82 and 0.82 in the Chinese and English
versions of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region, and 0.87 and 0.84 in the Chinese and English versions of the Macao Special
Administrative Region, respectively.
3.2.4. Teaching collaboration
The Teaching collaboration Scale mainly described teachers’ frequency ratings of how often they participate in teaching-related cooperation (e.g., Exchange teaching materials with colleagues”, “Attend team conferences”) in question TC046 were scaled into the
index of “Exchange and co-ordination for teaching”. Note that this scale was linked to the EXCHT scale in PISA 2018. Each of the four
items included in this scale had six response options (“Never”, “Once a year or less”, “2–4 times a year”, “5–10 times a year”, “1–3 times
a month”, “Once a week or more”) (OECD, 2024). The internal consistency of the test is good, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.81 and
0.83 in the Chinese and English versions of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region, and 0.79 and 0.74 in the Chinese and English
versions of the Macao Special Administrative Region, respectively.
3.4. Data analysis
According to the research objectives, this study mainly utilized Mplus 8.3 software for data analysis and divided it into two main
stages. Notably, teachers in the PISA 2022 dataset are nested within schools (i.e., clustering effect: teachers in the same school may
have similar characteristics due to shared school culture/policies), so the TYPE=COMPLEX command was applied in all analyses to
adjust for school-level clustering and the WTTEACH. This adjustment corrects for potential biases in standard errors caused by nonindependent observations within the same school, a critical step for valid inference with nested data (Muthén & Muthén, 2000).
The first stage was Latent Profile Analysis (LPA). The WLE scores of the three creative thinking dimensions served as indicators. The
modeling process proceeded as follows: starting from a single-class model (C-1), the number of classes was gradually increased, while
key values across different classes were observed and compared to determine the optimal number of classes. Among these criteria,
lower values of Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), higher
entropy, and significant Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) and Lo-Mendell-Rubin Test (LMRT) indicate a better model fit
(Nylund et al., 2007).
The second stage was multiple logistic regression analysis to further explain the formation mechanism of differences in creative
thinking classes and achieve in-depth interpretation and supplementation of LPA results. The classification of creative thinking from
the identified optimal classes was used as the dependent variable (with one of the classes serving as the reference), and gender, age,
region, subject, teaching initiative, ICT application, and teaching collaboration were used as independent variables respectively. The

modeling process included two key steps: first, multicollinearity diagnosis was conducted by calculating the Tolerance (T) and
Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) of all independent variables to ensure they met the basic requirements for regression analysis; second,

OR > 1 indicates a positive predictive effect (Menard, 2000).
4. Results
4.1. Latent profile analysis
Firstly, based on the single class model, gradually increasing the number of classes can fully reveal the optimal potential number of
classes for teachers’ creative thinking. Research has found that AIC, BIC, and aBIC values gradually decrease as the number of classifications increases, and no lowest values have been observed. The BLRT and LMRT reach significant levels (p<.01) when the number
of models is 2, 3, 4, and 5, and the Entropy value is the highest (0.93) when the number of models is 5. Therefore, it indicates that C-5 is
the optimal model (as shown in Table 2).
Secondly, plot the WLE mean values of five latent classes on creative thinking (as shown in Fig. 1), and name the five classes as “low
creative thinking” (Class 1, n = 522), “medium-low creative thinking” (Class 2, n = 1714), “medium creative thinking” (Class 3, n =
710), “medium-high creative thinking” (Class 4, n = 567), and “high creative thinking” (Class 5, n = 567). These labels were assigned
based on the ascending order of the WLE mean values of creative thinking across the five classes, where Class 1 exhibited the lowest
mean and Class 5 the highest mean on creative thinking. In addition, the WLE mean distribution of the five latent classes of teachers’
creative thinking shown in Fig. 1 exhibits a clear hierarchical state, which intuitively verifies the effectiveness of the latent profile
analysis and clearly distinguishes teacher groups with different levels of creative thinking. This indicates that there are essential
differences in the creative thinking structure of each class, rather than random distribution, and truly reflects the objective gradient of
creative thinking levels in the teacher group. Notably, identifying these five latent classes captures combinations of the three creative
thinking dimensions (e.g., distinct patterns where openness, values, and pedagogies co-occur at high or low levels). This advances
understanding beyond bivariate correlations and justifies using classes as a holistic dependent variable in subsequent regression
analyses.
4.2. Multiple logistic regression analysis
Firstly, based on the identification of five classes of creative thinking, a multicollinearity analysis was conducted with gender, age,
region, subject, teaching initiative, ICT application, and teaching collaboration as independent variables, with creative thinking class
as the dependent variable (as shown in Table 3). The results indicate that the T values of each variable are close to 1, and the VIF values
are all < 10, indicating that there is no significant collinearity issue between these independent variables and multiple logistic
regression analysis can be performed.

profile classes (as shown in Table 4). The results showed that compared to Class 1, “Gender” has a significant negative predictive effect

probability of becoming Class 2, Class 3, Class 4, and Class 5 class is lower (OR=0.982, OR=0.966, OR=0.956, OR=0.954). “Region”
has a significant positive predictive effect on the classes of Class 4 and Class 5 (b = 0.679, b = 0.523, p<.01), indicating that teachers in
Macao have a higher probability of being classified as Class 3, Class 4, and Class 5 compared to teachers in Hong Kong (OR=1.972,

5. Discussion and conclusion
5.1. Latent classes and characteristics of teachers’ creative thinking
The LPA identified five distinct latent classes of teachers’ creative thinking in Hong Kong and Macao Special Administrative Regions, showing a hierarchical distribution from low to high. The largest group was “medium-low creative thinking” (41.9 %), where
teachers have basic creative awareness but struggle to apply it in practice, which consistent with prior research on barriers like
curriculum standardization (Cheng, 2010; Mansor & Ming, 2023). The smallest group, “low creative thinking” (12.9 %), requires
targeted support (e.g., addressing gaps in teaching autonomy or ICT literacy). In contrast, “high” and “medium-high” creative thinking
classes (total approximately 30 %) balance creative awareness, values, and pedagogical application, providing a model for teacher
training (e.g., proactive curriculum design, collaborative innovation) (Kettler et al., 2018).
Notably, as a person-centered analytical method, LPA can identify the unique characteristics of different subgroups. This differs
from variable-centered methods that focus on average effects, and provides a basis for non-random differentiated support. Theoretically, this classification addresses the limitation of prior studies treating creative thinking as a continuous variable, providing
empirical support for its multidimensional, hierarchical nature in teaching practice from the SDT perspective. Practically, it enables
differentiated training: targeting basic ICT/autonomy for the “low” class, creative practice translation for the “medium-low” class, and
using high-level classes’ “proactive collaboration + innovation” as a template.
5.2. The impact of teacher’s teaching initiative, ICT application, and teaching collaboration on different latent classes
This study found teaching initiative, ICT application, and teaching collaboration exhibit a gradient-increasing predictive effect on
teachers’ creative thinking classes (predictive strength grows with class level), which verifies the core hypothesis of SDT: satisfying
autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs drives progressive intrinsic motivation and creativity (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Sheldon,
1995). For example, teaching initiative (an autonomy indicator) does not predict the “medium-low creative thinking” class
(OR=1.104, p>.05) but strongly predicts the “high creative thinking” class (OR=1.893, p<.001). Basic autonomy (e.g., control over
teaching content) is insufficient to promote creativity beyond the medium-low level. Instead, only deep agency in innovative design (e.
g., interdisciplinary projects) enables higher-level development.
Similarly, ICT application (a competence indicator) predicts even the “medium-low creative thinking” class (OR=1.148, p<.05, via
basic digital literacy), and its effect strengthens for higher classes (e.g., “high creative thinking” OR=1.726). This confirms ICT used for
“knowledge creation” (e.g., virtual experiments, AI-assisted creative tasks) is more impactful than basic use (UNESCO, 2018; Wu et al.,
2022). Teaching collaboration (a relatedness indicator) also shows a gradient effect: its predictive strength for the “high creative
thinking” class (OR=1.613) is nearly three times that for the “medium creative thinking” class (OR=1.211). This aligns with research
that deep collaboration (e.g., co-constructing creative lesson plans) scaffolds creativity, while superficial collaboration (e.g., casual
resource sharing) has limited impact (Egodawatte et al., 2011; Vangrieken et al., 2015).
While consistent with SDT, this gradient may be shaped by the study’s categorical approach. Dividing creative thinking into
discrete hierarchical classes may amplify perceived differences in predictor effects. As both teaching initiative and creative thinking
are continuous constructs in practice, their relationship may follow a nonlinear pattern (e.g., diminishing returns at high initiative
levels) that categorical analysis obscures. Additionally, PISA 2022 variables may correlate (e.g., teachers with high ICT application
may engage in more collaboration), which could strengthen the observed gradient. Future research could use continuous moderation
models to explore if predictor effects plateau at high creative thinking levels or if other factors (e.g., school leadership) moderate this
gradient.

5.3. The impact of gender, age, region, and subject on different latent classes
Multiple logistic regression showed demographic variables have differential effects: gender and age negatively predict creative
thinking classes, while region and subject positively predict them.
Male teachers are less likely than female teachers to be in “medium-low” (OR=0.812, p<.05) and “high” (OR=0.678, p<.01)
creative thinking classes. This relates to societal perceptions that female teachers use flexible methods (e.g., project-based learning)
aligned with creativity (Eagly et al., 2007; Kasirer & Shnitzer-Meirovich, 2021) and reflects systemic factors (e.g., male teachers’
pressure to prioritize academic rigor) rather than inherent differences (Brown et al., 2024). Age negatively affects higher classes: each
additional year reduces the “high creative thinking” class probability by 4.6 % (OR=0.954, p<.001). Younger teachers adapt better to
emerging tech (e.g., generative AI) and innovation (Kautz et al., 2014; Lin et al., 2023), while older teachers may face constraints from
habits or limited digital training. However, this trend is not absolute, for older teachers with continuous professional development may
still be creative, though the sample (average age 39.74 years) may not capture this subgroup. Future research could explore if
collaborative support mitigates age’s negative effect.
Macao teachers are more likely than Hong Kong teachers to be in “medium-high” (OR=1.972, p<.01) and “high” (OR=1.687,
p<.01) classes. This stems from Macao’s policy prioritizing creativity (e.g., funding for innovative projects via the “Macao NonTertiary Education Ten-Year Plan”) (Government of the Macau Special Administrative Region, 2021), while Hong Kong’s standardized curriculum and evaluation pressure limit creative practice (Brown et al., 2024). Macao’s younger teacher population (average age
42.1 vs. Hong Kong’s 44.5 years) also contributes, as younger teachers integrate new tech and pedagogies more easily.
Non-mathematics teachers (e.g., language, science, art) are more likely in higher creative thinking classes than mathematics teachers
(OR=2.003 for “high”, p<.01). Mathematics’ emphasis on logical rigor limits open-ended creativity (Hadar & Tirosh, 2019), while
general subjects allow flexible practices (e.g., creative writing, exploratory projects) (Mansor & Ming, 2023). This highlights the need
for subject-specific training (e.g., integrating real-world problems into math lessons) to foster creativity in rigorous disciplines.
6. Limitations and prospects
This study has several limitations to consider. First, the data from the PISA 2022 Teacher Questionnaire are cross-sectional and
correlational (Edwards & Lambert, 2007; Graziano & Raulin, 2007). While we examined how teaching initiative, ICT application, and
teaching collaboration predict latent classes of teachers’ creative thinking, the correlational nature of the data prohibits definitive
causal inferences. For instance, it is plausible that teachers with higher creative thinking might proactively pursue greater teaching
autonomy (appearing as “high teaching initiative”), engage more extensively with ICT to support creative practices, or initiate
collaborative activities to share creative ideas, reverse causality that cross-sectional data cannot eliminate (Kline, 2011).
Second, the study depends entirely on self-report measures from teacher questionnaires. Self-report data are vulnerable to response
biases (e.g., social desirability bias, where teachers may overstate their creative thinking or positive behaviors) (Podsakoff et al.,
2003). Objective measures (such as classroom observations of creative pedagogies or student ratings of teacher creativity) could offer a
more holistic perspective but were unavailable in the PISA 2022 dataset.
Third, the measurement of ICT application in this study has limitations. In SDT, “competence” centers on “efficacy experiences and
ability beliefs in specific tasks”, which is a construct that requires capturing ICT’s contextual use. However, PISA’s scale only assesses
the frequency of 14 tool types, not their application scenarios. This creates a logical stretch when inferring “competence for innovation” from “use frequency”, then linking this to “confidence in exploring new pedagogical forms”, which represents a discrepancy
from the cited prior studies (which tied ICT to competence via specific purposes). Future research could use tools measuring ICT
application quality (e.g., distinguishing tools’ pedagogical orientation) to better align with SDT’s competence dimension.
Fourth, despite analyzing Hong Kong and Macao as regions with aligned educational contexts, the sample remains geographically
limited to these two Special Administrative Regions. Generalizing findings to other Chinese regions or international contexts with
distinct educational systems (e.g., mainland China, Western countries) requires caution, as cultural and policy differences may shape
variable relationships (Eckhardt, 2002).
