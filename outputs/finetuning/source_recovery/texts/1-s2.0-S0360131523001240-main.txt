A B S T R A C T  
Information and communication technology (ICT) literacy is key to solving future societal chal­
lenges and to leading an independent and responsible life in an increasingly globalized and 
digitalized world. Hence, countries around the world have promoted research on students’ ICT 
skills and their inclusion in curricula. Despite these efforts, little is known about what predicts 
future ICT literacy, and prior research has almost exclusively conducted cross-sectional studies. In 
the present study, we addressed this gap by using data from a large-scale longitudinal study of 
6810 students in 311 schools from Germany. These students participated in the National 
Educational Panel Study over the course of secondary school (in Grades 6, 9, and 12). We were 
particularly interested in investigating potential spillover effects from skills and dispositions that 
are inherently fostered in school on ICT development. Thus, we considered a rich set of different 
types of predictors related to the students’ social, cultural, demographic, and learning back­
grounds; their subject-specific motivation; and the composition of their schools, and we assessed 
the extent to which these variables explained variation in ICT literacy development at two key 
stages in the educational system (Grades 9 and 12). Our results indicated that prior ICT literacy 
was the most important predictor of future ICT literacy but more so for the development between 
Grades 9 and 12. In addition, variables related to the students’ learning backgrounds, followed by 
variables related to the composition of their schools and students’ subject-specific motivation 
explained future ICT literacy. Overall, the predictors explained between 49% and 67% of the 
variation in future ICT literacy. Our longitudinal findings suggest that the learning environment 
and students’ skills and dispositions are crucial for understanding ICT development and are 
indicative of the deliberate design of ICT interventions.

1. Introduction 
Becoming digitally literate is essential for actively participating in today’s globalized and increasingly digitalized societies (Voogt 
& Roblin, 2012). Students with high information and communication technology (ICT) literacy are able to appropriately interact with 
digital technologies, which might include, among others, the use, access, transformation, and sharing of information (e.g., Fraillon 
et al., 2019; Katz, 2007). However, as outlined in the International Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS) 2018, students 
around the world are lagging behind in ICT skills. Across all participating countries, an average of about 18% of students did not reach 
the first of four proficiency levels. Thus, these students are not able to adequately use computers to create information without 
guidance. At the same time, only a few students reached the highest proficiency level (Fraillon et al., 2020b). 
Given these results and the increased need for ICT literacy nowadays, a lively scientific discussion has emerged over the past few 
years. This discussion is concerned with identifying variables that are associated with ICT skills (e.g., Hatlevik, Guðmundsd´ottir, & Loi, 
2015; Hu et al., 2018; Senkbeil & Ihme, 2017a). Most prior studies have utilized cross-sectional designs to identify such variables. 
However, such studies reflect only a snapshot of the predictors of students’ ICT skills, and cross-sectional studies are strongly limited 
when it comes to investigating developmental processes and predictors of such processes. Thus, researchers have recently emphasized 
the importance of extending cross-sectional work by adding longitudinal perspectives, particularly when questions related to the 
development of ICT literacy are the focus (Gnambs, 2021). Although several studies have made use of longitudinal designs to date 
(Gnambs, 2021; Lazonder et al., 2020; Senkbeil, 2022), research on relevant predictors in different stages of education is still emerging. 
The current state of research on the development of digital literacy was recently summarized by Lazonder et al. (2020), who wrote that 
“there is a dearth of evidence on how digital literacy develops in children” (p. 3). Notably, identifying predictors of ICT literacy 
development is a key prerequisite for being able to develop potential interventions for further enhancing students’ ICT literacy. Against 
this background, we aimed to investigate the predictive power of a comprehensive set of predictors when explaining the development 
of ICT literacy at two adjacent educational stages (Grades 6 to 9 and Grades 9 to 12) that constitute the end of mandatory schooling 
(Grade 9) and a common end of upper secondary school in Germany (Grade 12 in G8 systems; Hübner, Wagner et al., 2022). To achieve 
this aim, we utilized a large-scale, longitudinal data set from the National Educational Panel Study (NEPS) and examined students’ 
social, cultural, demographic, and learning backgrounds; subject-specific motivation; and school composition as predictors. As more 
closely outlined in Senkbeil et al. (2013), ICT in the NEPS study was conceptualized on the basis of prior work by the ICT Literacy 
Panel, who defined ICT literacy as follows: “ICT literacy is using digital technology, communications tools, and/or networks to access, 
manage, integrate, evaluate, and create information in order to function in a knowledge society” (ETS, 2002, p. 11). As further outlined 
in Senkbeil et al. (2013) and Ihme et al. (2015), ICT was conceptualized as a unidimensional construct that consists of facets of in­
formation and technological literacy. Importantly, it is defined from a functional perspective and thus focuses on the skills and 
knowledge that students need to successfully participate in modern-day societies. 
More specifically, to extend prior longitudinal studies on ICT literacy development (e.g., Gnambs, 2021; Lazonder et al., 2020; 
Senkbeil, 2022), we were particularly interested in further exploring how subject-specific skills, dispositions, and related composi­
tional school structures might contribute to ICT literacy development (e.g., subject-specific literacy and motivation) from a joint 
perspective. Investigating potential spillover effects from skills and dispositions that are inherently affected or fostered by schooling 
can contribute to a better understanding of the nature of ICT literacy development and might help prioritize how limited resources 
should be invested in advancing students’ learning. 
2. Literature Review 
The literature review consists of two parts. First, we outline the available literature on frameworks and conceptualizations of the 
central outcome variable of this study (ICT; 2.1). We then report the central empirical findings on predictors of skill development (2.2) 
with a focus on the different groups of predictors that resulted from our literature review (2.2.1–2.2.3). 
2.1. Information and Communication Technology Literacy 
Citizens from today have to deal with an increasing amount of information and technology. Thus, many scholars have repeatedly 
highlighted the importance of acquiring strong skills in this area, commonly referred to as ICT literacy (Fraillon et al., 2020b). ICT 
literacy can be understood as “the ability to appropriately use digital technology, communication tools, and/or networks to solve 
information problems in order to function in an information society. This includes having the ability to use technology as a tool to 
research, organize, and communicate information and having a fundamental understanding of the ethical/legal issues surrounding 
accessing and using information” (Katz, 2007, p. 4). This definition accentuates the idea that students with strong ICT skills are able not 
only to independently use technologies but also to use them as a tool to process (e.g., access, evaluate, and manage) information (e.g., 
Binkley et al., 2012; Fraillon et al., 2019; Katz, 2007). ICT literacy is most commonly operationalized as a domain-general construct, 
that is, a construct that can be applied across a wide variety of domains (Scherer & Siddiq, 2019). A prominent ICT framework was 
developed by the ICT Literacy Panel and consists of seven underlying components of ICT literacy (i.e., define, access, evaluate, manage, 
integrate, create, and communicate; see ETS, 2002; Katz, 2007). 
To date, several different frameworks exist that have informed the development of ICT literacy tests (Siddiq et al., 2016). For 
instance, the ICILS 2013 framework defined computer and information literacy (CIL) as “an individual’s ability to use computers to 
investigate, create, and communicate in order to participate effectively at home, at school, in the workplace, and in society” (Fraillon 
et al., 2013, p. 17). The framework underlying the prominent ETS iSkills assessment included seven ICT literacy performance areas:

definition, access, evaluation, management, integration, creation, and communication (Katz, 2007). Senkbeil et al. (2013) considered 
the ICT Literacy Panel definition of ICT as a foundation for the ICT test they developed, and they considered a similar set of seven 
components for describing the processes involved in ICT literacy. This framework constitutes the foundation of the ICT test considered 
in this study. 
Another framework to consider is the European Digital Competence Framework (DigComp 2.2) that identified five areas of 
competence and summarized a broad set of skills within them: information and data literacy, communication and collaboration, digital 
content creation, safety, and problem solving (Vuorkar et al., 2022). Finally, other authors have conceptualized ICT in terms of digital 
literacy or information literacy (e.g., Perdana et al., 2019; Reddy, et al., 2022). Notably, there have been some updates to the ICT 
literacy concepts over the past years. For instance, the conceptualization of the ICIL study has also included computational thinking 
since 2018. The DigComp has been updated as well. These new definitions include programming, the responsible use of ICT, and 
several aspects of problem solving (e.g., Fraillon et al., 2019). Overall, all of these frameworks define ICT literacy as a set of knowledge, 
skills, and sometimes even attitudes that are required to deal with ICT inside and outside of educational contexts. 
2.2. Predictors of Skill Development 
The overarching aim of this study was to explore how domain-general and domain-specific skills, dispositions, and related 
compositional school structures might contribute to ICT literacy development. To achieve this end, we first conducted a literature 
review on conceptual and empirical work related to skill development in general (e.g., Boudon, 1974; Eccles & Wigfield, 2002, 2020; 
G¨ollner et al., 2018; Hübner et al., 2021; Hübner et al., 2023; Hübner et al., 2022), ICT skills (e.g., Fraillon et al., 2019; Hatlevik, 
Guðmundsd´ottir, & Loi, 2015; Hatlevik et al., 2015; Scherer & Siddiq, 2019; Siddiq & Scherer, 2019), and ICT literacy development 
more specifically (e.g., Gnambs, 2021; Lazonder et al., 2020; Senkbeil, 2022). As outlined above, until now, only a few studies have 
explicitly focused on ICT literacy development (Lazonder et al., 2020), which is why evidence on predictors of such processes is 
strongly limited. The evidence base on the relevance of domain-specific skills, dispositions, and related compositional structures for 
these processes is even worse. This suggests that until now, it has largely been unclear how variables related to students’ social, 
cultural, and demographic backgrounds, students’ learning backgrounds, students’ motivation, and contextual school variables all 
influence ICT literacy development in concert. However, knowledge on how skills and dispositions that are inherently affected or 
fostered by schooling influence ICT skill development is a key prerequisite for being able to develop interventions that can further 
enhance students’ ICT literacy and foster evidence-based decisions regarding how to invest limited resources to advance students’ 
learning as a whole and their ICT literacy specifically. Against this backdrop, for this study, we decided to choose an inclusive 
framework that required us to also consider prior work on skill development in general as well as work on ICT literacy specifically. 
Given our review of the literature, we organized our findings into four groups of potentially relevant predictors for explaining students’ 
ICT literacy development. These groups of predictors constitute our working model and are related to: (a) students’ social, cultural, and 
demographic backgrounds, (b) students’ learning backgrounds, (c) students’ subject-specific motivation, and (d) the composition of 
their schools. In the following, we elaborate on each of these groups in greater detail. 
2.2.1. Students’ Social, Cultural, and Demographic Backgrounds 
Many studies have investigated how students’ social and cultural backgrounds are related to ICT skills (e.g., Scherer & Siddiq, 
2019). Most prominently, the recent ICILS cycle produced evidence that students’ socioeconomic background (i.e., a composite of 
parental occupational status, parental education, and the number of books at home) is related to students’ ICT skills. The associations 
ranged from Cohen’s d = 0.07 (Portugal) to d = 0.17 (France; Cohen, 1988), suggesting that higher socioeconomic status was asso­
ciated with better performance on ICT literacy assessments.1 Comparable results were found for computational thinking (CT; Fraillon 
et al., 2020a). Beyond ICILS, several other studies have more closely investigated such associations. Scherer and Siddiq (2019) 
comprehensively summarized most of these studies in a meta-analysis in which they considered 32 independent K-12 samples and 
found average associations of r = 0.21 between ICT skills and SES. This correlation varied by SES measure: Higher correlations were 
found in studies using capital SES measures (e.g., cultural possessions), compared with educational (e.g., highest educational level of 
parents) or occupational (e.g., highest occupation status of parents) SES measures. Notably, all correlations considered in this 
meta-analysis were based on cross-sectional study designs. 
Regarding student demographics, most prior research has focused on investigating the relevance of gender for ICT skills. In one of 
these studies, Gnambs (2021) examined gender differences in ICT literacy from cross-sectional and longitudinal perspectives. He found 
no gender differences in ICT literacy by the age of 15 (d = −0.03) but small differences 3 years later (d = −0.13) in favor of boys. 
Notably, Gnambs’ study provides an important longitudinal extension to the many cross-sectional studies that have reported mixed 
evidence on gender differences in ICT competencies. Specifically, older cross-sectional studies typically found advantages for boys (e. 
g., Hakkarainen et al., 2000; Kuhlemeier & Hemker, 2007), whereas more recent studies found no differences or even advantages for 
girls (Fraillon et al., 2020b; Punter et al., 2017; Siddiq & Scherer, 2019).

2.2.2. Students’ Learning Backgrounds and Subject-Specific Motivation 
Several studies have investigated the associations between student achievement and ICT literacy. For instance, Hu et al. (2018) 
analyzed data from 305,414 students from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2015 and found that 
self-reported ICT skills were positively and statistically significantly associated with achievement in reading, mathematics, and science 
at the country level. Similar results were found by Hatlevik, Guðmundsd´ottir, and Loi (2015): The authors reported that an average 
grade score, based on individual grades in Norwegian, English, social science, mathematics, and science, was positively related to 
students’ digital competencies. In the same vein, meta-analytic findings revealed positive associations (Hedges’ g = 0.64) between ICT 
literacy and students’ academic achievement (Lei et al., 2021). Senkbeil (2022) investigated the relationship between intelligence and 
ICT test scores in Grades 9 and 12. Intelligence was found to be the strongest predictor of ICT skills from cross-sectional and longi­
tudinal perspectives. In addition to these findings on student achievement, prior studies have detected that students’ perceptions of 
their abilities (e.g., their subject-specific self-concept and interest) are important for their achievement (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002, 
2020). On the basis of these findings, it seems reasonable that students’ subject-specific motivation and interest might be associated 
with ICT skills and their development. We are not aware of any previous studies that have investigated potential spillover effects of 
subject-specific motivation on ICT literacy development. 
2.2.3. Contextual School Effects 
Prior studies have indicated that school composition is an important variable in predicting students’ attainment later in life. In one 
of these studies, G¨ollner et al. (2018) found that the average level of socioeconomic background at a school was positively associated 
with educational attainment and expectations as well as income and occupational prestige 11 years later. Furthermore, Becker et al. 
(2022) found support for the positive effects of achievement-related peer composition, over and above tracking effects, suggesting that 
students’ learning progress might benefit from being surrounded by peers with a higher average level of performance. Other prior 
studies on this topic have found zero or even negative associations between school composition and student achievement (Dicke et al., 
2018). Regarding ICT, the ICILS 2018study reported substantial positive effects of school-average SES on CT and CIL learning, ranging 
from d = 0.27 (CT) to d = 0.32 (CIL). 
3. Research Questions 
Here, we aimed to examine the predictive power of four sets of variables related to students’ (a) social, cultural, and demographic 
backgrounds; (b) learning backgrounds; (c) subject-specific motivation; and (d) school composition for explaining the development of 
students’ ICT literacy at two subsequent stages in secondary school (Grades 6 to 9 and Grades 9 to 12). Specifically, we addressed three 
research questions (RQs): 
RQ1.
Which variables in the different sets of predictors are statistically significantly associated with ICT literacy after prior ICT 
literacy is controlled for? 
RQ2.
Which variables are statistically significantly associated with ICT literacy, when all sets of predictors (a-d) and prior ICT lit­
eracy are considered in a joint model? 
RQ3.
Which similarities and differences emerge when predictors are examined at two different stages in the education system? 
4. Method 
4.1. Sample and Procedures 
We used data from the National Educational Panel Study’s (NEPS) Starting Cohort 3 (NEPS Network, 2021). The NEPS is a 
large-scale longitudinal study conducted by the Leibniz Institute for Educational Trajectories in Bamberg, Germany (Blossfeld & 
Roβbach, 2019). We considered the available student data from Grades 5 (students’ ages ranged from 9 to 12 years) to 12. Our final 
sample consisted of 6810 students from 311 secondary schools all across Germany. For our first-stage analyses (development from 
Grades 6 to 9), we included all the data from our final sample. For our second-stage analyses (development from Grades 9 to 12), we 
included a subset of 1776 students who were still enrolled in upper secondary school. The two stages are of special importance because 
they constitute the end of mandatory schooling (Grade 9) and the end of the German high track G8 system or a very late timepoint in 
upper secondary school in the German G9 system (Grade 12; see Hübner, Wagner et al., 2022). 
4.2. Instruments 
ICT literacy. Students’ performances on an ICT literacy test administered in Grades 6, 9, and 12 served as indicators of ICT literacy. 
The framework and foundation of the ICT literacy test (see Ihme et al., 2015; Senkbeil et al., 2013) was largely comparable to other 
prominent ICT frameworks (e.g., ETS, 2002; Katz, 2007) and linked four processes (i.e., access, create, manage, and evaluate) to five 
software applications (i.e., word processing and operating systems, spreadsheet, presentation and graphics software, e-mail and other 
communication applications, and Internet/search engines). This framework provided the foundation for all ICT literacy tests 
administered in the NEPS. More specifically, the ICT literacy test in Grade 6 consisted of 30 items (see Senkbeil et al., 2014), and in 
Grade 9, it consisted of 60 items distributed across three booklets with low to high levels of difficulty (see Senkbeil & Ihme, 2017b). It

Sample items can be found in Ihme et al. (2015) and Senkbeil et al. (2013). In accordance with the general framework of the NEPS for

1776 students who were still enrolled in school in Grade 12, and 0.65 in Grade 12. To address the different degrees of unreliability of 
the tests, we applied a single-indicator approach (e.g., Hoyle, 2012) as outlined in the Statistical Analysis section. 
Predictors. When predicting ICT literacy in Grade 12, we controlled for ICT literacy in Grade 9, and when predicting ICT literacy in 
Grade 9, we controlled for ICT literacy in Grade 6. This is why ICT literacy in Grade 9 functioned as both a dependent and independent 
variable, depending on the model, whereas Grade 6 ICT literacy was used only as a predictor. Specifically, we considered variables to 
control for students’ (a) social, cultural, and demographic backgrounds (e.g., migration background, the highest international so­
cioeconomic index [HISEI] in the family, possessions of goods at home, age, and gender); (b) learning backgrounds (e.g., students’ 
achievement in mathematics, reading, and scientific literacy); and (c) subject-specific motivation (e.g., students’ subject-specific self- 
concepts and interests in reading and mathematics). In addition to these variables, we considered variables related to (d) school 
composition (e.g., school mean ICT achievement, the ratio of students with a migration background, and the school average HISEI). A 
list of all variables can be found in the Appendix Table A1 (also see FDZ-LIfBi, 2021). 
4.3. Statistical Analyses 
To address RQ1 to RQ3, we compiled the required NEPS data in R (R Core Team, 2022) and computed multiple regression analyses 
in Mplus 8.7 (Muth´en & Muth´en, 1998-2017) with the R package MplusAutomation version 1.0.0 (Hallquist & Wiley, 2018). We took a 
single-indicator approach (Hoyle, 2012) to control for measurement error on the ICT literacy variables. This approach defined a latent 
variable for each ICT score with a residual variance fixed to (1-reliability)*sample variance of ICT literacy performance (see Hübner, 
Spengler, et al., 2022). 
We specified two groups of models separately for the different stages in the education system (either Grades 6 to 9 [Stage 1] or 
Grades 9 to 12 [Stage 2]). In the Grade 6 to 9 models, we predicted Grade 9 ICT literacy from Grade 6 ICT literacy and the respective set 
of additional predictors from Grades 5 or 6. In the Grade 9 to 12 models, we predicted Grade 12 ICT literacy from Grade 9 ICT literacy 
and the respective set of additional predictors from Grades 8 or 9. The first stage considered all students from secondary school, 
ranging from low track students (Hauptschule) to high track students (Gymnasium). The second stage sample consisted only of stu­
dents from upper secondary schools (Gymnasium or comprehensive school). To meaningfully compare the predictive power of var­
iables at the two different stages for similar individuals (RQ3), we reestimated the Stage 1 analysis for those 1776 students we 
considered in the Stage 2 analysis.

decided to z-standardize all continuous variables (i.e., the outcome variable and all continuous predictors) but not to standardize the

constant). For dichotomous predictors (e.g., gender), β can be interpreted as the standardized difference between the two groups (e.g., 
girls and boys; while holding the influence of the other predictors constant). Note that in a simple linear regression with continuous

We specified six models to predict ICT literacy in Grades 9 and 12. In the first model (M1), we considered only students’ prior ICT 
skills as a predictor (i.e., in Grades 6 or 9). In the second model (M2), we added predictors related to students’ social, cultural, and 
demographic background. Next, we considered variables related to students’ learning backgrounds (Model 3; M3) before we took a 
closer look at the subject-specific motivational variables in the fourth model (M4). Next, we considered school composition variables 
(Model 5; M5), which were calculated on the basis of previously z-standardized, student-level variables (e.g., Hübner et al., 2021). 
Finally, in Model 6 (M6), we considered all the predictors from the previous models (M1 to M5) in a joint model.

5.1. Preliminary Analyses 
First, we examined the descriptive statistics (see Table A1 in the Appendix) for the three different data sets in this study. On 
average, students were about 12 years old in Grade 6 and about 14.5 years old in Grade 9. The data sets consisted of about 49%–53% 
girls, and the majority of students lived in western Germany (about 81%–82%). Regarding ICT development, we found an average 
increase (per year) of d = 0.32 between Grades 6 and 9 for the full sample of N = 6810 students and, as expected, a slightly higher ICT 
literacy development of d = 0.40 per year for the reduced sample of N = 1776 upper secondary school students. The ICT literacy 
development between Grades 9 and 12 amounted to d = 0.13 per year. Further descriptive information can be found in Table A1 in the 
Appendix.

5.3. RQ2: Relevant Predictors of ICT Literacy Development from a Joint Perspective 
ICT Literacy in Grades 6 to 9. To address RQ2, we specified a joint model including all predictors of ICT literacy in Grade 9 from M1 
to M5 (M6; see Table 3). With few exceptions, the results were similar to those for RQ1. First, ICT literacy in Grade 6 emerged as the 
strongest predictor of subsequent ICT literacy in Grade 9 (β = 0.389, SE = 0.030, p < .001). Next, we found that age (β = −0.049, SE =
0.014, p = .004), perceptual speed (β = 0.056, SE = 0.017, p = .008), mathematics test achievement (β = 0.124, SE = 0.023, p < .001), 
reading test achievement (β = 0.082, SE = 0.019, p < .001), scientific literacy (β = 0.154, SE = 0.020, p < .001), and subject-related 
interest in mathematics (β = 0.041, SE = 0.014, p = .021) were statistically significantly associated with ICT skills in Grade 9. Thus, 
these findings were largely comparable to the results for M1 to M5, except for school track (β = −0.045, SE = 0.059, p = .848), which 
was no longer statistically significantly related to ICT skills in the joint model M6. Statistically significant standardized regression 
coefficients ranged from β = −0.049 (Age) to β = 0.389 (ICT literacy in Grade 6). The variables considered in M6 explained about 68% 
of the overall variation in ICT literacy in Grade 9. 
ICT Literacy in Grades 9 to 12. Following the analyses with the Stage 1 data, we investigated the joint model when predicting Grade 
12 ICT literacy (Stage 2; see Table 3). Similar to the previous models, Grade 9 ICT literacy emerged as the strongest predictor of ICT 
literacy in Grade 12 (β = 0.657, SE = 0.041, p < .001). Furthermore, we found a negative association between gender and Grade 12 ICT 
literacy (β = −0.140, SE = 0.041, p = .006) in favor of boys. Again, scientific literacy predicted ICT literacy (β = 0.081, SE = 0.025, p =
.007). In addition, we found a positive association between school-average math achievement and Grade 12 ICT literacy (β = 0.357, SE 
= 0.105, p = .006) and a statistically significant negative association with school-average ICT literacy in Grade 9 (β = −0.424, SE =
0.114, p = .003). These findings indicate that, when all other variables were controlled for, the ICT skill development of students in 
schools with higher math achievement in Grade 9 was steeper, whereas the ICT skill development of students in schools with higher 
ICT skills in Grade 9 was shallower. The latter finding might constitute a ceiling effect: Students in schools that demonstrated a 
relatively high ICT performance earlier might improve less than students in schools with lower previous achievement. Statistically 
significant standardized regression coefficients ranged from β = −0.424 (School mean ICT literacy) to β = 0.657 (ICT literacy in Grade 
6). Overall, 62% of the variance in Grade 12 ICT literacy was explained by the variables considered in M6.

6. Discussion 
The aim of the present study was to identify and compare predictors of students’ ICT literacy development during two stages in the 
educational system (i.e., Grades 6 to 9 and Grades 9 to 12). Until now, few studies have investigated ICT literacy development (e.g., 
Gnambs, 2021; Lazonder et al., 2020), and evidence of how variables related to students’ social, cultural, and demographic back­
grounds, students’ learning backgrounds, students’ motivation, and contextual school variables jointly influence ICT literacy devel­
opment has been almost entirely unavailable. This lack of studies is likely also a result of high demands for the survey designs: To 
investigate predictors of ICT literacy development, longitudinal studies are needed to assess students’ digital literacy repeatedly over 
time, along with a large set of skills and dispositions that are inherently affected or fostered by schooling. In this regard, our study 
benefitted from the unique NEPS data set in which students were repeatedly assessed over the course of lower and upper secondary 
school. 
In times where education systems all around the world face tremendous challenges, and students’ competencies are stagnating (e. 
g., Betth¨auser et al., 2023), knowledge about predictors of ICT literacy development can be of particular use for the deliberate 
development and implementation of effective intervention programs. As resources for advancing students’ learning are limited, the 
perspective on potential “spillover effects” from domain-specific skills and dispositions that predict ICT development, as applied in this 
study, can be particularly useful for deciding how to choose from among a long list of potential target variables (e.g., scientific literacy) 
when implementing intervention programs that in turn might also go along with positive side effects on ICT development. Further­
more, investigating the differential predictive power of specific predictors across different grades can help better align specific

We first specified a series of multiple regression models with different sets of variables that are related to students’ social, cultural, 
demographic, and learning backgrounds, their subject-specific motivation, and school composition. Second, we specified separate 
models for each of the two stages, consisting of all predictors from the five separate models to better grasp the predictive power of the 
different variables from a joint perspective. 
Fig. 1 summarizes our key findings from the full models (M6), which considered all variables in one step, at both stages, and for all 
the different samples. As expected, ICT literacy at the previous time point (e.g., Grade 6 for Stage 1 and Grade 9 for Stage 2) 
consistently showed the highest association with subsequent ICT literacy. 
Next, when inspecting the Stage 1 results, we particularly found that the learning background variables (i.e., cognitive skills, 
standardized achievement in math and reading, and scientific literacy) were related to Grade 9 ICT literacy (see Fig. 1). In addition to 
these variables, one demographic variable (i.e., age) was negatively related and one motivational variable (i.e., math interest) was 
positively related to future ICT literacy. Notably, the result patterns for Stage 1 remained stable independent of the sample (i.e., N =
6810 or N = 1776) for all variables besides cognitive skills, which were not statistically significantly related to ICT literacy in Grade 9 
for students who remained in school until Grade 12. These results are generally in line with findings from prior studies, such as Hu et al. 
(2018), who used data from the PISA study, or the meta-analysis by Lei and colleagues (Lei et al., 2021). Both studies reported positive 
associations between students’ subject-specific achievements and their ICT literacy. However, we were also able to show that this 
positive association might be stage-dependent and more visible in earlier years than in later years. 
A different picture emerged for Stage 2 compared with Stage 1 (see Fig. 1). Whereas previous ICT literacy was still found to have the 
strongest association with subsequent ICT literacy in Grade 12, this association was substantially larger than in Stage 1 (β = 0.370 vs. β 
= 0.657). In addition, in M6 for Stage 2, only gender, scientific literacy, school mean math achievement, and the school’s mean 
previous ICT achievement were meaningful predictors of Grade 12 ICT literacy. Overall, this finding suggests that for Stage 2 processes, 
skills beyond prior ICT literacy might become less relevant, whereas prior ICT skills become more relevant for predicting later ICT 
literacy. 
In contrast to prior studies, we could not find many associations between ICT literacy development and the social, cultural, or 
demographic background variables after we controlled for the other variables in the models. There were only two exceptions, namely, 
a small negative association between age and ICT literacy development in the Stage 1 model and a negative association between gender 
and ICT literacy development in the Stage 2 model. The latter finding is particularly interesting because, as outlined in the theory 
section, more recent studies have typically found cross-sectional evidence for advantages of girls over boys in ICT literacy (e.g., Fraillon 
et al., 2020b; Punter et al., 2017; Siddiq & Scherer, 2019). When interpreting findings from this study, it is important to recall that the 
data from Stage 2 stemmed from upper secondary school students, whereas the majority of prior studies have typically investigated 
younger students in cross-sectional designs. Thus, most prior studies have investigated gender differences at a specific point in time, 
whereas we investigated disparities in ICT literacy development. Furthermore, our results are in line with results recently published by 
Gnambs (2021), who considered German longitudinal data from a different NEPS starting cohort and found negligible gender dif­
ferences for 15-year-old students but small advantages for boys by the age of 18. We were not able to ultimately answer questions about 
gender differences in ICT, but our findings clearly underscore the need for future studies to more closely investigate the development of 
gender-related disparities in different samples and at different stages in the education system. 
In addition to these results, we also found that the association between Grade 9 ICT literacy and Grade 12 ICT literacy was nearly 
twice as strong as the association between Grade 6 ICT literacy to Grade 9 ICT literacy. This finding suggests that, compared with Stage 
1, prior ICT literacy in Stage 2 had a much stronger influence on ICT skills later on. This trend can also be seen from the results in 
Table 3 and Fig. 1: Whereas several predictors (e.g., mathematics and reading achievement) explained variation in ICT development 
from Grades 6 to 9, they lost their predictive power when predicting ICT development from Grades 9 to 12. Interestingly, this pattern 
occurred in both Stage 1 samples (see Table 3). These findings might suggest that the more advanced ICT skills become, the less 
strongly they can be influenced by other constructs (considered in this study) beyond prior ICT skills. Compared with other skills (e.g., 
reading and math achievement), which are explicitly taught in school, ICT literacy in upper secondary school might be fostered mostly 
by out-of-school interests and activities, and these might already be developed by Grade 9 and thus captured by ICT literacy scores in 
Grade 9. Moreover, this finding might also suggest that minimum levels of reading and mathematics skills are relevant for ICT skills in 
early stages, but that once this threshold has been reached (e.g., in Grade 9), such skills do not explain much variation in future ICT 
literacy (Goldhammer et al., 2013). However, future longitudinal studies on this aspect of ICT development are needed to more 
formally test this assumption. 
Finally, we also found initial evidence for the importance of stage-dependent compositional effects: Whereas in Stage 1, none of the 
compositional variables were statistically significant, school mean math achievement positively predicted and school mean pre-ICT 
literacy negatively predicted ICT development in Stage 2. As outlined above, the latter finding might constitute a ceiling effect: 
Students in schools with a higher than average ICT performance in Grade 9 seem to improve less than students in schools with a lower 
than average prior ICT performance. Findings on effects of the class or school-related achievement composition on the development of 
students’ achievement have been mixed, with some studies suggesting positive effects (Becker et al., 2022; St¨abler et al., 2017) but 
others suggesting zero or negative effects (Dicke et al., 2018; Televantou et al., 2021). Notably, most of these studies have considered 
math or language achievement, and we are not aware of any studies that have considered ICT literacy in this context. Thus, it remains 
unclear to what extent these prior findings, discussions, and explanations about the existence or nonexistence of peer spillover effects 
for school subjects should also be considered in the context of ICT. Future research will be needed to more thoroughly theorize about

and test the impact of student composition on ICT development. 
6.1. Limitations 
To stimulate further research, we now point out specific limitations of the present study. First, as in most prior studies, we were not 
able to consider all possible variables as predictors in the current study. For instance, some prior studies highlighted the relevance of 
considering ICT-related self-concept or self-efficacy as relevant predictors of ICT skills (e.g., Hatlevik, Guðmundsd´ottir, & Loi, 2015; 
Senkbeil, 2022). In addition, factors related to the relevance of digital media in instructional processes, digital school leadership, and 
the detailed purposes and contexts of ICT use could be important variables to consider. Unfortunately, these variables were not 
assessed in the NEPS Cohort 3 study. 
Notably, although some variables were not available, we benefitted from the availability of a rich set of relevant predictor vari­
ables, most importantly, a baseline measure of ICT literacy, which should be able to account for much unexplained variation in the 
later measurement of ICT skills due to missing predictors or relevant variables related to students’ learning backgrounds (e.g., 
achievement in literacy or mathematics). This assumption was also supported by the large amount of variance explained by the full 
model (i.e., 67%), particularly in Stage 1. Other studies on longitudinal ICT literacy development resulted in slightly smaller per­
centages (e.g., Senkbeil, 2022). 
Another limitation that should always be considered in large-scale designs with long-range longitudinal data from the beginning of 
lower secondary school (Grade 5) to upper secondary school (Grade 12) is how long ago the data were collected. In our case, students 
were assessed between 2010/11 (Grade 5) and 2017/18 (Grade 12). This implies that whereas the Grade-12 data were only about 5 
years old, the first assessment (in Grade 5) had been collected about 12 years ago and might not exactly reflect the current state of the 
ICT skills of students from today. As one example, results from the ICIL study suggest substantial increases in teachers’ digital media 
use in Germany between 2013 and 2018 (Drossel et al., 2019). Furthermore, at least small improvements were documented regarding 
school IT equipment (Eickelmann, Gerick et al., 2019). Although no statistically significant changes in computer and information 
literacy were found for German students between 2013 and 2018 (Eickelmann, Bos et al., 2019), it seems reasonable to believe that the 
skills of students from 2010/11 might differ from those of today. Especially during the COVID-19 pandemic, students were forced to 
extensively engage with digital media during distance learning, which might also have affected their ICT literacy, most likely unevenly 
(e.g., Betth¨auser et al., 2023; Goudeau et al., 2021). However, if and how these changes in digital media use might have led to changes 
in the predictive power of specific variables remains an open question that needs to be investigated in future studies with more recent 
data. 
6.2. Potential Implications for Policy and Practice 
Keeping in mind the limitations of our study (see the Limitations section), several implications of our findings might be derived for 
policy and practice. First, it seems important to acknowledge that the strongest predictor of ICT literacy in subsequent grades was prior 
ICT literacy. Thus, in the face of these findings, one might ask how schools can positively affect students’ ICT literacy. Our findings 
provide at least two potential answers in this regard: (a) Improving students’ domain-specific learning backgrounds can positively 
affect students’ ICT literacy and (b) schools should probably do this earlier rather than later in secondary school. Evidence for these 
answers can be found in Fig. 1. As can be seen, variables related to students’ learning backgrounds (especially scientific literacy) were 
especially likely to be statistically significantly related to students’ future ICT literacy. Furthermore, whereas these predictors were 
found to be relevant when predicting Grade 9 ICT literacy, in both the reduced and full samples, almost all of them were not related to 
the development of ICT literacy between Grades 9 and 12. This finding suggests that the relevance of the learning background changes 
over the course of secondary school. There are at least two aspects specific to the German school system that might influence these 
findings. First, students in Germany are typically tracked into academic and nonacademic schools, and students in Grade 12 thus 
constitute a specific subset of students who, on average, perform higher than average students from lower secondary school. Regarding 
ICT literacy development, we found some descriptive differences between students in academic tracks and those from nonacademic 
tracks. Investigating how tracking might influence the development of ICT skills will be an important task for future research. Second, 
ICT is not taught as a subject in school in Germany, and it is mostly up to the teachers how and when they require students to make use 
of their ICT skills during teaching. As ICT literacy is not necessarily systematically fostered in school, extracurricular activities likely 
have a huge impact on students’ individual ICT literacy, which may explain why prior ICT skills become increasingly important as 
students progress through secondary school. This trend, along with the finding that supports the relevance of scientific literacy for 
predicting Grade 12 ICTliteracy, might result from the specific interest profiles of students in STEM. Enhancing interest in STEM 
subjects and providing students with adequate IT equipment in school (e.g., Eickelmann, Gerick, et al., 2019) might constitute ways to 
enhance students’ ICT literacy skills throughout lower and upper secondary school. The influence of these measures should therefore 
be more thoroughly investigated in future studies. 
7. Conclusions 
High ICT literacy is key for actively and independently engaging in current globalized and increasingly digitalized societies. Until 
now, few studies have investigated the developmental processes of ICT literacy or identified domain-general and domain-specific 
predictors thereof from a joint perspective. In these times where education systems all around the world face tremendous chal­
lenges, and students’ competencies are stagnating, information on predictors of ICT literacy development can be particularly useful for

the deliberate development and implementation of effective intervention programs. Particularly knowledge about “spillover effects” 
from skills and dispositions that are inherently affected/fostered in school can be useful for choosing from a long list of potential target 
variables when implementing specific intervention programs that in turn might also have positive side effects on ICT development. 
From a global perspective, we found that particularly variables related to students’ learning backgrounds seem to positively influence 
ICT literacy development. From a policy perspective, this finding suggests that the implementation of measures that promote, for 
example, students’ scientific literacy or their performance in reading or mathematics could also positively influence the development 
of ICT literacy. In addition, our results imply that intervening at an early stage in the education system might be more beneficial than 
intervening later on because variables related to students’ learning backgrounds seem to be particularly influential between Grades 6 
and 9 but lose much of their predictive power between Grades 9 and 12.
