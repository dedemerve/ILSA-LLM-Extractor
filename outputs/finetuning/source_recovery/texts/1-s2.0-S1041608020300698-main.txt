A B S T R A C T
Control-value theory (CVT) explains how achievement emotions arise. CVT assumes control and value appraisals
and their interaction are proximal antecedents of achievement emotions, and the structure of relationships
between these constructs are independent of culture and gender. Using the Programme for International Student
Assessment (PISA) 2006 data collected from nationally representative samples of 15-year-olds from 57 countries,
this study attempted to test these assumptions for enjoyment of science by employing structural equation models
with latent interactions. The results showed that control (science self-concept), personal value of science, and
utility value (instrumental motivation to learn science) had large positive effects on enjoyment of science. The
interactions were enhancing and statistically significant but effect sizes were very small. There were differences
among countries with respect to the relative importance of control and value appraisals on enjoyment. The
results showed that structural relationships can be considered equivalent across 57 countries and both genders.
1. Introduction
Enjoyment of science learning as an achievement emotion has a
central role in predicting and mediating different constructs relevant to
learning. Enjoyment of science learning is shown to predict engagement
in science learning tasks (Hampden-Thompson & Bennett, 2013), self-
control (Galla, Amemiya, & Wang, 2018), participation in extra-
curricular science activities (Lin, Lawrenz, Lin, & Hong, 2012;
Nagengast et al., 2011), interest in science (Ainley & Ainley, 2011a,
2011b; Ainley & Hidi, 2014), science-related career aspirations and
academic achievement (Jeffries, Curtis, & Conner, 2020), and colla-
borative problem solving (Camacho-Morles, Slemp, Oades, Morrish, &
Scoular, 2019). Hence, an understanding of how enjoyment of science
learning as an achievement emotion originates may help to deepen our
understanding of students' learning experiences and processes. More-
over, a knowledge of the nature of the antecedents of enjoyment of
science is highly relevant to the planning of interventions that foster
enjoyment and promote learning.
Control-value theory (CVT) of achievement emotions (Pekrun,
2006b; Pekrun, Frenzel, Goetz, & Perry, 2007) is an educational psy-
chology theory that explains how achievement emotions arise. CVT
posits that achievement emotions occur as a consequence of control
appraisals, value appraisals, and the interaction of these appraisals.

Moreover, most of the research had been conducted in Western settings

control and value appraisals would be universal (independent of gender
and culture) (Pekrun & Stephens, 2010), very few studies have explored
such generalizations. The purpose of this study is to address these
limitations in the existing research by examining how control and value
appraisals and their interaction predict enjoyment, one of the most
intense and frequent achievement emotions experienced by students.
This study also tests whether this prediction can be generalized in terms
of gender and across 57 countries, in the domain of science learning for
15-year-old students, using secondary analyses of data from the Pro-
gramme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2006 (Organiza-
tion for Economic Co-operation and Development, OECD, 2006).
1.1. Control-value theory of achievement emotions
Achievement emotions are directly related to achievement activities
or outcomes (Pekrun, 2006b). Achievement refers to the quality of
activities or their outcomes, referred to as object focus dimension, and
can be evaluated with respect to specified standards of excellence
(Paoloni, 2014). In addition to object focus dimension (activity versus
outcome), CVT organizes achievement emotions into their valence di-

(activating versus deactivating). Furthermore, outcome emotions can
be prospective or retrospective. For example, an achievement activity
might involve conducting an experiment in a science class and the as-
sociated activity-based achievement emotion experienced can involve
enjoyment of the science class. An achievement outcome can be the
result of a science exam. The associated prospective outcome emotion
can involve anticipatory joy if the student expects to obtain a good
grade before the exam. The retrospective outcome emotion associated
with the science exam can be retrospective joy about having experi-
enced success in the exam after the student learns that she has received
a good grade in the exam. In CVT, enjoyment is represented as an ac-
tivity-focused, positive, activating emotion and involves experiencing
pleasure in an activity. Enjoyment is distinct from the prospective
emotion of anticipatory joy of expecting success or from the retro-
spective emotion of joy about having experienced success.
Achievement emotions are regarded as either state or trait-like
constructs depending on the context (Bieg, Goetz, & Hubbard, 2013).
For example, enjoyment during a science activity can be taken as a state
like construct, whereas enjoying science lessons in general, can be re-
garded as a trait-like construct.
CVT posits that an individual's cognitive appraisals of control and
value are the most proximal determinants of achievement emotions.
When students regulate their control and value appraisals they can alter
the achievement emotions they experience (Buff, 2014). Control ap-
praisals pertain to the degree that an achievement activity and its
outcomes are perceived to be controllable, and are usually indicated by
competence perceptions such as self-concept of ability.
Value appraisals can be intrinsic or extrinsic (Pekrun, 2006b). In-
trinsic value refers to evaluating an activity or outcome as interesting
and important in its own right. Extrinsic values can come in different
forms including achievement value, the personal importance of getting
good grades and utility value, evaluating an activity or outcome as
instrumental in acquiring a desired outcome (Putwain et al., 2018).
Moreover, extrinsic values may involve the relevance of a domain to a
person's everyday life (Goetz et al., 2014). In PISA 2006, “the value of
scientific knowledge and inquiry for the students' current and future
everyday life” (Pekrun, 2006a, p.21) is conceptualized as the personal
value of science. Another subtle issue is the distinction between the
value of a domain in general versus the value of studying the domain in
school (Tytler, 2014). In PISA 2006, utility value items are worded with
respect to studying school science, whereas personal value of science
items are worded with respect to science in general. Previous research
showed that control appraisals usually have large effects and intrinsic
and utility value appraisals have moderate to large effects on enjoyment
(Goetz, Frenzel, Stoeger, & Hall, 2010; Putwain, Pekrun, et al., 2018).
According to CVT, as a social cognitive theory, more distal factors
(environmental antecedents) are significant. These factors include the
characteristics and the cognitive requirements of the task, the degree of
cognitive and emotional support the teacher provides, the overall
learning atmosphere, and the broader social and cultural context within
the school. These factors are posited to affect achievement emotions,
predominantly through their influence on control and value appraisals.
Achievement emotions in turn influence motivation, engagement,
learning, and performance outcomes. Therefore, these relationships are
reciprocal; environmental antecedents, cognitive appraisals, emotions,
and their consequences all influence each other (Pekrun et al., 2007).
For example, not only a student's self-concept of ability influences his or
her achievement emotions, but also his or her emotions will shape his
or her subsequent self-concept of ability beliefs.
CVT acknowledges the context-dependent nature of achievement
emotions and posits that they are organized in domain specific ways.
Contemporary approaches to emotions conceptualize them in relation
to specific contexts (Griffiths & Scarantino, 2009; Gross, 2014) and
control and value appraisals such as perceptions of one's skill,
achievement expectations, and interest have been shown to be domain
specific (Bong, 2001; Marsh, 1986). Therefore, emotions that are linked
to control and value appraisals are also considered to be domain spe-
cific (Brunner et al., 2010; Goetz et al., 2014; Goetz, Frenzel, Pekrun, &
Hall, 2006; Gogol, Brunner, Preckel, Goetz, & Martin, 2016). For in-
stance, one can have different control and value appraisals in mathe-
matics or in science.
According to CVT, control and value appraisals interact when pro-
ducing an achievement emotion (Pekrun et al., 2007). In other words,
the relationship between achievement emotions and control and value
appraisals are both additive and multiplicative (Pekrun & Perry, 2014).
Among the very few studies which explored this interaction, Goetz,
Frenzel, et al. (2010) reported that for a sample of 50 undergraduates
high scores of value amplified positive relationships between control
and enjoyment. Similarly, using a sample of 579 fifth grade students,
Putwain, Pekrun, et al. (2018) found neither an interaction effect be-
tween control and intrinsic value nor between control and utility value
for enjoyment but detected a significant interaction for control and
achievement value.
In CVT, the general structural features and causal mechanisms of
emotions are assumed to be universal; they are regarded as inherent
properties of the human mind (Pekrun, 2006b, 2009). Emotions depend
on control and value appraisals independent of gender or culture. On
the other hand, the content of the emotions, their frequency, and in-
tensity may vary between genders or cultures. Similarly, depending on
the extent that perceived control and values differ between genders or
cultures, the resulting emotional experiences can differ as well. Hence,
CVT again acknowledges context dependency and predicts that in dif-
ferent genders and cultural contexts, the structure of the relationships
between control appraisals, value appraisals, and emotions are ex-
pected to be equivalent, whereas the intensity of each may be different.
Regarding gender, previous research found differences in the mean
levels of enjoyment of mathematics favoring boys as well as perceived
control and value (Frenzel, Pekrun, & Goetz, 2007; Pekrun, Frenzel,
Goetz, & Perry, 2007; Goetz, Frenzel, Hall, & Pekrun, 2008; Holm,
Hannula, & Björn, 2017). Pekrun, Goetz, Frenzel, Barchfeld, and Perry
(2011) found that for psychology classes females reported higher levels
of class-related enjoyment. Frenzel, Pekrun et al. (2007) detected minor
discrepancies between the genders in structural relationships between
the enjoyment of mathematics and prior achievement, control apprai-
sals, and value appraisals, whereas Goetz et al. (2008) reported that the
structural relations between enjoyment of mathematics and prior
achievement and control appraisals were equivalent across genders, but
they have not measured value appraisals. Similarly, Raccanello, Hall,
and Burro (2018) found that the structural relations between enjoyment
and control and value appraisals were invariant across genders for
mathematics and literacy domains.
With respect to cross-cultural comparisons, Frenzel, Thrash, Pekrun,
and Goetz (2007) found that Chinese students reported a greater en-
joyment of mathematics than German students. However, the re-
searchers did not test for structural equivalence of enjoyment and
control and value appraisals across China and Germany because they
did not measure control and value appraisals. Indeed, a thorough re-
view of the literature did not locate any studies testing cross-cultural
comparisons of the structural relations between control and value ap-
praisals and enjoyment.
For many countries, by the age of 15 most students are approaching
the end of their compulsory education (OECD, 2009). It makes students
at this age an optimal population to study in respect of the cumulative
effects of compulsory schooling on achievement emotions. By the age of
15 adolescents develop the cognitive abilities necessary to distinguish
all types of achievement emotions and master the related appraisals of
competence and value (Pekrun, 2017; Pekrun & Stephens, 2012).
Compared to earlier ages, they can spontaneously report more emotions
and their antecedents (Raccanello et al., 2018). They have spent many
years in schools, receiving, interpreting, and incorporating feedback
into their academic self-concept. Students who are successful in school
incorporate their academic self-concept into their global self-concept by

1.2. Aims and hypotheses
The aim of the present study was both to examine the role of per-
ceived control and value in the domain of science as proximal ante-
cedents of enjoyment of learning science in 15-year-old students and to
test the generalizability of this role across countries and genders. The
research evidence on the cross-cultural generalizability of the links
between control and value appraisals and achievement emotions is
particularly scarce in the domain of science; hence, this study uses the
most extensive database available to investigate this problem.
In this study, enjoyment was prioritized over the study of other
achievement emotions for several reasons. First of all, the correlations
between self-concept and enjoyment in comparison with self-concept
and outcome based emotions such as pride are of a similar magnitude
for science-related domains. For example, Goetz et al. (2014) reported
that for physics the correlation between self-concept and enjoyment
was 0.79 versus between self-concept and pride 0.82 for students in
grade 11, which were similar to the correlations reported by Goetz,
Cronjaeger, et al. (2010). Second, because enjoyment is an activity
based emotion it is more readily related to learning tasks than pride, an
outcome based emotion. Enjoyment of learning is related positively
with flow experiences (total immersion in the task) and negatively with
task irrelevant thinking (Pekrun, Goetz, Titz, & Perry, 2002). Enjoy-
ment of learning can also help positively appraise the value of tasks and
the person's beliefs of competence to solve them, leading to increased
interest and motivation (Pekrun, 2017). Third, enjoyment is one of the
most intense and frequent achievement emotions experienced by stu-
dents (Putwain, Becker, Symes, & Pekrun, 2018) and the most often
cited emotion by high school students (Raccanello et al., 2018). Finally,
PISA 2006 only provides data on enjoyment but not pride.
Based on CVT the following research questions and associated hy-
potheses were investigated.
Research question 1: Do control and value appraisals and their en-
hancing interaction predict enjoyment of science?
Hypothesis 1. Perceived control and value appraisals of science and
their synergistic (enhancing) interaction positively predict enjoyment
of science. In the latent interaction structural equation model the path
coefficients of control, value, and control value interactions will be
positive.
Research question 2: How similar are the structural relations be-
tween control and value appraisals and enjoyment among different
countries? Is the prediction in Hypothesis 1 generalizable over 57
countries?
Hypothesis 2. The relationship between perceived control, value
appraisals, their enhancing interactions and enjoyment of science are
structurally equivalent across countries.
Research question 3: How similar are the structural relations be-
tween control and value appraisals and enjoyment between genders? Is
the prediction in Hypothesis 1 generalizable over genders?
Hypothesis 3a. The relationship between perceived control, value
appraisals, their enhancing interactions and enjoyment of science are
structurally equivalent across genders when the entire international
sample is considered.
Hypothesis 3b. The relationship between perceived control, value
appraisals, their enhancing interactions and enjoyment of science are
structurally equivalent across 114 gender by country groups.
2. Method
PISA is a continuing project commissioned by the Organization for
Economic Co-Operation and Development (OECD). Every three years
since 2000, PISA has collected cross-sectional data on achievement,
attitudes, and policy relevant background variables from nationally
representative samples of 15-year-old students. In order to test the
hypotheses of the present study, PISA 2006 data were used because
relevant constructs, such as science self-concept and personal value of
science, were measured in this cycle (OECD, 2009) and not in the more
recent cycle PISA 2015 (OECD, 2017).
2.1. Participants
In 2006, PISA focused on science literacy, aiming to assess the ex-
tent to which students can use their science knowledge to solve pro-
blems they might encounter in real life. PISA 2006 collected data from
398,750 students (50.5% female, 49.5% male) from 57 countries (30
OECD countries and 27 partner countries). A complex two-stage sam-
pling strategy was employed to ensure the representativeness of the
national
populations
of
15-year-old
students
in
each
country.
Participation in PISA was voluntary and required implicit or explicit
consent from the parents of the students (Jeffries et al., 2020). For the
present study, an institutional review board approval was not sought as
no data other than the pre-existing public PISA 2006 dataset was used.
2.2. Procedure and measures
The PISA 2006 consisted of a literacy assessment of science,
mathematics, and reading part and a context questionnaire part. The
entire item pool of the literacy assessment consisted of 108 items for
science (210 min of testing time), 48 items for mathematics (120 min of
testing time), and 31 items for reading (60 min of testing time).
Students did not answer the entire item pool. A rotated block design
was used to ensure comprehensive coverage. The items were distributed
to 13 item clusters, four of which were combined to form 13 booklets.
Each student was randomly given one of these 13 booklets. The context
questionnaire included items about students' family background as well
as several attitudinal measures including enjoyment of science, science
self-concept, personal value of science, and instrumental motivation to
learn science. The participating students completed the literacy as-
sessment and the context questionnaire in their schools. The literacy
assessment was of two hours' duration followed by the context ques-
tionnaire which took about 30 min to complete. The students finished
the entire assessment in a single session, but they were allowed to take a
short break after one hour (OECD, 2009).
The conceptual framework for the context questionnaire of PISA
2006 mapped many constructs from the literature and operationalized
them by adapting items from existing instruments or writing new items.
However, no further details about which items were adapted and which
items were written for the individual constructs were provided in the
PISA 2006 technical report (OECD, 2009).
The context questionnaire items were developed in three stages. In
the first stage, a pre-pilot study with a small convenience sample in
Australia was conducted. At this stage, students answered the items
while verbalizing their thoughts, generating data about how well the
items were understood and how appropriate they were; then the items
were refined. In the second stage, pilot studies using small convenience
samples were conducted in Japan, Germany, Canada and Australia in
four languages Japanese, German, French, and English respectively. At
this stage, group interviews with students were conducted to detect
issues with relevance and ambiguity. Then the items were further

in charge of translation and adaptation were trained.
Although it is highly unusual for such a huge assessment project not
to seek approval of ethics committees, a reference for such an ethical
review could be found neither in the PISA 2006 technical report (OECD,
2009) nor in the results of a thorough search of the OECD website.
Considering the domain specific nature of control and value ap-
praisals, this study focused on science as the domain and as such of the
measures were specific to science learning. The measures of the control,
value, achievement emotion constructs were selected from the student
context questionnaire administered as part of PISA 2006. All scales
included positively worded items using 4-point Likert-type scales ran-
ging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree”. For the analyses, the
variables were reverse coded so that higher numeric values indicated
higher values on the corresponding underlying construct.
2.2.1. Perceived control
Five out of six items from the science self-concept scale were used to
operationalize students' control over science learning:
PC1. When I am being taught school science, I can understand the
concepts very well.
PC2. I learn school science topics quickly.
PC3. I can easily understand new ideas in school science.
PC4. Learning advanced school science topics would be easy for me.
PC5. School science topics are easy for me.
The item “I can usually give good answers to test questions on
school science topics” was excluded because this item was judged to
measure self-efficacy rather than self-concept. Self-efficacy is measured
at a task specific level and its central element is perceived confidence,
whereas self-concept is measured at a more general level and its central
element is perceived competence (Bong & Skaalvik, 2003; Ferla, Valcke,
& Cai, 2009). The median reliability was calculated α = 0.91 for the
OECD countries and α = 0.85 for the partner countries. The science
self-concept scale seems to be largely based on Self-Description Ques-
tionnaire (SDQ) developed by Marsh, Ellis, Parada, Richards, and
Heubeck (2005).
2.2.2. Personal value of science
The personal value of science scale, with median reliability
α = 0.80 for the OECD countries and α = 0.76 for the partner countries
was used to operationalize the extent students' valued science in gen-
eral, but not necessarily school science, as relevant and useful to their
current and future everyday life. The scale consisted of the following
five items:
PVS1. I find that science helps me to understand things around me.
PVS2. I will use science in many ways when I am an adult.
PVS3. Some concepts in science help me see how I relate to other
people.
PVS4. When I leave school there will be many opportunities for me
to use science.
PVS5. Science is very relevant to me.
2.2.3. Utility value
The instrumental motivation to learn science scale, with median
reliability α = 0.92 for the OECD countries and α = 0.88 for the
partner countries was used to operationalize the extent students' valued
learning school science as instrumental to reach their goals. The scale
consisted of the following five items:
UV1. Making an effort in my < school science > subject(s) is worth
it because this will help me in the work I want to do later on.
UV2. What I learn in my < school science > subject(s) is important
for me because I need this for what I want to study later on.
UV3. I study < school science > because I know it is useful for me.
UV4. Studying my < school science > subject(s) is worthwhile for
me because what I learn will improve my career prospects.
UV5. I will learn many things in my < school science > subject(s)
that will help me get a job.
2.2.4. Enjoyment
Achievement emotions are considered as multicomponent complex
constructs and are usually measured using the Achievement Emotions
Questionnaire (AEQ) (Pekrun, Goetz, & Perry, 2005). AEQ measures
achievement emotions related to class, learning, and test contexts
considering the emotions experienced before, during, and after each of
these contexts. These different contexts elicit different levels of enjoy-
ment from students (Raccanello et al., 2013). However, in PISA 2006,
students' level of enjoyment relating to a set of activities involved in
learning science in general was measured, which is not inline with the
AEQ. Nevertheless, it can be argued that enjoyment of science in gen-
eral can be captured to a certain extent using the items in enjoyment of
science scale. Moreover, most of these items seem to be adapted from
AEQ (Pekrun et al., 2011). Four out of five items from the enjoyment of
science scale were used to operationalize enjoyment as an achievement
emotion:
ENJ1. I enjoy acquiring new knowledge in science.
ENJ2. I generally have fun when I am learning science topics.
ENJ3. I like reading about science.
ENJ4. I am happy doing science problems.
The item “I am interested in learning about science” was excluded
because this item was judged to capture intrinsic value rather than
enjoyment. The median reliability was calculated α = 0.895 for the
OECD countries and α = 0.83 for the partner countries.
2.3. Overlapping data and methods
The data and methods used in this study are similar to those used in
the study by Nagengast et al. (2011). They used PISA 2006 data to test
for the generalizability of expectation × value interaction for ex-
pectation-value theory. They operationalized enjoyment as value and
self-concept as expectation to predict participation in science activities
and career aspirations. The current study used self-concept as perceived
control, instrumental motivation as utility value and enjoyment as
achievement emotion. Hence, the theories tested are different as well as
the constructs used. Therefore, although the data and methods are
overlapping, the results and conclusions are completely different.

2.4. Data analysis
2.4.1. Data management
Before the analyses, as a means to handling missing data in the
responses to the questionnaire items, multiple imputation (Graham,
2009) was deployed. Five imputed data sets were created using the R
package Amelia II (Honaker, King, & Blackwell, 2011).
PISA 2006 was based on two-stage stratified sampling design and
hence provides both a student and a school weight. Following re-
commendations of Rutkowski, Gonzalez, Joncas, and von Davier
(2010), in all analyses, the standardized student weight was used to
obtain unbiased estimates of population parameters, and the complex
design correction was deployed to adjust standard errors of parameter
estimates for the nesting of students within schools. The analyses were
conducted using Mplus 7.3 (Muthén & Muthén, 2007) using the robust
maximum likelihood estimator (Satorra & Bentler, 1994) in which
standard errors and fit statistics are corrected for non-normality of the
indicators.
2.4.2. Preliminary analyses
First, a preliminary confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was con-
ducted with a measurement model including enjoyment, perceived
control, personal value of science and utility value. This model showed
a good fit (χ2 (146, N = 398,750) = 8304.126, CFI = 0.983,
TLI = 0.981, RMSEA = 0.012, SRMR = 0.02) and was used to estimate
latent bivariate correlations. As shown in Table 1, all latent variables
showed substantive correlations.
In order to test the effect of perceived control, personal value of
science, and utility value and the interaction between control and value
on enjoyment of science learning, the unconstrained approach to latent
interactions was used (Marsh, Hau, & Wen, 2004). The product in-
dicator pairs between predictor variables were constructed using mat-
ched pair-strategy using the results of the preliminary CFA. The factor
scores of indicators for each construct were ordered from largest to
smallest, then pairs at the same rank were used to form product in-
dicators.
2.4.3. Analysis steps
To test the hypotheses, the predictions were transformed into two
structural equation models with latent interactions (SEM-LI) that al-
lowed an accounting for measurement error. One of the models in-
cluded personal value of science (Model 1) and the other included
utility value (Model 2). The only restriction in these models was that
the mean of the latent product-variable (interaction) is fixed to the
covariance between the latent first-order variables (perceived control
and value). These two value appraisal variables were not merged into a
single model for two reasons. First, personal value of science and utility
value are strongly correlated, hence multicollinearity issues that may
arise can be handled by separating these variables into two models.
Second, utility value had been widely used in previous CVT research
whereas the personal value of science had not. Hence, one can use
Model 2 to interpret and compare the results with previous research
that included utility value. On the other hand, the results of Model 1
can be used to evaluate whether personal value of science can be
considered as a value appraisal that can be included in CVT.
A series of steps were then followed. First, a total-group analysis was
conducted in which the two SEM-LI models were fitted to the entire
international sample. All the indicators were standardized for the entire
sample in each imputed data set, but the product indicators were not
double mean-centered because Mplus automatically includes mean
structure (Marsh, Wen, Hau, & Nagengast, 2013). Following the re-
commendations of Hu and Bentler (1999) and Marsh et al. (2004) the
goodness of fit for the model was assessed using the comparative fit
index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), the root mean square error of
approximation (RMSEA), and the Standardized Root Mean Square Re-
sidual (SRMR) as well as the χ2 test statistic and an evaluation of
parameter estimates. The CFI and TLI ranges are between 0 and 1
continuum and values > 0.90 is taken to reflect an acceptable fit and
0.95 is taken to reflect an excellent fit to the data. SRMR values below
0.09 are regarded as indicating good fit, whereas RMSEA values
of < 0.06 are taken to reflect a reasonable fit, compared to RMSEA
values > 0.10 are considered unacceptable.
Second, multigroup country analyses were conducted. At this stage,
all the indicators were centered but not restandardized around country
means (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2019b; Nagengast et al., 2011). In order
to compare the CVT effects across the 57 countries, configural and
metric measurement invariance tests were employed. Configural in-
variance model required that the same pattern of factor loadings was
observed among the countries, but no further constraints were placed
on the parameters of the model. Metric (factorial) invariance model,
required the factor loadings to be invariant across the countries. Then
the fits of the configural and factorial invariance models were com-
pared. A small reduction in the fit of the latter model would be a strong
indication of comparability of the structural relationships across
countries. In order to compare the nested models using the differences
in fit indices, following the recommendations of Chen (2007) and
Cheung and Rensvold (2002), changes in CFI and TLI < 0.01, changes
in RMSEA < 0.015 and changes in SRMR < 0.03 were treated as sup-
port for the more constrained model. After factorial measurement in-
variance was established, the multigroup SEM-LI models (Model 1 and
Model 2) were then fitted simultaneously to each country, providing the
regression coefficients for the latent variables for each country. Then to
test whether the structural effects varied across the countries, SEM-LI
constrained models in which the regression coefficients in the structural
models were constrained to be equal across countries were simulta-
neously fitted to each country. Finally, the fit of the SEM-LI constrained
models were compared to the fit of the SEM-LI models (Goetz et al.,
2008; Nagengast et al., 2011).
Third, multigroup gender analyses including the entire international
sample were conducted. At this step, all the indicators were centered
but not restandardized around gender means. In order to compare the
CVT effects across genders, configural and metric measurement in-
variance tests were conducted for the entire international sample using
a two-group model (female and male). After establishing metric in-
variance, SEM-LI models (Model 1 and Model 2) were fitted simulta-
neously to both genders. In order to test whether the structural effects
varied across the genders in the entire sample, the regression coeffi-
cients in the structural models were constrained to be equal across
genders (SEM-LI constrained models). Then the fits of the SEM-LI
constrained models were compared to the fit of the SEM-LI models.
Finally, multigroup gender by country analyses were conducted. At
this stage, all the indicators were centered but not restandardized
around each gender by country mean. In order to compare the CVT
effects across genders and 57 countries, again, configural and metric
measurement invariance tests were conducted, using 114 groups
(2 × 57). After establishing metric invariance, SEM-LI models (Model 1
and Model 2) were fitted simultaneously to all 114 groups. Then to test
whether the structural effects varied across the gender by country, the
regression coefficients in the structural models were constrained to be
equal across gender by country groups (SEM-LI constrained models).

4. Discussion
The purpose of this study was to investigate whether perceived
control and value appraisals predict enjoyment of learning science in
15-year-old students, and to test the generalizability of this prediction
across countries and genders. In line with previous research (Frenzel,
Pekrun, & Goetz, 2007; Goetz, Frenzel, et al., 2010; Goetz, Pekrun, Hall,
& Haag, 2006; Putwain, Pekrun, et al., 2018), the results revealed that
perceived control and personal value of science, as well as perceived
control and utility value, predicted enjoyment of science with large
effect sizes, providing support for the CVT predictions.
In this study the personal value of science was conceptualized as an
extrinsic value appraisal referring to the relevance and utility of science
in general, but not necessarily school science, to a person's current and
future life. According to Tytler (2014), there is a distinction between
students' affect towards science in general versus school science. This
distinction is reflected in the wording of the items in the PISA 2006
scales. Enjoyment and the personal value of science items use the term
science, whereas utility value and perceived control scales use the term
school science. This distinction may lead to different interpretations
given that a student may find science relevant for her life whereas she
may consider school science as inconsequential. When school science is
substituted for science in the wording of the items in personal value of
science scale, the resulting items can be subsumed under utility value of
studying science. Personal value of a domain, not the value of studying
the domain in school, has not been included in previous research based
on CVT, which was one of the reasons it was investigated in a separate
model. The results showed that the model with the personal value of
science fitted the data well and that personal value of science was a
relatively more important predictor of enjoyment of science than per-
ceived control. Hence, as a means to enabling finer distinctions among
value appraisals the personal value of a domain can be included in CVT.
When the structural relationships were allowed to vary freely
among 57 countries, in general, the main effects of perceived control,
personal value of science and utility value on enjoyment were sub-
stantial for almost all of the countries. With the exception of Chinese
Taipei, Korea, and Poland (for both genders), the personal value of
science appeared to be more important than perceived control for en-
joyment of science. These outlier countries are important because they
show that there are exceptions to the general structure in which per-
sonal value of science is relatively more important than perceived
control. PISA 2015 scores in science, mathematics, and reading were
above the average for all three countries, meaning that these educa-
tional systems outperformed many others when literacy outcomes are
concerned (OECD, 2016). However, at least for Chinese Taipei and
Korea, students experience intense pressure originating with high
stakes high school and university entrance exams (Shin et al., 2018).
Students often have to attend cram schools in order to prepare for these
exams where ability grouping is the norm (Liu & Yang, 2016). Such
conditions may inevitably lead students to constantly compare their
competence with respect to others, and not with respect to the mastery
of content and skills. Hence, normative evaluation coupled with ability
grouping may be leading to the big-fish-little-pond effect on self-con-
cept (Marsh, 1987), where students' self-concepts are reduced when
they are placed in high ability classrooms. The increased regression
coefficient of self-concept on enjoyment may be compensating for this
big-fish-little-pond effect in respect of these countries. Still, why the
effect of self-concept on enjoyment is more important than the effect of
personal value of science for these countries is a question for further
research.
With regard to the relative importance of perceived control versus
utility value, for almost half of the countries utility value was more
important than perceived control. Israel was different from all of the
other countries with respect to the effect of utility value on enjoyment
of science, the effect was moderate and negative for both genders. This
means, on average in Israel, the more students valued science learning
as instrumental to reach their goals, the less they enjoyed it. This dif-
ference was more pronounced for females. In addition, Israel had the
largest path coefficient for perceived control, when combined with the
negative effect of utility value perceived control became even more
important for enjoyment of science. The reasons underlying this puz-
zling result probably are cultural and require further investigation.
Moreover, the effect of utility value was relatively more important than
that of perceived control on enjoyment of science for both genders for
13 countries, which were geographically located in Eastern Europe, the
Middle East, and Asia and were all developing economies. One ex-
planation of this finding might be that in these developing economies,
perhaps, students and their families see education as the only means for
upwards socioeconomic mobility and emphasize utility value. This
finding is significant in exemplifying that the salience of control and
value appraisals on achievement emotions may differ with respect to

The predicted interactions between perceived control and the per-
sonal value of science, and between perceived control and utility value
were statistically significant but were also too small to be considered as
meaningful, in line with the results of Putwain, Pekrun, et al. (2018).
Nagengast et al. (2011) stated that interaction effects in educational
psychology are often weak, which might be the case for CVT. However,
a moderate effect size was detected for Liechtenstein in respect of
perceived control × utility value. When country-level analyses are
considered, either interaction was significant for only about half of the
countries. Both interactions were simultaneously significant for about a
third of the countries, and for both female and male groups for only five
Western countries (Australia, Germany, Spain, Sweden, and the United
States). The significance of both interactions appeared to be sensitive to
gender at country level. Only Finland's female group had a negative
perceived control × personal value of science interaction. These results
imply that interactions in CVT are rather weak and may not be observed
with small sample sizes and with samples from some countries.
The effects of perceived control, the personal value of science, and
utility value on enjoyment of science were larger, though not sub-
stantial, for females than males in the multigroup analyses conducted
using the entire international sample. But the results of the country by
gender analyses showed that none of the countries exactly matched this
pattern when the interactions were included and almost half of the
countries deviated from this pattern when the interactions were ig-
nored. Such variability implies, gender differences in the structural
relationships among control and value appraisals and achievement
emotions may be more complicated and may need to take the country
into account.
In addition to looking for similarities across countries and genders,
another aim of this study was to test the generalizability of the struc-
tural relationships between perceived control, personal value of sci-
ence, utility value, and enjoyment of science across countries and
genders. To accomplish this task, the unstandardized path coefficients
of the latent predictors in SEM-LI models were constrained to be equal
across groups and the fits of these models were compared with the
models in which the coefficients were allowed to vary freely. The re-
sults were consistent with the claim that the structural relationships
between perceived control, personal value of science, utility value, and
enjoyment of science were invariant across the 57 countries studied.
Furthermore, the results also supported the hypothesis that these
structural relationships were also invariant across genders, congruent
with previous studies (Goetz et al., 2008; Raccanello et al., 2018). These
results provide substantial support for the generalizability of the effects
of perceived control, personal value of science and utility value on
enjoyment of science across countries and genders, corroborating CVT's
universality assumptions. However, it would be premature to claim that
CVT predictions are universal across cultures.
With respect to enjoyment of science, capabilities and character-
istics of 15-year-olds present opportunities and challenges. 15-year-olds
are capable of realistically judging their competence, values, and
achievement emotions. Using these abilities, they may be able to em-
ploy cognitive change strategies for emotion regulation where they
reevaluate their control and value judgments leading to a change in the
intensity of enjoyment they experience (Gross, 2014; Harley, Pekrun,
Taxer, & Gross, 2019). However, emotion regulation can be difficult
because students' ideas about the value of science in general and of
school science have formed; as a group their self-concept and enjoy-
ment have reached a bottom and stabilized; and most of them have
decided whether they identify with and want to study science further.
Among self-concept, the personal value of science, and utility value of
studying science, value reappraisals seem to be easier targets. While
self-concept is a relatively stable construct (Bong & Skaalvik, 2003),
utility value is more malleable and several interventions have already
been devised. Helping students generate their own reasons for the re-
levance and usefulness of science and studying science to their lives has
been shown to increase students' utility value (Gaspard et al., 2015) as
well as intrinsic and achievement values (Hulleman, Godes, Hendricks,
& Harackiewicz, 2010; Hulleman & Harackiewicz, 2009). Similar re-
sults were obtained with students from Korea (Shin et al., 2019). Based
on the results of this study, students from all countries except Israel
would benefit from utility value interventions that seek to increase their
enjoyment of science. It would be very interesting to see the results of
such an intervention for Israel, in which the intervention is expected to
lead to increased utility value but decreased enjoyment because the
effect of utility of value on enjoyment of science is negative. In addition
to utility value interventions, interventions employing praise and
feedback strategies may help increase students' self-concept (O'Mara,
Marsh, Craven, & Debus, 2006). Another way to increase self-concept
may involve designing learning experiences that are challenging yet not
too difficult, so that students eventually succeed (Osborne & Jones,
2011).
4.1. Limitations and directions for future research
Although the results of this study corroborate the CVT predictions
for the links between control and value appraisals and enjoyment of
science, there are several limitations. First, enjoyment of science mea-
sured in PISA 2006 was not aligned with the complex measurement of
achievement emotions. The time (before, during, and after) and context
(class, learning, and test) richness captured in AEQ (Pekrun et al., 2005)
is completely missing in PISA 2006; students were expected to ag-
gregate their many years of prior science learning experiences. Situa-
tional measurement of enjoyment may lead to different results obtained
from retrospective measures. Moreover, in the wording of the items the
term science was used instead of school science and that might have
added to the measurement complications of enjoyment. In addition, one
item from the enjoyment of science scale had to be removed because it
captured interest (intrinsic value). Similarly, one item from the science
self-concept scale had to be removed because it captured self-efficacy.
Moreover, intrinsic value and achievement value, which are considered
at least as important as utility value (Putwain, Pekrun, et al., 2018),
were not measured in PISA 2006.
Second, the differences found between countries and between gen-
ders are not easy to explain. There may be a plethora of reasons for
these changes ranging from culturally-specific characteristics, political
climate, economic development level, and the structure of educational
systems. Unfortunately, PISA does not provide data to generate such
explanations. Another question is whether the structural differences
found can be replicated. Given that PISA did not measure science self-
concept in 2015 it was not possible to check for reproducibility of the
results. If, in the future cycles of PISA all of the constructs relevant for
CVT are measured, then a more comprehensive test of generalizability
may become possible. Still, because of the cross-sectional design gen-
eralizations to other age groups or developmental claims are not war-
ranted.
Finally, the effects of self-concept, the personal value of science and
utility value of studying science on enjoyment of science were found to
be substantial for 15-year-olds. It is possible that these structural rela-
tions have stabilized by this age, however, further research is needed to
verify this assertion.
