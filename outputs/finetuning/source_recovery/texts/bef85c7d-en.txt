Abstract 
This study uses the PIAAC data to examine the relationships between education system 
characteristics (e.g. early tracking and vocational education orientation) and distributions 
of adult numeracy skills. It also investigates the effects of postponing the tracking age and 
easing university access for students on a vocational track on the average skills and 
different percentiles of the skills distribution.  
Correlational analysis suggests that education systems with more students enrolled in 
vocational tracks have on average higher levels of numeracy skills and more compressed 
skills distributions between the 50th and 90th percentiles.  
Further analysis suggests that postponing the tracking age among 14 European countries 
does not have a significant effect on the average skills of the population. However, it 
increases skills for individuals at the 10th, 20th, and 30th percentiles of the skill 
distribution.  
Expanding university access is associated with an increase in numeracy skills, 
particularly for individuals at the bottom three deciles of the distribution.

Résumé 
Cette étude utilise les données du PIAAC pour examiner les relations entre les 
caractéristiques du système d'éducation (par ex. dépistage précoce et orientation 
professionnelle) et la répartition des compétences en numératie des adultes. Il examine 
également les effets que le report du suivi de l'âge et un meilleur accès à l’université pour 
les étudiants en formation professionnelle ont sur les compétences moyennes et sur les 
différents percentiles de la répartition des compétences. 
L'analyse corrélationnelle suggère que les systèmes d'éducation comptant un plus grand 
nombre d'élèves inscrits dans les filières professionnelles ont en moyenne des niveaux 
plus élevés de compétences en calcul et une répartition des compétences plus resserrée 
entre les 50e et 90e percentiles. 
Une analyse plus poussée suggère que le report de l'âge de suivi dans 14 pays européens 
n'a pas d'effet significatif sur les compétences moyennes de la population. Toutefois, il 
permet d'accroître les compétences des personnes qui se situent aux 10e, 20e et 30e 
centiles de la répartition des compétences.  
L'élargissement de l'accès à l'université est associé à une augmentation des compétences 
en numératie, en particulier chez les personnes se situant dans les trois derniers déciles de 
la répartition.

Introduction  
Schooling plays a fundamental role in the accumulation of foundational skills. Policy 
makers, intergovernmental organisations, and researchers look to education and training 
systems to provide students with the foundation skills required to engage in further 
learning, and adapting to changes in future labour demands. Yet recent evidence 
demonstrates a decline in literacy and numeracy proficiency of adults between mid-1990 
and 2012 across a subset of the OECD countries (Paccagnella, 2016). The deterioration of 
these foundational skills is even acute for young adults (25-34 years old) and among 
individuals with upper-secondary and post-secondary degrees. This suggests that 
improving the effectiveness of schools in transmitting foundational skills, and at the same 
time designing institutions that can sustain those skills along the life cycle remain 
challenging for countries.  
In taking up these challenges, it is important to understand the differences in the design of 
educational systems1 and how these differences contribute to cross-country variations in 
the returns to schooling as measured by literacy and numeracy skills. Earlier studies 
suggested cross-country differences in school organisation contribute as much as family 
background does to the considerable difference in student achievement in early ages (i.e. 
10, 14, and 15 years old in Woessmann, 2016)2. Among those, tracking and prevalence of 
vocational education are two salient features of school organisation that are most 
pertinent to the accumulation of foundational skills (Brunello and Checchi, 2007[8]; 
Brunello and Rocco, 2015[3]). 
These two aspects of school organisation exert influence on the accumulation of skills 
through peer effects (Hoxby and Weingarth, 2005[3]), content of curriculum, and teacher 
efforts (e.g. Duflo, Dupas and Kremer, 2011[3];). Proponents of tracking systems stress 
the positive complementarity among peers with similar abilities, whereas opponents argue 
that tracking may exacerbate initial socio-economic disadvantages and thus prevent 
disadvantaged students from pursuing further learning. Vocational orientation of an 
education system not only affects the accumulation of general versus specific skills 
through the content of curriculum, but it can also change the composition of student body 
allocated to each track, therefore affecting skill accumulation through the channel of peer 
effect.

2 In this comprehensive review of both descriptive studies and studies using casual inference methods, Woessmann

Because of their direct influence on the initial cycle of skill accumulation and the widely 
accepted view that initial skill begets further skill (Cunha and Heckman, 2006[7]; 
Heckman, 2000[1]; 2008[2]), these two aspects of school design lie at the centre of 
education policy debates in many countries.   
Against the backdrop of these policy debates and following the earlier work by 
Brunello and Checchi (2007[8],) this paper provides new evidence on the correlational 
relationship between education system characteristics and the return to schooling (as 
measured by skills acquisition) and examines how education reforms affect the 
accumulation of foundational skills in the long run. To do so, the paper analyses data 
from the OECD PIAAC survey and aims to delineate a complete picture of the joint 
distribution of countries’ education system characteristics and individuals’ foundational 
skills in literacy and numeracy. More importantly, coupled with education reform data in 
17 European countries (Braga, Checchi and Meschi, 2013[9]), the long-term effects of 
education reform towards de-tracking and opening up university access on foundational 
skills in numeracy and literacy are examined.  
Two policy-relevant questions are addressed:  
1. How are education system features such as early tracking and vocational 
education orientation correlated with the distribution of skills?  
2. How do education reforms towards de-tracking and opening up university access 
affect individuals’ skills and the skill distribution in the long run? 
Previous studies investigating the role of school institutions on individuals’ skills have 
mainly focused on cognitive skill tests taken at age 10, 13 or 15 before the impact of 
school designs get fully unfolded, or restricted the investigation to either early tracking or 
vocational education orientation. This paper follows Brunello and Checchi (2007[8]), 
Braga, Checchi and Meschi (2013[9]), and Heisig and Solga (2015[10]), improving over 
previous studies in the following ways. First, it updates Brunello and Checchi (2007[8]) by 
drawing evidence from more recent cross-country data on adult skills (PIAAC 2012 and 
2014). Second, instead of examining the effects of education reforms on years of 
schooling, as done by Braga, Checchi and Meschi (2013[9]), this study investigates the 
long-term effects on adult skills—a more accurate measure of human capital (Hanushek 
et al., 2015[1]) and provides a complete picture of these relationships instead of focusing 
on a subgroup of the population as is the case in Heisig and Solga (2015[10]). 
Thirdly, this paper focuses on the differences of skill distributions across types of school 
systems. It examines the effects of changing tracking age and easing university access for 
vocational tracked students on individuals at different percentiles of the distributions as 
well as the average skills of the population.   
The main goal of this paper is to explore the relationships between schooling systems and 
individual skills. Educational system designs such as tracking regimes or vocational 
education concentration often vary slightly or not at all within countries (Braga, Checchi 
and Meschi, 2013[3]; Brunello and Checchi, 2007[2]; Hanushek and Woessmann, 2010[3]; 
Hanushek, Piopiunik and Wiederhold, 2014[4]). Therefore, it is difficult to identify the 
impact of school design just using national data sets; whereas an international perspective 
provides an opportunity for comparisons. This study exploits both the within- and across-
country variation among 17 countries in education reforms to examine their effects on 
adult skills in the long run.

1.  Related literature  
Classic human capital theory identifies formal education as the major channel towards 
human capital accumulation, and is based on the idea that schooling enables to acquire 
productive skills (Becker, 1964[4]; Mincer, 1975[4]). In particular, education can provide 
students with general and specific skills, and the balance between these two differs across 
educational systems. Two aspects of education systems, early tracking and vocational 
education orientation are the most salient features of education systems that affect this 
balance in individuals’ accumulation of skills and skill distributions at national level.  
1.1. Evidence on vocational education orientation and skills  
The United States is the classic example of an educational system strongly oriented 
towards providing general qualifications, while most European countries have established 
vocational training programmes as part of their education systems (Muller and Gangl, 
2003[3]). Among European education systems, wide variations exist regarding the 
institutional structures of vocational training. Germany, Austria and the Netherlands 
exemplify the traditional vocation-oriented systems that feature extensive vocational 
training through apprenticeship programmes or occupationally specific training in 
vocational schools. England (United Kingdom), France, and Sweden rely on a mix of 
academic and vocational tracks and secondary level schools in conjunction with small 
apprenticeship programmes3 (Muller and Gangl, 2003[3]). On average, 45% of upper-
secondary students enrol in vocational programmes across OECD countries in 2012 
(Kuczera, 2017). 
Proponents of vocational education emphasise the easy transition from school to work 
(Quintini and Manfredi, 2009); while opponents argue that vocational education may 
harm individuals’ employment and earnings prospects later in life because specific skills 
become quickly outdated in modern economies experiencing rapid technological changes 
(Krueger and Kumar, 2004[4]; Wolter and Ryan, 2011[4]). At the centre of the debate is the 
issue of how adaptable previously vocational educated individuals are when facing 
structural changes in the economy.  
However, identifying the effect of general versus vocational education on employment 
outcomes is challenging. The ideal data will come from a random assignment of two 
groups of individuals into vocational and general education tracks, and allocating them to 
the same type of jobs. Then any difference in their labour market outcomes during an 
economic change could be attributed to the initial differences in the type of education. 
However, such dataset is non-existent.  
In reality, the inherent challenge in using cross-sectional multi-country data to identify 
the impact of early education type on employment outcomes arises from the non-random 
sorting of students into different educational tracks. The assignment is a process

influenced by a number of individual characteristics such as family background, 
academic performances, parental expectation, individual ability, etc. Longitudinal cohort 
data containing rich individual information may overcome the self-selection issue by 
employing certain quasi-experimental methods. To the end of being able to control for 
pre-selection ability or academic performance, it has advantages over large-scale cross-
country data which often lacks information. 
The inherent challenge in using cross-sectional data to identify the impact of educational 
type on employment outcome partially explains the mixed findings in this line of 
literature. By and large, studies using large-scale cross-country data such as Brunello and 
Rocco (2015[3]), Hanushek et al., (2017[3]), Forster, Bol and van de Werhorst’s (2016) and 
Hampf and Woessmann’s (2017[4]) found a similar pattern: namely, that individuals with 
vocational education are more advantageous than individuals with general education in 
initial labour market outcomes, yet these advantages faded away later in life. It is worth 
noting that the cross-over age when general education becomes more advantageous 
differs when different labour market outcomes are examined. The turning point is earlier 
for lifetime income than for the probability of being employed.  
Results from using pooled data (Brunello and Rocco, 2015; Hanushek et al., 2017) differ 
from those country-by-country (Forster et al., 2016) or by country-group analyses (e.g. 
Hampf and Woessmann, 2017). Due to the inability of fully addressing the non-random 
allocation of workers to jobs using cross-sectional data, any different results obtained 
across countries might be largely capturing the labour market structure differences instead 
of identifying the real impact of education type on employment outcomes.  
As a matter of fact, the Hanushek et al. (2017) study found that the present value of 
lifetime earnings favors those with a general education in Germany and Denmark. 
However, workers in Switzerland with vocational education have eight percent higher 
lifetime earnings than workers with general education. The authors commented that these 
differences in age and lifetime earnings between the two education types across three 
countries might be attributed to differences in their economic structures.   
Similarly, different results found in Brunello and Rocco (2017[4]) and Golsteyn and 
Stenberg’s (2017) are due to the difficulty posed by using longitudinal cohort studies of a 
single country in addressing the non-random sorting into occupations. Brunello and 
Rocco (2017[5]) did not find any later employment disadvantages of vocational education 
among British individuals born in the United Kingdom in 1958. Golsteyn and Stenberg 
(2017) found a change to the disadvantage of vocational education at the start of the 
1990s for a cohort of males born in Sweden between 1955 and 1963. Yet, this pattern was 
not found among Sweden’s females.  
This present study cannot overcome the self-selection issue in educational sorting either. 
Yet, it answers a different set of questions from those addressed in the above-mentioned 
studies, particularly in Brunello and Rocco (2015[3]) where the effect of education type on 
adult skills is examined. Brunello and Rocco focused on individual variations within 
countries to examine the relationship between different types of education and skills. This 
study exploits cross-country variations in the prevalence of vocational education and 
tracking intensity to examine the correlations between these two dimensions of education 
system and individuals skills.  
In a further step, the paper investigates the effects on individual skills of easing university 
access for vocational-tracked high school students by exploiting the variations over time 
and across countries of these reforms.

1.2. Evidence on tracking and foundational skills  
Early tracking has been found to exacerbate the impact of socio-economic background on 
educational attainment (e.g. Dustmann, 2004[7]; Meghir and Palme, 2005[5]; Pekkarinen, 
Uusitalo and Kerr, 2009[4]; Schnepf, 2002[6]) and postponing tracking tends to increase 
the average years of schooling completed by the population and decrease 
intergenerational persistence in educational attainment (Braga, Checchi and Meschi, 
2013[4]). However, when it comes to the effect on average school performance or 
measured cognitive skills, there is no consensus among the existing empirical research 
(Duflo, Dupas and Kremer, 2011[4]; Figlio and Page, 2002[4]; Oosterbeek and Webbink 
2004[4]).  
Koerselman (2013) studied the incentive effects of curriculum tracking, and found robust 
evidence for such effects, reflected in higher test scores during the years before tracking 
starts. In the existence of incentive effects, the value-added specification controlling for 
pre-tracking achievement (e.g. Hanushek and Woessmann, 2006) leads to downward 
biased estimates of the mean effect of tracking.   
Heisig and Solga (2015[13]) incorporated both features of school design - early tracking 
and vocational education orientation, and studied how these two features correlated with 
country variation in the mean numeracy skills of a subgroup of the population – 
individuals attained less than tertiary level of education. Their findings indicated that 
tracking amplified skills gaps between adults with less than upper-secondary degrees and 
adults with upper-secondary or non-tertiary post-secondary degrees. This gap in 
numeracy skills was mitigated by a greater emphasis on vocational education in the 
upper-secondary level. Their explanation is that the labour market in vocational education 
prevalent countries incentivises and reward individuals graduating from vocational tracks 
therefore there is a greater level of learning efforts exerted than in general education 
oriented countries. 
1.3. Evidence on education systems and skills over the life cycle   
A couple of studies examined the evolution of skills after compulsory schooling by 
comparing skills of 15-year old students in the Programme for International Student 
Assessment (PISA) 2000 and the 25-29 age cohort in the Programme for the International 
Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) 2012. Although it is hard to discern the 
mechanisms of skill evolution into early adulthood without longitudinal datasets, these 
descriptive studies shed light on the skill evolution patterns across country post 
compulsory schooling. 
For instance, Borgonovi et al. (2017[13]) analysed the individual level PISA 2000 and 
PIAAC 2012 data sets, and found that between age 15 and 27 socio-economic disparities 
(measured by the number of books at home) in literacy increased in most countries that 
participated in both studies, the gap decreased in Germany and Korea.  
Drawing upon the country-level PISA 2000 and PIAAC 2012 data, Green et al. (2015[9]), 
and Green and Pensiero (2016[10]) found that the distribution of literacy and numeracy 
skills became more dispersed in England (United Kingdom), Canada, Ireland, Northern 
Ireland (United Kingdom), and the United States, and narrowed in Austria, Belgium, 
Germany, Czech Republic, Estonia, Poland, and the Slovak Republic.  
In a further analysis, the authors categorised countries by the percentage of students 
having college-educated parents in the academic or vocational track. The idea is the

2.  Data  
2.1. PIAAC 
The primary data sources for this study are the first two rounds of PIAAC. Also called the 
Survey of Adult Skills, PIAAC is an ongoing data collection effort of the OECD, with 24 
countries/subnational entities participating in the first round of data collection in 2008/13. 
The second round was administered in nine additional countries in 2012/16. Round three 
will be conducted in five additional countries and the United States between 2016 and 
2019.  
PIAAC is a household-based study. The target population is defined as “all non-
institutionalised adults between the ages of 16 and 65 whose usual place of residence is in 
the country at the time of data collection. Adults are to be included regardless of 
citizenship, nationality or language” (OECD 2013[2]). Trained interviewers administered 
the background questionnaire and the direct assessment to representative samples of the 
target population. Sample sizes varied from 3 761 in Northern Ireland (United Kingdom) 
to 27 285 in Canada. PIAAC assesses three domains of foundation skills - literacy, 
numeracy, and problem-solving in a technology-rich environment. The background 
questionnaire collects rich information on participants’ demographic characteristics, 
socio-economic status, educational attainment, etc.  
The subsequent analysis includes two domains of foundational skills measured in PIAAC, 
literacy and numeracy. The proficiency of each respondent in these domains is expressed 
by 10 plausible values that were obtained from calibrating, analysing, and scaling PIAAC 
cognitive item responses (OECD 2013[2]). All analyses in this study took into account 
both the sampling error component and the variance due to imputation of proficiency 
scores.  
The PIAAC Background Questionnaire contains a wide range of information that can be 
used as independent variable in regression analyses, such as completed years of schooling 
and demographic characteristic such as gender, immigration status, and parental highest 
level of education. Country fixed effects are also used in the empirical analysis to control 
for country-specific time-invariant characteristics, such as education spending, teacher 
quality, etc. Birth-year fixed effects are used to account for common factors affecting 
individuals born in different countries in the same year (for instance the global financial 
crisis).  
2.2. Analytical sample  
In the subsequent analysis, two different subsamples are used. In the simple correlational 
analysis, 27 countries were matched between the PIAAC participating countries and those 
having education system measures constructed in Bol and Werfhorst (2013[1]). These 
countries are Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Chile, Czech Republic, Denmark, 
England (United Kingdom), Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Ireland, Israel, Italy,

Japan, Korea, New Zealand, Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Slovak Republic, Slovenia, 
Spain, Sweden, Turkey, and the United States.    
The indicators are representative of countries’ education system characteristics in 2002. 
The sample is restricted to respondents aged 20-35 at the time of survey for each of these 
27 countries. Although covering a slightly broader age range, these individuals were most 
likely affected by education system features in 2002 when they faced upper-secondary 
level schooling decisions. Individuals who have lived in the surveyed country for less 
than five years are also excluded, as they were unlikely to be affected by the host 
country’s secondary and upper-secondary school designs in 2002. These restrictions 
yielded a sample of 53 831 observations including 16 birth-year cohorts from 27 
countries. 
When estimating the effect of educational reforms, both over-time and across-country 
variations in reforms are exploited. Yet available data points of education reforms were 
originally drawn from the Eurydice whose coverage only extends to European countries. 
Observations from other parts of the world thus were excluded from this part of the 
analysis. This is not ideal given that a large part of the variation in these two dimensions 
of education system designs comes from countries outside Europe, such as the United 
States and Canada. It renders the risk of not having enough cross-sectional variations of 
education policy changes by dropping those observations. Fortunately, the results indicate 
that even within the 14 European countries where education reform data is available, 
heterogeneous effects of education reforms on adult skills are still evident. All birth 
cohorts contained in the PIAAC dataset are used for this step of the analysis. This yields a 
sample of 102 391 observations across 17 European countries in examining changing 
tracking age and expanding university access.  
2.3. Tracking and vocational education orientation indices  
Indices on tracking and vocational orientation of national education systems are drawn 
from Bol and Werfhorst (2013[7]). The authors gathered data from the OECD and United 
Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO), including cross-
national student achievement tests such as Trend in International Mathematics and 
Science Study (TIMSS) and Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA). 
Indicators of early tracking and vocational orientation were compiled by conducting 
factor analysis using these data from the OECD and UNESCO across educational 
systems.  
The level of tracking was constructed by performing a principal factor analysis on three 
country-level variables including the age of first selection, the percentage of the total 
tracked curriculum, and the number of tracks that are available for 15-year-olds. As 
discussed by Bol and Werfhorst, all three indicators emphasised different aspects of

3.  Correlational analysis and identification of the impact of education

3.1. Education systems and individual skills  
A basic model specification in equation [1] is followed to explore how individuals’ 
characteristics are correlated with skills:  
𝑆𝑘𝑖𝑙𝑙𝑠𝑖𝑗= 𝛽0 + 𝛽1𝑋𝑖𝑗+ 𝛽2𝑌𝑒𝑎𝑟𝑠𝑖𝑗+ 𝜏𝑐+ 𝜏𝑡+ 𝜉𝑖𝑗                                    [1] 
where 𝑆𝑘𝑖𝑙𝑙𝑠𝑖𝑗 represents individual i’s PIAAC numeracy or literacy scores; 𝑋𝑖𝑗 is a 
vector of individual i’s demographic characteristics including gender, either parents has a 
university degree or not, and immigration status; 𝑌𝑒𝑎𝑟𝑠𝑖𝑗 indicates a continuous variable 
of individual i’s years of schooling. Country fixed effects 𝜏𝑐, and birth-year fixed effects 
𝜏𝑡 are also included in this basic model. Country fixed effects control for country specific 
characteristics that could affect individuals’ skills, for instance, average school quality, 
expenditure on education, etc. Birth-year fixed effects account for cohort effects 
influencing skills of individuals born in the same year.  
In exploring the correlational relationships between schooling characteristics and skills, 
equation [2] includes either tracking or vocational education index as discussed in the 
previous section, removing the country fixed effects from equation [1]. This is because 
the country fixed effects dummies in Equation [1] would otherwise be perfectly collinear 
with the education system indicators.  
𝑆𝑘𝑖𝑙𝑙𝑠𝑖𝑗= 𝛽0 + 𝛽1𝑋𝑖𝑗+ 𝛽2𝑌𝑒𝑎𝑟𝑠𝑖𝑗+ 𝛽3𝑒𝑑𝑢𝑐𝑎𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛_𝑠𝑦𝑠𝑡𝑒𝑚_𝑖𝑛𝑑𝑒𝑥𝑐+ 𝜏𝑡+ 𝜉𝑖𝑗 [2] 
Where 𝑒𝑑𝑢𝑐𝑎𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛_𝑠𝑦𝑠𝑡𝑒𝑚_𝑖𝑛𝑑𝑒𝑥 refers to either the tracking index or the vocational 
education orientation index of country j. 
The underlying idea is that the structure of educational institutions affect skills 
accumulated through formal schooling. In the case when students are streamed into 
different classrooms or school tracks based on previous academic achievements or future 
career aspirations, they are exposed to different peer groups from an early age. Extensive 
research has suggested the critical role peer effect plays in the education production 
function. Teaching content also varies between vocational- and academic-focused 
depending on the orientation of a track.  
It is important to note that this analysis only reveals the correlational relationships 
between these two characteristics of schooling and individual skills. Such specifications 
in equation [2] suffer from omitted variable bias. Unlike equation [1] where country fixed 
effects could be included, in this specification, education system dummies lack within 
country variations, thus perfectly collinear with country dummies. Other country-level 
factors such as education expenditure which are not included here could also affect 
individuals’ skills.

3.2. Education reforms and individual skills in the long run  
The average effects of the educational reforms on skills are obtained first from Ordinary 
least square (OLS) regressions. In addition, quantile regressions are conducted to test the 
heterogeneous impact of educational reforms along the distribution of skills because the 
distributional effects of education reforms are more of a concern here. It is possible that 
some reforms did not affect the mean level of skills, yet they only impacted skills of 
people at some parts of the distribution. For example, increasing the tracking age might 
potentially affect individuals at either the bottom or the top of the skill distributions but 
not individuals at the middle of the distributions. Therefore, unconditional quantile 
regression is a suitable strategy for this purpose (Firpo, Fortin and Lemieux, 2009[5]).  
Unconditional quantile regressions allow an estimation of the impact of a small change in 
an independent variable on the entire unconditional distribution of the dependent variable 
(Firpo, Fortin and Lemieux, 2009[5]). This makes it possible to study the impact of a 
certain variable on inequality of the distribution of the outcome variable (in this case it is 
the skill inequality, similar to the case of wage inequality as studied by Paccagnella 
(2015[5])). For instance, if the estimated effect is larger at the bottom of the skill 
distribution than at the top, a marginal increase in the independent variable being 
examined will decrease skill inequality. So nine different unconditional quantile 
regressions are estimated, covering percentiles from the 10th to the 90th along the 
numeracy skills distribution for the pooled sample.

𝜏𝑡+ 𝜖𝑖𝑐𝑡                                                                                                                 [3]

vector of individual i’s demographic characteristics including gender, age, either parents 
has a university degree or not, and immigration status; 𝑌𝑒𝑎𝑟𝑠𝑖𝑐𝑡 indicates a continuous

4.  Results  
4.1. Education systems and PIAAC skills 
The investigation begins with a basic model of how years of schooling and other 
individual characteristics such as gender, parental education, and immigration status are 
correlated with numeracy skills. Model 1 in Table 2 has the most basic specification, 
where an individual’s numeracy skills is a function of years of schooling, gender, parental 
education, immigration status, country and birth year fixed effects. Results show that each 
additional years of schooling is associated with about eight points increase in PIAAC 
numeracy scores, which are equal to 0.2 of a standard deviation (1 standard deviation 
equals 50 score points among all adults in PIAAC participating countries).  
Females have lower numeracy skills on average than males, the gap being 13 points 
measured by PIAAC numeracy score. First-generation immigrants perform worse in 
numeracy tests than the native-born, the average gap being twice as large as the gender 
gap. Although the estimated coefficient is still negative for second-generation 
immigrants, it is no longer statistically significant. Socio-economic status, measured by 
whether either parent has a tertiary education, has a large, positive and statistically 
significant association with PIAAC numeracy.  
Model 2 and 3 in Table 2 reports OLS regression results of equation 2, in which the 
correlational relationships between the return to education measured by PIAAC numeracy 
scores and education system characteristics in early tracking and vocational education 
orientation are presented. The sample included individuals aged 20-35 at the time of 
survey from 27 PIAAC participating countries. Due to the lack of data on tracking index 
for Estonia and vocational education orientation index for New Zealand, model 2 
excluded participants from Estonia, and model 3 excluded observations from New 
Zealand.  
Results from Model 2 and Model 3 suggest that each educational system characteristics is 
positively associated with individual skills, indicating that all else equal, individuals in 
countries with higher level of tracking and concentration of vocational education possess 
stronger numeracy skills. In particular, comparing the coefficients of the two education 
system characteristics suggests that vocational education orientation has a stronger 
association with skills than tracking does. The association between vocational education 
orientation and numeracy skills is 5.586, about 0.125 of a standard deviation. As 
mentioned in the previous section, albeit omitting other country-level factors that could 
affect skills, the coefficient of vocational education prevalence in this model specification 
indicates a positive correlation between the vocational education index and country fixed 
effects.   
The cross-sectional data such as PIAAC does not allow for an understanding of the 
mechanisms underlying the high performances of vocational-oriented educational 
systems. Yet, using individuals’ background information in the PIAAC data enables

hypothesis testing regarding possible channels of high proficiency of adults in vocational-
oriented education systems.   
First, high average PIAAC performances of adults in vocational-oriented education 
systems could be because of their high primary and secondary school quality. To test this, 
PISA 2000 scores were used as a proxy for primary and secondary school quality for the 
20-35 years old in PIAAC. Regressing PIAAC numeracy scores on PISA mathematics 
scores suggested that PISA scores explained more than 60 percent of variations in 
average PIAAC numeracy performances across 27 countries. Furthermore, using teacher 
as a proxy for compulsory education quality, Hanushek et al. (2014[5]) found that teachers 
in vocational education oriented systems such as Germany, Finland, Belgium, Austria, 
and Sweden scored significantly higher in PIAAC literacy and numeracy than in 
countries like Canada and the United States. In this study, teachers were identified by the 
International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO) code from the PIAAC 
background questionnaire.  
Second, the descriptive statistics from Section 3 and the correlational analysis indicated a 
high average performance and a compressed upper part of the skills distribution among 
adults in vocational education focused systems. This could indicate a lower skill premium 
associated with obtaining a tertiary education in vocational education prevalent countries 
than others. Skill premium is the difference between what skills would be in the absence 
of the tertiary education and what skills are in the presence of the tertiary education.  
This hypothesis could be tested by splitting the sample between individuals whose 
highest educational attainment is a tertiary education (ISCED 5 and ISCED 5A) and those 
who have only obtained a highest level of upper-secondary (ISCED 3A-B, C long) or 
post-secondary, non-tertiary (ISCED 4A-B-C) education. Comparing numeracy score 
differences between these two categories of educational attainment across high VET 
countries (Germany and Austria) and low VET countries (Canada and the U.S.), the score 
differences are significantly lower in high VET countries compared to low VET countries 
(18 numeracy scores versus 37). It might be the case that vocational education 
concentrated systems have high-quality non-academic tracks and this increases the skills 
of individuals who do not acquire a tertiary education and consequently decreases the 
skill premium related to obtaining a tertiary education. 
Third, the high proficiency in numeracy among adults in vocational education focused 
systems could be related to their frequent use of numeracy skills at work and in everyday 
life. Analysing the skill use information collected in the background questionnaire in 
PIAAC demonstrated some interesting patterns across education systems. Comparing the 
numeracy skill use at work between high VET countries (Germany and Austria) and low 
VET countries (Canada and the U.S.) among 20-35 year olds indicated that young adults 
in Germany and Austria exercise advanced mathematics skill such as simple algebra or 
formulas more often than young adults in the United States and Canada do. The former 
group on average practices these skills between less than once a month and less than once 
a week but at least once a month; whereas individuals in the latter group never or only use 
those skills less than once a month. These differences are statistically significant when 
skill use frequency at work or in everyday life is examined.  
At the same time, young adults in the United States and Canada practice simple numeracy 
tasks more often than young adults in Germany and Austria do. These simple tasks 
involve calculating costs or budgets, and using or calculating fractions or percentages.

4.2. Education reforms and effects on skills  
A further step of this paper examined the long-term effects of education reforms towards 
de-tracking and opening access to universities on adult skills. This is achieved by 
combining the education reform data across 17 European countries (Braga, Checchi and 
Meschi, 2013[10]) with PIAAC. OLS regressions are run to first gauge the average effects 
of education reforms on numeracy and literacy skills.  More importantly, unconditional 
quantile regressions analysis is conducted to estimate the impact of each of the education 
reforms on the unconditional distribution of skills.  
4.2.1. Raising tracking age and long-term effects on skills  
The OLS regression results following equation [3] indicate that postponing tracking by 
one year is associated with 0.083 points increase in numeracy skills (results in Table 3).  
Yet the coefficient of tracking age is not statisticially significant, implying that tracking 
on average does not alter the return to education measured by skills.  
This could happen when early tracking hurts low performing students by putting them on 
tracks where schooling stops early or by preventing them from interacting with high-
performing students. Whereas high performing students may benefit from early tracking 
and interacting with other strong peers; thus positive complimentarity may arise as a 
result of the early tracking for high performers. If this is the case, tracking on average 
does not affect the returns to education for the population.  
Results from the unconditional quantile regression analysis following equation [3] 
confirmed these hypotheses. The results are plotted in Figure 5. They suggest that most of 
the benefits of increasing tracking age accrues to individuals with lower numeracy skills. 
The associations between postponing tracking and the increase in numeracy skills for 
individuals at the bottom 20 percentile of the distribution are higher than the average 
effect on the population. The effects of delaying tracking on individuals whose skills are 
between the 20th and 50th percentile are not significantly different from zero. For 
individuals at the upper half of the numeracy skill distribution (70th – 90th perentile), 
raising tracking age has negative associations with their numeracy skills, with the 
coefficient at the 90th percentile being the smallest.  
Overall unconditional quantile regression results suggest a tradeoff between equality and 
efficiency of affecting skills through postponing tracking age. Although on average 
raising tracking age does not affect the numeracy skills of the population, heterogenous 
effects along the distribution of skills are evident. In the long run, raising tracking age 
benefited individuals at the bottom of the skill distribution. Yet individuals with high 
proficiency level in numeracy benefited from tracking at an early age.  
4.2.2. Expanding university access and effects on skills in the long run  
The OLS regression results indicated that opening university access, on average, 
increased numeracy skills by 2.37 points (the first column in Table 4).  Results from the 
unconditional quantile regressions are both presented in Table 4 and plotted in Figure 6. 
They indicate that individuals whose numeracy skills are between the 10th and 30th deciles 
benefited the most from expanding university access reforms. The size of the effects on 
the 10th and 20th percentiled individuals is equivalent to the effect of an additional year of 
schooling.

Overall, results from the unconditional quantile regressions demonstrate that education 
reforms towards de-tracking and opening access to university benefited individuals at the 
bottom more than at the top of the skill distributions. These reforms therefore reduced the 
skill gaps between the bottom and the top. Especially in the case of changing tracking 
ages, those changes significantly reduced skill inequalities in numeracy, the reduction 
mainly coming from increasing numeracy scores of individuals at the lower half of the 
skill distribution. The reforms towards opening up university access benefited individuals 
towards the lower end of the skills distribution, as well as elevating the average 
performance of the population. 
It is worth noting that in the majority of these 17 European countries being examined, the 
tracking age changes happened around the time that compulsory schooling laws also 
changed (Dincer, 2016) (see Appendix table 3 for years of law changes). For instance, 
Koerselman (2013) reported that the Swedish education reform in 1962 included pre-test 
curriculum, teaching style, and amount of compulsory education. So the coefficient 
estimates of changing tracking age are the combined effects of multiple mechanisms. As 
commented in Koerselman (2013), a policy experiment more relevant to tracking polices 
today would be if a country such as Germany with a modern vocational track were to 
postpone its tracking age.

5.  Discussion  
This study set out to examine how different education systems fare in equipping 
individuals with foundational skills. It is well acknowledged and accepted that skills beget 
skills, and that foundation skills will help individuals regain new skills quickly in a fast 
changing technology-rich environment. The lack of ability to re-learn new skills prevents 
individuals from taking advantage of technological advances and from fully participating 
in the labour market. Thus, the strength of initial cycle of formal schooling in cultivating 
foundation skills in literacy and numeracy becomes a concern for policy makers, and an 
important issue to examine for researchers. In particular, education systems differ 
substantially in the level of tracking students by ability or career aspirations, and the 
prevalence of vocational education. These two aspects of school system designs are likely 
to affect skill accumulation in important ways, e.g. through peer effect or curriculum 
content. This study investigated how these features of formal education systems 
contribute differently to the accumulation of foundational skills in literacy and numeracy 
across education systems.  
To do so, this study coupled PIAAC 2012 and 2014 data with data on education system 
characteristics and education reforms to examine these relationships. Results suggested 
that education systems with strong orientation towards vocational training exhibit high 
mean scores in both numeracy and literacy. These systems also demonstrated compressed 
upper part of the skill distribution compared to education systems that are not vocational-
oriented. These patterns were related to the high quality of compulsory education of 
vocation education oriented systems, and smaller skill gap between college-educated 
individuals and those with only high school diplomas. By analysing the background 
information collected by the PIAAC, results also indicated that young adults in high VET 
countries such as Germany and Austria are more frequently exercising advanced 
mathematics skills such as algebra or formulas than young adults do in countries such as 
the United States and Canada.  
With the data at hand, this study could not distinguish whether the high performances of 
vocational education focused systems were due to their educational system design. Yet, 
pairing the PIAAC data with education reforms data on raising tracking age and opening 
up university access showed interesting results.  
The analysis on the impact of education reforms towards easing university access for 
vocational high schools suggested that these reforms significantly affected individuals at 
the lower deciles of the skill distribution rather than the top decile when numeracy was 
concerned. Thus, these reforms reduced skills inequalities between individuals at the top 
and bottom of the numeracy skill distributions. 
The age at which tracking takes place was not found to have a significant effect on the 
average performance in numeracy. This is consistent with studies using longitudinal data 
of a single country. For instance, Kerr, Pekkarinen and Uusitalo’s (2015[9]) found that the 

34 │ EDU/WKP(2018)18 
 
  
Unclassified 
de-tracking reform had no effect on the mean performance in the arithmetic or logical 
reasoning tests in Finland.  
In examining the effect of changing tracking age along the skills distribution, results from 
this present study suggested that raising tracking age positively affected individuals at the 
bottom three deciles; yet the effects on individuals at the top deciles were negative. 
Overall, postponing tracking significantly reduced skill inequalities both in literacy and 
numeracy. The reduction comes from significantly increase skills of individuals at the 
lower half of the distribution.  
It is worth noting that the majority of these 17 European countries being examined 
changed compulsory schooling laws around the time that the tracking age changed. So the 
coefficient estimates of changing tracking ages captured the combined effects of multiple 
mechanisms.  
This exercise answered the questions this paper proposed to investigate, and it also leaves 
remaining questions for further investigation when richer individual longitudinal data 
becomes available. For instance, these results call for further examination of the black 
box of early tracking. One pattern observed in this study is that countries with high levels 
of tracking have less dispersed skill distributions at the upper half. Empirical studies such 
as Green and Pensiero (2016[5]) and Borgonovi et al. (2017[6]) shed light on different 
patterns of skill evolution among countries with varying levels of tracking. However, 
without longitudinal micro-level data, it is hard to further analyse how patterns of skill 
distribution can be attributed to the tracking feature of education system design.  
Moreover, other features of an educational system might interact with tracking or de-
tracking policies in an important way. A field study by Duflo, Dupas and Kremer (2011) 
suggested that teachers’ efforts can crucially affect the success or otherwise of reforms 
that target the tracking age. Providing incentives to teachers to tailor their instruction 
level and to teach to the median students, even low-achieving students in the lower track 
would benefit from tracking (Duflo, Dupas and Kremer, 2011). 
Thiemann (2017) also focused on the school design and distribution of students’ 
performances. She developed a model of student decision making that shows that it 
depends on the culture of competitiveness in a country or region whether it is optimal to 
choose a school design with ability tracking or comprehensive schooling. Taking into 
account students with heterogeneous abilities, Thiemann (2017) showed that the average 
performance in competitive cultures is maximised under comprehensive schooling and in 
non-competitive cultures under ability tracking. Segregation of abilities in non-
competitive cultures, however, leads to a higher dispersion of performances.  
These recent empirical and theoretical work related to tracking indicate that much is still 
unknown about tracking itself and the effects of tracking on student performances or 
measured skills. This paper can be seen as setting the stage for future research on 
examining the long term effects of early tracking on skills.
