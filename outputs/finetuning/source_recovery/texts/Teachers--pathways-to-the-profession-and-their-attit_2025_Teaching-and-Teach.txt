Two trends are dominating K-12 education in the United States: more individuals are pivoting into teaching
careers from other professions, and classrooms are becoming more diverse. Structural equation modeling was
applied to data from the 2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey. The sample (N = 2554) consisted of
teachers from grade 7–9 with an average 15-years of experience and an average age of 43 years. Ninety-eight
percent of teachers had a bachelor’s degree or higher. The results revealed a significant relationship between
teachers’ career-choice and their attitudes toward diverse classrooms that have important implications for
teacher recruitment and training.

1. Introduction
Educator preparation programs across the United States are tasked
with recruiting and preparing cohorts of highly qualified novice teachers
every year. However, identifying and measuring the qualities of an
effective teacher are difficult, and validating such a system involves a
complicated process of systematic sorting of students to teachers (Kane
et al., 2013). In today’s classrooms, students come from a wide variety of
ethnicities, income levels, special needs, and languages that require
teachers to stretch further and further to effectively meet the needs of all
learners (Beasley et al., 2013; de Brey et al., 2019). In fact, research
clearly indicated that Black and Hispanic students do better with
teachers who resemble their own backgrounds, but the pipeline of
incoming teachers are not as diverse as classroom rosters (Lamb, 2022;
Schaeffer, 2021). This problem may be shifting, however, as
non-traditional teacher preparation programs throughout the United
States are steadily becoming more popular, and these programs are only
46.8 % white compared to traditional program’s that are 69.6 %
(Weisberg, 2020).
There is substantial ongoing research to understand both how
teacher-student racial congruence affects student success and why individuals choose the teaching profession (Kelchtermans et al., 2017;
Minus-Vincent, 2022). However, there is a significant gap in the

literature that brings these two topics together: how are teacher-student
relationships, particularly in diverse classrooms, affected by an educator’s pathway to the profession? The goal of this research is to specifically understand if a teacher whose first choice of occupation was
teaching (First Career Teachers, or FCTs) has a different attitude toward
teaching a diverse classroom compared to a teacher whose first career
choice was not teaching (Second Career Teachers, or SCTs).
This research is particularly crucial for two reasons. First, the demographic diversity of students in public classrooms is increasing. One
projection estimates that the percentage of white students enrolled in
public schools will drop down to 42 % by 2031, while the figure originally sat at 51 % in 2012 (NCES, 2024). Second, alternative pathways to
licensure are becoming increasingly popular for K-12 teacher candidates, and they appear to appeal more to non-white teachers who are
switching from other careers or those who didn’t originally choose
teaching as their first choice of profession (Woods, 2016). If there is a
pipeline of increasingly diverse teachers available, it would benefit
students to better understand teachers’ attitudes toward classrooms with
diverse student populations.
Educators’ capacity to adapt strategies for diverse classrooms may
depend on their own teacher preparation experience and motivation to
become an educator (Abacioglu et al., 2020; Chisholm, 1994). For
example, a rigorous and inclusive educator preparation program may

Traditional educator preparation programs typically follow a standard
undergraduate degree plan over four years with specialized coursework
in pedagogical knowledge, content specialty, and instructional practice
(Futterer et al., 2022). While both traditional and alternative preparation programs may require clinical teaching experience, such as observations and student teaching, traditional preparation programs often

programs may also have several required observation hours, practicum
requirements, and preservice teaching responsibilities (U.S. Department

On the other hand, SCTs may have had one or more occupations
before engaging in a teaching career. When they begin to pursue the
profession, they typically already have college credits or a college degree in another subject. While FCTs have historically made up the majority of teachers in classrooms in the United States, the prevalence of
SCTs is steadily increasing across the nation (Futterer, 2022). For the
purposes of this paper, SCTs (also frequently referred to as “career
changers” in the literature) may be defined as teachers who did not
complete traditional educator preparation programs because their first
choice of career was not teaching (Cuddapah & Stanford, 2015). SCTs
typically complete an alternative pathway to teacher licensure which
usually provides a “fast-track” to classroom teaching compared to a
traditional four-year undergraduate degree (Matsko et al., 2022, p. 226).
Alternative pathways to teacher licensure, here forward denoted as
alternative preparation pathways or alternative preparation programs,
originated in the 1980s in response to teacher shortages (Futterer et al.,
2022; Goodwin et al., 2019; Heijst et al., 2023; Lamb, 2022; Matsko
et al., 2022). Prior to that, states had to rely on emergency teaching
permits when a classroom had a teacher vacancy, potentially resulting in
an individual with no training whatsoever serving as the
teacher-of-record (Lamb, 2022). As concerns about teacher shortages
have grown, so have the presence of alternative routes of teacher
licensure. At the start of the new millennium, there were only 70
alternative preparation programs across the nation, but that number
swelled to 660 by the 2017–2018 academic year (Lamb, 2022). A year
later, in the academic year 2018–2019, SCTs from alternative preparation programs made up 23 % of all education program completers
(Lamb, 2022).
This “fast-track” to teacher licensure does not have the same requirements as traditional educator preparation programs. In fact, many
SCTs in alternative preparation programs become employed as full-time
teachers while engaging in coursework. Several states use such an
approach to train SCTs. For instance, the state of Tennessee utilizes a
“job-embedded” approach, which is currently popular among SCTs
(Tennessee Department of Education, 2023). SCTs may also be trained
through residency programs where they spend up to a year working in a
school (though not as a teacher-of-record), under the supervision of a
mentor teacher (Matsko et al., 2022). These “on the job” and residency
models are different pathways to a teaching license, but both complete
the pathway at a much faster pace than a traditional program (Matsko
et al., 2022).

2. Conceptual framework
The theoretical basis for this analysis is the Factors Influencing
Teaching (FIT) Choice Framework, originally developed by Watt &
Richardson, 2007. This framework bridges the gap between the existing
literature on teacher career-choice and attitudes toward teaching
diverse students with our analysis by accounting for prior experiences,
attitudes toward teaching profession, teachers’ training to teach diverse
classrooms, student demographics, and backgrounds that influences
teachers’ pathways to profession for both FCTs and SCTs. In total, the
framework identifies twelve constructs that serve as motivation for an
individual to choose teaching as a career: ability, intrinsic factors, second career choice, job security, family time, job transferability, making a
difference for future generations, social justice and contributions,
working with kids, prior experience as a student, and social influences
(Watt & Richardson, 2007). These constructs can easily be sorted into
extrinsic and intrinsic factors that influence an individual to choose
teaching, and they align with the constructs used in our analysis to
evaluate teachers’ attitudes toward working with diverse students.
Low et al. (2017) found the FIT Choice Framework to capture individuals’ motivations to choose teaching vary considerably, with motivators such as “intrinsic career value” and “social utility value”
capturing intrinsic factors and motivators like the “fallback career”
(Watt & Richardson’s identifier for SCTs) being an extrinsic factor of its
own. The motivations related to teachers’ career satisfaction are
altruistic-type motivations mostly attached to teaching and
self-evaluations of teaching-related skills (Watt & Richardson, 2007).
Intrinsic, extrinsic, and altruistic motivation are the most important
reasons for entering into the profession (Brookhart & Freeman, 1992).
Some of these motivations include the opportunity to work with children
and adolescents, positive contributions to society, making meaningful
impact on people’s lives, job security and benefits, and the compatibility
of family life with a teacher’s typical work schedule (OECD, 2005). For
this study, the utility and validity of FIT-Choice scale (Watt &
Richardson, 2007) provides similar measures to examine teachers’
pathways to profession for diverse classrooms.

3. Literature review

The literature on the difference between FCTs and SCTs is varied,
complex, and sometimes even contrary. The following section highlights
differences between FCTs and SCTs, along with their respective pathways to the profession: specifically, their preparedness for teaching,
program differences, efficacy and student achievement, self-efficacy, job
satisfaction, and retention rates.

readers should understand the different pathways that bring these two

Most alternative preparation programs assume that SCTs have strong
subject matter expertise but need training in instructional practice and
pedagogy, resulting in more emphasis on instructional strategies and
classroom management than specific content knowledge (Matsko et al.,
2022). Matsko et al. (2022) used Schools and Staffing Survey data to find
that completers of alternative preparation programs reported “significantly shorter practice teaching experiences” than teachers in traditional educator preparation programs.
Several studies have examined the operational and procedural differences in coursework for traditional educator preparation programs
and alternative preparation programs, but our own extensive research
revealed a dearth of available literature on the quality of preparation by
each pathway. The existing literature did not reveal major differences
between traditional educator preparation programs and alternative
preparation programs, instead suggesting that there are more differences within programs than across different types (Matsko et al., 2022).
However, there are certain statistics that make the pathways stand apart
from one another. For instance, alternative preparation programs tend
to enroll more candidates in science and mathematics content areas,
which are the highest shortage areas for teaching vacancies (Lamb,
2022). These findings are consistent with Matsko et al.’s (2022) study,
which found that alternative program completers were more diverse
than traditional cohorts of teacher candidates.
Once teacher candidates complete their respective pathways to
licensure, the efficacy of both FCTs and SCTs’ work as educators is a
highly prioritized area in research. In terms of academic success of
students, Boyd et al. (2011) found no major differences between FCTs
and SCTs. Lamb (2022) also found no significant difference in students’
academic success between FCTs and SCTs who completed alternative
preparation programs. However, SCTs who completed alternative
preparation programs that were more selective tended to outperform
high school math teachers compared to those who finished traditional
educator preparation programs.
When investigating the difference between FCTs and SCTs, selfefficacy, or an individual’s belief in their capacity to do their job well,
is another area of great research interest. Research found that FCTs
report higher self-efficacy in the beginning of their careers (Hogg et al.,
2023). On the other hand, Troesch and Bauer (2017) found that SCTs
maintain a higher self-rating of self-efficacy than FCTs over time and
hypothesized that this may be due to a transfer from their past experience and knowledge of their original career(s).
The findings on teacher job satisfaction are mixed. Research suggests
SCTs are more satisfied with their jobs as teachers because they made a
deliberate choice to change occupations (Troesch & Bauer, 2017).
However, the TALIS Technical Report from 2019 found a statistically
significant positive relationship between FCTs and job satisfaction
(OECD, 2019). These results align with an analysis performed on the
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) data in 2022,
which found FCTs report higher job satisfaction (Futterer et al., 2022).
One reason SCTs may not rank their job satisfaction as high as FCTs
could be due to their prior working experience, which may influence
their ratings for the work they do in the classroom (Futterer et al., 2022).
On the other hand, Troesch and Bauer (2017) hypothesize SCTs may
experience higher job satisfaction as a result of their higher self-efficacy,
which also reduces their job stress.
Teacher attrition, which is frequently cited as the very reason why
alternative preparation programs exist, is a problem for all teacher
career pathways. Only 48 % of individuals who begin alternative
preparation programs complete the program (Heijst et al., 2023).
However, another analysis of Teach for America teachers, who complete
highly selective alternative preparation programs and work in schools
with high levels of teacher vacancies across the country, reported that
older SCTs are much more likely to stay in schools as teachers compared
to younger SCTs (Cuddapah & Stanford, 2015). These results are
corroborated by another study which found SCTs with more than eight
years of working experience prior to changing careers have high

intentions to remain in teaching, but overall differences in retention
between FCTs and SCTs evened out within two years on the job (Hogg
et al., 2023).
3.3. Motivations for teaching
Individuals choose to enter the teaching profession with a variety of
expectations toward the work and motivations for wanting to partake in
it. In this section, we identify why teachers opt into the profession,
whether as FCTs or SCTs, and the benefits they expect to gain from their
work. This context is critical to understand how people choose teaching,
especially those who choose teaching as a second- or third-choice career,
and how they view their employment and therefore their students. It is
well-documented that students thrive under high expectations from
their teachers, and if placed in front of a teacher who has negative beliefs
about education or who has negative beliefs about their students, statistically they are much more likely to fail to thrive (Flanagan et al.,
2020). Therefore, the beliefs and motives of teachers are critical to understanding how career choice order relates to a teacher’s attitudes and
actions around students, especially for under-privileged and
under-resourced students. Aragon et al. (2014) argue that a teacher’s
beliefs around teaching diverse students are related to their commitment
to the teaching career, especially their commitment to teaching students
in urban or low-income settings.
FCTs, often first begin to consider teaching as a career when they are
students. These individuals tend to see teaching as a positive contribution to society with a good salary, high job security, and good working
conditions that offer long holiday periods (Balyer & Özcan, 2014). For
SCTs, the conscious decision to pivot from another career to one in the
classroom is frequently the result of comparison to their old career –
better working hours and working conditions, and an opportunity to
contribute to society and work with children and adolescents (Futterer
et al., 2022).
Motivation to pursue a career in education can be broken down into
two major categories: extrinsic factors and intrinsic factors. Day and Gu
(2010) define extrinsic factors as workplace and policy factors, whereas
intrinsic factors are defined as personal motivations. Their research
found that teachers’ motivation for choosing and staying in the teaching
profession is influenced by a mixture of extrinsic and intrinsic factors,
with the balance varying based on the personality and experiences of
each individual teacher. Paulick et al. (2013) took this research one step
further by asking if teachers who choose the profession for primarily
intrinsic reasons teach differently than those who chose the profession
for extrinsic factors. In fact, they found that teachers with more intrinsic
motivation reported higher self-efficacy and pedagogical knowledge,
whereas those with higher extrinsic motivation for the profession were
found to have less pedagogical knowledge (Paulick et al., 2013).
Both extrinsic and intrinsic factors of motivations can be further
broken down into subcategories, with examples of extrinsic motivation
including benefits of teaching such as salary, income and job stability, a
favorable schedule, and status (Hogg et al., 2023). These social and
cultural benefits have been found to have a major influence on individuals’ motivation to choose teaching as a career (Kass & Miller,
2018). In fact, many characterize pragmatism as an extrinsic motivator
to become a teacher, with family responsibilities, a schedule and
working hours conducive to childcare, and working hours of teaching,
especially females, is particularly enticing, especially for females
(Richardson & Watt, 2007).
On the other end of the spectrum, Friedman (2016) identified an
array of intrinsic factors that influence individuals to choose teaching as
a career, including natural ability and affinity for working with children,
altruism, and corrective experience from past experiences in the education system. Indeed, the social contribution value is a major intrinsic
factor for many teachers, with many preservice teachers citing a desire
to “shape the future of children and adolescents” as their most important
reason for choosing teaching (Lin et al., 2012, p. 236). However, many

low-income and English-language-learners are much more likely to
experience a decline in commitment to teaching, with many stating if
they could start over, they would be less likely to become a teacher

experiencing deficiencies in job satisfaction depending on the racial
composition of the school. One study found that white teachers were less
likely to be satisfied with their jobs in schools where they were the

were the minority demographic (Frankenburg et al., 2010, p. 315). This

Ideally, coursework in teacher education programs would remedy
this problem and create more open-minded teachers. However, one
aspect of career-changers that may be particularly problematic in this
light is the idea of autonomy. Many career changers listed autonomy in
their work as a key motivation for switching from a previous occupation
to teaching. As Lin et al. (2012) point out, teachers are typically isolated
in their rooms, granting them that freedom to choose what to do and
how to do it, which may be beneficial for diverse students if they have a
teacher with a commitment to diversity, equity, and inclusion. Students
who are assigned to teachers with other beliefs and motivations, however, may find themselves engendered in the status quo, which will most
likely inhibit any chance of transformational change in their outcomes.
This autonomy can then be in conflict with the needs of the students,
especially those from diverse backgrounds, who value a more collectivist or democratic community.
The dominant culture in U.S. schools values freedom and autonomy,
but other cultures that value “family, traditions, hierarchy, and group
norms” do not tend to value autonomy in the same way (Awang-Hashim
et al., 2017, p. 5). For example, teachers may adopt either a democratic
or an autocratic teaching style. Teachers’ who adopt democratic
teaching styles are more likely to allow students to have some control in
their classroom and take part in the decision-making process, but it is
only possible teachers can adjust to their students’ perspectives and have
appropriate expectations (Burgueno et al., 2021). On the other hand,
autocratic teachers typically limit students’ autonomy by “imposing
their own perspective” and expecting students to adapt to the teacher,
not the other way around (Burgueño et al., 2021, p. 5). Under an
autocratic teacher, a student from a different background may not feel
welcome and it is possible that all of their needs will not be met.

3.4. Teacher gender
Women predominantly represent the teaching force around the globe
as well as in the United States (NCES, 2022; UNESCO, 2023). As of 2022,
77 % of public-school teachers were female in the United States (NCES,
2022). Additionally, it is reported that male teachers are more likely to
leave the profession than female teachers (UNESCO, 2023). Such
research suggests differing opinions regarding the role of teacher gender
in education. However, research indicated no differences in teaching
styles, capacities, and effects on student achievement, well-being and
attitude due to teacher gender (Sabbe & Aelterman, 2007).
A small amount of research indicated some inconsistent differences
between male and female teachers. In their research, Watt et al. (2013)
found that female teachers have stronger motivations for the desire to
work with children and higher relatedness to teaching style dimensions
compared to male teachers, but teachers’ choice of teaching profession
to become STEM teachers did not differ based on gender.
In educational research, the differences between male and female
teachers’ opinions about the importance of workplace experiences,
teachers’ well-being, and motivation have been explored for positive
teacher outcomes, but limited research considers the role of teacher
gender (Collie, 2021). Thus, to achieve the goals of higher teacher
satisfaction, schools’ should provide cultural and social constructs to
shape the teachers professional identities, their working conditions, and
the way they perceive teaching as a profession (Sabbe & Aelterman,
2007).
3.5. Teaching in diverse classrooms
Teaching a diverse classroom is fundamentally different from
teaching a culturally homogenous classroom. Failure to recognize such
differences are recognized as color-blindness, a microaggression that
assumes all individuals have overall similar experiences (Aragon et al.,
2014). This is one of many harmful assumptions about working in
classrooms with diverse students, so it is important to understand
teachers’ attitudes toward teaching such students. Whenever teachers
lack the resources and knowledge to work with students from different
backgrounds, those learners tend to experience higher rates of discipline
issues and their achievement decreases (Bonner et al., 2017).
One way that researchers have assessed teachers’ attitudes toward
working with diverse students is multicultural efficacy. The concept of
multicultural efficacy is based on self-efficacy and multicultural education, and it measures a teacher’s confidence in their ability to be
effective in a diverse classroom setting (Guyton & Wesche, 2005). When
teachers developed a sense of multicultural efficacy, there were better
academic and social outcomes from students from all backgrounds
(Taylor & Wendt, 2022). Teachers who demonstrate efficacy for diversity may also be more reflective, and when teachers take the time to
look back on their beliefs and values, they are more likely to have
positive, unbiased attitudes toward their students (Taylor & Wendt,
2022).

4. Methods
4.1. Sample design
In TALIS 2018, 48 countries/economies participated, and data were
collected on two questionnaires: a principal questionnaire and a teacher
questionnaire conducted by the Organisation for Economic CoOperation and Development (OECD, 2019; TALIS 2018 is a two-stage
stratified dataset. The first stage involved selecting schools based on
the estimated number of teachers in each school, and the second stage
involved selecting teachers from the selected schools (OECD, 2019). In
the United States, the main survey consisted of 220 schools and 20
teachers within each school. For the present study, we drew a total of 52
questions from teacher questionnaires representing national samples of
lower secondary teachers (grades 7–9) with an average teacher age of
43-years-old and 15 years of teaching experience. Additionally,
ninety-eight percent (98 %) of all U.S lower secondary teachers have an
educational qualification of bachelor’s degree or higher (OECD, 2019a).
Of the participating schools, the distribution of the schools based on the
regions are Northeast (14.1 %), Midwest (23.5 %), South (46.8 %), and
West (15.6 %) respectively. We obtained 2554 responses out of 2560
teachers who were invited to participate in the survey. Among the survey participants 837 (32.7 %) were male, 1717 (67.1 %) were female,
and 6 (0.2 %) indicated no response. Additionally, drawn data suggested
that 1059 (41.4 %) teachers stated that teaching is their FCT, 1465 (57.2
%) teachers mentioned being a teacher is not their SCT, and 36 (1.4 %)
teachers did not respond at all.

4.2.3. Teachers’ Practices for Diversity Implementation construct (TPD)
The Teachers’ Practices for Diversity Implementation construct was
created using four survey questions that represent schools’ and teachers’
practices in relation to diversity implementation in teaching and
learning. The teachers are asked if the school provides students with an
opportunity to express their diverse and cultural identities, organizes
multicultural events such as cultural diversity day for diverse students,
teaches students skills to deal with cultural and ethnic discrimination,
and adapts curriculum to teach global issues on diversity. The survey
questions are listed in Table 2 with their factor reliability. Teachers were
asked four questions on these issues on a 2-point scale (1 = yes, 2 = no).
Since the questions asked weigh higher point-value for no and lowerpoint value for yes, we have reverse coded to assign higher-value for
yes and lower-value for no using 2-point scale (0 = no, 1 = yes). Selection of these questions for this construct also relied heavily on
research on teaching in diverse classrooms.

4.2. Measures

4.2.4. Teachers’ class composition construct (TCC)
The Teachers’ Class Composition construct was created using three
questions that teachers asked about their class composition and what
percentage of the students in their class identify as multicultural or
ethnically diverse. The teachers were asked to describe students’ native
languages, the students’ socioeconomic status, and if their students are
migrants or refugees. The survey questions for the construct are listed on
Table 2 with factor reliability. Teachers responded on a 5-point scale (1
= none, 2 = 1 %–10 %, 3 = 11 %–30 %, 4 = 31 %–60 %, 5 = more than
60 %). We collapsed these 3 questions to represent two groups for the
teacher class composition on a 2-point scale (0 = no students with diversity, 1 = 1 % or more diverse students in the class), which allowed the
analysis to categorize responses of teachers with experience working

7.1. Teachers’ pathways into the profession in United States: first and

The motive to become a teacher is closely related to how novice

equity (TIP) indicated a higher association with teachers’ preparation
for culturally diverse classes (TCT) on FCTs compared to SCT. Teachers
value job security, time for family, and social equity as the motivational

7.4. Practical implications for practitioners, teachers, and teacher
preparation programs
The results suggest that a teacher’s motivations for choosing the
profession may influence their attitudes toward diverse classrooms. As
such, school leaders should be intentional and strategic in their
recruitment and hiring practices to ensure they are implementing opportunities for training that will help new teachers meet the challenges
of diverse classrooms. For the staff of educator preparation programs,
both traditional and alternative, indicate a need to include a curriculum
that prepares novice teachers to work in diverse classrooms.
Our study raises several practical implications. First, we strongly
argue that for strengthening FCTs and SCTs’ preparation for diverse
classrooms, both traditional and alternative teacher education programs
should include specific instruction and preparation for novice teachers
to gain more experience and exposure to teach diverse classrooms.
Second, our results indicated that the female teachers tend to have lower
variation in their attitudes toward teaching diverse classrooms, as well
as higher importance for job stability, steady career path, and consistent
schedule. To resolve the situation, school principals should bring all the
subgroups together (male/female, FCT/SCT) and cultivate the culture of
openness, shared responsibilities and networking so that all groups
recognize their importance in the educational system. This will help all
teachers see legitimate value to claim their teacher status. Third, teacher
professional development affects teachers’ importance for teaching and
their role of working with students. For instance, female teachers’
exposure to well-designed professional development tended to improve
overall teacher job satisfaction (Toropova et al., 2020). Thus, we argue
that similar opportunities could produce improved results for all the
subgroups (male/female, FCT/SCT).

7.3. Limitations and ideas for future research
To interpret the results, a few limitations must be considered. First,
the study used a dataset from TALIS 2018 which imposed limitations in
terms of statistical analysis. TALIS is a publicly available dataset, and
post hoc analysis is performed, which might not represent the most
significant result for all constructs. Although we conducted various
sensitivity analyses considering this issue and found that our results
surrounding teachers’ pathways to profession did not change substantially. The finding was inconclusive for teachers’ class composition for
diverse classrooms for teacher gender and the construct TCC indicated
low reliability.
