Adolescents’ meaning in life as a resilience factor between bullying
victimization and life satisfaction
Youngwoon Seon *, Sondra Smith-Adcock
School of Human Development and Organizational Studies in Education, University of Florida, USA

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Meaning in life
Bullying victimization
Life satisfaction
Adolescents
Resilience

Meaning in life (MIL) was examined as a resilience factor buffering and compensating for the negative relationship between bullying victimization and life satisfaction using a nationally representative sample of U.S
adolescents from the 2018 round of the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA). Using the latent
moderated structural equations (LMS) approach, results indicated that the interaction effect of bullying
victimization and MIL on life satisfaction was significant with an adequate model fit, suggesting adolescents’ MIL
as a buffer against low life satisfaction associated with bullying victimization. MIL was also significantly related
to life satisfaction, accounting for bullying victimization, suggesting that adolescents’ MIL compensates for the
relationship between bullying victimization and low life satisfaction. Findings suggest the importance of promoting MIL to protect adolescents’ life satisfaction against bullying victimization. Limitations of this study and
future research directions are discussed.

School bullying is a repetitive and intentional pattern of aggressive
behavior based on a power imbalance between children (Centers for
Disease Control and Prevention, 2014; Olweus, 1993). The incidence of
bullying is known to generally peak during early adolescence (Guerra
et al., 2011; Hymel & Swearer, 2015). According to nationally representative surveys in the United States, approximately one in four adolescents are victimized by bullying in schools (OECD, 2019c; Wang et al.,
2020). Experiences related to bullying victimization are associated with
short- and long-term adverse effects for students across physical and
mental health, social, behavioral, and educational functioning (National
Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2016). Therefore,
professionals who work with adolescents who are bullied or at risk of
bullying victimization are responsible for providing appropriate interventions to minimize the detrimental effects of victimization (Carney
et al., 2019). However, existing antibullying programs (e.g., Kärnä et al.,
2013; Olweus & Limber, 2010) rarely include components to help individual students who are bullied to be resilient against the adverse
outcomes of victimization experiences, focusing more prominently on
school-level prevention. For school bullying interventions to be most
effective, adolescents’ individual-level assets should be addressed, in

Because experiencing bullying may threaten adolescents’ overall life
satisfaction, finding ways to build adolescent’s resilience against exposure to school bullying may help prevent reduced life satisfaction.
Fergus and Zimmerman (2005) define adolescent resilience models
that help explain how adolescents who experience adversity overcome
the effects of risk exposure that can lead to resilience processes. According to Fergus and Zimmerman’s framework, resilience processes can
be compensatory or protective. A compensatory factor is a promotive
factor that counteracts the negative effect of a risk factor, having a direct
effect on the outcome independently of the risk factor, whereas a protective factor moderates or buffers the effects of a risk factor on the
outcome. Fergus and Zimmerman postulated that adolescents’ internal
assets (i.e.., individual strengths) or external resources (i.e., social support) may be compensatory or protective. In applying Fergus and Zimmerman’s resilience framework to bullying victimization, it is important
to understand how adolescents’ internal assets or resources compensate
for or buffer the negative effect of bullying victimization on their life
satisfaction. Improving resilience factors (i.e., compensatory or protective factors) may help adolescents maintain relatively high life satisfaction even when they are exposed to bullying. Thus, research may help
elucidate how adolescents’ assets or resources compensate or protect
against the risk of lower life satisfaction associated with exposure to
bullying victimization.
Several researchers have found compensatory factors for the negative relationship between bullying victimization and life satisfaction
among youth (e.g., Alcantara et al., 2017; Guhn et al., 2013; Lázaro-Visa
et al., 2019; Nozaki, 2019). However, prior studies mostly focused on
adolescents’ external resources, such as family, friend, and school
connectedness or support, rather than adolescents’ internal assets,
though individual-level resources are also emphasized as crucial protective factors against bullying (Zych et al., 2019). Moreover, protective
factors buffering the association between bullying exposure and low life
satisfaction have been rarely identified. In a recent literature search, it
appears that only Flouri and Buchanan (2002) found such a protective
factor, identifying that adolescents’ father involvement significantly
buffered the relationship between bullying victimization and low life
satisfaction. To better inform intervention efforts and educational policies for school bullying, research needs to focus on adolescents’ internal
assets as potential compensatory factors and also protective factors
against the risk of decreased life satisfaction associated with bullying
victimization.
As an internal asset that may help adolescents who are exposed to
bullying victimization, we focused on meaning in life (MIL), people’s
beliefs that their lives are comprehensible, significant, and have a purpose (Steger, 2009). MIL has been viewed as a malleable protective
factor for adolescents’ psychological heath (Dulaney et al., 2018; GarcíaAlandete et al., 2019). MIL and life satisfaction are related constructs
that reflect people’s positive perceptions regarding their lives (Steger &
Kashdan, 2007). However, while life satisfaction represents people’s
general evaluation of the quality of their lives, MIL concerns more
specific beliefs that their lives are understandable, important, and purposeful, suggesting MIL and life satisfaction are related but distinguishable constructs. According to Steger and Kashdan (2007), though
many people who believe their lives are meaningful may be satisfied
with their lives, those with high life satisfaction might not necessarily
feel that their lives have a strong meaning.

likely be a compensatory factor that counteracts the negative effect of
adversity on life satisfaction. However, MIL has not been examined that
compensates for the negative relationship between bullying victimization and life satisfaction.
MIL has also been theorized as a stress-buffering resource (Halama,
2014; Hooker et al., 2018; Park, 2010). According to Hooker et al.
(2018), MIL as a stress buffer can be understood by two core components: comprehension and purpose. Comprehension, the belief that
one’s life makes sense, may lessen stress by minimizing the perception
that events as potential stressors are unpredictable and uncontrollable.
Additionally, a strong sense of purpose in life could help people view
negative events as challenges rather than stressors, directing them to
focus on what is most important to them (Hooker et al., 2018), and
reducing the likelihood of dwelling on stressful events and looking toward the future (Blackburn & Owens, 2015). Prior studies have shown
MIL to buffer the relationship between stressful life events and negative
outcomes significantly (e.g., Halama & Bakosova, 2009; Liu et al., 2020;
Szymanski & Mikorski, 2016). For example, in a study among lesbian,
gay, and bisexual persons (Szymanski & Mikorski, 2016), the association
of heterosexist discrimination and psychological distress was significantly moderated by MIL, suggesting MIL as a protective buffer. Liu et al.
(2020) found that MIL significantly buffered the relationship between
abusive supervision and suicidal ideation in Chinese employees.
For adolescents, MIL has also been posited to serve as a personal asset
or a resource of resilience that protects against risk factors to mitigate
adverse outcomes (Benson & Scales, 2018; García-Alandete et al., 2019;
Lin & Shek, 2019). For instance, Dulaney et al. (2018) found that MIL
significantly buffered the relationship between stress exposure and
depression symptoms among U.S. adolescents. Other studies have
examined MIL as a buffer against bullying victimization, a chronic social
stressor (Chen et al., 2018). In one study, Henry et al. (2014) identified
that MIL buffered the relationship between bullying victimization and
suicidal ideation in U.S. adolescents, although the effect was significant
only in male students. In Zhao et al.’s study (2020) among Chinese early
adolescents, MIL significantly buffered the association between bullying
victimization and online game addiction. However, no prior research we
could find examined adolescents’ low life satisfaction associated with
bullying victimization with MIL as a protective buffer.

In testing our hypotheses, gender and socioeconomic status (SES)

2. Method
2.1. Data and sample
We used nationally representative data of U.S. adolescents aged 15
years from the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA)
2018. The PISA dataset is publicly available at the official website of the
Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), http
s://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/. PISA employed a two-stage stratified
design for sampling. At the first stage for the U.S. sample, a total of 164
schools were sampled within eight strata, defined by the combination of
four regions (Northeast, Midwest, South, and West) and two school types
(public and private) using probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling. At the second stage, a total of 4,838 students were selected within
schools with equal probability. Of these students, 2,376 (49.1 %)
responded as female, and 2,462 (50.9 %) answered as male. Most students, 4,636 (95.8 %), were attending a public school, while 202 (4.2 %)
were private school students. A total of 578 (12.0 %), 2,222 (45.9 %),
883 (18.3 %), and 1,155 (23.9 %) students were residing in the Northeast, South, Midwest, and West, respectively. When the final student
weight for national representativeness was applied using IBM SPSS
Statistics 26, the total number of students was 3,559,045 (48.9 % for
females and 51.1 % for males; 93.0 % for public and 7.0 % for private
schools; 15.7 %, 38.8 %, 20.8 %, and 24.7 % for the Northeast, South,
Midwest, and West, respectively).

2.3. Data analysis
To test the hypothesized buffering moderation, we used Mplus 8.4
and employed the latent moderated structural equations approach (LMS;
Klein & Moosbrugger, 2000). The LMS method is currently regarded as
the best method to estimate moderation in that it provides high accuracy
and power and relatively small standard errors, controlling for measurement errors (Cheung et al., 2021). Prior to employing the LMS
method, we evaluated the measurement model through confirmatory
factor analysis (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988). As indicators of the latent
MIL variable, the three individual items of MIL were set. For bullying
victimization, we formed three indicators using item parceling with the
content-based method (Matsunaga, 2008): verbal, physical, and relational bullying victimization (Olweus et al., 2019). Life satisfaction was
included as a single indicator because it was measured by a single item.
To evaluate the overall model fit, we used the comparative fit index
(CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).
Model fit was considered adequate if CFI > 0.95, TLI > 0.95, RMSEA <
0.06, and SRMR < 0.08 (Browne & Cudeck, 1993; Hu & Bentler, 1999).
To assess our LMS model (see Fig. 1), we followed Maslowsky et al.’s
(2015) two-step procedure in which the relative fit of Model 0 (the
structural model without the latent interaction term) and Model 1 (the
LMS model, where the interaction is estimated) is compared. The reason
that Model 0 is estimated is that fit indices, such as CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and
SRMR, are unavailable for an LMS model (i.e., Model 1). Because those
fit indices can be obtained for Model 0, after evaluating Model 0 based
on the indices, researchers can infer the overall fit of Model 1 by
comparing the relative fit of Model 0 and Model 1. For the comparison,
Maslowsky et al. recommended a log-likelihood ratio test. Because loglikelihood ratio test values are approximately distributed as chi-square,
the significance of such a value can be tested in the same way as a chisquare difference test. Due to the use of the MLR estimator, we included
scaling correction factors for MLR in the test calculation (Cheung et al.,
2021). If (a) Model 0 fits the data well and (b) Model 1 is better than
Model 0 in fit, then we can conclude that Model 1 fits the data
adequately. If Model 1 is not significantly better, Model 0, which is more
parsimonious, is preferred.
In estimating models, we used the MLR (maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors) estimator with the TYPE = COMPLEX RANDOM option in Mplus (Cheung et al., 2021), taking into
account the stratification variable and schools as a clustering variable.
This method produces standard errors robust to the non-independence
of observations due to cluster sampling (Muthén & Muthén, 2017).
Though multilevel modeling can also be used to adjust standard errors
for non-independence, using the LMS approach at multiple levels is

2.2. Measures
2.2.1. Bullying victimization
To measure exposure to bullying victimization, PISA 2018 used six
items asking students how often they had been victimized by bullying in
school or social media during the past 12 months. The items include
verbal, physical, and relational forms of bullying, such as, “Other students made fun of me; I was threatened by other students; Other students
took away or destroyed things that belong to me; I got hit or pushed
around by other students; Other students left me out of things on purpose; and Other students spread nasty rumors about me” (OECD, 2019c,
p. 214). Students responded on a scale ranging from 1 to 4 (1 = never or
almost never; 2 = a few times a year; 3 = a few times a month; 4 = once a
week or more). The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.85 in
the present study.
2.2.2. Meaning in life
To measure adolescents’ MIL, PISA 2018 used the three items of the
MIL index reflecting “the extent to which 15-year-olds comprehend,
make sense of, or find significance in their lives” (OECD, 2019c, p. 165).
These items include “My life has clear meaning or purpose; I have
discovered a satisfactory meaning in life; and I have a clear sense of what
gives meaning to my life” (OECD, 2019c, p. 165). Students indicated
how much they agreed or disagreed with each statement on a scale from
1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The Cronbach’s α was 0.89.

overall life satisfaction scale asking, “Overall, how satisfied are you with
your life as a whole these days?” (OECD, 2019b, p. 262). Students

3.3. Structural model
First, Model 0 provided adequate fit to the data, χ 2 (20) = 196.939, p
<.001, CFI = 0.982, TLI = 0.969, RMSEA = 0.043, 90 % CI [0.037,

latent interaction term of bullying victimization and MIL contributed to
a significant gain in fit.
Additionally, shown in Fig. 1, the bullying victimization × MIL
interaction effect was statistically significant in Model 1 (B = 0.231, SE
= 0.054, p <.001, β = 0.074), suggesting that MIL significantly
moderated the relationship between bullying victimization and life
satisfaction. The paths from bullying victimization and MIL to life
satisfaction were also significant; bullying victimization was negatively
related to life satisfaction (B = − 0.369, SE = 0.038, p <.001, β = −
0.168), while MIL was positively and strongly associated with life
satisfaction (B = 1.861, SE = 0.060, p <.001, β = 0.511). Therefore, MIL
significantly counteracted the negative relationship between bullying
victimization and life satisfaction, supporting MIL as a compensatory
factor as hypothesized. To visualize the moderation effect, we plotted
the relationship between bullying victimization and life satisfaction at
three values of MIL: M – SD, M, M + SD (see Fig. 2). The plotting shows
that the relationship weakened as the MIL score increased, supporting
the study hypothesis that MIL would buffer the relationship between
bullying victimization and low life satisfaction.

lower MIL, meaning that the harmful effect of bullying victimization on
adolescents’ life satisfaction is buffered by a stronger sense of MIL.
Theoretically, current findings are consistent with the idea that MIL
is a basic psychological need that helps people face adversity (Frankl,
1969; Hadden & Smith, 2019; Klinger, 1977). With this fundamental
need fulfilled, adolescents with a strong sense of MIL may be more
satisfied with their lives (Steger, 2018), and maintain relatively high life
satisfaction even when victimized by bullying than those with a lower
sense of MIL. That is, MIL may serve to compensate for lower life
satisfaction when adolescents are exposed to bullying. Our findings also
support the stress-buffering hypothesis of MIL, in which MIL is assumed
to contribute to an individual’s psychological health as a stress-buffering
asset (Halama, 2014; Hooker et al., 2018; Park, 2010). In our model,
bullying victimization is a social stressor (Chen et al., 2018), and life
satisfaction is a crucial indicator of psychological health (Proctor et al.,
2017). Furthermore, when adolescents have a high level of MIL, it may
work as a personal asset helping adolescents to handle the stress of
bullying events more successfully and experience satisfaction with life.
Specifically, when adolescents have higher levels of MIL, this sense of
meaning may help them lessen the perception that their life events are
uncontrollable, and may help them to look toward the future, not
dwelling on the negative events, but having a strong sense of the purpose
and direction of their life (Blackburn & Owens, 2015; Hooker et al.,
2018). Findings for the current study extend our understanding of MIL
and its relationship to adolescents’ satisfaction in life, and situates MIL
as a construct that may change the association between bullying experiences and young people’s life satisfaction.
4.1. Implications for practice
From an adolescent resilience framework (Fergus & Zimmerman,
2005), our findings suggest two possible roles of MIL in adolescents that
may help adolescents navigate the stress of bullying and its effect on
their well-being: as a protective factor buffering the relationship between bullying victimization (i.e., a risk factor) and low life satisfaction
(i.e., an adverse outcome) and as a compensatory factor counteracting
that negative relationship. Supporting both compensatory and protective effects of MIL on life satisfaction in adolescents, current findings
highlight the importance of promoting adolescents’ MIL as a personal
asset helping them be resilient against decreases in life satisfaction that
are often associated with bullying victimization. However, much like
prior research, antibullying programs that are currently widely used (e.
g., Kärnä et al., 2013; Olweus & Limber, 2010) rarely address adolescents’ assets as resilience factors against bullying victimization, focusing
more prominently on promoting external resources (e.g., family, peer,
and teacher support) or environmental protective factors (e.g., antibullying classroom climate or school policy). Our findings suggest
antibullying interventions need to include components focused on
enhancing adolescents’ MIL as an internal asset in addition to elements
for facilitating their external resources. Because our findings are
explanatory, we caution direct interpretation of the current findings for
informing antibullying interventions. However, we believe that educational professionals who work with adolescents who are bullied may
consider how meaning in life may protect adolescents’ life satisfaction in
the case of bullying exposure. In this way, promoting MIL may buffer
and compensate for the loss of adolescents’ life satisfaction by bully
victimization. Further, school policies may be reconceptualized to focus
on students’ assets, intentionally expanding on current antibullying to
include assess to programs for building students’ personal assets, with a
focus on meaning in life.

4.2. Limitations and future research directions
The current study expands on prior research by finding that MIL
buffers and compensates for the negative relationship between bullying
victimization and life satisfaction in adolescents. Nevertheless, limitations of this study need to be discussed for careful interpretation of the
findings and to provide direction for future research. First, our findings
were based on cross-sectional data, so causal inferences cannot be made.
A longitudinal research design would be beneficial to come closer to
establishing causality. Additionally, we used a nationally representative
sample of U.S adolescents who were 15 years old, so caution should be
taken when generalizing the results to other population groups. The
findings should be replicated in more diverse samples, such as other age
groups or students in other countries, to expand generalizability. Lastly,
due to the use of relatively small numbers of measurement items, the
measures of study variables could be potentially limited. In particular, a
single-item approach was used to measure adolescents’ life satisfaction.
Though several researchers have acknowledged the reliability and validity of a single-item life satisfaction scale (Diener, 1984; OECD, 2019a;
Tang, 2019), a multi-item approach could provide more accuracy (e.g.,
Huebner, 1991; Seligson et al., 2003). In replicating our findings, it may
be worth using other validated measurement scales that consist of more
items (e.g., Huebner, 1991; Olweus, 1996; Steger et al., 2006).
Further, while we treated bullying victimization as a multidimensional construct, future researchers may want to address specific forms
of bullying victimization (i.e., verbal, physical, and relational bullying
victimization; Olweus et al., 2019) as separate constructs. In doing so,
researchers could identify how adolescents’ MIL plays a differential role
between bullying victimization and life satisfaction depending on
victimization types, which could further inform antibullying
