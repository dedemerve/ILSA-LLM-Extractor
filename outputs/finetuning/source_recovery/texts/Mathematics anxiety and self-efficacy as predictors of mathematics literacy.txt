ABSTRACT

ARTICLE HISTORY

Prior research has suggested that mathematics specific anxiety and
self-efficacy are strong predictors of mathematics performance. The
purpose of this study was to examine this relationship using two
nationally representative samples of Greek students in 2003 and
2012. Findings indicated that both mathematics anxiety and mathematics self-efficacy have showed a significant relationship with mathematics performance, and when these variables were incorporated
in prediction models, as much as 34% of the total variation in mathematics performance was explained. However, socioeconomic status
was the most potent predictor of mathematics literacy. Findings indicated the presence of a strong interaction effect between mathematics anxiety and mathematics self-efficacy. Educational implications
and directions for future research are discussed.

1. Introduction
The relationship between mathematics anxiety and self-efficacy has been an ongoing focus
of research and is relevant to the study of mathematics education in countries throughout the world, such as Australia, Germany, Greece, and the United States (Goetz et al.,
2013; Kriegbaum & Spinath, 2016; McConney & Perry, 2010; Pitsia et al., 2017; Stevens
et al., 2004). A variety of research studies have suggested that mathematics anxiety threatens the development of positive self-efficacy in mathematics (Fan et al., 2019; Wang et al.,
2020; Wigfield & Meece, 1988). Overall, the impact of anxiet on student mathematics
self-efficacy, and ultimately student performance, limits student aspirations.
Mathematics anxiety refers to a student’s apprehension in engaging in mathematics
related activities and can be magnified based on prior negative experiences or setbacks
within mathematics instruction (Ashcraft & Moore, 2009; Bandura, 1997). That being
said, anxiety is a persistent threat to a student’s mathematics performance (Chan &
Bauer, 2014; Lee & Cho, 2018; Simzar et al., 2015), regardless of individual differences,
cultural backgrounds, or geographic location. Eager students who develop mathematics

of study, or dropout from Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics (STEM)
educational programmes entirely (Johnson & Niemi, 2015; Johnson & O’Keeffe, 2016;
Moses et al., 2011).
Mathematics anxiety can typically vary based on performance assessments or
classroom-based tasks (Luttenberger et al., 2018) and is reflective of test taking concerns
and/or a fear of failure for specific requirements. For example, students may exhibit domain
related anxiety for problem solving with proportional reasoning, yet be quite comfortable
and proficient at solving and graphing algebraic equations. In these instances, assessing
mathematics anxiety can have multi-tiered facets which correspond with subject strands,
individual experiences, and self-motivational beliefs.
On the other hand, research on self-efficacy, which refers to the extent to which an individual believes in their capabilities to accomplish a specific task (Bandura, 1997), shows
positive correlations with achievement outcomes and influences student motivation to
engage in learning (Bandura, 1997; Zimmerman & Kitsantas, 1999). Self-efficacy is susceptible to high levels of anxiety (Bandura, 1997; Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2020). In fact,
the combination of increased anxiety and lowered self-efficacy in mathematics hinders science progress, diminishes motivation, and threatens potential success in advanced courses
(Chan & Bauer, 2014).
In general, students with higher self-efficacy exhibit pronounced levels of competency
for mathematics problem solving, while students hampered by mathematics anxiety are
less able to complete more complex mathematics processes (Lee & Cho, 2018; Pajares
& Miller, 1994). From a practical perspective, when a learner encounters mathematics
anxiety in the classroom over multiple experiences, their ability to grasp specific topics
diminishes. Mastery experiences, along with anxiety, are sources of self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997). For this reason, a student in school who has anxiety and cannot demonstrate
proficiency at mathematics task over the course of several attempts, most certainly will
have diminished self-efficacy. Since mathematics anxiety and self-efficacy often correlate,
some researchers have conceptually integrated mathematics anxiety and self-efficacy as one
construct. Wigfield and Meece (1988) concluded that these two constructs are distinct; students with mathematics anxiety have detectable emotional reactions distinguishable from
their perceptions of mathematics capabilities.
Both mathematics anxiety and self-efficacy are particularly vulnerable to factors such
as socioeconomic status and gender (Cheema & Galluzzo, 2013; Karakolidis et al., 2016;
McConney & Perry, 2010; Pitsia et al., 2017). These factors are prevalent across national
backgrounds (Lee, 2009), and as with other adolescents around the world, is an acute
concern within the Greek educational framework (Cheema, 2018; Kalaycioğlu, 2015;
Karakolidis et al., 2016; McConney & Perry, 2010; Pitsia et al., 2017).
The present study focuses on relationships among mathematics anxiety, self-efficacy
and literacy while controlling for socioeconomic status and gender for Greek high school
students. As opposed to mathematics achievement, which focuses on what a student has
learned, mathematics literacy related to how a student can use the knowledge gained in
school in order to solve real life mathematics problems in everyday life (OECD, 2003).
Below, we provide a review of the literature of the targeted constructs.

1.1. Self-efficacy, anxiety, and mathematics achievement
When considering students’ mathematic achievement, there is a complex network of factors beyond demographics and performance. Specifically, there are integral associations
related to prior experiences, intellectual capabilities, and motivational constructs, such as
self-efficacy (Kriegbaum & Spinath, 2016). Anxiety, as an emotional state, can serve as an
important source of a one’s self-efficacy beliefs (Stevens et al., 2004). When a learner is
experiencing anxiety about their capabilities to solve a problem or take a test in mathematics, this negative emotion can lower their self-efficacy beliefs and lead to lower levels
of performance. In this regard, self-efficacy beliefs mediate the influence of anxiety and
academic outcomes (Cheema, 2018; Wigfield & Meece, 1988) and predict mathematics
achievement to an even greater extent; students who believe they can perform a task are
more likely to be successful (Pajares & Miller, 1994).
In a study of motivation and achievement for adolescent students in mathematics, Wigfield and Meece (1988), studied mathematics anxiety for students (N = 564) in Grades
6–12. Across grade levels and gender, students demonstrated concern over underperformance in mathematics. The intimation of this study was that students who are overly
anxious manifest negative emotions, which have a paralyzing impact in terms of attention
focusing and mastery of classroom-based tasks.
In another illustration, Stevens et al. (2004) analyzed the predictive nature of self-efficacy
in mathematics for ninth and 10th grade students in Texas using path analysis. For both
White and Hispanic students (n = 317), self-efficacy served as a mediator between ability
and mathematics performance, interest, and course selection. Hispanic students perceived
they had less capabilities in mathematics than their White peers, and these students were
influenced more heavily from past performance in mathematics courses. Researchers concluded that for both groups, self-efficacy was a critical connection to performance and
that Hispanic students likely had lower self-efficacy due to limited mastery experiences in
previous coursework, thereby shaping student perceptions of their capabilities.
As a parallel of the two U.S.-based studies, numerous works have highlighted the integral
relationship between mathematics self-efficacy and anxiety across international boundaries for secondary students. For example, Schulz (2005) examined the role of mathematics
self-efficacy, self-concept, and anxiety in conjunction with mathematics literacy based on
the 2003 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) with data from 40 countries. Self-efficacy showed the strongest correlation with test performance consistently
across these countries. Self-concept and anxiety were strongly influenced by the school
context of the individual (e.g. self-comparison with peers and demands within schools and
study programmes), while there was a strong relationship between socioeconomic status,
self-efficacy, and mathematics literacy (Schulz, 2005).
Across the 2000 and 2003 PISAs, Lee (2009) investigated whether mathematics anxiety, self-efficacy, and self-concept were discernible, particularly in diverse cultural contexts
with 250,000 15-year-old students across 41 countries. Findings showed that students
from Asian countries demonstrated low mathematics self-concept, self-efficacy and high
mathematics anxiety, whereas learners from western countries showed high mathematics self-efficacy and low mathematics anxiety. Lee attributed these differences to societal/educational expectations within the country. Specifically, in Asian countries, academic
achievement is highly valued resulting in competitive situations with students reporting

higher anxiety, lower self-concept, and lower self-efficacy for mathematics performance.
In contrast, students from Western countries tended to be less critical of their academic
performance and less tense than students in other parts of the world. National identification, self-efficacy and anxiety were critical in the establishment of high levels of student
advancement.
Levels of achievement in comparison with mathematics anxiety can fluctuate based on
the countries of interest and changes over time. Fan et al. (2019) examined mathematics
anxiety for Korean students in comparison to American and Finnish students with the
2012 PISA. Fan et al. assessed low, medium, and high levels of mathematics anxiety for
15-year-old students (N = 11,0003). Analyses revealed that American students had high
mathematics anxiety compared to North Korean students, most likely because of lower performance in mathematics. The Finnish students had the highest achievement and lowest
mathematics anxiety of the three groups. These authors surmised that mathematics anxiety
can have diminishing effects of achievement, regardless of cultural backgrounds.
Cheema (2018) narrowed the focus of the 2012 PISA by examining the relationship
between mathematics anxiety, self-efficacy, and literacy as well as a variety of demographic
factors such as gender, family wealth, and school socioeconomic status in Greece with 2748
participants. Findings from bivariate analysis and hierarchal regression revealed that there
was a significant positive relationship between mathematics self-efficacy and literacy, while
controlling for demographic factors. A marked finding within Greek schools was that students in private schools had higher mean scores on all variables than students in public
schools. As the impact of self-efficacy on mathematics in a Greek context was substantial,
Cheema recommended that future studies of Greek student performance in mathematics
should include measures related to self-efficacy.
These works offer insight into the dynamics of student mathematics self-efficacy, anxiety, and achievement, irrespective of societal differences. Specifically, students who do
not have positive experiences learning new tasks over time develop anxiety and are less
able to be proficient in the classroom and in test taking; this cycle is one that educators
should strive to avoid for their students. The relationship between anxiety and student performance often becomes more pronounced when considering differences among student
groups based on gender and socioeconomic status. Below, we examine empirical research
on the relationships among these variables.
1.2. Gender differences, socioeconomic status, and mathematics achievement
Mathematics self-efficacy serves as an underpinning of student achievement and is predictive of student success across a variety of variables such as demographics (age, gender,
and socioeconomic status), motivational factors, cultural contexts, and forms of assessments (Cheema & Galluzzo, 2013; Fan et al., 2019; Goetz et al., 2013; Schulz, 2005; Simzar
et al., 2015). For exFor example, students from wealthier countries, especially those from
families with higher socioeconomic status, outperform their peers due to greater access to
literature, increased family involvement, as well as a greater value placed on mathematics
(Cheema, 2018; Chiu & Xihua, 2008; Pitsia et al., 2017).
Gender related motivational variances stemming from self-efficacy and anxiety continue to emerge in elementary and secondary schools. A number of studies have examined
the mediating role of gender in the dynamics of mathematics self-efficacy, anxiety, and

achievement. For instance, Goetz et al. (2013) assessed the role gender plays in regards to
mathematics anxiety through elementary and secondary grades in Germany. Participants
included students in Grades 5–10 (N = 584) in a first study, and (N = 111) students in
Grades 8 and 11 for the second study. In the first study, test anxiety was the focus of data
collection. In the second study, the measures targeted anxiety for class-based mathematics
tasks. Throughout these grade levels, significant differences existed with females experiencing greater mathematics anxiety due to their lower levels of perceived mathematics
competence as an enduring self-motivational belief. Interestingly, females exhibited anxiety for specific classroom-based activities; however, there were no gender differences for
anxiety in relationship to test taking.
In terms of mathematics attainment, Liu and Wilson (2009) examined gender differences on the 2000 and 2003 PISA based on mathematics domains and assessment types and
found that while males outperformed females, the effect sizes were small for both years. In
addition, there were no differences on multiple choice items; however, the largest difference was for complex multiple-choice items. This difference in performance was possibly
attributable to that unfamiliar problems may create some emotional setbacks for females,
resulting in lowered performance.
Furthermore, in consideration of mathematics application, Schulz’s (2005) findings,
which centered on gender-based differences of self-efficacy, socioeconomic status and
mathematics, revealed that females had lower self-efficacy than males, with an even more
pronounced gap in mathematics literacy. That is to say, females with lowered mathematical
literacy had less confidence in applying and interpreting mathematical concepts in varied
situations.
Moreover, in an analysis of 2003 PISA data with American students (N = 4,733),
Cheema and Galluzzo (2013) analyzed the role of race, socioeconomic status, mathematics anxiety, and mathematics self-efficacy as predictors of mathematics achievement,
while controlling for age, disability, and language proficiency. From this work, clear factors
emerged which created inequities in mathematics performance. Racial and socioeconomic
differences, mathematics anxiety, and self-efficacy all contributed significantly to variances in mathematics achievement. Moreover, self-efficacy and anxiety for mathematics
created differences in gender performance. Evidence that the gender gap lessens when
researchers control for mathematics self-efficacy and mathematics anxiety emerged as
these two variables accounted for 19% of the variance in achievement (Cheema & Galluzzo,
2013).
With a focus on Australian students, McConney and Perry (2010) analyzed the 2003
PISA data using multilevel modelling to interpret the impact of socioeconomic status and
mathematics self-efficacy with mathematics achievement. Findings showed that notwithstanding self-efficacy levels or individual socioeconomic status, student performance
was most closely associated with the school level socioeconomic status. These findings
might indicate that those students who demonstrate high self-efficacy and low individual
socioeconomic status, are most likely to benefit from a high socioeconomic school.
While a variety of studies highlight researcher conclusions based on the 2000 and 2003
PISA, mathematics anxiety was a factor in a study based on student self-reports of course
grades. For Italian secondary students (N = 1043), Wang et al. (2020) determined that
gender moderated direct paths between mathematics anxiety and student self-perceptions
of mathematics capabilities. Overall, mathematics anxiety had a more pronounced effect

In conjunction with transnational work centered on student mathematics anxiety, selfefficacy, and literacy, recent studies have examined the relationship of these constructs
within Greek instructional settings. To assess educational progress in mathematics from a
Greek perspective, the PISA data are particularly instrumental as there are limited national
criteria through formal audits to assess educational progress and limited research on interplay between achievement and student motivation (Pitsia et al., 2017). As a representation
of this line of research, Kalaycioğlu (2015) found that mathematics self-efficacy and mathematics anxiety corresponded with mathematics achievement across six countries with
self-efficacy as a dominant predictor of mathematics achievement for students from varied
national backgrounds. For countries reflective of students with high mathematics performance (Hong Kong, England, and the Netherlands), there was no statistically significant
path coefficients for mathematics anxiety. However, for countries with lower mathematics
performance (Turkey, Greece, and the United States), anxiety had a statistically significant path coefficient with student achievement. These findings further support that as
mathematics anxiety increases, self-efficacy and mathematics performance diminishes.
In a subsequent study, Usta (2016) compared mathematics achievement and motivation
between students in Greece, China-Shanghai, and Turkey with the 2012 PISA. Usta examined both school level and student level differences related to self-efficacy for adolescents
(N = 5177, N = 4818, and N = 50,279 in China-Shanghai, Turkey and Greece respectively). In terms of mathematics self-efficacy, Greek students outperformed students from
China-Shanghai and Turkey. An advantage for Greek students was that preschool experiences had an overall positive effect compared to their counterparts in other countries (Usta,
2016).
Another study focused on the Greek education system (Karakolidis et al., 2016) assessed
the impact of achievement predictors in a sample of Greek adolescent students in mathematics using the 2012 PISA. Participants consisted of 5,125 students from 192 schools.
Findings showed that differences between students accounted for 58.7% of the variance in
mathematics achievement for Greek students, while school-level differences accounted for
41.3% of variance explained for achievement in mathematics. For gender differences, males
demonstrated advanced performance in mathematics as compared to females, though the
overall findings indicated the gender gap had narrowed since prior research. In regards
to motivational factors, Greek students performed much better when they had higher
self-concept and self-efficacy as well as lower anxiety for mathematics.
In a subsequent study of the 2012 PISA 2012, Pitsia et al. (2017) analyzed mathematics performance for Greek students (N = 5125) based on a variety of factors, including
demographic variables (e.g. gender and socioeconomic status at school level); mathematics anxiety, self-efficacy, and self-concept; intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, attitudes
towards school; and mathematics literacy as an achievement measure. In this case, the
greatest predictor of mathematics achievement was the type of school students attended
based on socioeconomic status. Mathematics self-efficacy was the strongest predictor of
achievement while mathematics anxiety and self-concept had strong associations with

achievement predictions. While Karakolidis and colleagues (2016) reported that disparities in gender performance (in favour of males) had improved from previous years, Pitsia
et al. cited that the low levels of self-efficacy, self-concept, and achievement, as well as high
levels of anxiety for female students was indicative of substantial gender inequities within
the Greek educational context (Pitsia et al., 2017).
In corroboration, Cheema (2018) identified a continued relationship between mathematics anxiety, self-efficacy, and literacy based on the 2012 PISA when controlling for
individual and school-based variables. A marked finding within Greek schools was that
students in private schools had higher mean scores on all variables than students in public
schools and that there were significant differences in mathematics literacy based on gender
with males outperforming females.
Overall, previous research consistently demonstrates that mathematics self-efficacy is
predictive of student advancement, and that anxiety can hamper both self-efficacy and
achievement. In addition, there is a strong negative relationship between mathematics anxiety and student performance with additional variances attributed to gender and
socioeconomic status differences. To our knowledge, limited research has been conducted
on the relationship of mathematics self-efficacy, anxiety, and literacy, particularly within a
Greek context as established by the present study. In the present study, it was hypothesized
that both mathematics anxiety and self-efficacy would have a significant relationship with
mathematics literacy while accounting for individual student characteristics, such as grade
level, gender, and socioeconomic status.

2. Method
2.1. Data source
The sample used in this study was extracted from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2003 and 2012 student data files (OECD, 2012b), which are openly
available in the PISA database at https://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/. There were no potential
competing interests in this study by the authors. Selection of both years extended research
on an international level and in the Greek context based on previous work with the 2003
and 2012 PISA data sets. PISA is an international assessment of 15-year-old students in
key areas of literacy such as mathematics, reading, and science. For 15 years, more than 70
countries, including both OCED and non-OECD members, have participated in its administration. PISA surveys are administered in 3-year cycles, and although all three literacy
areas are assessed in each survey year, one of these strands is selected as an area of a focus
in any given year. Mathematics literacy was the area of focus in 2003 and 2012 survey years.
In addition to routine assessment and demographic information components, in these 2
survey years, participants completed items on a variety of mathematics-related measures
representing student attitudes, perceptions, and beliefs. PISA samples center on a complex
random sampling scheme which render them representative of their target populations.
Thus, any findings based on these samples are generalizable to the national population of
15-year-old students in Greece.
PISA sub-samples for Greece from survey years 2003 and 2012 included 4627 and 5125
students enrolled in 171 and 188 schools respectively. These 9752 students enrolled in 359
schools are representative of a total of 201,771 students (M = 100,885, SD = 6004) and

5293 schools (M = 2646, SD = 375). The original dataset contained missing values on
several variables of interest. Since the proportion of missing data did not exceed 20% on
any individual variable, we imputed all missing values. For this purpose, we used an iterative Markov Chain Maximum Likelihood imputation method based on an assumption of
arbitrary missingness. This method is implemented in two separate stages. In the first stage
a prediction model is built for each individual variable. The chosen variable in this model
is the outcome while all other variables act as predictors. The prediction model produces
predicted values for the chosen variable. These steps are repeated for each individual variable. Once all the predicted values have been obtained, in the second stage these values
are used jointly using a maximum likelihood algorithm to impute missing values for all
variables (Cheema, 2014).
2.2. Measures
2.2.1. Mathematics literacy
Mathematics literacy in PISA was assessed with a variety of items administered in various
formats, such as multiple choice and open-ended questions, and literacy scores for each
student were reported as a set of five plausible values. These plausible values represent random draws from the distribution of all possible scores that can be reasonably attributed
to a student (Mislevy, 1991; Mislevy et al., 1992). In order to properly deal with plausible values, either the entire analysis should be conducted with one plausible value chosen
at random or conducted as many times as there are plausible values and the results combined using special mathematics formulas (Brown & Micklewright, 2004; von Davier et al.,
2009). In this study, we followed the former approach. For technical details of PISA assessment methodology, including samples and psychometric properties of assessment items
and procedures adopted for plausible value estimation, we refer the interested reader to
OECD (2003, 2006, 2012a). Following the practice used by PISA for reporting literacy performance, mathematics literacy scores in our sample were standardized to have a mean of
500 and a standard deviation of 100 with individual student scores ranging from 117.81 to
832.61.
2.2.2. Mathematics self-efficacy
This scale was based on eight items that measured a student’s self-efficacy when performing mathematics related tasks. A sample item included, ‘How confident do you feel about
having to do the following calculations’? Response choices for each item were 1(very confident), 2 (confident), 3 (not very confident), and 4 (not at all confident). In this scale, the
higher values are representative of higher self-efficacy. This variable was standardized in
our sample with a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1, and its values range between
−3.87 and 2.89. The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was .75 in our 2003 sample, and .83 in
the 2012 sample.
2.2.3. Mathematics anxiety
This scale included five items that measure a student’s anxiety when performing mathematics related activities. The stem statement included, ‘How much do you disagree or
agree with the following statement about how you feel when studying mathematics?’ A
sample item included, ‘I get very nervous doing mathematics problems.’ Response choices

INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF MATHEMATICAL EDUCATION IN SCIENCE AND TECHNOLOGY

2141

for each item were 1(strongly agree), 2 (agree), 3 (disagree), and 4 (strongly disagree). For
scale construction, responses on all items were inverted. Thus, higher values on this scale
were representative of higher anxiety. This variable was standardized in our sample with a
mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1, and its values range between −2.74 and 2.66. The
Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was .80 in the 2003 sample, and .81 in the 2012 sample.
2.3. Demographic controls
2.3.1. Socioeconomic status
This variable measures a student’s socioeconomic status and was extracted from PISA student data files as an as an index of economic, cultural, and social rank. Socioeconomic
status is based on three subscales which are parental education, parental occupation, and
an index of home possessions. The reported reliability of this measure in PISA surveys has
ranged between .71 to .73 for Greece. For a detailed technical discussion of how this measure was created and its psychometric properties, we refer the interested reader to OECD
(2003, 2009, 2012a). The variable was scaled to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation
of 1 in our sample with values ranging between −3.72 and 3.36.
2.3.2. Gender
This nominal variable represents a student’s gender and can take two values, male and
female. Our overall sample included 4,773 boys (48.9%), and 4,979 girls (51.1%).
2.3.3. Grade
This ordinal variable captures a student’s school grade level and can take values from 7 to
11. In our sample of 9,752 students, 28 were in Grade 7 (0.3%), 156 were in Grade 8 (1.6%),
507 were in Grade 9 (5.2%), 8,367 were in Grade 10 (85.8%), and 695 were in Grade 11
(7.1%).
2.3.4. Survey year
This is an indicator variable for the year in which survey data were collected. Of the students included in our sample 4627 (47.4%) responded in 2003, and 5125 (52.6%) responded
in 2012.
2.4. Analytical method
We computed descriptive statistics and correlations for all variables included in our study.
In order to predict mathematics literacy from mathematics anxiety, self-efficacy, and demographic controls, we estimated two ordinary least squares multiple regression models.
The first of these models predicted mathematics literacy from demographic controls only.
The purpose of this model was to estimate the variation in mathematics literacy that is
attributable to demographic differences among students such as grade, gender, and socioeconomic status. In addition to these three demographic variables, we included survey year
as a predictor. This inclusion was justified because the sample data came from 2 different
years. By including this variable as a predictor in our model, we were basically controlling
for changes in macro level factors of mathematics literacy that affect all students equally
in any given survey year (e.g. change in national educational policies). Our first regression

4. Discussion
Prior research has suggested that mathematics-specific anxiety and self-efficacy are important predictors of mathematics progress (Fast et al., 2010; Luo et al., 2014; Simzar et al.,
2015; Stevens et al., 2004; Wigfield & Meece, 1988). Moreover, a decline in mathematics performance assessments often relates to anxiety, which diminishes conceptual

2146

S. E. HILLER ET AL.

understanding and mastery of learning (Luttenberger et al., 2018). Identifying sources of
mathematics anxiety for varied ability levels is a responsive approach to addressing student
academic needs while fostering mathematics self-efficacy and literacy.
We examined this tripartite relationship using two nationally representative samples of
Greek students collected in 2003 and 2012. In the present research study, both mathematics anxiety and mathematics self-efficacy had a significant relationship with mathematics
literacy, explaining as much as 34% of the total variation in student mathematics literacy. As a result, these findings highlight the presence of a moderate interaction effects
between mathematics anxiety and mathematics self-efficacy and have several far-reaching
implications.
Specially, mathematics self-efficacy and mathematics anxiety together explained
approximately 14% of the variation in mathematics literacy. A 1 SD change in either variable caused mathematics literacy to change by 1/5 to 1/4 of a standard deviation, which
is quite a large effect. The omission of these two variables from any model that seeks
to examine mathematics performance has the potential to cause a serious specification
error and biases related to such error. As with previous literature related to mathematics achievement, self-efficacy and anxiety are critical elements of student mathematics
progress (Lee, 2009; McConney & Perry, 2010; Wigfield & Meece, 1988). As such, mastery
experiences in classroom settings in conjunction with teacher responsiveness, or other positive social interactions, are key guideposts for bolstering student mathematics proficiency
through heightened self-efficacy (Fast et al., 2010; Usher & Pajares, 2009). From a practical perspective, struggling students of mathematics benefit from a scaffolding approach
to instruction, providing multifaceted opportunities to build mastery for mathematical
processes and problem-solving applications, particularly by using various modalities of
instruction (e.g. manipulatives) and authentic real-world contexts (Luttenberger et al.,
2018). This approach builds on mathematics self-efficacy and mathematics literacy while
diminishing mathematics anxiety.
Secondly, the significant interaction between self-efficacy and anxiety suggests that even
though improvement in self-efficacy improves mathematics literacy, at all levels of anxiety,
a most pronounced effect occurs when the anxiety level is medium. In other words, some
anxiety when dealing with mathematics related problems may sometimes be helpful. Too
little, or too much, anxiety on the other hand negatively affects the positive relationship
between self-efficacy and mathematics performance. This pattern in the interactive effect
of anxiety and self-efficacy on mathematics performance may be attributable to highly
anxious students being unable to sufficiently concentrate or focus when dealing with mathematics related problems, and not anxious students being either over confident or not
taking the mathematics problems seriously. Notably, achievers often seem more comfortable in classroom settings than low performing students, they often underestimate their
actual anxiety. The implications are that although both groups may experience anxiety,
the overall self-concept of high achievers buffers negative effects of anxiety (Roos et al.,
2015).
4.1. Gender differences and socioeconomic status
When examining gender differences, females typically exhibit greater mathematics anxiety for test taking and task specific requirements (Luttenberger et al., 2018). In fact, within

INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF MATHEMATICAL EDUCATION IN SCIENCE AND TECHNOLOGY

2147

an Italian setting, there is support that female students develop mathematics anxiety in
primary and early secondary school even when there are no differences in mathematics performance (Hill et al., 2016). From a Greek perspective, the present study generally
mirrors the findings of the Karakolidis et al. (2016) and Pitsia et al. (2017) studies which
utilized the 2012 PISA assessment data to examine mathematics achievement among 15year-old students in Greece. The Karakolidis et al. study was more exploratory wherein the
researchers examined background characteristics (gender, economic social cultural status
(ESCS), immigrant status, early childhood education), self-constructs (mathematics selfconcept, mathematics self-efficacy, mathematics anxiety), and the role of school. The Pitsia
et al. study examined the same constructs, while including gender differences. The overall
findings of both studies were similar. Notably, Pitsia et al. found gender gaps in achievement that could not be explained by their model, even after accounting for variables such
as mathematics self-efficacy, self-concept, and anxiety.
In the present study, gender differences and socioeconomic status, as well as mathematics anxiety and mathematics self-efficacy, were all examined as predictors of mathematics
literacy. Our findings were similar to former studies within a Greek setting (Cheema, 2018;
Karakolidis et al., 2016; Pitsia et al., 2017) in that socioeconomic status was a strong predictor of mathematics performance. Unadjusted findings showed males outperforming
females in mathematics literacy. Although our model, in contrast to Pitsia et al., shows
the gender gap disappears when self-efficacy and mathematics anxiety are held constant.
The existence of higher mathematics anxiety for females and higher mathematics selfefficacy for males are still factors that cannot be excluded, but the results of our model
demonstrated when all is equal, there is no difference in mathematics literacy between
females and males. This outcome aligns with the findings of prior literature (Cheema, 2018;
Cheema & Galluzzo, 2013).
Although given the non-experimental nature of our study, this result is not sufficient to
rule out the possibility that females tend to get more anxious as compared to males when
dealing with mathematics-related problems, or that males tend to have higher mathematics self-efficacy than females. These findings showed that in any two groups of males and
females that are comparable in terms of demographic differences, self-efficacy, and anxiety,
there is no performance difference in mathematics.
Finally, we found that socioeconomic status remains one of the most significant predictors of mathematics performance. In our samples, socioeconomic status by itself was
responsible for explaining large chunks of total variation in mathematics performance.
In our full model, in absolute terms the magnitude of effect of socioeconomic status on
mathematics performance was very similar to that of mathematics anxiety and mathematics self-efficacy. Thus, for any two groups of students that are equal in all respects
(including efficacy and anxiety) except socioeconomic status, we expect high socioeconomic status students to perform better on mathematics related tasks. The present study
replicates the debilitating effect of socioeconomic status on mathematics performance and
self-motivational beliefs (Cheema & Galluzzo, 2013; Kalaycioğlu, 2015; Karakolidis et al.,
2016; McConney & Perry, 2010; Wigfield & Meece, 1988). Indeed, for Greek students, the
negative impact of low socioeconomic status aligns with global studies, as well as research
specific to the country of Greece.

4.2. Limitations and implications for future research
The findings of the present study may only be generalizable to Greek educational contexts.
Furthermore, information on measures such as self-efficacy, anxiety, and socioeconomic
status were self-reported by students. Although internal reliabilities of these measures were
high in our sample, our statistical results and conclusions derived from those results are
accurate only to the extent to which the self-reported information is accurate. One caution
that applies to our statistical findings is the assumption of a one-way relationship between
achievement and its psychological predictors. Although this hypothesis is grounded in
recent research, the possibility of a reverse or even a bi-directional relationship between
achievement and related predictors should be examined in future research.
To extend the findings of the current study, interested researchers should focus on
replicating these results with samples from countries other than Greece, subject areas
other than mathematics, and target populations other than 15-year-old students. The
implications of this work strengthen the notion that students with increased opportunities to heighten self-efficacy overcome anxiety threats. In this instance, students with
a medium range of anxiety, benefitted the most from raised self-efficacy. Interventions
which include targeted self-regulatory strategies (e.g. attention focusing, help seeking, elaboration, rehearsal), mastery experiences, and instructor mentoring and modelling may
inherently build self-efficacy while minimizing anxiety (Hiller & Kitsantas, 2015, Pape
et al., 2013).
In the endeavor to diminish the negative impact of anxiety while fostering increased
mastery experiences, students benefit from instructors who guide students to learn how
to reflect, tackle mathematics activities, and then monitor their level of performance to
advance from novice levels of understanding to heightened expertise (Zimmerman & Kitsantas, 1999). Incorporating self-regulatory strategies, particularly in the middle school
years, encourages strong motivational beliefs and serves as a necessary precursor to more
cognitively demanding mathematics challenges, which may occur in secondary and collegiate coursework (Cleary & Chen, 2009). Findings from the present study align with
previous literature in the notion that educators can identify students with low, medium,
and high levels of anxiety to make instructional decisions (Fan et al., 2019).
The integral relationship between mathematics anxiety, self-efficacy, and literacy served
as the focus of the present study. Since mathematics anxiety corresponds with classroom
requirements and activities, there is potential to reshape students’ negative self-perceptions
through instructional practices. This work supports and extends research within the Greek
context on mathematics education and highlights the need to focus on ways to support struggling learners, females, and students of low socioeconomic status by mitigating
mathematics anxiety, while increasing mathematics self-efficacy and literacy.
The present study in tandem with previous research on mathematics education, highlights the critical need to find ways to reduce mathematics anxiety so high school students
can develop confidence and improve their mathematics literacy. Further, this work provides continued evidence that there are disparities in mathematics anxiety, self-efficacy,
and literacy for females and students of low socioeconomic status in Greece. Continued
support for these students through dynamic instructional activities, self-regulatory practices, and consistent interactions with educators offers a foundation for low performing
students often inherently excluded from STEM careers.
