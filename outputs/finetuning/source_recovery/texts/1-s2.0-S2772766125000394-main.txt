A B S T R A C T
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has become increasingly integrated into education, human life, and 
work as we continue to enter the 21st century, resulting in an increased expectation that learners 
and humans should possess more skills, referred to as 21st-century digital skills. These skills have 
become essential for learning, working, and living with Artificial Intelligence and the latest 
generation of Information and Communication Technologies (ICTs). While recent studies have 
examined Artificial Intelligence, particularly ChatGPT, in terms of its ability to assist language 
learners in developing their language skills as well as their subskills, they have not explored its 
role in the development of their 21st-century digital skills. For this sake, in this study the re­
searchers integrated ChatGPT into the language classroom procedure in three high schools in 
Tehran, where the language teacher tried to cultivate 21st-century digital skills with ChatGPT and 
led students to take advantage of its features to improve their 21st-century digital skills 
throughout the academic year of 2023–24 and then filled out the study survey. The results of the 
Partial Least Square Modelling Approach (PLS-SEM) showed that ChatGPT’s personalization, 
interactivity, accuracy, and responsiveness, along with its anthropomorphism, significantly sha­
ped language learners’ 21st-century digital skills, including critical thinking digital skills, infor­
mation evaluation skills, creative digital skills, and problem-solving skills. In addition, the study 
found a sign of digital self-authenticity, where language learners perceived that AI-assisted lan­
guage learning enhanced their 21st-century digital skills more than previous language learning 
contexts, albeit at the expense of their digital collaboration skills. Therefore, the study broadened 
the existing literature by focusing on the 21st-century digital skills of language learners, going 
beyond their language skills and sub-skills. It suggests that ChatGPT has the potential to enhance 
and develop language learners’ 21st-century digital skills. However, language teachers must 
devise metrics to encourage learners to engage in both collaborative and personalized language 
learning with ChatGPT, thereby fostering the development of all 21st-century digital skills.

ChatGPT into our language learning classrooms to cover students’ 21st-century digital skills and explore what ChatGPT’s features can 
cover language learners’ 21st-century digital skills.
2. Theoretical framework
2.1. 21st-century digital skills
The integration of Artificial Intelligence and Chatbots into the educational landscape, along with their growing significance as 
research hotspots, has heightened the importance of 21st-century digital skills since Artificial Intelligence now requires a person to 
learn, interact, and collaborate, which calls for the evaluation, critical thinking, and effective management of the information it 
provides. Furthermore, recent educational research has validated the factorial structures of 21st-century digital skills in blended (Fitria 
et al., 2024), flipped classrooms (Aslan, 2022), and online education (Hatlevik et al., 2018; Schmid & Petko, 2019), but these factorial 
structures have not been validated in artificial education research, which led the researchers to select this theoretical framework and 
operationalize the following 21st-century digital skills for the AILL and ChatGPT Assisted Language Learning (CHATGPTALL) contexts 
as follows: 
Information management (INFOM): Language learners’ skills to manage and maintain the information that was provided to them 
by ChatGPT.
Information evaluation (INFOE): Language learners’ skills in judging the usefulness, relevance, and reliability of the retrieved 
information that was provided by ChatGPT.
Digital Communication skills (DCM): Language learners’ ability to transmit information provided by ChatGPT in the best 
possible way in order to present that information to a specific audience within a language learning context.
Collaboration digital skills (CL): Language learners’ ability to work effectively and respectfully in a team to accomplish a 
common objective and to accept shared responsibility for completing tasks with ChatGPT.
Critical-thinking digital skills (CRT): Language learners’ ability to make informed judgments about ChatGPT’s feedback.
Creative digital skills (CRE): Language learners’ skills to appropriately use ChatGPT to create language content.
Problem-solving digital skills (PS): Language learners’ skills to use ChatGPT to analyze a problem situation and deploy 
knowledge in finding a solution to the problem.
2.2. ChatGPT’s related-factors in language learning
2.2.1. Personalization
One of the most significant features that ChatGPT has provided to the educational landscape, without a doubt, is the extension of 
the learning and teaching context from one centered on the teacher and the student to a personalized one. In fact, ChatGPT’s 
personalization features enable learners to provide specific requirements based on their contexts and individual needs, as well as 
feedback on assignments and assessments (Hadi Mogavi et al., 2024; Rajabi et al., 2024). By offering constructive suggestions and 
tailored guidance, students can personally improve their understanding and performance (Memarian & Doleck, 2023). It is the primary

objective of personalization to deliver the response based on an understanding of the user’s requirements and context (Schmid & Petko, 
2019). As compared with previous types of learning, personalized learning places a greater emphasis on two important factors: choice 
and voice (Schmid & Petko, 2019). It is the freedom of choice that requires learners to process digital content, to evaluate it, and to 
manage it, and as a part of the voices, to actively use them and to share them with others. These personalization features were reported 
by the “Programme for International Student Assessment” (PISA) in digital learning environments, where the researcher reported that 
learners’ 21st-century skills and digital skills were primarily dependent on their individual ICT access and ICT use at home; in other 
words, the personalized digital learning environment significantly impacted learners’ 21st-century skills (Zhong, 2011). This finding is 
also supported by the “International Computer and Information Literacy Study” (ICILS), which reported that digital skills are primarily 
influenced by the personalized learning experience of students, whereas in-school experiences seemed to have a comparatively low 
effect, and in some countries, such as Germany and Switzerland, it had a negative correlation (Hatlevik et al., 2018). Schmid and Petko 
(2019) observed, however, that the personalization features of ICT had no impact on Swiss students’ 21st-century skills.As yet, this 
personalized feature of Artificial Intelligence, which is an integral part of it, has not been fully explored in relation to how it may affect 
learners’ 21st-century digital skills. Recent studies reported that this choice and voice in personalized Artificial Intelligence learning 
had an important role to play in accepting and preparing for their usage behavior across a variety of backgrounds and educational 
contexts (Albdrani & Al-Shargabi, 2023; Maheshwari, 2023). Particularly with a rising need for learner-based personalized education, 
chatbots offer a foundation for digital learning environments to form the systematic adoption for each student (Tetzlaff et al., 2021). 
Therefore, AI has the potential to address this challenge effectively due to its flexibility and accessibility, which can capture students’ 
evolving behaviors and individualized information as input (Ingkavara et al., 2022). The personalized feature of ChatGPT has been 
reported in several studies to have a significant impact on learners’ intentions to use the application or its acceptance for schooling 
(Albdrani & Al-Shargabi, 2023; Maheshwari, 2023). With regard to its practical applications, studies have reported that ChatGPT’s 
personalized features have enhanced language learners’ writing (Mahapatra, 2024) and speaking skill (Pratiwi et al., 2024). Never­
theless, ChatGPT’s personalized features have not been sufficient to explore its practical impacts on learners due to the fact that 
language learners’ needs in the current era have surpassed language skills and sub-skills outside the classroom to 21st-century digital 
skills (Rahimi, 2023b). As part of this personalized learning environment, language learners have the opportunity to increase their 
skills in evaluating the information provided by ChatGPT critically, maintaining the accurate information, and using the appropriate 
critical digital skills to solve their problems with ChatGPT. Due to these gaps, and as an additional step forward, the researchers 
developed the following hypotheses: 
• H1a: The ChatGPT’s personalization feature significantly shapes language learners’ information management.
• H1b: The ChatGPT’s personalization feature significantly shapes language learners’ information evaluation.
• H1c: The ChatGPT’s personalization feature significantly shapes language learners’ collaborative digital skills.
• H1d: The ChatGPT’s personalization feature significantly shapes language learners’ digital communication skills.
• H1e: The ChatGPT’s personalization feature significantly shapes language learners’ critical-thinking digital skills.
• H1f: The ChatGPT’s personalization feature significantly shapes language learners’ creative digital skills.
• H1g: The ChatGPT’s personalization feature significantly shapes language learners’ problem-solving digital skills.
2.2.2. Anthropomorphism
ChatGPT is also distinguished by its anthropomorphism. Anthropomorphism involves assigning human characteristics to inanimate 
objects and animals so that their actions can be rationalized more easily. There are two main reasons for this behavior. A first reason for 
this is that humans are interested in understanding the environment in which their agents are located in order to minimize un­
certainties (Epley et al., 2007; Xie et al., 2023). The second major reason is that humans in nature like to have social interaction (Epley 
et al., 2007; Xie et al., 2023). In this regard, external anthropomorphic characteristics, such as facial expressions like human or internal 
characteristics, such as language style and other traits and behaviors, were among the critical anthropomorphic features of ChatGPT, 
particularly the internal ones (Polyportis & Pahos, 2024). Recent studies have demonstrated that anthropomorphism contributes 
positively to perceptions of social presence and attitude, which are essential for meaningful human-computer interactions (Araujo, 
2018). The concept of anthropomorphism has been explored as a significant factor in the adoption and acceptance of chatbots in a 
variety of studies (Polyportis & Pahos, 2024; van Es & Nguyen, 2024). The voice control narration of ChatGPT as one of the internal 
anthropomorphisms has been highly cited to increase language learners’ speaking skills (Pratiwi et al., 2024; Üstünbas¸, 2024); 
however, it has not been explored to what extent it can respond and cover language learners’ 21st-century digital skills. Based on this 
gap, the researchers developed the following hypotheses: 
• H2a: The ChatGPT’s anthropomorphism feature significantly shapes language learners’ information management skills.
• H2b: The ChatGPT’s anthropomorphism feature significantly shapes language learners’ information evaluation.
• H2c: The ChatGPT’s anthropomorphism feature significantly shapes language learners’ collaborative digital skills.
• H2d: The ChatGPT’s anthropomorphism feature significantly shapes language learners’ digital communication skills.
• H2e: The ChatGPT’s anthropomorphism feature significantly shapes language learners’ critical-thinking digital skills.
• H2f: The ChatGPT’s anthropomorphism feature significantly shapes language learners’ creative digital skills.
• H2g: The ChatGPT’s anthropomorphism feature significantly shapes language learners’ problem-solving digital skills.

self-regulation in ChatGPT, where they could reach future and academic goals and evaluate their language learning progress and 
academic schedule in a personalized language learning context than in their previous learning contexts. To follow this path, explore the 
new authenticity gap, and extend ChatGPT’s digital self-authenticity in shaping learners’ practical skills, especially their 21st-century 
digital skills, we propose the following hypotheses: 
• H3a: Students’ perceived digital self-authenticity in ChatGPT significantly shapes their information management skills.
• H3b: Students’ perceived digital self-authenticity in ChatGPT significantly shapes their information evaluation.
• H3c: Students’ perceived digital self-authenticity in ChatGPT significantly shapes their collaborative digital skills.
• H3d: Students’ perceived digital self-authenticity in ChatGPT significantly shapes their digital communication skills.
• H3e: Students’ perceived digital self-authenticity in ChatGPT significantly shapes their critical-thinking digital skills.

2.3.4. Interactivity
Another critical feature of ChatGPT and AI is its interactivity (IN). Which refers to their ability to engage in interaction with users, 
leading to a sense of responsibility and adaptability among them (Maheshwari, 2023). In the educational context, instructional 
interactivity is perceived from the students’ standpoint and is only established once a message loop, involving communication from the 
learner and back, is completed (Maheshwari, 2023). During the exchange of information between the chatbot and the user, both 
parties collaborate to understand what is being said by the user and offer the most appropriate response. The interactivity features of AI 
have the potential to improve communication and collaboration among learners, as pointed out by Seo et al. (2021). Further, recent 
studies have reported that the interactivity features of ChatGPT increased learners’ self-efficacy, heightened their knowledge and 
performance, and paced their learning (Al Shloul et al., 2024; Essel et al., 2024). It was also efficient at completing questions and 
answering questions, which enhanced learners’ critical thinking skills (Al Shloul et al., 2024; Essel et al., 2024). To further explore its 
role in shaping language learners’ other practical skills, particularly 21st-century digital skills, we developed the following hypotheses: 
H4a: Students’ perceived interactivity in ChatGPT significantly shapes their information management skills.
H4b: Students’ perceived interactivity in ChatGPT significantly shapes their information evaluation.
H4c: Students’ perceived interactivity in ChatGPT significantly shapes their collaborative digital skills.
H4d: Students’ perceived interactivity in ChatGPT significantly shapes their digital communication skills.
H4e: Students’ perceived interactivity in ChatGPT significantly shapes their critical-thinking digital skills.
H4f: Students’ perceived interactivity in ChatGPT significantly shapes their creative digital skills.
H4g: Students’ perceived interactivity in ChatGPT significantly shapes their problem-solving digital skills.
2.3.5. Accuracy and responsiveness (AR)
When it comes to Chatbots, users expect accurate and timely responses, which reflect their responsiveness. Malik et al. (2021)
defined responsiveness as the readiness to offer immediate help and services. In light of this, we operationally defined it in this study as 
the precision and rapid response time of ChatGPT. According to recent studies, ChatGPT’s accuracy and responsiveness did not have 
any relationship with learners’ intention to use ChatGPT (Acosta-Enriquez et al., 2024; ); however, Awal (2024) reported the con­
tradictory results where they reported that it shaped learners’ attitudes towards ChatGPT. As far as its practical aspects are concerned, 
recent surveys reported that ChatGPT’s accuracy significantly predicted learners’ engagement as well as their critical thinking skills 
(Youssef et al., 2024). As students doubted the validity of ChatGPT due to the prompt response it provided, they sought additional 
clarification or evidence regarding the information provided and sought alternative viewpoints or sources of information when uti­
lizing ChatGPT to ensure a comprehensive understanding. Further, they agreed that they needed clarification regarding the accuracy 
and suitability of ChatGPT’s output, which often prompted additional independent research or evaluation, which led them to have 
higher engagement and indirectly cultivate critical thinking skills (Youssef et al., 2024). To further explore the accuracy and 
responsiveness of ChatGPT in shaping other language learners’ skills, particularly their 21st-century digital skills, the researchers 
develop the following hypotheses: 
• H5a: The ChatGPT’s accuracy and responsiveness can significantly shape language learners’ information management skills.

2.4. Literature gap, and study aim
Our review of the literature and recent studies revealed several gaps that were intended to be addressed in this study. To begin with, 
the majority of studies in educational domains, applied linguistics, CALL, and AILL have extensively investigated ChatGPT factors, 
including its personalization, interactivity, and accuracy, in shaping learners’ psychological factors, particularly their attitudes and 
intentions to use it (Acosta-Enriquez et al., 2024; Al Shloul et al., 2024; Essel et al., 2024). However, little research has been conducted 
on how these features shape learners’ practical skills, particularly their 21st-century digital skills. Secondly, the majority of the CALL 
and Applied Linguistic studies examined ChatGPT’s critical features, such as personalization, interactivity, and accuracy, on language 
learners’ practical skills, including their language skills and sub-skills such as writing and speaking (Mahapatra, 2024; Pratiwi et al., 
2024; Punar ¨Ozçelik & Yangın Eks¸i, 2024). However, recent CALL researchers recommended to language researchers and teachers 
during the current 21st century that both language teachers and researchers should teach beyond the language skills and sub-skills and 
cover language learners’ 21st-century digital skills, which are becoming much more important than before, particularly with the 
progress and integration of AI and other large language models, since learners need to work and live with them, and they need to 
acquire 21st-century digital skills alongside language skills (Rahimi, 2023a, 2023b; Rahimi & Mosalli, 2024b; Rahimi and Sev­
illa-Pav´on, 2025b).
Regardless of our limited literature review, the recent systematic and integrative literature reviews are sorting our literature gaps, 
since most of them reported that studies covered the role of ChatGPT in shaping language learners’ language skills, particularly their 
writing skills (Lo et al., 2024), or exploring its effect on language learners’ psychological factors such as their motivation and attitudes 
in this realm, and they recommended exploring other trends and factors as well (Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). As a follow-up to the 
above recommendations and literature gaps, this study will explore what ChatGPT’s related factors can do to shape language learners’ 
21st-century digital skills.
3. Methodology
3.1. Study context, participants, and its procedure
The second author conducted the present study while teaching students in three different schools during the second semester of the 
academic year 2023–2024, located in Baharestan 2, Nasimshahr, and Tehran. He received permission from the school administrators 
and requested their approval to conduct his research in the context of his language class. After securing ethical consent from the 
Ministry of Education and the school heads, he entirely integrated ChatGPT into the syllabus and activities of English classes.
All the students who participated in this study maintained the same level of English language proficiency and were expected to pass 
Vision 3, the national course book taught in Iran for high school students, in the third year of high school. The teacher implemented 
ChatGPT in the process of teaching and learning and guided his students, both directly and indirectly, to engage with ChatGPT and 
acquire 21st-century digital skills through it alongside various activities in the book, including Get Ready, Conversation, Reading, 
Grammar, Listening, and Writing. For clarification, the teacher played a video clip, which had already been created by the use of 
ChatGPT, including some information about the theme of the first lesson. Then, he asked students to look at the pictures in the Get 
Ready section and have a discussion in groups of five, using ChatGPT as one of the members of their group.
They were given 20 min to organize some information by the usage of ChatGPT. Each group was expected to pose 3 questions by the 
usage of ChatGPT, which boosted collaborative digital skills of students. They discussed the questions, and ChatGPT played its role as 
one of the participants (information management, information evaluation). The teacher then asked 2 groups to go in front of the class 
and discuss the topic together, presenting the information that was provided by ChatGPT. In this way, the communication and digital 
skills of the students were evaluated and promoted. The teacher had asked other students to take notes while listening to the groups 
discussing together and give a summary of the information. At the same time, ChatGPT was administered the task of summarizing as 
one of the members of the class. After the teacher presented the summarized information provided by ChatGPT, students received 
practical feedback on the task and were given the opportunity to improve their information management skills. Additionally, students 
had the opportunity to assess the usefulness and reliability of their own information against ChatGPT’s (critical thinking digital skills) 
presentation.
In the next activity, the teacher first pre-taught the most important words of the conversation by means of some pictures provided 
by ChatGPT. After teaching the new complicated words to the students, the teacher played the recording for the students to listen to the 
conversation. Then he asked 2 students to take the roles of the characters and read the conversation. After all, the teacher assigned 
students to create a conversation around the central theme of the conversation (creative digital skills; digital communication skills) by 
the usage of ChatGPT. So the teacher first divided the students into groups of three to facilitate this task and then asked them to engage 
in creating a conversation in companionship with ChatGPT within 30 min. Each student in all groups attempted to do their part 
effectively, as ChatGPT had been used to provide a model conversation by giving some key words and sentences. Some checked the

structure and grammar of the sentences, while others were analyzing the sentence organization of the conversation. Every student 
actively participated in the task that allowed all students to enhance their communication and collaboration skills, although when

information, which was misleading students about the main topic of the conversation. Working under the expert guidance of the

As part of another activity, the teacher played a clip created with the help of ChatGPT related to the reading strategy, which was 
about question generation. In this clip, students were taught how to use wh-question words in the interrogative sentence to make 
various questions. After watching the clip, the teacher asked students to generate 5 questions by scanning the text of the reading and 
implementing ChatGPT. ChatGPT was given the text and asked to produce questions using what, where, when, and why. Provided with 
the questions generated by ChatGPT, students were able to have an evaluation of creative digital skills in their language usage.In the 
grammar section, the teacher shared a PowerPoint slideshow about the grammar of the lesson, provided by ChatGPT. Students were 
exposed to the grammar of active and passive voice of 6 tenses (simple present and simple past, present continuous and past 
continuous, and present perfect and past perfect) through the slideshow created by ChatGPT. In the first phase, students were taught 
the structure, and in the second phase, students were supposed to do the exercises by using ChatGPT. Students were administered 
multiple-choice questions in the slideshow, and as they chose the answer, they were provided with the correct answer.
The presentation of the grammar provided by ChatGPT enhanced students’ learning and engagement in the learning process. After 
that, the teacher wrote some sentences on the board and asked students to fill in the blanks using the appropriate form of the sentences. 
Students were able to receive effective feedback when they were provided with the answers given by ChatGPT. As a part of homework, 
the students were assigned to do a writing task outside the classroom using ChatGPT. They were expected to write about “giving a 
solution to a common problem of their own country.” The teacher seized the opportunity for students to learn how to use GPT on their 
cell phones, tablets, or laptops to create the paragraph. ChatGPT was given some words and phrases related to the topic. Providing 
ChatGPT with the needed information, a sample paragraph was created according to the given information.
After receiving the model answer, students had the opportunity to evaluate their problem-solving digital skills as well as their 
critical thinking digital skills. Following the integration of ChatGPT into his language syllabus and resulting in his students learning 
language with it both inside and outside of class, he then sent the study questionnaire to them before passing the final exam. He 
provided the consent to the students and assured them that he would keep their information anonymous and that only researchers 
would have access to it, and all of them signed it. Thus, a total of 236 language learners participated in the study, of which 221 were 
males and 15 were females. Most of them (97 %) were aged 17–18. Table 1 provides detailed demographic data.
3.2. Instrument
The study items were adapted from several studies, and before they were implemented, three experts from the fields of CALL, 
applied linguistics, and AILL reviewed them, and after one round of review and applying their comments, they finally agreed with the 
final versions. Thereafter, the scholars designed their study scale in four components. The first block consisted of informed consent, in 
which the researchers explained the purpose of the study and assured the participants that their information would remain confidential 
and would only be used for the purposes of the study. Additionally, the second block gathered language learners’ demographic in­
formation regarding their gender, age, and informed consent; the third one was dogged in exploring language learners’ 21st-century 
digital skills; and the last one tapped into ChatGPT’s features and learners’ 21st-century digital skills. The appendix includes the study 
scale and its items, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with a normal distribution from −2 to +2.

4. Result
4.1. Reflective model
PLS-SEM provides a formative model for determining the reliability and validity of the variables. In order to measure reliability, 
Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability were used. As a general rule of thumb, Hair et al. (2023) suggest a value above 0.70 for both 
types of reliability. To test convergent validity, we calculated the average variance extracted (AVE), which must be greater than 0.50. 
The validity and reliability of all latent variables were displayed in Table 2.
As a means of estimating discriminant validity, Fornell and Larker’s (1981) criteria were applied. According to them, √A of each 
variable should be higher than its correlation with other variables. Table 3 exhibits the result of the discriminant validity.
A heterotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT) method, proposed by Henseler et al. (2015), was used to investigate discriminant validity. 
For conceptually similar constructs, an HTMT threshold of 0.85 is generally preferred. Table 4 shows that all HTMT values are 
comfortably below the 0.85 threshold. As a final metric in the PLS-SEM measurement model, the latent variables exhibited strong 
correlations with their indicators, and their reflective loadings were above 0.5, as illustrated in Fig. 1.
4.2. Formative model
The formative model, or structural model in PLS-SEM, evaluates the correlation, regression, and overall model through various 
metrics incorporating multicollinearity, path coefficient effect size, coefficient of determination (R2), Stone-Geisser’s Q2 value 
(Geisser, 1974), and standardised root mean square residual (SRMR).
Firstly, before going to evaluate the hypothesized’ results, we used variance inflation factors (VIF) to evaluate the common method 
bias of the SEM. As you can see, all of them were below 0.5, showing that the study data does not have any problem regarding the 
collinearity. As a next step, we run the bootstrap analysis to test study hypotheses; the result in Table 5 shows that H1b (β=0.334), H1c 
(β=0.185), H1e (β=0.232), H1f (β=0.218), H2g (β=0.179), H3e (β=0.159), H3f (β=0.171), H4b (β=0.174), H4d (β=0.149), H4e 
(β=0.447), H4f (β=0.249), H4g (β=0.379), H5a (β=0.212), H5b(β=0.142), H5c (β= 0.143), H5d (β=0.183), H5f (β=0.147), H5g 
(β=0.254) were accepted, and H2c (β= - 0.237), H3c(β= −0.120), were rejected, and the rests were not significant as their t-values 
below 1.96 and p-values above 0.05.
The coefficient of determination (R2) is the most common metric used to determine the predictive power of structural models (Hair 
et al., 2023). According to Chin (1998), values of 0.19, 0.33, and 0.67 are considered weak, medium, and strong, respectively. Thus, 
the coefficients of determination of ChatGPT’s anthropomorphism, personalization, digital self-authenticity, interactivity, and accu­
racy and responsiveness predicted and changed 8 percent of information management, 23 percent of information evaluation, 7 percent 
of digital communication skills (0.076), 11 percent of collaboration digital skills (0.114), 30 percent of critical-thinking digital skills 
(0.308), 24 percent of creative digital skills (0.240), and 35 percent of their problem-solving digital skills. Further, the model’s pre­
dictive accuracy was evaluated through a Stone-Geisser. As noted by Hair et al. (2023), values above zero indicate that the model is 
adequate in terms of predictive relevance, which is the case illustrated by Table 6. Lastly, to evaluate the goodness of fit for the 
structural model, the researchers applied SRMR. An SRMR of <0.08 is recommended, which reached substantial and significant levels.

5. Discussion
5.1. The factorial validity of the 21st-century digital skills in artificial intelligence language learning
Our study’s measurement model validated the factorial structures of 21st-century digital skills in the context of Artificial Intelli­
gence Language Learning, building on previous validations in language teaching with ICT (Rahimi, 2023b), blended learning (Fitria 
et al., 2024), and flipped classrooms (Aslan, 2022), which we have now extended to AI in education, particularly chatbot-assisted 
language learning.
5.2. Relationship between ChatGPT’s feature and learners’ 21st-century digital skills
Moreover, the result showed that ChatGPT’s personalized feature positively shaped language learners’ 21st-century digital skills, 
including their information evaluation, collaborative digital skills, critical-thinking digital skills, and creative digital skills. Accord­
ingly, the higher language learners had choice and voice in CHALL, where they were free to search for anything and to choose the 
information they needed; the higher they could judge the usefulness, relevance, and reliability of the retrieved information, the better 
they could solve their target problems. These findings align with previous studies by PISA and ICILS, which demonstrated that a 
personalized ICT environment can enhance learners’ 21st-century digital skills (Hatlevik et al., 2018; Zhong, 2011). We have now 
extended those findings to ChatGPT-Assisted Language Learning.
In addition, studies reported that ChatGPT provided inaccurate information with fake sources (Crompton & Burke, 2024; Hadi 
Mogavi et al., 2024), but on the other hand, it leads learners to be more accurate and critically evaluate information. Indeed, when 
learners apply ChatGPT’s feedback in the learning context and discover that it is not valid or, to some extent, inaccurate (as in our 
study procedure), it prompts them to question the validity and accuracy of the information provided by AI and chatbots, thereby 
leading them to evaluate its information accurately and fostering critical thinking, digital skills, and information evaluation. This is 
also supported by the significant path between ChatGPT’s personalization and learners’ collaborative digital skills since, in those 
cooperative activities, language learners can exchange information and feedback together, which leads them to evaluate information 
and acquire more critical thinking digital skills. This can respond to what Tyack and Mekler (2020) observed that learners had a low 
level of autonomy, as ChatGPT can do everything, which led them to have a low level of critical thinking skills; however, Rahimi and 
Sevilla-Pav´on (2025b) reported that it is in the hands of the teacher to regulate this autonomy in their language teaching procedure by 
providing standard activities and metrics where learners use AI in a more active manner than a passive one, which would develop their 
critical thinking digital skills.

Furthermore, our results exhibited that the higher the language learners’ choice and voice in personalized language learning, the 
more they had the skills to work effectively and respectfully in a team to accomplish a common objective and to accept shared re­
sponsibility for completing tasks. Thus, the personalized features of ChatGPT have brought a new insight into education, where 
language learners can have both personalized and collaborative learning. This might be related to learners’ L2 self-identities, as 
ChatGPT can cover language learners’ future, current, academic, and personal objectives, leading them to evaluate their language 
learning progress, ask for more help, and set more goals (Rahimi et al., 2025). Hadi Mogavi et al. (2024) also report this finding, 
asserting that the provision of translation services and the facilitation of idea exchange foster an inclusive learning environment that 
empowers students to collaborate and share their insights to reach their personal and academic goals.
Our study’s structural model indicated that ChatGPT’s anthropomorphism notably enhanced learners’ problem-solving skills (β =
0.179) but decreased their collaborative digital thinking (β = −0.237), leading to the rejection of H2c. Accordingly, the higher 
ChatGPT’s human characteristics, the more language learners will use it to solve problems, although they prefer to take advantage of 
its features for their personal use rather than for collaborative ones. This finding may relate to language learners’ confidence, as 
learners with lower confidence were more inclined to use ChatGPT’s anthropomorphic aspects for individual language learning, 
especially its internal features like voice control for conversation (Karatas¸ et al., 2024) and writing feedback (Alkamel & Alwagieh, 
2024; Barrot, 2023). This was because they received more personalized feedback, which made ChatGPT more suitable for personal 
language learning than collaborative learning, which might decrease their collaboration digital skills, which was revealed in H1c. 
Additionally, recent educational research has shown that anthropomorphism significantly affects learners’ intention to use ChatGPT 
(Ding et al., 2023; Polyportis & Pahos, 2024) or their deep language learning (Rahimi & Sevilla-Pav´on, 2025a), highlighting its positive 
impact on language learners’ psychological factors in AILL. We have now extended this influence to include their practical skills, 
particularly their 21st-century digital skills.

6. Pedagogical, and practical implications
This study is expected to have several theoretical implications for the fields of CALL, Applied Linguistics, Artificial Intelligence 
Language Learning, Chatbot-Assisted Language Learning, and Artificial Intelligence in Education. In the first place, to the best of the 
researchers’ knowledge, this is the first study to cultivate and integrate 21st-century digital skills into the field of Artificial Intelligence 
in Education and Chatbot-Assisted Language Learning. This is in response to the highly recommended application of 21st-century 
digital skills by important pedagogical institutes like ATC21S, OECD, EU, CSTA, ISTE, and IEA (Digital education action plan 
(2021-2027), (2023); Rahimi, 2025, 2023a; 2023b; Rahimi & Mosaali, 2024a; Rahimi & Sevilla-Pav´on, 2025a, 2025b; Rubach & 
Lazarides, 2021), which has been integrated into blended learning environments (Fitria et al., 2024), flipped classrooms (Aslan, 2022), 
and online education (Hatlevik et al., 2018; Schmid & Petko, 2019), and now extended to Artificial Intelligence Language Learning. 
Furthermore, we addressed the recommendations made by Rahimi (2023a, 2023b) and Rahimi and Mosalli (2024b) for language 
teachers and researchers, encouraging them to go beyond traditional language skills and subskills and embrace 21st-century digital 
skills in the current digital era.Thirdly, the study validated the factorial structure of 21st-century digital skills in the field of Artificial 
Intelligence in Education and chatbot-assisted language learning, since they have been validated in Fitria et al. (2024), flipped 
classrooms (Aslan, 2022), and online education (Hatlevik et al., 2018; Schmid & Petko, 2019). Furthermore, the study shifted the view 
from exploring what ChatGPT’s factors, such as interactivity (Maheshwari, 2023), anthropomorphism (Polyportis & Pahos, 2024b), 
accuracy, responsiveness (Hasan et al., 2024), and personalization (Al-khresheh, 2024), are in shaping learners’ attitudes or their 
intention to their practical skills, particularly 21st-century digital skills. Having integrated the 21st-century digital skills into Artificial 
Intelligence Language Learning, the researchers heeded the previous recommendations that recent systematic and integrative liter­
ature reviews addressed to cover other language learners’ skills in this environment (Li et al., 2024; Lo et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). 
Lastly, the study also found the new sign of digital self-authenticity in the context of AILL since recent studies reported this in other 
digital learning environments to shape learners’ attitudes (Rahimi, 2023c), self-regulation (Rajimi et al., in press), and effort (Smith 
et al., 2020), and now we extended this to the 21st-century digital skills, demonstrating that we have shifted the focus from exploring 
the authenticity gap between language learners’ psychological factors in their previous language learning environment and their 
current one to practical skills instead.
Practical implications
The study will have several implications for practice at three levels, including micro, meso, and macro, as follows:
It is important for language teachers to utilize Artificial Intelligence and Chatbots on a micro level, since most 21st-century digital 
skills cannot be addressed by previous language learning environments, especially in the areas of creativity and critical thinking digital 
skills, as indicated by H3. To accomplish this, language teachers should leverage AI and ChatGPT in various ways, especially when 
covering skills beyond basic language ones. As such, they should incorporate the personalized language learning features of ChatGPT 
into their syllabus design to ensure a balance between teacher-centered approaches and personalized learning.
For learners to effectively use ChatGPT for personalized language learning, they must be aware of its limitations, such as its po­
tential to provide inaccurate responses and fabricated references. This awareness will help them assess the information and feedback 
they receive, thereby enhancing their critical digital thinking skills, creative digital skills, and information evaluation skills.
However, our results showed that ChatGPT’s anthropomorphism feature negatively correlated with language learners’ collabo­
ration digital skills, leading learners to isolate themselves and prefer to work with Artificial Intelligence instead of humans, which can 
hinder their collaboration digital skills since interaction with others is a critical aspect of language learning, and teachers need to solve 
this by implementing high levels of innovation in language teaching through ChatGPT (Rahimi & Sevilla-Pav´on, 2024), as well as

The pedagogical expert, executive manager, and curriculum developer should revise and update the national curriculum both

terms of practical aspects, they should encourage language teachers to integrate the latest generation of Artificial Intelligence and 
Chatbots into their instruction to promote the development of 21st-century digital skills in tandem with language skills and sub-skills. 
These skills are essential not only for navigating and learning alongside AI but also for preparing students for their future careers. For 
this reason, they should implement teacher training programs to help educators leverage ChatGPT’s features, such as its anthropo­
morphism, interactivity, and personalization, to enhance language learners’ 21st-century digital skills. Additionally, teachers must 
receive training in collaborative language teaching with AI and learn to design collaborative learning experiences that foster learners’ 
collaborative digital skills.
Moreover, language learners have widely embraced ChatGPT’s anthropomorphic features, such as its voice control and Talking 
Avatar, which can inadvertently reduce their digital collaboration skills. Therefore, teacher trainers should encourage language 
teachers to establish metrics that promote natural language speaking and collaborative digital skills among learners. They should also 
train teachers to help students recognize the importance of not blindly accepting ChatGPT’s outputs. Instead, learners should be taught 
to validate and verify information through various methods, such as peer interactions, internet searches, or consulting their teachers. 
This can develop their 21st-century digital skills, such as information evaluation, information management, critical digital skills, and 
creative digital skills.
The pedagogical expert, executive manager, and curriculum developer should update the national curriculum in both pedagogical 
and practical manners. In fact, regarding the pedagogical manner they should provide and update their metrics that cover 21st-century 
digital skills so language teachers are then urged to cover those skills alongside language skills and sub-skills. For the practical aspects, 
they should encourage language teachers to incorporate the latest generation of Artificial Intelligence, or Chatbot, and recommend the 
development of 21st-century digital skills alongside the acquisition of language skills and subskills, as these skills are not only 
necessary for them to live and learn with AI, but they can also be necessary for their future work. For this reason, they should conduct 
some teacher training programs so that they can learn how to take advantage of ChatGPT’s features, including its anthropomorphism, 
interactivity, personalization, and digital self-authenticity, to develop language learners’ 21st-century digital skills. They must also be 
trained to have collaborative language teaching with AI and trained to design collaborative learning with AI to cover learners’ 
collaborative digital skills.
Moreover, language learners have widely embraced ChatGPT’s anthropomorphism features, particularly its voice control and 
Talking Avatar, as a language partner that decreases learners’ digital collaboration skills. Thus, the teacher trainers should encourage 
language teachers to learn how to provide some metrics for language learners to have natural language speaking and collaborative 
digital skills with each other. They should train teachers to make their learners aware to not blindly follow what ChatGPT provides; 
instead, validate and verify its source through various methods, such as peer interaction, internet searches, or asking their teachers.
At the meso level, the study opens up a new avenue for researchers to embrace 21st-century digital skills in the field of Artificial 
Intelligence in Education in general, and CALL and AILL in particular, to cover more practical skills, particularly 21st-century ones that 
are needed by learners with AI and chatbots.
Limitation
This study has several limitations that provide avenues for future research. First, the participants of the study were high school 
students, and all of them were English language learners. Future studies should consider assessing this with other language learners at 
different levels or with participants from different fields of study. Additionally, our study had a gender imbalance, which we 
recommend future research address. Furthermore, we used self-reported data and questionnaire items to explore how ChatGPT can 
shape language learners’ 21st-century digital skills, which have common limitations and potential biases (e.g., social desirability or 
recall biases). Memory aids like visual timelines, diaries, or other prompts can mitigate the bias by helping participants recall events 
more accurately. Moreover, future studies need to use longitudinal studies, case studies, or observations and integrate pedagogical and 
contextual factors in order to generalize our findings. We also recommend that researchers in various fields, particularly applied 
linguistics and CALL, investigate methods for cultivating 21st-century digital skills using ChatGPT and other AI systems. Additionally, 
it would be beneficial if researchers could incorporate 21st-century digital skills into Artificial Intelligence in Educational research, 
specifically in the field of language learning, while taking into account other variables such as learners’ psycholinguistic factors, such 
as online self-regulation, online motivation, or their attitudes towards AI in education.
