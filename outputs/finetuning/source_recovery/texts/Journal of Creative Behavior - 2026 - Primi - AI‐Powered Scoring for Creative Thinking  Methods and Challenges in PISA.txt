ABSTRACT
The introduction of the PISA 2022 Creative Thinking assessment underscores the growing need for scalable, valid, and re-
liable methods to evaluate creativity in international large-­scale assessments. Traditional human scoring, while nuanced, is 
time-­consuming, costly, and subject to inconsistencies. This paper explores recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and 
natural language processing (NLP)—particularly transformer-­based large language models (LLMs)—as promising alternatives 
for automated scoring. We review three methodological approaches: (1) unsupervised methods using semantic distance, (2) su-
pervised fine-­tuning with labeled data, and (3) few−/zero-­shot learning using prompt-­based inference. Empirical findings from 
verbal and visual creative tasks show that AI-­based scoring systems can approximate human ratings with substantial accuracy 
(r = 0.70–0.85), even across different languages and task formats. A case study using the PISA Book Covers task demonstrates 
convergence between AI and human scores, with reliability levels comparable to traditional scoring. However, key challenges 
remain, particularly regarding cross-­cultural comparability, bias mitigation, and interpretability. We discuss psychometric strat-
egies (e.g., Many-­Facet Rasch Models) to model these issues and propose future directions, including scoring of distinct creativity 
dimensions and developing transparent, open-­source platforms. If rigorously validated, AI-­based scoring offers a feasible and 
equitable path forward for assessing creativity globally.
In an increasingly complex and innovation-­driven world, the 
ability to think creatively is widely recognized as an essential 
21-­century skill. As education systems worldwide strive to pre-
pare students for the demands of a rapidly changing global land-
scape, assessing creative thinking has become a key priority 
(Organization for Economic Co-­operation and Development—
OECD  2024). However, measuring creativity on a large scale 
presents significant methodological challenges, particularly in 
developing reliable, valid, and scalable assessment frameworks.
While psychological research has explored creative think-
ing extensively since the mid-­twentieth century, with seminal 
contributions from Guilford (1950) and Torrance (1968), large-­
scale creativity assessments have remained rare. Traditional 
measures of creativity, such as divergent thinking tasks that 
evaluate fluency, originality, and elaboration, have primarily 
been administered in controlled research settings rather than 
in standardized international assessments. The Program for 
International Student Assessment (PISA), however, marked a

The PISA 2022 Creative Thinking Assessment represents the 
most comprehensive effort to date to measure creativity at scale. 
The assessment is designed to capture students' ability to gener-

generate creative ideas (resembling originality measures) eval-

By embedding creative thinking into its assessment framework, 
PISA has introduced significant pressure to develop scalable, 
valid, reliable, and cross-­culturally comparable measures of 
creative thinking. However, assessing creativity on a large scale 
presents unique challenges. Unlike mathematics or reading, 
where correct answers can be objectively determined, creative 
thinking requires evaluating the quality, originality, and useful-
ness of student-­generated ideas—criteria that are inherently sub-
jective and influenced by cultural and contextual factors. This 
complexity complicates efforts to standardize assessment while 
ensuring fairness and consistency across diverse countries.
One of the major hurdles in PISA's creative thinking assess-
ment has been the reliance on human raters to score student 
responses. This process demands a substantial workforce and 
remains constrained by both subjectivity and variability in judg-
ment. Even with extensive training, raters may introduce incon-
sistencies. Moreover, achieving a fine-­grained, detailed analysis 
of responses requires a level of manual effort that is prohibitively 
time-­consuming and costly in large-­scale assessments.
This is where AI-­powered scoring emerges as a necessary solu-
tion. Can technology automate the scoring of creative thinking? 
If so, what methods are available, and what challenges remain? 
In this paper, we answer yes—advancements in large language 
models (LLMs) and AI-­based scoring algorithms have made au-
tomation feasible but not without new challenges. We provide an 
overview of current techniques, present the results achieved so 
far, and explore the challenges ahead.
1   |   A Bird's-­Eye View of Transformers as Language 
Models
At the core of modern artificial intelligence (AI) advancements 
in language processing lies neural networks, a class of machine 
learning models inspired by the structure and function of the 
human brain. Neural networks process information through 
layers of interconnected artificial neurons, adjusting their in-
ternal weights based on training data to recognize patterns and 
make predictions. Over the past decades, various architectures 
have been proposed to enhance neural networks' ability to pro-
cess language, but the transformer model has emerged as the 
foundation of today's most powerful AI-­driven language models 
(Vaswani et al. 2017).
The transformer is a neural network architecture designed to 
process and generate text by predicting the probability of the 
next word given a sequence of words. Transformers take in a 
text, or a sequence of words, as its input and predict the proba-
bility distribution over a large vocabulary of possible next words 
(typically N≈50,000 in state-­of-­the-­art models). Once a word is 
chosen based on this probability, it is fed back into the model, 
and the process repeats, generating coherent and contextually 
relevant text. In essence it is a conditional probability language 
model, that predicts the next word conditioned on the sequence 
of words in its input.
Instead of processing words sequentially like older recurrent 
neural networks (RNNs), transformers analyze all words in an 
input simultaneously using self-­attention. This mechanism al-
lows the model to weigh the importance of each word in a sen-
tence and capture long-­range dependencies. Unlike traditional 
models, transformers condition new word generation not just on 
individual words—main effect—but also on their interactions. 
As models advanced, the sequence length they can process in-
creased—from 512 tokens in BERT GPT2 (Devlin et al. 2019) to 
1024 in GPT-­2 GPT2 (Brown et al. 2020), 200 K in ChatGPT, and 
1 million in Google's Gemini. This expansion significantly im-
proved their ability to capture broader and deeper syntactic and 
semantic structures across diverse domains.
Because transformers are trained on vast amounts of textbooks, 
articles, dialogs, and code—they not only learn the rules of lan-
guage but also develop an internal representation of linguistic 
structures and contextual meaning. By processing billions of 
words, they grasp how terms relate across different contexts, 
generating fluent, meaningful responses. Recent models, such 
as OpenAI GPT-­5, DeepSeek R1, and Google's Gemini 2.5 Pro, 
incorporate advanced training techniques for reasoning, en-
abling them to generate chain-­of-­thought sequences before 
producing final answers. This refinement has led to substantial 
accuracy improvements, particularly in math and coding tasks.
2   |   Distributed Representations, Contextual 
Embeddings, and Latent Semantics
A core aspect of Large Language Models (LLMs) lies in how 
they numerically represent words. Since neural networks op-
erate through numerical computation, textual data must be 
transformed into mathematical representations to be processed 
effectively. Earlier Natural Language Processing (NLP) models 
adopted discrete word-­level representations, where each doc-
ument—a sequence of text—was mapped into a vector of size 
V, with V representing the total vocabulary size of the corpus. 
These vectors typically encoded word frequency information, 
treating each word as a distinct semantic unit.
One foundational method for extracting semantic structures 
from such high-­dimensional word vectors was Latent Semantic 
Analysis (LSA). This technique decomposed the document-­
term matrix into topic vectors, representing co-­occurring 
word groups, and document vectors, capturing topic distribu-
tions within a text. However, LSA and similar methods suf-
fered from a fundamental limitation: they relied on a rigid, 
one-­to-­one mapping between words and their semantic mean-
ings (Rosenbusch et al. 2020; Sievert and Shirley 2014). This 
approach ignored the inherent polysemy of language—where

A breakthrough in addressing these limitations emerged from 
the Parallel Distributed Processing (PDP) group at Stanford, led 
by David E. Rumelhart and James L. McClelland (Rumelhart 
et al. 1986) proposing that knowledge is represented in a dis-
tributed manner across many simple units (neurons) rather than 
localized discrete structures like words or symbols. Their dis-
tributed representation approach introduced a new paradigm: 
instead of representing words as discrete symbols, a simple 
neural network trained on large text corpora learned to encode 
words as dense, continuous vectors—known as word embed-
dings developed by Hinton (1986) and later by Mikolov, Chen, 
et al. (2013), Mikolov, Sutskever, et al. (2013) at Google through 
the creation of Word2Vec.
These embeddings captured semantic relationships by lever-
aging co-­occurrence patterns across large corpora, such as the 
entire Wikipedia dataset. Unlike traditional discrete represen-
tations, embeddings learned from models like Word2Vec and 
GloVe (Pennington et  al.  2014) encode words as vectors in a 
continuous space (dimensions ranging from 764 to 3072), where 
dimensions reflect latent semantic properties. Similar words—
those frequently appearing in similar contexts—are assigned 
closely aligned vectors. Knowledge is represented in a contin-
uous vector space, where each entity (like a word or concept) 
is encoded as a point in this space. This allows for capturing 
semantic similarities based on the proximity of these points. 
Each unit (or dimension) in a vector may represent a semantic 
feature, and the complete vector serves to decompose a word's 
representation into smaller, semantically meaningful com-
ponents. Consequently, a discrete symbol (such as a word) is 
transformed into a more efficient, continuous representation in 
a high-­dimensional feature space. The similarity between two 
words can then be computed based on the extent to which they 
share these semantic features. Therefore, two words that are or-
thographically distinct and treated as categorically different in 
discrete symbolic systems may, in vector space, exhibit a high 
degree of semantic similarity if they share the same or closely 
related feature representations.
This advancement mitigated the shortcomings of previous 
methods by ensuring that synonyms were mapped to similar 
representations, reducing the impact of lexical differences.
However, these static embeddings were limited by the fact that 
they assigned a single vector per word, regardless of context. 
This rigid mapping failed to account for polysemous words, such 
as “port,” which could refer to a harbor, a network endpoint, or 
a physical interface like a USB connection. The introduction of 
transformers and attention mechanisms revolutionized this par-
adigm by enabling context-­dependent embeddings.
Transformers leverage attention mechanisms to dynamically 
adjust word representations based on surrounding context. 
Instead of using a fixed embedding, the model reconfigures 
word vectors by attending to relevant tokens within the se-
quence, thereby reconstructing latent semantic structures that 
are context-­sensitive. This capability was transformative, as 
it allowed models to distinguish between different meanings 
of the same word, adapting their representations dynamically 
during processing.
This innovation not only enriched word embeddings but also 
enabled transformers to capture complex, long-­range dependen-
cies within text. By attending to context and dynamically refin-
ing word vectors, LLMs significantly enhanced the coherence 
and relevance of generated outputs. Ultimately, the foundation 
of modern NLP—contextual embeddings—derives from this 
ability to construct meaning-­rich representations, which are at 
the core of the unprecedented advancements seen in AI-­driven 
text generation and understanding.
3   |   Metaphors and Early Foundations of 
Measuring Creativity via Embeddings
Why are embeddings important? In tasks such as those found 
in the PISA creative thinking assessment, where students gen-
erate multiple story titles for an image or invent a narrative in-
spired by an arbitrary number like “2983,” the challenge lies in 
quantifying the originality (how rare and unique a response is), 
flexibility (how varied the responses are in terms of conceptual 
categories), and appropriateness of responses (how well the re-
sponse aligns with task constraints and meaning). Traditional 
approaches relied on human raters to evaluate novelty and se-
mantic distance, but embeddings now offer an automated way 
to assess the creative value of textual productions. Embeddings 
provide a computational method to assess semantic similarity 
and conceptual distance between words and ideas.
The cognitive psychology foundations for metaphor assessment, 
established long before embeddings, implicitly contained the 
principles later formalized in computational models. Rumelhart 
and Abrahamson (1973) laid the foundational groundwork for 
understanding metaphor and analogical reasoning, proposing 
that reasoning functions as a retrieval process dependent on 
structure rather than content. They suggested that conceptual 
similarity could be mapped in vector space, where elements are 
represented as points with measurable distances.
Tourangeau and Sternberg  (1981) extended Rumelhart and 
Abrahamson's ideas in their Domain-­Interaction Model, offer-
ing a cognitive explanation for how metaphors function. Their 
model introduced two core components of creative metaphors: 
(a) equivalence: the mapping of a concept onto another domain 
based on shared structural features (e.g., “The mustache is the 
antenna of the cat” because mustaches and antennas are thin, 
positioned on the body, and interact with the environment) and 
(b) remoteness: the greater the conceptual distance between the 
domains (e.g., animals and communication devices), the more 
creative the metaphor (Primi 2014).
This cognitive framework aligns with the idea that creativity 
relies on analogical reasoning, where structured memory re-
trieval plays a key role. However, at the time, such models re-
lied on human-­judged multidimensional scaling to determine 
word distances—an approach that was labor-­intensive and lim-
ited in scope. Today, AI-­generated embeddings allow for large-­
scale, automated measurement of these semantic relationships,

Tourangeau and Sternberg's work, building on Rumelhart and 
Abrahamson's model, highlighted the necessity of quantifying 
semantic distance. Yet, their studies were constrained by the 
lack of computational tools to represent words as points in a 
high-­dimensional space. Instead, they relied on manually rated 
similarity scores of pairs of concepts. Interestingly, much later 
Mikolov's (2013) word embeddings demonstrated that semantic 
relationships are preserved through vector arithmetic, allowing 
analogies such as: King−Man+Woman = Queen. This computa-
tional discovery provided empirical evidence that word embed-
dings capture the very structural semantic distances theorized, 
by cognitive psychologists, decades earlier. However, Mikolov 
did not explicitly reference these prior models, despite their con-
ceptual alignment.
This approach sets the initial foundation for new methodolo-
gies in AI-­assisted creativity measurement, allowing for cross-­
linguistic, domain-­independent scoring systems. In doing so, it 
advances the longstanding goal of quantifying creative thought 
processes—an endeavor that began with human-­judged similar-
ity ratings and has now reached the realm of machine-­encoded 
semantic representations.
4   |   From Human Judgments to AI-­Driven 
Creativity Scoring
Recently, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has been successfully 
applied to assess creativity in these divergent thinking tasks 
(see a review in Nakano  2025). These methods fall under 
three broad categories: unsupervised approaches, supervised 
approaches, and few-­shot learning approaches. Each of these 
has demonstrated various levels of effectiveness in automat-
ing creativity assessments, particularly in tasks like those 
used in PISA.
Unsupervised Methods rely on transfer learning of semantic 
meaning from LLM's pre-­trained in a large corpus of data to 
a specific task requiring no labeled training data. Techniques 
use pre-­trained word embeddings (Glove, Word2Vec, BERT) 
and cosine similarity to help evaluate how semantically distant 
responses are from given stimuli. Supervised Methods involve 
fine-­tuning pre-­trained models (e.g., BERT, GPT-­3, T5) using a 
small, labeled dataset. This approach enables models to learn 
creativity scoring patterns based on human-­rated data, enhanc-
ing prediction accuracy. Finally, few-­shot learning leverages 
the ability to learn the scoring task if provided with clear in-
structions and few examples. Unlike full fine-­tuning, few-­shot 
learning provides efficient adaptability with no need for training 
(Brown et al. 2020).
4.1   |   Unsupervised Approaches to Automated 
Creativity Scoring
Beaty and Johnson  (2021) successfully used unsupervised 
techniques to score Alternate Uses Divergent Thinking Tasks 
(AUT e.g., alternative uses for “rope,” “box,” “brick,” etc.). These 
approaches, leveraging transfer learning, involve extracting 
word embeddings from GloVe, Word2Vec (static embeddings), 
or BERT (contextual embeddings) and computing one minus 
the cosine similarity to measure the semantic distance between 
the embedding of a stimulus (e.g., a “box”) and the participant's 
response. They called this index semantic distance (SEMdis, 
see http://​semdis.​wlu.​psu.​edu). The authors developed and pub-
lished the Creativity Assessment Platform (CAP; https://​cap.​ist.​
psu.​edu/​claus​; accessed August 5, 2025), an online tool designed 
to assist researchers in scoring AUT among other creative tasks.
For multi-­word ideas, embeddings can be averaged or computed 
via cumulative multiplication, or the semantic distance of the 
most remote word in a response relative to the AUT prompt 
before cosine similarity is calculated (called MAD). Responses 
that are semantically distant from the stimulus tend to be rated 
as more original by human judges with correlations ranging 
from 0.68 to 0.81 (Acar et al. 2024; Buczak et al. 2023; Dumas 
et al. 2021; Forthmann et al. 2023; Goecke et al. 2024; Grajzel 
et al. 2023; Luchini et al. 2025; Maddock et al. 2001; Patterson, 
Merseal, et al. 2023; Patterson, Barbot, et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2023). 
A variation of this method, called Divergent Semantic Index 
(DSI), was developed for short creative writing tasks. Instead of 
comparing responses to a stimulus, DSI computes the average 
semantic distance among all words within a response. High DSI 
values indicate responses that incorporate diverse concepts, cor-
relating strongly with human creativity ratings around r = 0.80 
(DiStefano et al. 2024; Johnson et al. 2021, 2023).
4.2   |   Supervised Approaches and Multilingual 
Applications
Patterson, Barbot, et  al.  (2023) and Patterson, Merseal, 
et  al.  (2023) study exemplifies a different technique of fine-­
tuning using Multilingual BERT (MBERT) and XLM-­RoBERTa 
(XLMR) to score AUT responses across 12 languages. Their 
study showed moderate correlations with human ratings 
(r = 0.35–0.45), demonstrating the feasibility of multilingual 
AI-­based scoring, a crucial aspect for large-­scale assessments 
like PISA. DiStefano et al. (2024) also fine-­tuned RoBERTa and 
GPT-­2 for scoring metaphor creativity. These models signifi-
cantly outperformed traditional semantic distance measures 
and elaboration-­based approaches, achieving correlations of 
r = 0.70–0.73 with human ratings. This result highlights the ad-
vantage of task-­specific tuning in accurately replicating human 
scoring criteria.
In line with the study described above, other supervised-­
learning approaches have extended automated scoring of 
the AUT task to non-­English contexts, providing additional 
evidence for the feasibility of AI-­based scoring across multi-
ple languages. For example, XMLR was fine-­tuned to score 
German-­language AUT responses, achieving strong con-
vergence with human ratings (r = 0.80; Goecke et  al.  2024). 
Zielińska et al. (2023) tested whether Polish AUT responses 
could be scored accurately after translation into English. Using 
semantic distance measures and fine-­tuned GPT-­3.0 models 
available through the public Open Creativity Scoring with AI 
(OCSAI) platform (https://​opens​coring.​du.​edu/​; Organisciak 
and Dumas 2020), they analyzed over 6000 ideas and found

4.3   |   Few-­Shot and Zero-­Shot Learning 
Approaches
Finally, the first demonstration of the third method was pre-
sented by Organisciak et al. (2023) who tested both zero-­shot 
and few-­shot learning approaches for scoring creativity in the 
Alternate Uses Task (AUT). They compared T5 and GPT-­3 
fine-­tuned models against GPT-­4 few-­shot learning. Few-­
Shot Learning with GPT-­4. The fine-­tuned models achieved 
r = 0.76–0.81, significantly outperforming traditional static em-
beddings (r = 0.12–0.26). Few-­shot learning with GPT-­4 with 20 
labeled examples demonstrated strong performance reaching a 
correlation of r = 0.70, showcasing its adaptability and efficiency 
in scoring divergent thinking responses.
Building on the two previously described approaches, Saretzki 
et  al.  (2025) compared semantic distance scoring from the 
Creativity Assessment Platform (CAP-­CLAUS) and few-­shot 
learning from the Open Creativity Scoring with AI (OCSAI) 
platform with GPT-­4.0 in a zero-­shot configuration, using a 
large German-­language AUT dataset of approximately 50,000 
responses from 2320 participants across 15 objects. The mod-
els were evaluated against human ratings for both the original 
German responses and translations into English. All three ap-
proaches showed substantial correlations with human scores; 
however, OCSAI achieved the highest correlations (r = 0.66 for 
German), followed by GPT-­4 (r = 0.55) and CLAUS (r = 0.46). 
Translation did not produce consistent improvements. Overall, 
these latest studies found that the few-­shot approach aligned 
more closely with human ratings than semantic distance 
approaches.
4.4   |   Automated Scoring of Figural Tasks
Whereas most work has focused on verbal tasks, recent stud-
ies have extended AI-­scoring to drawings and multimodal re-
sponses. Patterson, Barbot, et al. (2023) and Patterson, Merseal, 
et al. (2023) introduced AuDrA, a convolutional neural-­network 
platform that predicts creativity from simple line drawings. 
Using over 13,000 drawings and ratings from nearly 60 human 
judges, the authors trained a ResNet-­based regression model and 
tested its generalization to new tasks and raters. AuDrA scores 
were highly correlated with human creativity ratings for new 
drawings from the same task (r = 0.65–0.81; mean = 0.76), and 
these correlations exceeded those between elaboration (amount 
of ink) and human ratings, indicating that the model captures 
more than mere complexity.
Using a similar methodological approach that combined 
ResNet50 with textual embeddings of drawing titles, Zhang 
et al. (2025) developed a multimodal model to assess novelty, flu-
ency, and flexibility, achieving correlations with human scores 
ranging from r = 0.56 to r = 0.81. Cropley et al. (2024) evaluated 
an off-­the-­shelf classification tool based on Google's Gemini 
multimodal transformer models and built a classifier for the Test 
of Creative Thinking—Drawing Production (TCT-­DP), obtain-
ing correlations of approximately r = 0.95 with human scoring 
on a test set.
Extending this line of work, Acar et al. (2025) conducted a com-
prehensive comparison of three methodological approaches for 
automated scoring of figural creativity tests: (a) feature-­based 
machine-­learning classifiers using CLIP-­derived image em-
beddings, (b) fine-­tuned vision transformer models (BEiT and 
ViT), and (c) unsupervised semantic-­distance methods. Across 
data from the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking—Figural 
and the Multi-­Trial Creative Ideation task, supervised learning 
approaches consistently outperformed unsupervised methods, 
with BEiT-­Large vision transformers achieving the strongest 
correlations with human ratings (up to r = 0.85). This work 
underscores the superiority of supervised vision-­transformer 
approaches for scalable, accurate scoring of figural divergent-­
thinking tasks.
5   |   PISA Creativity Thinking AI-­Driven Scoring
How might these methods apply to the PISA Creative Thinking 
tasks? In an initial feasibility study, Primi et al. (2024) tested 
them on a single PISA Creative Thinking item in which students 
wrote a creative story based on an image (the Book Covers task). 
SEMdis scores were computed as the semantic distance be-
tween XLM-­RoBERTa embeddings of the stimulus description 
and student short-­story responses in three languages (English, 
German, and Portuguese). Unlike in Alternate Uses Tasks, 
SEMdis correlated negatively with human scores (r = −0.48 to 
−0.53). Possibly this result may reflect the nature of the task: 
stories averaged 47 words, and averaging word embeddings may 
capture the semantic proximity of the response to the stimulus, 
thus indexing appropriateness more than originality. The study 
also tested few-­shot learning with GPT-­4.1 and DeepSeek mod-
els, achieving correlations (r = 0.57 to 0.60) comparable to the 
inter-­rater reliability among human scorers (r = 0.77). When 
combining semantic distance measures and few-­shot learning 
in a multiple regression predicting human scores, automated 
scoring reached r = 0.77.

6   |   Challenges and Future Directions of AI Scoring 
Systems in PISA
It is clear that artificial intelligence (AI) can score creative 
thinking assessments with reliability comparable to human 
raters. However, implementing such systems at scale in 
international assessments like PISA introduces challenges 
that must be addressed to ensure validity, fairness, and 
interpretability.
6.1   |   Validity, Reliability, and Cross-­Language 
Invariance
A foremost challenge is ensuring psychometric reliability and 
validity—particularly measurement invariance across multiple 
languages and cultural contexts. AI scoring systems may rely on 
various large language models (LLMs), each trained on differ-
ent corpora and in different languages. These variations effec-
tively introduce different “raters” into the assessment process. 
To manage this complexity, Many-­Facet Rasch Models (MFRMs, 
Primi 2014) offer a viable psychometric framework. In this con-
text, facets could include the specific LLM used (e.g., OpenAI, 
Gemini), the original language of the response (or whether it 
was translated to English), the task type, content domain, rating 
criteria, and scoring scale. Modeling these facets enables identifi-
cation of interactions—such as model-­by-­language effects—that 
could compromise score comparability. There is a pressing need

Beyond ensuring comparability, AI scoring opens novel pos-
sibilities for PISA. One compelling direction is to investigate 
whether it is possible to develop a common metric for creativ-
ity across countries by treating LLMs as anchor judges. For in-
stance, responses sampled across the score distribution from 
multiple countries could be translated into English and scored 
using a prompted LLM rubric for originality, flexibility, and ap-
propriateness, with a few benchmarked examples. Correlations 
between human and LLM ratings could then be examined both 
overall and within countries. If country-­level score patterns 
based on LLM ratings align with human scores, and if measure-
ment invariance holds, LLMs could be incorporated as anchor 
judges in an MFRM framework. This could partially control for 
country-­specific rater biases and enable cross-­national compar-
isons on a common scale. Such an approach would allow stud-
ies to ask whether originality scoring achieves measurement 
equivalence or exhibits country-­by-­rubric interaction—in other 
words, whether originality is a generalizable construct or a cul-
turally specific criterion.
6.2   |   Scoring Core Dimensions of Creativity 
Separately
Another important opportunity lies in scoring the core di-
mensions of creative thinking—appropriateness, originality, 
and flexibility—independently. In current PISA scoring ru-
brics, appropriateness is assessed first, and responses deemed 
inappropriate are not evaluated further for originality or 
flexibility. This sequential approach has impeded the study 
of the relationship between originality and appropriateness 
(Benedek and Beaty 2025). With LLMs, it is possible to score 
each dimension separately, as some evidence suggests (Grajzel 
et al. 2023; Kern et al. 2024; Luchini et al. 2025). However, 
this would also require rescoring PISA data by human judges 
on each dimension separately to study convergence with auto-
mated scores.
6.3   |   Interpretability of Embeddings 
and Scoring Rules
A major limitation of AI scoring systems lies in the transpar-
ency of their scoring rules. Even when AI-­generated scores ex-
hibit high correlations with human ratings, the rationale behind 
these scores often remains opaque. Unlike earlier computational 
approaches, which employed simple vector-­based models in met-
aphor studies with clear theoretical underpinnings, contempo-
rary embedding-­based methods lack interpretability (Rumelhart 
and Abrahamson 1973; Tourangeau and Sternberg 1981). The 
high-­dimensional nature of LLM-­generated embeddings ob-
scures the specific semantic features represented in each di-
mension. Studies in mechanistic interpretability suggest that 
multiple semantic features may be superimposed within the 
same embedding space, complicating efforts to explain why a 
particular response receives a given score (see https://​trans​forme​
r-­​circu​its.​pub/​2024/​scali​ng-­​monos​emant​icity/​index.​html from 
the Anthropic research group on mechanistic interpretability).
One important area of investigation with PISA data is what 
semantic information embeddings capture. Can they be 
used, for instance, to run topic modeling (e.g., BERTopic, 
Grootendorst  2022) to classify responses into different themes 
and study the universe of responses and their statistical distri-
butions? Since multimodal models create a shared embedding 
space for images and text, can embeddings from these models be 
used to run topic modeling on images? How is this semantic in-
formation moderated by the number of words or overall response 
length? Initial evidence shows that semantic distance works well 
to assess originality in very short AUT responses but behaves dif-
ferently in longer narratives like in PISA, where the closer the 
response is to the stimulus, the higher the human score (Primi 
et al. 2024). Does this result indicate that PISA scoring is valuing 
elaboration and appropriateness more than originality, or does it 
imply that what semantic measures capture changes as a func-
tion of text length? These are important questions for improving 
understanding of the scoring patterns implemented by semantic 
distance measures.
6.4   |   AI's Limits in Capturing Originality 
and Creativity
A fundamental question in AI-­driven assessment—especially 
when using few-­shot learning—is whether LLMs can truly rec-
ognize originality, or, more provocatively, whether they can be 
creative themselves. If a student produces a genuinely novel idea 
that is not represented in the model's training data, an AI sys-
tem may fail to recognize its value, potentially assigning lower 
scores or classifying it as irrelevant. This paradox raises con-
cerns about AI's ability to fairly evaluate responses that fall out-
side the distribution of its training data. An important question 
for PISA when using a few-­shot learning regimen is to assess the 
types of errors the model makes relative to human scoring: Are 
LLMs more likely to underscore original responses or overesti-
mate conventional ones? Addressing this will clarify the limita-
tions of LLMs in scoring ideas.
A controversial yet intriguing aspect of AI models is their ten-
dency to generate hallucinations—plausible yet factually in-
correct relationships. While traditionally seen as a flaw, these 
hallucinations can, in some cases, be viewed as an emergent 
form of creative thinking. AI-­generated responses that synthe-
size disparate concepts, even if not entirely factual, might ex-
hibit creative potential. This perspective raises the possibility 
that AI could serve as both an assessor and a facilitator of cre-
ativity, steering models toward generating novel, albeit specula-
tive, solutions (Sun et al. 2024).
Beyond educational assessment, the ability to steer AI models 
toward recognizing and fostering creativity has implications 
for artificial intelligence research itself. If AI models can be 
fine-­tuned to assess creativity, they could, in turn, be used to 
evaluate other AI systems, forming a reinforcement learning 
feedback loop that incentivizes creative problem solving. This

6.5   |   Open Access to Tasks, Responses, and Scoring 
Platforms
One practical constraint in advancing this agenda with PISA 
data is that most materials (student responses, items, and soft-
ware) are not publicly available. They can be requested for re-
search purposes, but access is not guaranteed, especially for 
student responses. All the developments proposed here for 
creativity assessment—and the practicality of large-­scale as-
sessments—depend on coordinated work across the research 
community. Any effort to make these data more open and pub-
licly available would have a substantial impact on the advance-
ment of creativity assessment.
Another important direction is the development of open-­source 
platforms with creativity tasks—such as those used in PISA—
that can be adopted by the research and teaching communities 
in their assessments. These platforms should incorporate au-
tomated scoring systems, following the example of initiatives 
like the Creativity Assessment Platform (CAP; https://​cap.​ist.​
psu.​edu/​claus​) and Open Creativity Scoring with AI (OCSAI; 
https://​opens​coring.​du.​edu/​). The scoring burden that once 
hindered the inclusion of creativity assessments in large-­scale 
systems has already been overcome. Automated platforms can 
now make such assessments widely accessible and accelerate our 
understanding of creativity as they are regularly integrated into 
educational evaluations (Benedek and Beaty 2025). It would be 
especially fruitful to develop such platforms using publicly re-
leased PISA items.
In conclusion, realizing the promise of AI-­powered scoring for 
PISA and other large-­scale creativity assessments requires a 
dual commitment to rigor and transparency. Psychometric safe-
guards—such as MFRMs with LLM facets and cross-­language 
measurement invariance studies—are essential to secure com-
parability across countries and languages. Methodologically, 
treating LLMs as anchor judges, independently scoring core 
dimensions (appropriateness, originality, flexibility), and au-
diting error profiles under few-­shot regimes can strengthen 
validity and fairness. Technically, investments in interpret-
ability for embedding-­based scoring and systematic analyses 
of semantic distance behavior across response lengths will 
clarify what is being measured. Finally, enabling secure, ethi-
cal access to response data will accelerate cumulative science 
and practice. Together, these steps can position PISA to use 
AI not only to scale reliable scoring but also to deepen our 
understanding of how creativity manifests across cultures, 
thereby supporting more equitable, meaningful international 
comparisons.
