What about Tests Make Students Switch Off?
Item-Level Predictors of Disengagement on the PISA
Juste M. Mehou and Brian C. Leventhal
James Madison University
International assessments, such as the Programme for International Student As-
sessment (PISA), play a critical role in monitoring educational progress, evaluating
teaching effectiveness, and supporting institutional accountability. The validity of
these interpretations rests on the assumption that students exert sufﬁcient effort and
perform to the best of their abilities, condition necessary for tests to reﬂect stu-
dents’ true proﬁciency. However, researchers have documented concerns about low
effort in low-stakes test contexts where results carry no direct consequences for ex-
aminees. Despite growing awareness of this issue, research examining low effort
correlates in large-scale assessments like PISA remains limited. Moreover, exist-
ing approaches often overlook the correlation between low effort and ability, which
can bias estimates of both constructs. The current work used an explanatory IRTree
model that jointly estimated science ability and rapid-guessing propensity to exam-
ine item-level predictors of disengagement in PISA 2022 science, including Item Po-
sition, item interactivity, cognitive demand, and assessment sequencing. Interactive
items decreased disengagement which rose with Item Position. Cognitive demand
was the strongest predictor, with high-demand items sharply increasing disengage-
ment. Also, science items were less likely to be rapidly guessed when students started
with adaptive math or reading rather than science, even after controlling for posi-
tion. As expected, disengagement was strongly negatively related to science ability,
underscoring the value of jointly modeling proﬁciency and effort in low-stakes con-
texts. We discuss these results and explore avenues for future research.
Introduction
Benchmark international assessments enable governments to evaluate their
youth’s literacy and development on a global scale, underscoring the strategic im-
portance of education (OECD, 2023). A fundamental but tacit assumption guiding
the administration of these assessments is that students will exert effort and per-
form to the best of their ability. This assumption is crucial because only when it is
met can the resulting scores meaningfully reﬂect students’ abilities and support the
uses of these assessments (Wise & Kong, 2005). However, research has observed
that students often display low effort, especially on low-stakes tests where the re-
sults do not have direct consequences for them (Wise, 2017). The Programme for
International Student Assessment (PISA) falls into this category of low-stakes tests.
Therefore, lack of effort on the PISA introduces construct-irrelevant variance into
the scores, undermining their validity for the intended purposes (AERA et al., 2014,
p. 14). Such variance negatively affects the psychometric information derived from

factors contributing to low test-taking effort and develop strategies to address this is-

Several factors, that can be classiﬁed into test design (e.g., Wise et al., 2009),

examinees (e.g., DeMars, 2007) have been investigated by previous research, but
this prior work was conducted within a theoretical framework that must be outlined
to clarify the added value of the approach used in the present work.
Examinee Low Effort
Cognitive approaches to motivation, such as the Eccles and Wigﬁeld’s (2000) Ex-
pectancy Value Theory (EVT) have shifted the focus in psychology from stimulus-
response models to internal cognitive processes that inﬂuence how individuals in-
terpret and respond to tasks (Pintrich & Schunk, 2008). EVT identiﬁes two key
cognitive components that shape achievement behavior: expectancy for success
(“Can I do this task?”) and subjective task value (“Why should I engage in this
task?”). Expectancy refers to students’ self-perceptions of ability, while task value
includes a range of motivational factors. The EVT model offers a theoretical ba-
sis for understanding low-effort behavior in testing contexts. When examinees hold
low expectancy-value beliefs, their engagement tends to decline, resulting in scores
that may not accurately reﬂect their actual knowledge and abilities (DeMars, 2007;
Schiel, 1996; Wise & DeMars, 2005). Numerous studies have explored how low
effort affects performance and psychometric quality using varied operational deﬁni-
tions of effort.
Early efforts to measure low test-taking effort primarily relied on post-test self-
report instruments, such as single-item surveys or Likert-type scales (Schiel, 1996;
Sundre, 1999), which offered limited validity due to their dependence on student
self-report accuracy and self-awareness. To address these limitations, researchers be-
gan analyzing response patterns, though these patterns were not uniquely diagnos-
tic of low effort. Moreover, both self-report and response pattern methods assumed
that effort remained stable throughout the test, overlooking evidence that motivation
can ﬂuctuate across items (Pastor et al., 2019). This recognition, coupled with the
growth of digital assessments, shifted attention toward process data, especially item-
level response time, as a more precise and unobtrusive means of detecting low effort
behavior at the item level (Alahmadi & DeMars, 2024).
The use of response times to assess test engagement was ﬁrst formalized by Wise
and Kong (2005), who introduced the concept of rapid guessing on low-stakes, un-
speeded assessments as a behavioral indicator of low motivation. They proposed that
each test item represents a decision point: the examinee either engages in solution
behavior or opts for non-solution behavior such as rapid guessing. However, a central
challenge in this approach has been the establishment of a reliable line of demarca-
tion to distinguish between these behaviors.

A widely used method to detect rapid guessing applies a ﬁxed time threshold, such
as 3 or 5 seconds, uniformly across items (Wise & Ma, 2012). While this k-seconds
rule is simple and scalable, it risks misclassiﬁcation. For instance, a short vocabulary
item may prompt legitimate responses within the threshold, resulting in false posi-
tives. A more reﬁned approach models item response times as a mixture of two dis-
tributions, one for rapid guesses and one for solution behavior, and sets the threshold
at their intersection. Although theoretically grounded, this method is susceptible to
overlapping or unimodal distributions, which can reduce its reliability across items.
More sophisticated techniques integrate response time and accuracy, based on the
assumption that non-effortful responses should not exceed chance-level correctness
(Lee & Jia, 2014). As another alternative, Wise and Ma (2012) introduced norma-
tive thresholds that offer a practical and scalable solution for large item banks. These
thresholds are set as a percentage of the average item response time, capped at 10
seconds. The NT10, for instance, deﬁnes a response-time threshold equal to 10%
of the item-level mean response time (capped at 10 seconds), under the assumption
that responses below this threshold are unlikely to contain ability-relevant cognitive
processing. This approach maintains accuracy while avoiding manual inspection or
complex parametric modeling. Wise and Ma (2012) demonstrated that the Normative
Threshold (NT) method is less susceptible to false positives and false negatives and
remains practical for scale applications, offering advantages over visual inspection
and mixture model approaches.
Once item thresholds for identifying rapid guessing are established, the next chal-
lenge involves determining how to handle responses ﬂagged as non-solution behav-
ior. A common method is to exclude examinees who exceed a certain percentage of
rapid guesses across items (Kong et al., 2007; Sundre & Wise, 2003; Wise & De-
Mars, 2005). While this method is straightforward and practical, it can lead to the
loss of valid data and inﬂate sample-level performance metrics (Rios et al., 2022). In
response to these limitations, alternative strategies have emerged that aim to retain
valid data while accounting for low effort. These include scoring rapid guesses as
incorrect (Wright, 2019), treating them as missing and applying an Effort Moderated
(EM) IRT model (Wise & DeMars, 2006), or using an Effort Moderated Imputation
model (Hauser & Kingsbury, 2009). However, these models often assume that ef-
fort is unrelated to ability treating rapid-guessing responses as a Missing at Random
(MAR) process. Rios and Soland (2021b) found that when rapid guessing was dis-
proportionately common among low-ability students, the EM model introduced bias
in ability estimates, ranging from .06 to .18 standard deviations. This suggests that
effort-related missingness may be better characterized as Missing Not at Random
(MNAR), as other examinee characteristics, such as examinee gender, may inﬂuence
the “missing” or non-effortful responses, but are not fully captured by the data (De
Boeck & Partchev, 2012; Rose et al., 2010).
Another class of methods has emerged using a latent variable approach to model
effort as a continuous variable, without assuming any relationship between effort and
ability, whether evidenced by external measures or test-taking true ability (Leventhal
& Pastor, 2024; Liu et al., 2019). The Item Response Tree Model for Disengage-
ment (Leventhal & Pastor, 2024) used in the present study is an example of such
methods.

Item Response Tree Model for Disengagement
Item Response Tree (IRTree) models belong to the family of sequential response
models (Tutz, 1990). IRTree models are inherently multidimensional with each se-
quential decision governed by a latent variable. The IRTree Model for Disengage-
ment proposed by Leventhal and Pastor (2024) incorporates two latent variables: the
tendency to engage in rapid guessing, referred to as test-taking disengagement, and
the ability parameter for the trait of interest. This model posits that a latent vari-
able (disengagement) governs disengaged responses. This is analogous to missing
values not at random (MNAR) conceptualized as resulting from a “missingness” la-
tent variable (De Boeck & Partchev, 2012; Glas & Pimentel, 2008; Holman & Glas,
2005). This latent variable drives the observed response to either the terminal node
(indicating a rapid guess) or to the solution-behavior node, which is governed by the
ability latent variable, which then determines the probability of a correct or incor-
rect response, as shown in Figure 1. This IRTree approach has been supported in
low-stakes testing applications and in simulation work (Leventhal & Pastor, 2024;
Leventhal & Hunsberger, 2025).
Investigating Item-Level Predictors of Disengagement
Several studies have examined potential correlates of test-taking disengagement.
The literature identiﬁes test-situation correlates (e.g., the test is multistage or not),
examinee-level correlates (e.g., the examinee gender), and item-level correlates (e.g.,
Item Position). Of the three, item correlates are the focus of the present study.
Item Position is a well-established predictor of test-taking disengagement, with
later items typically associated with reduced engagement. Wise et al. (2009), using a
two-level hierarchical model on Natural World Assessment data, found Item Position
to be the second strongest predictor of solution behavior, reporting a .279 decrease in
the log-odds of engagement for every 1 standard deviation increase in position (about

approach suited to PISA’s rotating cluster structure.
In addition to Item Position, several item characteristics such as use of graphics,
format, difﬁculty, and length have been studied as predictors of test-taking disen-
gagement. Wise et al. (2009) found that items containing graphics (e.g., a table or
ﬁgure) increased the likelihood of engagement, raising the log-odds of solution be-
havior by .46 standard deviations, and that graphics could mitigate the negative im-
pact of Item Position. They also reported that Technology-Enhanced Items (TEIs)
were less susceptible to rapid guessing than traditional formats. In the PISA context,
Rios and Soland (2022) found that interactive (non-static) stimuli signiﬁcantly re-
duced rapid guessing by .24 standard deviations, though item type (e.g., open-ended
vs. multiple-choice) had no signiﬁcant effect. Wise et al. (2009) and Csányi and
Molnár (2024) reported that higher difﬁculty predicted lower engagement, though
Rios and Soland (2022) did not replicate this. Additionally, Wise et al. (2009) found
that longer items decreased engagement, with a one standard deviation increase in
character count associated with a .189 drop in the log-odds of solution behavior.
These ﬁndings are shaped by differences in how low effort is deﬁned and mea-
sured. However, none of these studies formally conceptualized disengagement as
a latent construct or examined its potential relationship with ability, an opportunity
uniquely afforded by the IRTree model. The IRTree model allows disengagement and
ability to be estimated simultaneously, enabling the two latent variables to inform
each other. This results in a more nuanced measure of disengagement and a more
accurate analysis of its item-level predictors. We further adopt a modern modeling
approach that treats item parameters, like difﬁculty, as random effects (De Boeck,
2008), enabling a more reﬁned decomposition of difﬁculty as a function of item
characteristics through the Random Effect Logistic Test Model (Sauder, 2020). Fi-
nally, our analysis draws on the post-pandemic PISA 2022 dataset, offering fresh
insights into student engagement in a global, low-stakes assessment context shaped
by pandemic-era educational disruptions.
Method
Measure Description
PISA is an international assessment designed to evaluate the ability of students
nearing the end of compulsory education in solving real-world problems (OECD,
2023). In its most recent cycle, PISA 2022, approximately 690,000 students from
81 countries and economies participated. The assessment covered three core do-
mains: Mathematics, Science, and Reading as well as an optional innovative do-
main, Critical Thinking. Administered in either computer- or paper-based formats,
the test consists of two one-hour assessments in two subject areas, followed by a
35-minute background questionnaire. This study focuses on the science domain,
which was administered to approximately 40% of students.

Sample. A total of 4,552 students from 154 schools participated in PISA 2022
in the United States. Of these, 2,201 students were assigned the standard 2-hour test
booklet consisting of two 1-hour sections covering two of the three PISA domains,
with science necessarily being one of the two. Each of these students therefore re-
ceived two 30-minutes science clusters. The two science clusters were drawn from a
bank of six mutually exclusive clusters comprising 115 items in total. Each cluster
contains 4 to 6 units, and each unit includes 1 to 5 items, yielding 18 to 20 items
per cluster. Items within a unit share a common stimulus or “context prompt” (e.g.,
Wild Oat Grass, Comparing Light Bulbs). Because each student received only two
of the six science clusters, the 38 to 40 items administered varied across students by
design (planned missingness). This design is accommodated in our analysis under
a Missing at Random assumption, using all available information in the dataset. To
identify rapid guessing, we employed the Normative Threshold 10 (NT10) method,
which assigns a value of 1 to responses where students spent less than 10% of the
U.S. average response time for that item, and 0 otherwise. If 10% of the average ex-
ceeded 10 seconds, the threshold was capped at 10 seconds. Missing item responses
were also coded as missing for rapid guessing. The NT10 method was selected due to
its suitability with large item pools and its prior application in PISA disengagement
research (e.g., Rios & Soland, 2022)

Depth of Knowledge (COG_DEM). PISA categorizes three levels of item cogni-
tive demand: Low, Medium, and High. Prior studies (e.g., Wise et al., 2009) suggest
that items with higher cognitive demands tend to elicit higher disengagement. There-
fore, we hypothesize that low-depth items will exhibit lower levels of disengagement,
as they are expected to be associated with higher resistance to disengagement (i.e.,
greater item difﬁculty in terms of disengagement). The variable was dummy coded
into COGDEMlow (1 when COG_DEM is low, 0 otherwise) and COGDEMmedium (1 when
COG_DEM is medium, 0 otherwise).

content. Based on Rios and Soland (2022), we hypothesized that items featuring in-
teractive stimuli will have lower disengagement rates compared to those with static
stimuli. This variable was dummy coded as STIMINTERACT taking the value of 1
when the item was interactive and 0 otherwise.

Item Position within a booklet POSIIBO. Item Position is a well-established pre-
dictor of disengagement, with several studies showing that items appearing later in
a test are more likely to elicit disengaged responses, likely due to accumulating cog-
nitive fatigue (Csányi & Molnár, 2024; Wise et al., 2009). However, in PISA 2022,
items do not occupy ﬁxed positions across all test booklets. Among the 66 total
forms, Forms 13 through 36, used in our sample, each include two science clus-
ters. While items have ﬁxed positions within their respective clusters, the clusters
themselves vary in position across forms. As a result, the same item can appear
in different test positions depending on the booklet. For example, Science Clus-
ter 1 appears as the third cluster on Form 13 but as the fourth cluster on Form
18.
To determine the actual position at which a student encountered a speciﬁc item,
we combined the item’s ﬁxed position within its cluster (from Annex A of the PISA
2022 technical report, OECD, 2024) with the cluster’s variable position across forms
(OECD, 2024, p. 34). Practically, we ﬁrst identiﬁed students who received a speciﬁc
booklet, as they all receive items in identical positions. For each booklet, we exam-
ined the science clusters’ placement. When a science cluster appeared ﬁrst, the items
retained their permanent positions. For science clusters in the second position, we
added the number of items from the ﬁrst cluster to each item’s permanent position.
These calculations apply when science clusters lead the booklet. When mathematics
or reading clusters preceded science clusters, we added their total item count to the
previously calculated positions.
The dummy variables Math ﬁrst and Reading ﬁrst help capture the nuanced inﬂu-
ence of Item Position on the disengagement latent variable. For instance, when both
equal zero, the Item Position effect reﬂects the average for students who received
science clusters ﬁrst.

Model Calibration
MCMC. The Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) procedure is used here fol-
lowing standard practice in the IRTree literature, where Bayesian estimation is well
suited to models with hierarchical latent structures and conditional branching (e.g.,
Ames & Leventhal, 2023, De Boeck & Partchev, 2012; Leventhal & Pastor, 2024;
Stone & Zhu, 2015). MCMC generates samples from the posterior distributions of
the model parameters and provides a ﬂexible framework for jointly estimating the
multiple latent subprocesses represented in the IRTree.
Parameter speciﬁcation. We used the SAS software to implement this procedure
for both models through the MCMC procedure (SAS Institute Inc., 2014). Because
the Bayesian estimation framework requires speciﬁcation of prior distributions, we
used weakly informative normal priors with large variances for item and person pa-
rameters, consistent with conventions in the IRTree and hierarchical IRT literature
(e.g., Leventhal & Pastor, 2024; Stone & Zhu, 2015). These priors provide mini-
mal constraints on the posterior and prior simulation has shown them (Leventhal &
Hunsberger, 2025) to yield stable parameter recovery even when they do not match
the true generating distributions. The speciﬁc priors used in this work are summa-
rized in Table 2.
For both models, we retained a total of 300,000 iterations thinned by 30, after
the ﬁrst 10,000 were discarded, to avoid posterior approximations sensitive to initial
values.
Analysis Plan
Assessment of model convergence. We used both visual and numerical diagnos-
tics to evaluate convergence (Stone & Zhu, 2015). For visual methods, we examined
trace plots to assess mixing and stationarity by observing parameter values across
iterations, and we reviewed autocorrelation to evaluate the degree of dependency
between samples at increasing lags. For numerical methods, we applied Geweke di-
agnostic (Geweke, 1992), which compares the means of early and late segments of
the Markov chain using a z-test. We also used the Heidelberg and Welch diagnos-
tic (Heidelberger & Welch, 1981), which performs a two-part test: ﬁrst, it assesses

Model comparison. After conﬁrming convergence, we compared model ﬁt us-
ing the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC). A difference of more than 10 in DIC
typically provides strong evidence in favor of a model (Stone and Zhu, 2015). How-
ever, Plummer (2008) showed that when the number of parameters is signiﬁcantly
smaller than the sample size, the DIC might not penalize enough the more complex
model. Therefore, we supplemented the DIC comparison with an additional heuris-
tic, namely a 1% increase in DIC as a threshold for preferring a more complex model
(Ames and Leventhal, 2023).
Predictor interpretation. After selecting the ﬁnal model, we used posterior sum-
maries to interpret the results. We reported the Expected A Posteriori (EAP) mean
and standard deviation (SD) of each posterior to summarize the estimated effects of
predictors, with smaller SDs indicating greater precision. We also reported the 95%
Highest Posterior Density (HPD) intervals to provide the most credible range for
each parameter estimate.
Results
Descriptive Statistics
Three out of four examinees (1,683 out of 2,201 U.S. students) who received forms
containing the science discipline exhibited solution behavior on all items. Among the
remaining 518 examinees, 183 rapid guessed on one item, 110 on two items, 50 on
three items, and 175 on more than three items. A total of 2,063 responses represent-
ing 3% of all the responses, were ﬂagged as a rapid guess. For simple four-option
multiple-choice items, NT10-classiﬁed responses had an average proportion-correct
of approximately .23, close to the chance level of .25, suggesting that examinees
responding below the NT10 threshold were indeed likely not using ability-relevant
information, which is consistent with the intended interpretation of disengagement
in this study. Out of the 115 science items distributed across six mutually exclusive
science clusters, 79% (91) were standard items (i.e., without built-in interactivity),
while 21% had some form of built-in interactivity. Most items were medium in cog-
nitive demand (64%) followed by low demand (27%) and high demand (9%). On
average, 18 examinees (SD = 15, Min = 1 and Max = 75) rapid guessed on a given
item out of the 2,201 examinees. Table 3 presents the contingency table of Stimulus
Type by Depth of Knowledge. A chi-square test of independence between the two
variables shows that the two variables are independent (χ2(2) = 3.432, p = 0.18).
Moreover, the Pearson correlation between the two variables is –.0448, indicating
that each will likely contribute differently to explain Stage 1 item difﬁculty variance.

Table 6 presents the model-predicted probability of rapid guessing for the baseline
(most disengagement-eliciting) condition and for several practical, one-change-at-a-
time scenarios. Practically, the most disengagement-eliciting scenario is an average
examinee (Examinee A) facing a high Depth of Knowledge (DoK), standard (non-
interactive) science item that appears at the very end of the science section on a
form where science is administered ﬁrst. Under these baseline conditions, our model
predicts a .94 probability of rapid guessing (RG). Holding those baseline conditions
constant, moving science later in the overall test substantially lowers disengagement.
If Math adaptive clusters appear on the form before the science clusters, Examinee
A’s predicted probability of rapid guessing drops to .37. If reading adaptive clusters
appear before science instead, the predicted probability drops to .49.
Also, keeping everything else the same as in the baseline scenario, changing the
last science item from a standard format to an interactive format reduces the pre-
dicted probability of rapid guessing from .94 to .58. If instead, the last item stayed
standard but was a medium or low DoK item, the model predicts that the probability
of rapid guessing decreases to approximately zero. Finally, If the baseline conditions
are maintained but the item is moved from the 40th science item (last) to the 20th sci-
ence item, the predicted probability of rapid guessing decreases to .73. If it is moved
to the ﬁrst science item, the predicted probability decreases further to .35.
Discussion
This study aimed to identify item-level predictors of disengagement in the science
domain of PISA 2022 using an explanatory IRTree model that simultaneously esti-
mated disengagement and ability. We examined the effects of Item Position, Stimu-
lus Type, cognitive demand, and the ﬁrst discipline encountered on the likelihood of
rapid guessing.
Interactive item stimuli emerged as a meaningful protective factor against disen-
gagement. In our model, the odds of rapid guessing on a standard item were 11.13
times the odds on an interactive item. This direction is consistent with prior re-
search in both PISA and other operational settings. For example, Rios and Soland
(2022) found, across countries, that an interactive stimulus was the only signiﬁcant
item-level predictor after controlling examinee-level factors. Similarly, the OECD
working paper on PISA 2018 reading (Avvisati et al., 2024) reports across coun-
tries lower odds of rapid guessing when interactive material is present (OR, odds ra-
tios: .81). Wise et al. (2021) showed descriptively that rapid guessing is less common
on technology-enhanced items than on multiple-choice items (e.g., science: 3.4%
vs. .1%). Taken together, the consistency in direction across contexts supports the
interpretation that interactive features can reduce disengaged responding, though the
magnitude of the beneﬁt appears to depend on how “interactivity” is operationalized,
the test it is operationalized on and the sample considered (e.g., the United States vs.
OECD countries).
Item Position was also a strong predictor: each successive item had 1.089 times
the odds of rapid guessing compared to the preceding item. This aligns with the
broader literature that rapid guessing increases as students progress through a test.
For instance, the OECD analysis (Avvisati et al., 2024) reports pronounced “testing

Cognitive demand (Depth of Knowledge) emerged as the most dominant item-

ing for high cognitive demand was 3,092 times that of the medium cognitive de-
mand items. In practical terms, our predicted-probability scenarios show that chang-
ing only cognitive demand (from high to medium/low) can shift rapid guessing from
near certainty to near zero. These results differ sharply from Rios and Soland (2022)
PISA 2018 ﬁndings where neither medium nor high cognitive demand was a robust
predictor. A comparable construct in the OECD PISA 2018 reading models, item
difﬁculty, shows a clear but smaller association with rapid guessing (OR = 3.061
for the highest difﬁculty level vs. Level 1). In our PISA 2022 science model, cogni-
tive demand yielded substantially larger effects, suggesting that demanding science
items are especially prone to eliciting disengaged responding. The IRTree framework
provides a coherent explanation for why these effects can be large. Because disen-
gagement and science ability were estimated jointly and allowed to correlate, rapid
guessing propensity varied systematically with ability through the estimated latent
association (ρ ≈−.64), indicating that lower-ability examinees were more likely to
disengage. At the same time, cognitive demand was linked to performance: highly
demanding items had lower average success rates than low-demand items (proportion
correct ≈.52 vs. .66), implying that these items disproportionately challenge lower-
proﬁciency students. Therefore, cognitive demand concentrates disengagement risk
among examinees who are already struggling, such that the “cognitive demand ef-
fect” reﬂects both an item inﬂuence and the ampliﬁcation of disengagement among
lower-ability students (on average) under high-demand conditions.
A novel and practically important ﬁnding concerns discipline order: after control-
ling for position and other item features, science items were substantially less likely
to be rapidly guessed when students began the assessment with an adaptive math-
ematics or reading cluster rather than with science. Speciﬁcally, the odds of rapid
guessing on science items were 26.3 times higher when science was taken ﬁrst (vs.
Math-ﬁrst) and 15.7 times higher when science was taken ﬁrst (vs. reading-ﬁrst).
This effect is not reducible to “later items elicit more disengagement” because posi-
tion was already accounted for. Instead, it suggests that the testing experience prior
to science, potentially including adaptivity, early success/failure experiences or pac-
ing, may alter subsequent engagement. Although no prior research has examined
this speciﬁc sequence effect, existing studies have shown that adaptive tests gener-
ally promote greater engagement than non-adaptive ones (Rios & Guo, 2020; Wise
& Kingsbury, 2016). While it remains unclear whether this engagement carries over

Limitations of This Study and Future Research
Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the ﬁndings of this
study. First, in Model 1, item “difﬁculty” was modeled using only a ﬁxed effect
rather than both a ﬁxed and a random effect, as was done in Model 2. This modeling
choice was made to maintain alignment with the speciﬁcations used in Leventhal
and Pastor (2024), allowing for consistency and comparability across studies. While
incorporating a random effect in Model 1 might have yielded a more precise baseline,
we prioritized methodological coherence with prior work.
Second, Model 2 did not include interaction terms among item-level or examinee-
level predictors. This decision reﬂects the absence of speciﬁc theoretical or empir-
ical hypotheses in the disengagement literature regarding how these characteristics
should interact to inﬂuence rapid guessing. Most prior work conceptualizes the pre-
dictors considered here as exerting additive effects (Csányi and Molnár, 2024; Wise
et al., 2009). While interactions could, in principle, capture more nuanced patterns
(e.g., whether the inﬂuence of cognitive demand varies across student subgroups),
investigating such effects would substantially broaden the scope of the study.
Third, although rapid guessing is relatively infrequent, we suggest exploring a 2PL
model at Stage 1 to evaluate whether incorporating variation in item discrimination
yields additional insight into how strongly items differentiate between students with
higher versus lower tendencies to disengage.
Fourth, although NT10 captured meaningful instances of rapid responding on
interactive items in our data, the navigational demands of interactive tasks may
differ from traditional multiple-choice formats. Future work could therefore con-
sider whether item-type-speciﬁc time thresholds or alternative indicators of disen-
gagement provide improved classiﬁcation for interaction-rich items. Future research
could also extend our ﬁndings by formally re-estimating the IRTree models under al-
ternative normative thresholds such as NT15 and NT20. Such work would be particu-
larly valuable in settings where response-time distributions differ across populations,
test formats, or subject areas, or where the prevalence and nature of disengagement
may vary.
Additionally, applying the framework to other PISA-participating countries could
reveal whether the item-level predictors of disengagement identiﬁed in the U.S. sam-
ple generalize cross-nationally or reﬂect country-speciﬁc patterns. This might in-
clude re-analyzing prior PISA cycles (e.g., 2018) using the IRTree approach to eval-
uate whether discrepancies observed in earlier work are attributable to differences

Practical Implications
This study offers guidance for improving large-scale, low-stakes assessments like
PISA. First, interactive item formats such as drag-and-drop and multi-step tasks sig-
niﬁcantly reduce disengagement. Expanding the use of Technology-Enhanced Items
(TEIs) could thus enhance engagement. Compared to the scenario that elicits the
highest propensity to disengage, the probability of rapid guessing decreased from .93
to .58 when the item changed to be interactive. Also, disengagement increases later
in the test, highlighting the need to consider sequence as a dynamic factor in test
design as well as overall test length. Introducing engaging or easier items later may
help sustain effort. Similarly, higher cognitive demand items, although essential for
assessing complex skills, should be thoughtfully placed to mitigate disengagement.
For example, the model predicts that, on average, a highly demanding interactive
item in the 5th position has a 6% probability of rapid guessing, compared with a
highly demanding non-interactive item in the 20th position, which has a 73% proba-
bility of rapid guessing. Thoughtfully considering item format, sequence, and cogni-
tive demand can improve assessment construction to promote greater student effort
and therefore more meaningful results.
