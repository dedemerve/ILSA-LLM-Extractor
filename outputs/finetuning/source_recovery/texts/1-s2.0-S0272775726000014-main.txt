ABSTRACT
This paper estimates the causal effects of math instructional time on student achievement using within-student
variation across math topics in the TIMSS 2011 data. Based on the assessment’s detailed measurement of math
skills, it shows that each weekly hour of math instruction in a given topic increases student test scores in
that topic by 4.3% of a SD. However, the productivity of instructional time varies significantly based on the
implementation of teaching practices that prioritize student active participation: teachers emphasizing these
practices are more than twice as productive as other teachers. This result holds true regardless of student
gender, social origin, and is observed internationally in 42 countries.

Introduction
The development of human capital in schools primarily depends
on the quantity and quality of instructional time to which students
are exposed. A growing literature in economics consistently finds substantial differences in the productivity of instructional time, not only
across countries (Bietenbeck & Collins, 2023; Lavy, 2015a; Rivkin
& Schiman, 2015), but also across schools and individual teachers
within the same country (Jackson et al., 2014). These differences in
instructional productivity have long-term consequences, manifesting in
adult outcomes years after students have left the educational system.
In this context, math skills are of particular importance: they are both
a core component of educational achievement and a key predictor of
individual earnings and economic growth (Goodman, 2019; Hanushek
& Woessmann, 2008; Joensen & Nielsen, 2009; Rose & Betts, 2004). Yet
despite growing evidence that instructional time is not equally effective
across settings, we still know relatively little about what makes it
productive.
This paper examines the extent to which instructional time contributes to student learning in mathematics, and how its effectiveness
varies with classroom teaching practices. Using U.S. data from the 2011
wave of the Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study
(hereafter, TIMSS) international assessment, it first estimates the effect
of instructional time by comparing performance across math topics
within the same student, taught by the same teacher. This strategy

controls for any systematic relationship between individual determinants of student math achievement – including individual student math
ability and math teacher quality – and the total amount of math instructional time to which students are exposed. It identifies the causal effect
of math instructional time under the assumption that the allocation of
math instructional time across math topics is unrelated to student math
topic-specific skills.
This assumption is made credible by two key features of the U.S. institutional and assessment context. First, the decentralized structure of
the education system generates substantial variation in the amount and
distribution of instructional time across school districts (Kraft & Novicoff, 2024). While states set minimum instructional time requirements,
school districts retain considerable autonomy in implementing and
allocating time across subjects and topics. This institutional structure
induces variation in topic-specific instructional time that is arguably
exogenous to individual students and teachers, and largely driven by local curricular standards rather than classroom-level decisions. Second,
the TIMSS assessment plays no evaluative role for teachers or students:
individual test results are not reported to schools, and students face no
consequences for their performance. As a result, there is no incentive to
align instructional time with test content, and time allocation decisions
are unlikely to be driven by anticipation of TIMSS items.

Building upon the results of this falsification test, I estimate the

were taught over the year. The results show that student test scores
in a given math topic increase by about 4.3% of a standard deviation
with each weekly hour of instructional time devoted to that topic.
Using the same methodology, I then investigate the extent to which
the effect of instructional time varies with the teaching practices implemented in the classroom. Specifically, I explore the relationship
between instructional productivity and the implementation of practices

interactions between teachers and students, as opposed to teachercentered practices based on teacher lecturing, student memorization or
basic problem solving. I explore these issues using an ‘‘Active Learning’’

These analyses unveil substantial differentials in instructional productivity among U.S. math teachers based on the teaching practices
they implement in the classroom. The effect of each weekly hour of
instructional time on student test scores varies from 3% of a standard
deviation for teachers at the 25th of the AL index distribution to 5.6%
of a standard deviation for teachers at the 75th of this distribution.
This result is robust to the inclusion of controls for other dimensions of
teaching quality, in line with the notion that active learning practices
may have an independent positive effect on student learning. Heterogeneity analyses also reveal that all groups of students are positively
affected by the implementation of active learning practices, irrespective
of gender or social background.
To explore the external validity of these results, I then take advantage of the international dimension of the TIMSS data and replicate
the analyses in the 42 other countries participating in the 2011 assessment. The results confirm a robust positive relationship between
the implementation of active learning practices and instructional productivity within this international sample. Given the diverse economic
and educational contexts represented in this sample, this analysis lends
strong external validity to the results presented in this paper.
This paper contributes to the literature evaluating the causal effect
of instructional time on student math skills. These skills represent a
fundamental dimension of human capital and are important predictors
of both aggregate economic growth (Hanushek & Woessmann, 2008)
and individual’s future earnings (Goodman, 2019; Joensen & Nielsen,
2009; Rose & Betts, 2004). Several studies based in the U.S. and other
countries provide evidence of a positive impact of instructional time on
student math achievement. While most of these studies exploit changes
in instructional time caused by school-day or school-year extensions,
weather shocks or bundled educational interventions that include additional learning time, fewer studies examine the direct link between
subject-specific instructional time and student achievement.1 Notable
exceptions are Lavy (2015a), Rivkin and Schiman (2015), Cattaneo
et al. (2017) and Bietenbeck and Collins (2023) who exploit within student between subject variations in instructional time across countries,
and rely on the assumption that these variations are independent from
student subject-specific skills.

internationally.

1.1.2. Math skills and instructional time in the TIMSS data
The TIMSS math assessment encompasses four math topics: (i)
Number, (ii) Algebra, (iii) Geometry, and (iv) Data and Chance. Each topic
is further divided into subtopics (e.g., Number: Fractions and decimals),
for a total of 19 subtopics. A unique feature of the TIMSS data is that it
provides fine-grained measures of student achievement at the subtopic
level: performance scores can be derived for each subtopic, conditional
on the student having responded to at least one item associated with
that subtopic.
To reduce testing time while covering a broad curriculum, TIMSS
uses a matrix sampling design. Students are randomly assigned one of
14 booklets, each containing a distinct combination of mathematics
items that span different topics and subtopics. As a result, not all
students are tested on the same subtopics, and subtopic-level scores
are only available for student-subtopic pairs when the booklet assigned
to the student includes at least one corresponding item. In this paper,
I restrict the analysis to those observed student-subtopic pairs. This
ensures that the outcome variables reflect direct student performance
on actual test items, rather than imputed scores or aggregate-level
scales.
An important implication of this assessment design is that students
within the same classroom are administered different booklets. Teachers do not know which specific items or subtopics will be tested for
which students, and the content of the TIMSS booklets is not disclosed publicly before implementation of the test. Moreover, test results
have no consequences for students, teachers, or schools, and TIMSS
is not used for grading, placement, or evaluation purposes. As such,
there is no incentive for teachers to tailor instruction toward specific
test content. This setup rules out teaching-to-the-test and reinforces
the credibility of instructional decisions as being independent of test
content.
Each mathematics teacher who teaches a participating class is asked
to complete a questionnaire reporting the total amount of weekly
instructional time devoted to mathematics, as well as the distribution
of that time across the four broad math domains. In the U.S. 8th grade
sample, a single teacher typically teaches all math topics to the class
and is solely responsible for reporting both the total and the topicspecific instructional time. This ensures consistency in the measurement
of instructional exposure at the classroom level.
The variation in instructional time and math content described in
Section 1.1.1 is clearly visible in the data. On average, students receive
4.4 h of math instruction per week, with values ranging from 3.75 h
per week at the 25th percentile to 5 h per week at the 75th percentile
(Table A5). There is also considerable variation in how this time is
allocated across math topics. For example, while 25% of students are
taught Algebra for less than 1.26 h per week on average, 25% are taught
it for more than 3.33 h.
The teacher survey also provides detailed information on which
subtopics were actually covered during the school year. For each of
the 19 subtopics assessed in TIMSS, teachers indicate whether they
devoted any instructional time to that subtopic. This allows me to
identify, for each observed student-subtopic pair, whether instruction
on that subtopic was received. The fact that TIMSS tests both taught and
untaught subtopics – randomly across students – and that teachers have
no information about which ones will be assessed, creates a unique
opportunity to implement falsification tests. In Section 3.1, I leverage
this feature to validate the identifying assumptions of the empirical
strategy.

1.1. Instructional time, curriculum and student math skills
1.1.1. The U.S. decentralized school system
The U.S. education system is characterized by a high degree of
decentralization. In contrast to most countries, educational policies
are not decided by a ministry at the national level, but each state is
responsible for setting its own minimum thresholds in terms of school
year length, total number of hours of instruction and/or number of
hours in a school day.5 The resulting variability across states in the
number of hours and days is further enhanced by the flexibility given
to school districts to increase requirements as long as their budgets
allow.6 This translates into marked differences among U.S. schools in
terms of daily instructional time: students attending schools at the 90th
percentile of the distribution benefit from more than one additional
hour each day than those at the 10th percentile (Kraft & Novicoff,
2024). More specifically, students attending schools at the 90th percentile of the distribution benefit from 7.42 h per day against 6.33 h
per day for students attending schools at the 10th percentile of the
distribution (Kraft & Novicoff, 2024). The authors further show that
36% of differences in instructional time is explained by variation across
states.
The U.S. education system is also characterized by the absence of a
national curriculum. There is, in particular, no national mathematics
curriculum for 8th grade students (Mullis et al., 2012). Each state
is responsible for developing its own curriculum frameworks in core
subject areas and implementing accountability systems tied to curriculum standards. Individual curriculum frameworks always include
detailed, grade-level instructional benchmarks, approaches to learning,
and instructional resource material. Local school districts then decide
what curricula are actually taught. This institutional setting induces a
lot of variation that is arguably exogenous to teachers and students, not
only with respect to total math instructional time but also with respect
to the allocation of this time across topics. Although the TIMSS dataset

1.2. Teaching practices: Background and measurement
The role of pedagogy in the student learning process is a central issue in educational research. It is possible to distinguish two main pedagogical approaches in this literature: the transmissive approach, which is
teacher-centered and based on the idea that knowledge is best acquired
through direct transmission from the teacher to the learner (Kirschner
et al., 2006); the active approach, which is student-centered and based
on the idea that learning requires active participation on the part of the
learner (Lave & Wenger, 1991). The transmissive approach is typically
based on teacher lecturing, one of the most basic and common teaching
practices. By contrast, the second approach grants more autonomy
to the student in the learning process and involves more interaction
between the teacher and the student.
An important literature in education sciences has demonstrated the
strong potential of the active approach, especially for mathematics
learning.7 A growing body of evidence in the economics literature also
supports the view that such teaching practices can be effective for
raising both student learning and positive school attitudes in various
contexts. A first set of studies mostly based on survey data from
international tests similar to those used in this paper focuses on the
relationship between teaching practices and student achievement. A
majority of them find a positive link between student achievement
and the implementation of active teaching practices (Aslam & Kingdon,
2011; Bietenbeck, 2014; Blazar, 2015; Burgess et al., 2023; HidalgoCabrillana & Lopez-Mayan, 2018; Lavy, 2015b), although some find
no effect (Van Klaveren, 2011) or even negative effects (Schwerdt &
Wuppermann, 2011). A second body of research based on experiments
or quasi-experiments evaluates the causal impact of public policies
aimed at promoting these practices in various contexts. These studies
consistently point to the positive effects played by these teaching
practices on the development of students’ cognitive and non-cognitive
skills (Angrist & Lavy, 2001; Bando et al., 2019; Briole et al., 2025;
Machin & McNally, 2008).8
This paper contributes to this literature by studying the links between teaching practices and the hourly productivity of instructional
time. Measures of teaching practices used in this paper are drawn from
the TIMSS math teacher survey. While this type of measures are widely
used in the economic literature,9 a common criticism is that they are
not free of reporting/social desirability biases, as is the case with any
self-reported measure (Moorman & Podsakoff, 1992). Yet, a consistent
literature in education reports very high correlations between selfreported measures of teaching practices and measures from classroom
observation (Desimone, 2009). A study comparing survey and observation measures of teaching practices of U.S. math teachers shows very
high correlations (around 0.85) of composite measures — rather than
measures of single practices (Mayer, 1999). This literature also shows
that the risk of social desirability bias is present when data collection
is not anonymous and/or is intended for teacher evaluation purposes,
which is not the case for the TIMSS teacher survey.

2. Empirical strategy
2.1. The causal effect of math instructional time
In the remainder of the paper, I estimate the causal effect of instructional time devoted to a given math topic on student test scores in
that math topic and explore the relationship between teaching practices
and instructional productivity. The underlying educational production
function assumes that student math achievement depends on the quantity and the quality of math instructional time, on student math ability,
as well as on other school and family inputs related to math learning.
School inputs include classroom peer quality and the overall quality of
the school environment. Family inputs relate to parents’ efforts to help
their children succeed in math (e.g., private math lessons or help with
homework).
Assessing the causal impact of instructional time on student achievement raises an identification challenge due to the potential endogeneity
of instructional time. For example, due to sorting of students both
across and within schools, students with higher academic ability could
benefit from more instructional time. To address this concern, previous
studies have relied on within student between subject variation in
instructional time (Bietenbeck & Collins, 2023; Cattaneo et al., 2017;
Lavy, 2015a; Rivkin & Schiman, 2015). This approach relies on the
assumption that, once student overall academic ability is accounted for,
differences in instructional time across subjects are not systematically
related to unobserved (subject-specific) determinants of achievement.
This assumption may be violated if students receive relatively more
instructional time in subjects in which they have a comparative advantage or stronger preferences. For instance, students with high math
ability or highly motivated to learn math may be tracked into advanced
classes that allocate more hours to math, thereby confounding instructional time with student subject-specific ability and therefore creating

The second part of the empirical strategy explores the heterogeneity
in the causal effects of instructional time on math achievement. More
specifically, it investigates the extent to which the implementation of
teaching practices requiring active student engagement and frequent
student-teacher interactions relates to higher instructional productivity.

rich topic-level structure of the TIMSS assessment, which allows me to
compare performance across topics of the same subject (math) within
the same student. The identification of the causal effect of instructional
time is made possible by exploiting differences in the amount of time

classroom environment, and peer composition. Importantly, the variation in instructional time exploited here reflects the allocation of time
across math topics within a fixed total amount of math instruction,
rather than changes in total math instructional time. The estimated
coefficient therefore captures the relative productivity of time across
topics, holding total time constant. This distinction matters because
such allocations are less likely to generate fatigue or strong diminishing

eliminates the possibility of teaching to the test and reinforces the
exogeneity of instructional choices with respect to test content.

3. Results

To check the validity of this assumption, I conduct a falsification
test in Section 3.1, showing that instructional time has no effect on
student performance in subtopics that were not taught during the year.
Based on this result, I include as controls student score in untaught
subtopics, which proxy for topic-specific math skills and help reinforce
the identification strategy.

3.2.2. Instructional productivity and active learning practices
The results outlined in Table 2 also show that the use of teaching
practices emphasizing student active participation in the lesson is
systematically associated to higher levels of instructional productivity.
As can be seen in columns (2) to (4), the coefficient associated to the
interaction term between instructional time and the AL index is positive
and strongly significant in all specifications. In addition, this coefficient
is remarkably stable across specifications. It is, in particular, very little
affected by the inclusion of teacher controls in the regression, reducing
concerns about omitted variable bias. Generally speaking, these results
are in line with the notion that active learning methods may positively
affect student learning independently from other dimensions of teaching quality, as outlined in a recent research by Burgess et al. (2023). In
addition to this, the frequency of assessment is positively correlated to
teachers’ instructional productivity.
The estimated coefficient associated to the AL index is large compared to estimates of the total variability in teacher value-added (TVA).
In a literature review on TVA estimates, Hanushek and Rivkin (2010)
show that for mathematics teachers, a one standard deviation increase
in TVA is associated to a 15% standard deviation increase in student
test scores over the year, on average. In my context, a one standard
deviation increase in the AL index is associated with an increase in
student test scores by 1.8% of a standard deviation for each weekly
hour of instruction time. Computing the effect for a typical amount
of instructional time (4.4 h per week) and assuming linearity in the
productivity of instructional time, a one standard deviation increase
in the AL index is associated with an increase in student test scores
by 8% of a standard deviation. Alternatively, previous studies show
that moving from the teacher at the 25th percentile of the valueadded distribution to the teacher at the 75th percentile during one
single year implies a 20% of a standard deviation increase in math test
scores. Moving from the teacher at the 25th to the teacher at the 75th
percentile of the AL index distribution is associated with a 20% of a
standard deviation increase in math test scores, which is equivalent to
a 86% increase in instructional productivity (Table 3). In other words,
every hour of math instruction spent with the latter teacher is around
twice as productive as an hour spent with the former.
The main results outlined in this section are robust to several
alternative specifications regarding the definition of teaching practices
variables. In particular, the conclusions drawn from the main analyses
are robust to alternative scoring of the teaching practices variables (Tables C5 and C7), to considering non-centered values of these variables
(Table C9), or to considering more dimensions of teaching practices
than those captured by the AL index and the frequency of assessment
(Tables C10 and C11). The results of these robustness checks are
presented in detail in Appendix C.

3.3. Heterogeneity analyses
This section explores the heterogeneity of the effects of math instructional time and active learning practices according to student
gender and social origin. To do so, I replicate the main analysis separately on high SES and low SES students on the one hand and on female
and male students on the other.16 Results are respectively presented in
Tables 4 and 5.
These analyses first reveal that math instructional time has a positive and significant effect on math achievement for all groups of
students. However, the average impact of instructional time is significantly stronger for high SES (5.8% SD) than low SES students
(2.4% SD). This result suggests that increases in instructional time may
contribute to widening the gap between students from advantaged and
disadvantaged backgrounds. One possible explanation is that students
with higher aptitude are better able to benefit from additional time, or
that more advantaged students attend schools that make more effective
use of instructional time (Carroll, 1963). This interpretation is consistent with recent evidence from Agüero and Beleche (2013), Huebener
et al. (2017), and Figlio et al. (2018). Yet, more instructional time
could in principle also help reduce achievement gaps by keeping disadvantaged students in structured learning environments for a greater
share of the day, thereby limiting inequalities in out-of-school learning opportunities. Overall, evidence remains mixed and reflects this
ambiguity (Kraft & Novicoff, 2024).
Heterogeneity analyses also show that math instructional time is
more beneficial to female (5.4% SD) than male students (3.1% SD), suggesting that increases in instructional time may be an interesting tool
to reduce the gender gap in math achievement. Given the importance
