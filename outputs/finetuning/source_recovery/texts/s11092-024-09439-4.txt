Abstract
In international large-scale assessments, students may not be compelled to answer
every test item: a student can decide to skip a seemingly difﬁcult item or may drop out
before the end of the test is reached. The way these missing responses are treated will
affect the estimation of the item difﬁculty and student ability, and ultimately affect
the country’s score. In the Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS),
incorrect answer substitution is used. This means that skipped and omitted items are
treated as incorrect responses. In the present study, the effect of this approach is investi-
gated. The data of 2006, 2011, and 2016 cycles of PIRLS were analyzed using IRTree
models in which a sequential tree structure is estimated to model the full response
process. Item difﬁculty, students’ ability, and country means were estimated and com-
pared with results from a Rasch model using the standard PIRLS approach to missing
values. Results showed that the IRTree model was able to disentangle the students’
ability and their propensity to skip items, reducing the correlation between ability and
the proportion of skipped items in comparison to the Rasch model. Nevertheless, at the
country level, the aggregated scores showed no important differences between models
for the pooled sample, but some differences within countries across cycles.
Keywords IRT · IRTree · PIRLS · Skipped items
1 Introduction
In educational large-scale assessments, it is not feasible to administer every item to
each student, inevitably leading to missing values by design. Since these missing
values are linked to a random assignment of booklets, they are entirely independent
of any item property or student characteristic, hence, they have no inﬂuence on the
estimation (Mislevy & Stocking, 1989).

However, an important source of missing values in large-scale assessments does
not come from the test design. Missing values are also generated when students do not
answer questions assigned to them by design. Mainly, two types of missing values can
be generated in this way: skipped items and not-reached items (Lord, 1974). Skipped
items are those items that the student (deliberately) chose not to respond. Not-reached
items are those items the student could not reach toward the end of the test booklet
and therefore, they did not have the opportunity to answer them.
In both cases, these missing values do not come from a random mechanism and
probably are related to students’ or item characteristics. Thus, these missing values
are non-ignorable, and if they are inappropriately accounted for, parameter estimates
are subject to bias, leading to inaccurate trait measurements (Pohl & Becker, 2020;
Rose et al., 2016).
The present study analyzes the impact of an alternative strategy, IRTrees for miss-
ing responses, to deal with missing responses in an educational assessment context,
and shows an application to the data of an international large-scale assessment. In
the following section, the mechanisms that produce missing responses according to
Rubin’s classiﬁcation are discussed ﬁrst. Then, different strategies to deal with this
phenomenon are examined and it is discussed how an expanded item response the-
ory (IRT) framework allows to obtain more information about the missing response
pattern , and the concept of IRTree models is introduced.
1.1 Treating missing responses in educational assessments
1.1.1 Rubin’s classiﬁcation
Following Rubin’s classic deﬁnition (Rubin, 1976), missing item responses can be
classiﬁed by the mechanism that underlies their generation. When an item response is
missing for reasons that are not related to observed or unobserved data, it is assumed to
be missing completely at random (MCAR). On the other hand, if the missing response
to an item is generated by observed data, these responses are considered missing at
random (MAR), as is the case of the missing responses due to an incomplete design.
Finally, when the reasons for omitting a response are not measurable or are outside
the scope of the study, the missing values are considered to be missing not at random
(MNAR) (Little & Rubin, 2019; Rubin, 1976).
If the mechanism for generating missing values ﬁts into an MCAR scheme, it is
reasonable to ignore the missing values entirely. In psychometric practice, this means
to omit item responses with missing values in difﬁculty and ability estimation. The
advantage of this approach is that it does not generate biased results in any of the
estimated parameters (Lord, 1974). However, in educational assessments, assuming
that the missing pattern is MCAR is not sustainable. As several studies show (Holman
& Glas, 2005; Huang, 2020; Wigﬁeld & Eccles, 2000), there is strong empirical
evidence to assume that the omission of assigned items is related to students’ cognitive
and non-cognitive characteristics.
Therefore, in educational testing settings, it is more suitable to treat missing
responses as MAR or MNAR. Two main strategies are distinguishable to deal with

incorporating the missing pattern as part of the measurement model, where a missing
value can be considered valid and gives information to the model. Both approaches

1.1.2 Response imputation
Huisman (2000) classiﬁes imputation methods in two axes. The ﬁrst axis distinguishes
between deterministic and stochastic methods. The former refers to obtaining the same
imputation values regardless of repetition, i.e., no randomness is involved; while the
latter refers to methods that will provide different imputation values every time they
are applied, i.e., elements of randomness are introduced. A second axis takes into
account the complexity of the missing values and their dependence on other obser-
vations and differentiates between naive (i.e., a simple method without dependence
on other observations) and principled methods (i.e., a complex method that takes into
account dependence on other observations). Under this classiﬁcation, it is noteworthy
that the method for dealing with missing values used by the vast majority of interna-
tional large-scale assessments (e.g., PIRLS, PISA, TIMMS), i.e., considering missing
answers as incorrect or incorrect answer substitution (IAS), would be classiﬁed as a
naive deterministic approach. Speciﬁcally, international large-scale assessment uses
IAS in different steps in their analyses. For example, PIRLS, TIMSS, and ICCS do
not impute skipped items as incorrect answers for item calibration but do for ability
estimation, therefore, this has an impact on individual and country results but not on
item difﬁculty. On the other hand, PISA uses IAS for skipped in both steps, there-
fore, missing responses inﬂuence individual ability as well as item difﬁculty. Finally,
these four studies considered not-reached items as not administered (Organisation for
Economic Co-operation and Development, 2017; Martin et al., 2017, 2016).
IAS is problematic both from a theoretical and a practical point of view. Its theoret-
ical inconsistency is straightforward: IAS assumes that the missing values are related
to the student’s ability; nevertheless, this relationship is omitted in the measurement
model (Pohl & Becker, 2020; Rose et al., 2010). More speciﬁcally, in IAS, the proba-
bility of answering correctly an omitted item is considered to be zero regardless of the
student’s proﬁciency. In essence, IAS relies on a theoretical MNAR scheme, where
there is a strong and strict relation between not answering and ability, but proposes
a MAR modeling, where there is no correction for missing values. From a practical
point of view, Rose (2013) noted that IAS leads to biased item parameter estimates,
ultimately overestimating item difﬁculty and underestimating the latent trait.
1.1.3 Incorporating missing responses into the measurement model
In contrast to imputation methods, a more comprehensive strategy for dealing with
missing values is to incorporate them into the measurement model. Within this
approach, the tendency to omit responses is measured within a MAR framework and

its relation with proﬁciency and other explanatory variables is analyzed (Huang, 2020;
Pohl & Becker, 2020).
Mainly two types of models can be distinguished: latent regression models (LRM),
and multidimensional IRT models (MIRT). LRM models condition the observed data
via a regression model by an aggregate of the missing response pattern (Rose et al.,
2010; Pohl et al., 2013). On the other hand, MIRT models assume the existence of
a latent variable that explains the missing data pattern, hence, they combine an IRT
model for missing data with an IRT model for proﬁciency (Rose et al., 2016; Debeer
et al., 2017).
The IRTree approach is an example of a MIRT model. In this approach, the cognitive
process of choosing whether or not to answer an item can be depicted via a tree-based
item response model or IRTrees (De Boeck & Partchev, 2012). The next section gives
a general introduction to IRTrees as this model framework is used in the present study
to investigate missing responses. Afterward, the modeling of not-reached and skipped
items within this framework is explained.
1.2 IRTrees
An IRTree is capable of describing an individual’s internal cognitive process using
a sequence of interconnected steps (De Boeck & Partchev, 2012; Jeon & De Boeck,
2016; Huang, 2020), allowing the use of a single observed response to estimate mul-
tiple latent variables (Blacksmith et al., 2019). In an IRTree, intermediate individual
decisions are represented as intermediate nodes, and categorical observed responses as
ﬁnal nodes. Intermediate nodes and end nodes are connected by branches that depict
all possible outcomes of a subprocess. For each of these branches, it is possible to
estimate a distinct probability. Therefore, a ﬁnal node’s probability is the joint prob-
ability of passing through each branch that precedes it (De Boeck & Partchev, 2012;
Jeon & De Boeck, 2016). Under standard IRT notation, the probability of student p
going through a branch r for item i is determined by a logistic regression or Rasch
model determined by the individual contribution (θpr), or propensity to pass through
that branch, and the subitem threshold (βir):

1.5 The present study
The present study compares the effects of two strategies for dealing with missing
responses: IAS and IRTree modeling for skipped items. These effects are investigated
via an application in the Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS),
and also their general outline is shown via a simulation study in Appendix B.
IRTree models for missing responses are applied to the PIRLS 2006, 2011, and
2016 data. PIRLS is an international comparative study developed by the Interna-
tional Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) that assesses
students’ reading ability in their fourth year of school. PIRLS has been held every ﬁve
years since 2001 and serves as a complement to the IEA’s assessment in mathematics
and science, TIMMS (Martin et al., 2017). PIRLS uses different grading rules to treat
123

missing responses for item calibration and ability estimation (Martin et al., 2017). In
these rules, PIRLS distinguishes between not-reached items and skipped items. How-
ever, the percentage of not-reached items in the whole sample was lower (1.7%) than a
reasonable threshold which could have an effect on the parameter estimation (Debeer
et al., 2017). Furthermore, PIRLS considers not-reached items as not administered for
both the calibration process and the ability estimation; therefore, no IAS is used for
these items. Skipped items are considered not administered in item calibration, but are
replaced by incorrect values during ability estimation (IAS). Therefore, skipped items
are expected to have an impact on item and person parameters. Thus, we focus on the
effects of using IAS or the Skip model on item difﬁculty. Moreover, we compare the
correspondence between PIRLS and the Skip model students’ and country rankings.

3 Method
3.1 Data
Data from PIRLS 2016, 2011, and 2016 cycles were pooled from the 61 participating
countries, totaling 786,041 students. Considering the whole sample, the proportion of
missing responses was rather low in the three studied cycles of PIRLS. The percentage
of not-reached items was 2.1% in 2006, 1.4% in 2011, and 1.7% in 2016. The percent-
age of skipped items was 5.5% in 2006, 4.5% in 2011, and 4.1% in 2016. Thus, the
total of missing responses was 7.6% in 2006, and 5.9% in 2001 and 2006. However,
as can be seen in Table 1, the percentage of missing responses varied signiﬁcantly by
country and cycle. For example, Morroco and Kuwait are among the countries whose
students skipped or did not reach a higher number of items.
3.2 Modeling approach
PIRLS uses a combination of the two (2PL) and the three-parameter (3PL) IRT model
with a mixture of full credit and partial credit items (Martin et al., 2017). Due to
computational constraints, it was necessary to reduce the complexity of the original
PIRLS model. Two types of simpliﬁcations were applied: ﬁrst, partial credit items
were transformed into full credit items, i.e., only the answers that obtained the highest
score were categorized as correct. Secondly, the number of parameters to be estimated
was reduced, omitting the parameters for discrimination and guessing by using a
Rasch model to analyze the PIRLS data. Hence, it became necessary to check, ﬁrst,
the differences and similarities between both modeling approaches. This results are
detailed in Appendix A.

As a second step, we compared the correspondence between the Rasch model
and the Skip model. Using standard PIRLS’s treatment of missing responses, the
Rasch model considered IAS only for estimating ability and not for item difﬁculty.
Thus, skipped items were treated as not administered for the item calibration step.
Moreover, also following PIRLS’s procedures, for both models not-reached items were
considered as not administered for item calibration and ability estimation. Models were
estimated by pooling the three cycles together, using senate weights, and assuming
measurement invariance.
Correspondence between the Rasch model, and the Skip model was analyzed at
three levels: items, students, and countries. First, item difﬁculty estimates were com-
pared considering different item types. In the second step, the correlations between
students’ scores were calculated. Finally, students’ scores were aggregated by country,
and country means were compared by score and by ranking.
All analyses were done using the programming language R (R Core Team, 2021)
and the TAM library (Robitzsch et al., 2020) for the IRT modeling.
4 Results
4.1 Item difficulty
To analyze the effect of using IRTree models on the entire PIRLS sample the Rasch
model and the Skip model were compared. The correspondence between item difﬁ-
culties is perfect (see Fig.3); that is, the use of the IRTree model does not vary the
item difﬁculty estimation greatly. This was expected, given that the PIRLS approach
considers skipped items as not administered in this stage.

4.3 Country means
At the country level, as shown in Fig.5, we do not observe great differences between
models. Both the Rasch model and the Skip model retain similar country results
and country rankings. Nevertheless, we can still spot some countries that do not fall
perfectly on the identity line, i.e., regardless of the pooled results, the trend for each
country can be affected by the choice of the psychometric model.

5 Discussion
This study’s main objective was to investigate an alternative model for treating miss-
ing responses in educational assessments, focusing on PIRLS. We chose the IRTree
model for missing responses proposed by Debeer et al. (2017) because it is capable
of estimating the propensity to skip items alongside the student’s ability. We applied
the IRTree Skip model to the three last cycles of PIRLS (2006, 2011, and 2016) and
compared it with the Rasch model.

items at the item calibration stage, we found no differences between models in item
difﬁculty. Nevertheless, as we show in Appendix B, we would expect a different
scenario if we apply IAS during the item calibration.
5.1 Individual level
On the contrary, at the individual level is where most differences between models
occur. The correlations between model estimates pass from 0.99 (at the item level) to
around 0.85 at the individual level. Although this may seem a small difference, when
estimations are plotted it is easy to distinguish that the relationship between estimates
is high but no longer linear. Moreover, the Rasch model using PIRLS strategy for
missing responses penalizes low and high achievers that tend to skip items.
Furthermore, we can also see a moderate correlation between the Rasch’s ability
and the propensity of skipping items (θ1) from the Skip model. This relationship
shows that the Rasch model is capturing a relevant proportion of the θ1, result that
is consistent between cycles and countries. Of course, the question remains opened
if this is intended by PIRLS administrators, i.e., if the deﬁnition of reading literacy
includes or not the propensity of not skipping items.
Nevertheless,thisconnectionbetweenreadingliteracyandpropensityofanswering,
or skipping, is also recovered by the Skip model. As we showed, the correlation
between θ2 and θ1 exists, although it can vary signiﬁcantly by country. This reveals
that indeed there is a relationship between these traits, i.e., students with low ability
tend to skip more items than students with high ability.
It is also clear, that both models can generate different answering strategies. If
the PIRLS approach is used, the dominant strategy would be to answer randomly all
questions that the student does not know the answer to. If the Skip model is used,
the dominant strategy would be to only answer those items in which the student is
conﬁdent in knowing the answer.
5.2 Country level
When the students’ scores are aggregated, these differences tend to be diluted. There
is an almost perfect relationship between the country mean scores and the country
rankings of both models.
This could lead to the incorrect conclusion that choosing between models would
have no indent in results. Nevertheless, much more important than league tables are
the comparisons each country makes with itself at a certain cycle or through cycles.
This is where the comparison between these models, or any other IRT model, becomes
relevant.
As we showed, for speciﬁc populations the overall results can change between
models, therefore, the conclusions or policies driven by these results could be different.
For example, in the case of Morocco, two phenomenons occurred at the same time,
we saw an increase in ability alongside a decrease of skipped items, thus we observed

351
a decrease in the gap between models but we still do not know how much of this
decrease is due to better reading literacy or better test-taking strategies.
In conclusion, we do not necessarily recommend to change the modeling approach
of PIRLS or another ILSA that uses IAS. These studies are already complex and their
estimationsarechallengingtocommunicate.Nonetheless,wearemoreworriedonhow
their results are usually regarded as monolithic trues. Essentially we believe that the
hard numbers and league tables provided by an ILSA should be always accompanied
by alternative models, and therefore, alternative conclusions.
5.3 Limitations
Finally, it is important to address which future research can help alleviate some of
this study’s limitations. First, although the results show that using a single parameter
model did not impact the ﬁndings, developing and testing an IRTree model that incor-
porates the full complexity of the PIRLS data would make a stronger case for this
study’s results. Also, due to the nature of the data, it was not feasible to include other
information that would shed more light on the students’ cognitive processes, e.g., the
time the student spent on an item before deciding to skip it. If this information was
available, a more complex model could differentiate between students that tried to
solve an item and decided not to answer it and students who directly skipped it. Lastly,
although this study explores a possible alternative to the common practice of IAS, it
does not make an exhaustive review of other methods that can alleviate the theoretical
and practical problems derived from this approach. Hence, it becomes necessary to
expand these ﬁndings by comparing IAS with other imputation methods and models
that further develop the treatment of missing responses.
