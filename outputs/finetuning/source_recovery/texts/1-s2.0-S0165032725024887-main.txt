Background: Adolescent health is a worldwide concern. Previous research identified many factors of mental and
physical health issues, which typically focused on a single or a few domains of predictors.
Methods: To identify the optimal predictors for adolescent mental and physical health, this study adopted multiple machine learning methods to examine the effect of nine-domain 46 predictors in predicting mental and
physical health among adolescents from Hong Kong, China, and the Netherlands using data from PISA, 2022.
Results: The results showed that, of all predictors considered, the emotional and social capital domains were most
crucial for adolescent mental and physical health in both societies. The results also revealed nuanced patterns in
the two societies, with the environmental domain being more critical in Hong Kong and the physical domain
being more critical in the Netherlands. The model trained based on the data of one society performed worse in
predicting health outcomes in another society.
Conclusion: The results suggest that interventions targeting emotion and social capital domains may be useful in
addressing mental and physical health issues among adolescents. However, culturally specific strategies should
be considered for interventions, as the results highlight the importance of considering cultural backgrounds
despite the overlapping patterns observed in the two societies.

While maintaining good mental and physical health is essential at
every stage of life, examining what promotes or undermines better
health during adolescence is critical. Despite the debate on the age range
for adolescence (e.g., aged 10–24 suggested in Sawyer et al., 2018; aged

stages, adolescents experience increased cognitive abilities and physical
changes, making them vulnerable to challenging mental and physical

most morbidity in adulthood has its roots during adolescence (Das et al.,
2016), including anxiety or depressive disorder, unsafe sex, and tobacco
use (Fergusson and Woodward, 2002; Gutiérrez and Atienzo, 2011).

1.3. The current study
Considering the critical influence of adolescent health (Das et al.,
2016; Yurgelun-Todd, 2007), we aimed to provide a comprehensive
understanding of the key predictors/domains for adolescent physical
and mental health using machine learning methods. Our study used data
from PISA, 2022 (OECD, 2023), an education-oriented cross-cultural
dataset with a comprehensive list of measures related to school, peers,
and family, which occupy a major part of adolescent life and thus are
essential contexts shaping adolescent health (Shackleton et al., 2016).
Furthermore, the PISA dataset assessed variables in multiple domains
across societies, allowing us to simultaneously consider multifaceted
factors that are potentially influential for adolescent health: demographic, physical, emotional, academic, future aspiration, personality, parental, social capital, activities, and environment domains (Lukács
et al., 2018; Pan and Cutumisu, 2024; Rothenberg et al., 2023; Sales and
Irwin Jr, 2013). To improve the reliability of results and ensure that
identified predictors are not governed by the selected machine learning
methods, we compared the results with several frequently used methods,
e.g., ANOVA, Mutual Information, Recursive Feature Elimination, Lasso
regression, and Random Forest Regression methods (Pan and Cutumisu,
2024).
Some research discussed cultural differences in predictors of
adolescent physical and mental health. For example, the variations in
parenting styles, family environment, and diet shape adolescent health
across cultures (Al-Hazzaa et al., 2013; Bogt et al., 2024). However,
cross-cultural studies considering a wide range of predictors were
limited (Mutumba and Schulenberg, 2020; Pan and Cutumisu, 2024). To
provide a reference for developing interventions that benefit adolescent
mental and physical health worldwide, we analyzed data from both
Eastern and Western societies, using data from Hong Kong, China,1 and
the Netherlands as the representatives. This was the first study to
simultaneously investigate the effect of multi-domain predictors on
adolescent mental and physical health in different cultural contexts.
Ultimately, these findings would provide valuable information to relevant organizations from different societies with a set of priority intervention targets when making policies aimed at improving adolescent
health.

1.1. Predictors of mental and physical health among adolescents
According to the biopsychosocial model, adolescents' current and
future health is greatly shaped by biological, psychological, and social
systems (Sales and Irwin Jr, 2013). The social-ecological model
(Koffman et al., 2005) highlights the importance of integrating multiple
factors, such as intra− /inter-personal, community, and socialization
factors. These models have also been supported through a review of
empirical findings. For example, poorer emotional regulation, greater
interpersonal stress, and heightened emotional reactivity can place adolescents at great risk of developing different mental disorders
(Chervonsky and Hunt, 2019; Magson et al., 2021). Factors in social
capital and experiences, like bullying, poor peer relationships, and less
social support, were associated with poor mental health (Arseneault
et al., 2010; McPherson et al., 2014).
Some of the factors mentioned above also affect physical health.
Emerging literature suggests that early life stress is associated with
various chronic health conditions (Sheppard et al., 2024). Social capital
factors, such as peer victimization (Schacter, 2021) and involvement in
the community (Morgan and Haglund, 2009), also matter for adolescent
physical health. These overlapping factors may suggest that adolescent
physical and mental health is possibly shaped by a wide range of shared
factors. However, some studies showed varying patterns for mental and
physical health (e.g., Hakulinen et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2011). For
instance, Zhang et al. (2011) found that community-level education only
benefits one's physical health. Overall, previous studies identified plenty
of predictors for adolescent health, but the key factors that simultaneously affect physical and mental health have not yet been identified.
This research gap can be addressed by considering a more comprehensive list of predictors and using machine learning approaches to identify
key features and explore their predictive power for both adolescent
mental and physical health. These findings are crucial for developing
effective interventions to promote better health.

2. Methods
2.1. Participants
Our data was from the Program for International Student Assessment
(PISA, 2022: https://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/) survey (OECD, 2023).
This secondary data complied with the ethics guidelines of the data
collection site. We focused on the data from Hong Kong and the
Netherlands. From a conceptual perspective, Hong Kong and the
Netherlands represent two cultures with distinct characteristics (e.g.,
Hofstede's collectivism-individualism dimension: Hong Kong = 25 vs.
the Netherlands = 80; power distance: Hong Kong = 68 vs. the
Netherlands = 38, see https://exhibition.geerthofstede.com/hofsted
es-globe/), which helps carefully evaluate the generalizability of the
obtained results in cultures with diverse characteristics. From a methodological perspective, these two societies have the most overlapping
predictors in the current dataset, which maximizes the number of predictors for a comprehensive evaluation.
The original sample included 5907 15-year-old students (47.9 %
female) from Hong Kong and 5046 15-year-old students (47.4 % female)
from the Netherlands. After removing missing values, 3253 students
from Hong Kong (54.1 % female) and 2781 students from the
Netherlands (49.0 % female) were included in the final analyses. We did
not use imputation methods to prevent potential bias and enhance

1.2. Methods for identifying the most important predictors of adolescent
health
Many previous studies used an explanatory approach and conventional statistical methods to identify predictors associated with adolescent health (Bogt et al., 2024). However, conventional approaches have
disadvantages, including a high risk of overfitting, poor generalizability
and replicability, and an inability to explore nonlinear relationships
(Sajeev et al., 2021; Yarkoni and Westfall, 2017). To detect multifaceted
predictors more accurately and reliably, nascent work has used machine
learning (e.g., random forest, k-nearest neighbors) to identify the key
predictors of adolescent health and predict relevant outcomes (Lee and
Chun, 2024; Pan and Cutumisu, 2024). However, they mainly focused
on one aspect of health, such as mental health indicated by internalizing/externalizing behavior (Rothenberg et al., 2023), life satisfaction
(Pan and Cutumisu, 2024), or specific physical health problems like
obesity (Lee and Chun, 2024). Therefore, research examining both
mental and physical health outcomes is needed to provide a comprehensive picture of adolescent health.

3. Results
3.1. Feature importance
The average ranking of the predictors of each feature selection
method is shown in Fig. 1 (See Supplementary Tables S2–S5 for the 10
important features selected by each feature selection method over 30
repetitions).
As discussed previously, we used five feature selection methods for
the sake of robustness or stability. To interpret the results, we considered
the results of all adopted methods, counting the selected frequency of
each optimal predictor and focusing only on the shared variables
selected among all five feature selection methods. Fig. 2 lists the overlapping important features identified by all methods for each outcome in
each society.
The results showed both commonalities and differences between the
two societies and two types of health (see Fig. 2). Two predictors in the
emotional domain, i.e., emotional control (EMOCOAGR) and communication about worries and concerns with others (SOCCON), were
important for both mental and physical health outcomes in both societies. For mental health, stress resistance (STRESAGR) was important in
both societies. For physical health, body image (BODYIMA) and social
connection to parents (SOCONPA) were the shared key features in both
societies.
The results also revealed cross-cultural differences. The frequency of
being bullied (BULLIED, the predictor in the social capital domain) and
body image (BODYIMA) were key predictors of mental health among the
Netherlands students. In contrast, feeling safe (FEELSAFE) and stress
resistance (STRESAGR) were key predictors of physical health among
Hong Kong students.

As different training-testing sets were generated across the 30 repetitions, the optimal feature subsets varied accordingly, leading to
different prediction models. Therefore, the measures of prediction performance from these 30 repetitions were averaged to derive an overall
evaluation of the model's performance. We also ranked the feature
importance from high to low based on their average importance score or
coefficient across 30 repetitions. Finally, the 10 features with the highest
average ranking were chosen as each method's final most important
subset of predictors.

4.2. Nuanced patterns in the two societies
Some nuanced patterns were observed in the two societies. The
physical domain (i.e., body image) was identified as a common important predictor for the Netherlands' adolescent health. In contrast, the
environmental domain (feeling safe) was identified as an important
predictor only for adolescent physical health in Hong Kong. It was
shown that negative body image was linked to lower physical activity
and higher body image dissatisfaction, which led to poorer physical and
mental health (Meland et al., 2007; Sabiston et al., 2019). However,
there is insufficient evidence on whether the physical domain is highly
associated with adolescent mental health in Eastern societies. The current findings might suggest that the physical domain could be less
important (compared to other predictors) in Eastern societies.
The greater importance of the environmental domain in predicting
adolescent physical health in Hong Kong might be explained from a
cultural perspective. Eastern cultures promote holistic thinking and high
interdependence, while Western cultures promote analytic thinking and
high independence (e.g., Ji et al., 2000; Kitayama et al., 2010), leading
to a greater sensitivity to contexts among people in Eastern cultures.
Thus, adolescents from Hong Kong may be more susceptible to the influence of environmental characteristics.
The predicted performance of the models provides further support
for the nuanced patterns in the two societies. First, the overall prediction
results of adolescent mental and physical health in the Netherlands were
better, suggesting the examined predictors may be more relevant to
adolescent health in the Netherlands. Second, for the cross-society
model, the prediction results were worse when using trained models
based on data from one society to test the outcomes in another, showing
the specific patterns across the two societies. These results both suggest
the importance of considering culture in better understanding adolescent health, which aligns with previous work showing the moderating
effect of cultures in the predictors of health (e.g., Li et al., 2024; Lou and
Li, 2023). Thus, it is noteworthy to consider the influence of cultural and
ecological characteristics when attempting to promote adolescent
health.

4. Discussion
Identifying the most important factors that can simultaneously predict adolescent physical and mental health can help select effective
intervention measures. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first
study to simultaneously investigate the effect of multi-domain predictors on adolescent mental and physical health in different cultural
contexts using multiple feature selection methods. Our results indicated
that the emotional and social capital domains are important in mental
and physical health. Interestingly, all models considering the same set of
predictors performed better in the Netherlands than in Hong Kong.
Moreover, the model trained based on the data of one society performed
worse in predicting health outcomes in another society, suggesting differences between the two societies. These nuanced patterns between the
two societies may suggest the influence of cultural and ecological
characteristics.
4.1. The identified shared important factors in the two societies
Our findings showed that the most consistent key features fell in the
emotional domain (emotional control and ease of communication about
worries and concerns) in the Netherlands (individualistic culture) and
Hong Kong (collectivist culture). Previous studies (Cheetham-Blake
et al., 2019; Schweizer et al., 2020) suggested that effective control is a
cognitive building block of successful emotion regulation, and sharing
worries and concerns helps cope with stress and fear. These two predictors are both effective emotion regulation methods. Moreover,
adolescence is a period of rapid physical and emotional development,
increased stress, and greater emotional fluctuations (Schweizer et al.,
2020; Yurgelun-Todd, 2007). Hence, better abilities to shift flexibly
between different emotional regulation strategies are important for their
mental health. As for physical health, previous work indicates that
emotion dysregulation can affect social functioning and peer relationships, further increasing the risk of physiological dysregulation (Crowell
et al., 2015). Therefore, better emotional regulation abilities are also
important for maintaining physical health.
Despite the identified varying predictors from the social capital
domain, the current findings reveal the importance of lacking (being
bullied) and gaining social capital (social connection to parents and
belonging) in promoting better mental and physical health among adolescents from both societies. This finding is consistent with previous
work showing the essential role of social capital for people with diverse

4.3. Practical implications
The current findings also provide insights into practical implications.
As discussed previously, the emotional domain was identified as the
most important facet in predicting mental and physical health between
the two societies. These results suggest the potential usefulness of
emotional regulation intervention in promoting adolescent health.
Consistently, the emotional aspect has been stressed in some existing
intervention programs. For instance, Brackett et al. (2019) developed
RULER, an evidence-based approach that stresses the integration of
emotional, cognitive, and behavioral processes in the curriculum. Thus,
enhancing adolescents' skills and knowledge in the emotional domain
may serve as a starting point, which may potentially be an effective and
generalizable strategy as suggested by the current findings.

4.4. Limitations and future directions

Declaration of Generative AI and AI-assisted technologies in the

This study has some limitations. First, all models performed poorly in
predicting physical health compared to mental health in the two societies. One possible explanation is the lack of physical-related predictors,
making the trained models unable to effectively predict physical health.
Future studies can include more physical-related predictors. Second, we
had a single item to assess physical health outcomes and relied on selfreports for most target measures, which could be vulnerable to other
factors such as social desirability. Previous studies have supported that
self-reported single-item measures demonstrate good test-retest reliability in assessing adolescent physical health (Ho et al., 2020; Ng et al.,
2019). Nevertheless, future research should employ well-established
and objective measurements to assess predictors and outcomes. Third,
the PISA dataset only recruited students aged 15; whether the current
findings can be generalized to a wider age-range adolescent population
requires further investigation. Finally, robust evaluations of the generalizability of the findings with more cultural groups are needed.
Although the PISA team has provided evidence of good internal consistency across different cultural contexts for their measurements
(OECD, 2023), future studies are needed to evaluate the current findings
by conducting more rigorous checks on measurement invariance across
cultures (e.g., multiple-group confirmatory factor analysis) and
recruiting more diverse cultural groups. Considering the social desirability bias, response bias (Van de Mortel, 2008), and age differences
(Baldursdottir et al., 2017), future studies are suggested to use multiple
types of measures and evaluate the results across different age groups in
varying cultural contexts to minimize these biases.

5. Conclusions
This study identified a set of the most important predictors for
adolescent mental and physical health in Hong Kong and the
Netherlands using machine learning methods. We found commonalities
for physical and mental health problems, highlighting the importance of
emotional and social capital domains. Culturally nuanced patterns were
also observed. Taken together, while acknowledging the commonalities
in the process related to mental and physical health among adolescents,
the effect of culture should not be neglected. These sets of identified
important predictors may serve as primary and priority considerations
in developing interventions to improve adolescent health worldwide.
