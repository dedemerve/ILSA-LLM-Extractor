Abstract
Job automation can undermine economic security for workers in general, and older workers, in particular. In this respect,
consistently updating one’s knowledge and skills is essential for being competitive in a technology-driven labor market.
Older workers with lower adult literacy skills experience difficulties with continuous education and skills development,
which can contribute to their economic uncertainty. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the correlation between adult
literacy skills and job automation risk. We analyzed a nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older workers
(age 50+ years; n = 1,880) from the 2012/2014/2017 Program for the International Assessment of Adult Competencies
(PIAAC) dataset. The survey-weighted linear regression results showed that lower job automation risks (0%–100%) were
a function of higher adult literacy skills (score 0–500 points: b = −.052, p < .05), after adjusting for relevant covariates (Rsquared = .19).
Keywords
adult literacy skills, job automation risk, older workforce, PIAAC

Introduction

Theoretical Framework

Human labor is increasingly substituted with job automation technology (Frenette & Frank, 2020), thereby resulting
in the redistribution of jobs across industries (Autor, 2015).
Of particular concern are the poorer economic security and
health outcomes correlated with unemployment from job
automation (Abeliansky et al., 2020). In this respect, older
workers are likely to face a higher risk of employment insecurity in general, and in technology-related industries, in
particular (Alcover et al., 2021). Furthermore, older workers are more likely to face economic insecurity as well as
possible early retirement than their younger counterparts,
partially due to a lack of human capital or mismatched/outdated job-related skill sets in the era of job automation
(Cummins et al., 2018). Consequently, constant upgrading
of knowledge and skill sets is required for the attainment
and maintenance of gainful employment among older workers (Frenette & Frank, 2020). As such, adult literacy skills,
which are the foundation for learning, are essential for continued adult education and training (AET; National Center
for Education Statistics, 2020). Despite strong theoretical
propositions, however, limited empirical evidence is available for correlations between adult literacy skills and risk of
job automation among older workers.

This study is guided by the human capital theory which postulates that employment and economic outcomes are dependent on one’s human capital, such as credentials and skills
(Becker, 2009). Having greater human capital is correlated
with an increased likelihood of advantageous employment
outcomes, subsequent opportunities for on-the-job skill
development (Yamashita et al., 2023), and an overall higher
level of job security (Cairó & Cajner, 2018). As such,
human capital informs employment, which in turn has

implications for economic outcomes (Ishikawa & Ryan,
2002). Yet, on average, older adults tend to have not only
lower adult literacy skills on average, but also lower AET
participation rates than younger populations (e.g., 66% in
age 26–35 years and 49% in age 56–65 years; Barrett &
Riddell, 2016; Desjardins, 2014; Paccagnella, 2016).
Therefore, promotion of adult literacy skills and continuous
education and training are essential for one’s human capital
and in turn, for remaining on par with expectations in the
dynamic labor market (Cummins, Taylor, & Kunkel, 2015).

Background and Problem Statement
Job automation disrupts the labor market (Bessen et al.,
2020) as technological advancement results in human labor
being continuously nullified by job automation technology
(Deschacht, 2021). However, non-routine jobs (e.g., data
analysis, computer programming) that often require higher
educational attainment and training are representative of
jobs that are not easily automated (Arntz et al., 2016;
Deschacht, 2021). Therefore, job security in an increasingly
technologically advanced labor market mandates adaptation by continuous skill acquisition to maintain employment in jobs changed by automation (Acemoglu & Restrepo,
2019b) or to attain employment in jobs less likely to be
automated (Frey & Osborne, 2017). In both instances, persons with limited human capital—lower educational attainment as well as lower adult literacy skills—are placed in a
disadvantaged position (Arntz et al., 2016). As obtaining
greater educational attainment in later life can be challenging, a more viable course of action for older workers to
enhance their job security can be to improve their adult literacy skills, which are considered more malleable than educational attainment in later adulthood (Montez & Hayward,
2014; Reder, 2019; Reder et al., 2020). Consistent with the
human capital theory, a protective factor against job automation risk is the acquisition and maintenance of human
capital, in the form of adult literacy skills (Acemoglu &
Restrepo, 2022).
In addition to adult literacy skills proficiency, additional
factors correlated with job automation risk include demographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, and race and ethnicity), socioeconomic status indicators (e.g., income level
and educational attainment), social relationships (e.g., living with a spouse), health, nativity status, and civic engagement (e.g., volunteering). Being older (Abeliansky et al.,
2020), female (Black & Spitz-Oener, 2010), and a racial
and ethnic minority (Broady et al., 2021) are correlated with
employment in jobs at higher risk of automation. Although
not explicitly attended to in this study, it should be noted
that these demographic characteristics are suggestive of the
underlying implications of gender- and race-based stratification systems that can act to disadvantage the human capital development (e.g., lower educational attainment and

adult literacy skills proficiency) and labor market outcomes
(e.g., higher job automation risk) of older gender and racial
minority workers (Jones et al., 2017; Wilson & Roscigno,
2018). Both higher income levels (Nedelkoska & Quintini,
2018) and educational attainment (Acemoglu & Restrepo,
2022) are correlated with employment in jobs at lower risk
of automation. Living with a spouse can influence job selection based on the economic and instrumental support provided by a spouse (Torr, 2011) particularly in relation to
caregiving responsibilities in later adulthood. Having
poorer health has been found to be predictive of higher job
automation risk (Hessel et al., 2018). The human capital and
employment pathway differs between U.S.-born and nonU.S.-born workers which subsequently differentiates their
job automation risk level (Liu & Portes, 2021). Volunteering
can support skills acquisition and contribute to the overall
enhancement of human capital, which can protect against
job automation (Baert & Vujić, 2018; Choi & Chou, 2010;
Vera-Toscano et al., 2017).

Research Questions and Hypotheses
Taken together, the importance of malleable human capital
(i.e., adult literacy) in a time of rapidly progressing job
automation across the labor market, age-related decline in
the average adult literacy (Paccagnella, 2016), as well as
negative employment outcomes in the second half of career
stages (e.g., early forced retirement), this study examines
the relationship between adult literacy skills and job automation risk among older workers in the United States.
Findings from this study can inform education and labor
policies that address differential risks of job automation in
later career stages, and thereby prevent economic insecurity
and early retirement in the older workforce. The research
question is as follows:
1.

Are adult literacy skills correlated with job automation risk among older workers in the United States?

It was hypothesized that there would be a negative correlation between adult literacy skills and job automation risk.
Simply put, higher adult literacy skills would mean lower
job automation risk.

Methods
Data
The 2012/2014/2017 Program for the International
Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) U.S.
Restricted Use File (RUF) data (National Center for
Education Statistics, 2019) were used in this study. PIAAC
is a nationally representative large-scale assessment of adult
literacy skills, including literacy, numeracy, and digital

(n = 1,880) for this study was restricted to respondents who

covariates were excluded (<1%).

Measures
Outcome Variable: Risk of job automation by occupations.
The measurement of occupations in PIAAC as 43 submajor groups is in line with the International Labor
Organization’s (ILO) International Standard Classification
of Occupations (ISCO-08) codes (International Labor
Organization, 2016). Job automation risk was determined
for 31 of these 43 sub-major groups, which were more
broadly categorized across 16 different occupations (Narine
et al., 2024). To do this, Frey and Osborne’s (2017) calculation of job automation risk, along with the adaptation of
their calculations by Yamashita and Cummins’ (2022) for
the measure of job automation risk in their study, were utilized to match a risk value to 31 of the sub-major groups as
per the ILO’s ISCO-08 codes. Job automation risk is
expressed as the percentage of jobs to be automated in the
next few decades, ranging from 0% to 100%.
Predictor Variable: Adult literacy skills are measured as
a score ranging from 0 to 500 points whereby the higher
values indicate greater proficiency. In PIAAC, adult literacy “…is accessing, understanding, evaluating and reflecting on written texts in order to achieve one’s goals, to
develop one’s knowledge and potential and to participate in
society” (OECD, 2021). PIAAC respondents completed the
systematic literacy assessment consisting of a series of
information-processing tasks and answered questions based
on provided texts. For example, the respondents need to
identify the latest time that children arrive at preschool from
the preschool rule book. More detailed methodological
descriptions have been published elsewhere (OECD, 2019).
Covariates: Age is a continuous measure of years (van
Walraven & Hart, 2008). Gender is a dichotomous measure
of women versus men (reference group). Race and ethnicity
is measured using a series of dichotomous measures including non-Hispanic White (reference group), non-Hispanic
Black, non-Hispanic Other, and Hispanic. The income measure is in quintile points (0–5: no income—the fifth quintile). Education is measured using three dichotomous
measures including less than high school (reference group),
high school, and college (associate degree) or higher. Living
with a spouse (vs. not living with a spouse) is a dichotomous measure. Self-rated health is dichotomized into good
health (excellent, very good, and good) and poor health (fair
and poor) due to the skewed distribution. U.S.-born is a
dichotomous measure (vs. non-U.S.-born). Volunteering

Analytic Approach
The survey weights and literacy proficiency scores were
used to compute the weighted descriptive statistics. Then,
survey-weighted linear regression was used to model job
automation risk (0%–100%) as a function of adult literacy
skills (0–500 points) and the covariates (see Figure 1).

Results
The weighted descriptive statistics are presented in Table 1.
Job automation risk has a mean of 38%. The average adult
literacy skills score was 271 points. The estimated regression coefficients are presented in Table 2. Adult literacy
skills (b = −.052, [0.016], p < 0.05) are negatively correlated with job automation risk (Model 2), which is consistent with the finding from the baseline model (i.e., Model
1). For the covariates, higher income levels, higher educational attainment (i.e., having an associate degree or higher),
and living with a spouse are correlated with lower job automation risk. However, being female is correlated with
higher job automation risk. About 19% (R-squared = .189)
of variability in job automation risk is explained by the final
model.

educational levels are usually a requirement for non-routine
jobs that tend to be at lower risk of job automation (Arntz
et al., 2016; Deschacht, 2021). Given the positive correlation between higher educational attainment and higher
information-processing skills proficiency (National Center
for Education Statistics, 2020), it stands to reason that
higher levels of information-processing skills would be correlated with lower job automation risk. Additionally, higher
adult literacy skills promote greater participation in AET
(Desjardins et al., 2019), which in turn enhances overall
human capital throughout one’s career stages (Acemoglu &
Restrepo, 2019b). Furthermore, adult literacy skills proficiency is considered a transferable skill that can support
career changes across different industries, and thus job displacement brought on by automation can be less disruptive
for persons with higher adult literacy skills as they are more
likely to transition to and be employed in jobs at lower risk
of automation (Frey & Osborne, 2017). The role of human

capital in ongoing job automation, age-related decline in
average adult literacy (Paccagnella, 2016), and possible
undesired employment consequences in later life are relevant to the significant adult education and labor issues.
Building on the proposed theoretical framework, findings
from the current study provided nationally representative
empirical evidence to link older workers’ literacy skills—
which are known to promote additional AET to acquire
timely human capital—to job automation risk. Findings are
consistent with previous studies linking adult literacy skills
to intermediate outcomes such as AET participation and
subsequent employment outcomes (Cummins, Taylor, &
Kunkel, 2015; Desjardins et al., 2019; Narine et al., 2024).
Income was negatively associated with job automation
risk for two possible reasons. First, in light of the human
capital theory (Becker, 2009), income is reflective of skills
and qualifications, which are linked with position levels and
industries with differing job automation risk. Second,
higher income leads to greater motivation as well as fear of
job automation (e.g., loss of a high-income job),

development for retaining the current job (Innocenti &
Golin, 2022). Living with a spouse might have been linked
with lower job automation risk in two ways. As job loss is
often associated with marital dissolution (Doiron &
Mendolia, 2012), living with a spouse could have been a
form of survivorship bias. That is, those who stayed with
their spouses might have had stable jobs with lower job
automation risk. Also, workers living with a spouse might
have been more motivated to find and retain jobs with
higher employment security to support significant others
(Basbug & Sharone, 2017). Furthermore, considering the
gender differences in job automation risk among older
workers, future research should explore the higher job automation risk among older women compared to older men.
Added to this, although racial and ethnic differences in job
automation risk were not evident in this study, given
employment in jobs at higher risk of automation is more
likely among racial and ethnic minorities (Broady et al.,
2021), further examination of job automation risk for the
older racial and ethnic minority workforce should not be
discounted, and is in fact warranted. After all, beyond the
more obvious link between human capital indicators (e.g.,
educational attainment and adult literacy skills) and job
automation risk, there are interlocking age-based, genderbased and race-based experiences shaping the development

of human capital and informing the employment outcomes
of age, gender and/or racial minority groups (Jones et al.,
2017; Wilson & Roscigno, 2018).
The current study is not without limitations. Firstly, the
classification of job automation risk by occupation is not
without critique (Willcocks, 2020)—despite being based on
the rigorous work of Frey and Osborne (2013)—and can be
considered a limitation of this study, particularly considering within-occupation variation might have been masked.
Secondly, other relevant skills—such as communication
and leadership skills—that can influence workers’ risk of
job automation are not directly accounted for in this study.
However, it should be noted these skills can form part of
some workers’ repertoire of human capital that might set
them apart, including providing protective effects against
job automation risk; for instance, as much as job automation can lead to job displacement, workers with more expansive forms of human capital are expected to be better
positioned to leverage their knowledge and skills for competitive advantage in dynamic labor environments
(Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019c). That said, the fast-advancing capabilities of artificial intelligence and its thus increasing potential to disrupt the job security of workers with
advantageous and protective characteristics (e.g., greater
human capital) should not be discounted. Although automation displaces workers, this is usually paired with a

reshaping and reimagining of labor tasks and activities
(Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019a). These automation-driven
changes to demands in the labor market are anticipated to
favor advantaged workers. However, unprecedented technological advancements might mean unprecedented labor
demands that might challenge even the more advantaged
workers. Thus, this should be further explored in future
research. Thirdly, the measure of volunteering as formal
volunteering might not accurately capture the potential
effects of other less formal forms of volunteering and is a
noteworthy limitation of this study. Civic engagement
remains a viable option for boosting human capital and
thereby supporting job security, but volunteer participation
can take on different forms among different social groups
(Baert & Vujić, 2018; Choi & Chou, 2010; Vera-Toscano
et al., 2017). Thus, although volunteering was not statistically significant in this study, future studies should not
overlook the potential role of volunteering—in its varying
forms—for shaping human capital and reducing job automation risk. Fourthly, there is a possibility of omitted variable bias because relevant measures, such as a conventional
marital status variable, are not included in the PIAAC data.
Limitations notwithstanding, the current study made contributions to the literature by documenting the populationlevel correlations between adult literacy skills and job
automation risks, specifically among older workers in the
United States. Findings from this current study offer preliminary implications for education and labor policies. Promoting
adult literacy skills training as a means of increasing the
human capital of middle-aged and older workers can potentially reduce the risk of later life economic and employment
insecurity. In technology-rich societies, job automation
might be more of a foregone conclusion; and as such, the
related skillset needed for workers to remain competitive is
expected to continue evolving (Means, 2017; Willcocks,
2020). Thus, investment in adult literacy skills training
should not be understated, because workers can rely on the
sustained relevance of foundational skills—such as adult literacy skills—to continuously expand and update their skillset. Importantly, enhancement of adult literacy skills among
older workers in particular would be in agreement with
existing policies, such as the Workforce Innovation and
Opportunity Act, by potentially easing the financial burden
of unemployment (Workforce Innovation and Opportunity
ACT, Public Law 113–128, 2014). At the same time, it could
be a potentially limiting approach to focus exclusively on
later life adult literacy interventions. Rather, there is a case
to be made for promoting lifelong learning participation,
with intentional efforts to support enhancement of literacy
skills among adults with lower proficiency levels, inclusive
of older workers (Boeren, 2016, 2017; Cummins, Harootyan,
& Kunkel, 2015).

Conclusion
Due to technological advancement and economic trends,
job automation is unlikely to slow down. Enhancement of
malleable and continuous AET promoting human capital—adult literacy—is one of the reasonable strategies to
counteract negative employment consequences among
workers in general. Yet, national data clearly show agerelated decline of average literacy skills (Paccagnella,
2016). Lower adult literacy skills are linked not only to
necessary job-related AET participation, but also to
employment security. Thus, adult literacy skills are critical
to counteract potential employment insecurity and job displacement issues in later life. Unlike the conventional
human capital indicator of educational attainment, which
is often stable over the life course (Montez & Hayward,
2014), the enhancement of adult literacy skills, which are
malleable at any life stage (Reder, 2020), is important
among workers of all ages, but is particularly crucial
among the older workforce given ongoing job automation
and the dynamic skill requirement in the technology-rich
labor market.

Author Biographies
Donnette Narine, MA., is a Ph.D. candidate in gerontology at the
University of Maryland, Baltimore (UMB) and the University of
Maryland, Baltimore County (UMBC). Her research focuses on
the sociocultural determinants of healthy aging among Caribbean
populations. Narine has a BA in Liberal Studies and a MA in
Clinical and Community Psychology from St. George’s University,
Grenada, and a MA in Applied Sociology from the University of
Maryland, Baltimore County, United States.
Takashi Yamashita, Ph.D., MA., MPH, is a professor of sociology and the co-director of the doctoral program in gerontology at
the University of Maryland, Baltimore County. His primary areas
of research are wider benefits of lifelong learning, social determinants of health and well-being, health literacy, and gerontology
education. Yamashita has a Ph.D. in Social Gerontology from
Miami University, United States.
Runcie C. W. Chidebe, MSc., is a Ph.D. student of social gerontology and a research assistant at the Scripps Gerontology Center
at Miami University, United States. His research interests include
aging and access to cancer care, workforce issues of older adults,
and veterans’ health. Chidebe has a diploma in Social Work and
BSc in Psychology/Sociology & Anthropology from the
University of Nigeria, Nsukka, and an MSc in Transforming and
Leading in Health Care from Birmingham City University, United
Kingdom.
