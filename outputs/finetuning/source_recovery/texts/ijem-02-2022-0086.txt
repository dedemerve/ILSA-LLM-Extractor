Abstract
Purpose – In 2014, Turkish policymakers implemented a policy change in the school inspection system that
encouraged school principals to conduct classroom observations and provide feedback to teachers as a means
to improve teaching. However, the question of whether or to what extent such feedback has an impact on
teaching has not previously been researched. The study presented in this article scrutinizes the relationship
between classroom observation feedback from principals and teachers’ classroom practices, as well as the
mediating role of teachers’ self-efficacy in terms of instructional practices, student engagement and classroom
management.
Design/methodology/approach – Confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation modeling are
employed to examine the relationship among principals’ feedback, teacher self-efficacy and teachers’ instructional
practices using data from the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS), compiled by the OECD in 2018.
Findings – These analyses indicate a small but significant direct correlation between principals’ feedback and
teachers’ instructional practices, as well as an indirect relationship mediated by teacher self-efficacy in
instructional practices.
Research limitations/implications – The findings support the policy shift in Turkey by concluding that
classroom observation feedback from principals makes contributions to the improvement of instructional
practices.
Originality/value – This study establishes a connection between teachers’ classroom practices and
leadership behaviors, which has not been extensively researched in developing nations.
Keywords Turkey, Principal leadership, Instructional practice, TALIS, Principal observation feedback,
Teacher self-efficacy

Introduction
There exists a broad consensus among educational researchers that high-quality teaching
matters and is, in fact, the most effective school-related factor influencing student
achievement (Dimosthenous et al., 2020). However, despite the conventional wisdom that
teachers are important, the qualities that characterize effective teachers and/or teaching
remain less certain, precluding researchers, practitioners and policymakers from
developing a reliable method for measuring the quality of teaching (Huber and Skedsmo,
2016). Among the many existing options, teacher self-assessment and peer assessment,
value-added models based on measuring improvements in student test scores over time
(Amrein-Beardsley and Holloway, 2019), and students’ (Chauhan et al., 2019) and principals’
observational feedback have garnered considerable recognition among practitioners,
€
policymakers and researchers (Balyer and Ozcan,
2020). With the greater emphasis on
accountability in education over the past two decades, educational policymakers in many

Educational policymakers in Turkey have also shown a growing interest in classroom
observations for a number of decades, but more recently, there has been a significant shift in
policy. Historically, the Turkish Ministry of National Education (MoNE)—which is
responsible for almost all educational matters, including staffing schools, developing the
curriculum, making the budget and allocating resources—has utilized classroom
observations for the purpose of inspection and teacher evaluation by assigning inspectors
to schools from either the central or the provincial branches of the ministry. In 2014, however,
the MoNE implemented sweeping changes to its school inspection policy, empowering school
principals to perform classroom observations while redefining the purpose of such
observations from “evaluation/inspection” to “supervision in support of professional
development.” Regulations now encourage school principals to visit classrooms at least
twice a year and provide teachers with constructive feedback that helps them improve their
instructional practices (Koç, 2018). While this policy shift is in keeping with contemporary
discussions regarding the purpose of classroom observations (Zepeda, 2017), it caused debate
among Turkish scholars regarding whether principals could or should take on such a task
(Deniz and Saylik, 2018; Tunç, 2020).
However, a more important and relevant question for policymakers, practitioners and
researchers is whether principals’ observation feedback has any positive effects on classroom
practices: a question that lies at the core of this article. The literature on the contribution of
principal observation feedback to teacher practices is considerably limited and

whether and to what extent such feedback from school principals could improve classroom
teaching. To address this need, the present study examines the relationship between
principals’ observation feedback and teachers’ instructional practices in Turkey, using data
from the 2018 cycle of the OECD Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS).
Previous research (Bellibaş et al., 2021) indicates that the relationship between leadership and
teacher practices could be both direct and indirect, suggesting that principals’ leadership
might influence teachers’ practices through their beliefs about the capability to overcome
instructional problems and enhance student learning. The present study, therefore, examines
the direct association between principals’ observation feedback and teacher practices, as well
an indirect association through the mediating role of teacher self-efficacy.

Instructional practice
A focus on teaching is indispensable when studying school improvement because of its
impact on long and short-term student learning outcomes. At the same time, educational
researchers studying the implementation of school reforms underline that any attempt to
increase student achievement through educational reform is doomed to failure unless it
successfully develops and supports effective teaching and teachers (Bryk et al., 2010).
Meanwhile, attempts to measure whether teaching is effective have long been a matter of
debate among educational policymakers and practitioners. We know that classroom
observations are universally used as one of a wide range of tools to examine the quality of
instructional practices (Cohen and Goldhaber, 2016). One of the key issues often raised about
such observations is the question of which specific practices are deemed “effective” and
therefore worthy of observation.
The OECD (2018) developed a theoretical framework based on prior research to support
their conceptualization of instructional quality. Their framework for effective instruction
includes three components that are proven to have a positive impact on student learning
outcomes: clarity of instruction (Seidel et al., 2005), cognitive activation (F€ortsch et al., 2017)
and classroom management (Wang and Degol, 2016). This suggests that an effective teacher
should be able to provide clear instruction, activate students cognitively and maintain
classroom order. The OECD (2018) suggests that teachers who provide clear instruction are
those who set learning goals, let students know what they are expected to learn and show
them how old and new topics are related. Cognitive activation involves instructional practices
that help students think critically, work together collaboratively to find solutions to a
problem and complete complex tasks. Teachers’ instructional practices regarding classroom
management include calming disruptive students, getting students to follow classroom rules
and listen to instructions and quieting down students quickly when lessons begin.
The argument at the core of this study is that these practices can be improved when teachers
receive constructive observation feedback that also supports their development of
self-efficacy.
Self-efficacy
The concept of self-efficacy, which is based on the idea of human agency in social cognitive
theory (Bandura, 1977, 2006), was introduced to the psychological literature by Bandura
(1977), who defined it as an individual’s belief in his/her ability to produce desired outcomes
(Bandura, 2006). He argues that an individual with a higher self-efficacy will be less likely to
give up when encountering obstacles (Bandura, 1977). Self-efficacy is, as such, a strong belief
that has considerable effects on the individual’s emotions, actions (Bandura, 1982), choices,
behaviors and performance (Bandura, 1977), as well as on cognitive functioning and thought
processes (Bandura, 1989). The notion that individuals’ beliefs in their capabilities might have
important consequences for learning outcomes was acknowledged by educational
researchers, leading them to pay considerable attention to the concept, mostly in relation

Skaalvik and Skaalvik (2007) defined teacher self-efficacy as “teachers’ beliefs in their own
abilities to plan, organize, and carry out activities required to attain given educational goals”
(p. 612). Empirical studies supported the theoretical work of Bandura by revealing a positive
relationship between teachers’ belief in their capacity and their actual practices. For instance,
in a longitudinal study of German teachers and students, Holzberger et al. (2013) reported a
causal association between teachers’ perceived self-efficacy and both teachers’ and students’
ratings of instructional quality. Similarly, Depaepe and K€onig (2018) examined pre-service
teachers’ beliefs and practices about teaching and found that their self-efficacy was a
significant predictor of their instructional practices. Based on these theoretical and empirical
works, the present study hypothesizes:
H1. Teachers with high self-efficacy will have better instructional practices.
The concept of teacher self-efficacy in the present study is based on the definition by
Tschannen-Moran and Hoy (2001) as one teacher’s judgment of his/her ability to produce
desired outcomes concerning student engagement and achievement, even when working
with students who are particularly challenging or lacking in motivation. Tschannen-Moran
and Hoy (2001) highlight the multi-dimensional nature of teacher self-efficacy and introduce
three essential dimensions to the educational literature: self-efficacy in instructional
practices, self-efficacy in student engagement and self-efficacy in classroom management.
This particular definition and the associated dimensions garnered considerable recognition
among educational researchers, policymakers and international agencies. The OECD (2019),
for instance, used this conceptualization to assess teachers’ sense of efficacy in the second and
third cycles of their renowned international survey focusing on the effectiveness of teaching
and learning in schools: the TALIS.
Teacher self-efficacy in instructional practices refers to teachers’ assessment of their
ability to implement effective teaching practices, such as asking students questions that
range between basic knowledge and those that promote higher-order thinking, addressing
students’ questions, using a wide variety of teaching and assessment strategies, clarifying
when students are confused and planning and providing individualized instruction
(Tschannen-Moran and Hoy, 2001). Strengthening teachers’ belief in their abilities has
considerable potential to enhance their actions and practices (Bandura, 1982). However, this
empirical research often linked the self-efficacy of teachers with sub-components of
classroom practice. Among few researchers examining how sub-components of self-efficacy
might be related to teacher practice, Poulou et al. (2019) reported a positive relationship
between teachers’ perception of efficacy in their teaching and their practices of adaptive
teaching. Therefore, one of this study’s hypotheses is
H2. Teachers with greater belief in their efficacy to teach will have better instructional
practices.
The second dimension is self-efficacy in student engagement. Student engagement, which
involves cognitive, behavioral and emotional aspects, refers to students’ active
involvement in classroom teaching (Lei et al., 2018). In this sense, teachers’ efficacy in
student engagement is defined as the extent to which they believe in their ability to
motivate students to take part in lessons, including those who struggle, find the work
difficult or uninterested, by helping them appreciate learning and enhancing their belief in
their abilities (Tschannen-Moran and Hoy, 2001). Bandura (1977) theorized that
self-efficacy is such a strong psychological state that it influences behavior and
performance. Apart from this theoretical work, several empirical studies have been
conducted; however, their show mixed evidence. For instance, Polou et al. (2019) found no
association between teachers’ self-efficacy in classroom management and their teaching

Teacher self-efficacy in classroom management is teachers’ perceived skills to create a
classroom environment that is conducive to teaching and learning (Poulou et al., 2019). More
specifically, it is teachers’ assessment of their ability to help students follow rules, establish
clear expectations for student behavior and calm disruptive students (Tschannen-Moran and
Hoy, 2001). Classroom management is a central dimension of the teaching profession,
requiring that teachers manage students individually and as a group, keep their attention,
deal with their disruptive behaviors and adapt the classroom climate to improve student
motivation and engagement (Jones et al., 2014). Depending on the size and grade level of
classes, teachers may face a multitude of problems regarding classroom management. Their
perception of efficacy in classroom management could help them deal with such problems
(Poulou et al., 2019). Marzano et al. (2003) suggests that teachers’ actions in the classroom play
a crucial role in determining their success in terms of having a positive impact on student
learning outcomes and that many such actions are related to classroom management.
Bandura (1989) stresses that teachers with higher self-efficacy can visualize success
scenarios that provide cognitive support in dealing with problems. A classroom with fewer
disciplinary problems is an environment conducive to high-quality teaching. This leads to

Principals’ observation feedback
Lamb and Swick (1975) provide a historical overview of classroom observation in the USA.
They trace the history of classroom observation as far back as 1642 when schools were
established by law in the USA. The practice of observing teaching was conducted by either
laypeople or principals until the 19th century when the position of principal became common
in public schools. While principals observed classrooms to control and inspect teaching
before the beginning of the 20th century, it was only then that specific instruments were
developed to assess and describe teachers’ classroom behaviors, practices and interactions
with pupils. Classroom observation by principals gained further attention from researchers
during the famous “effective school research” and the subsequent emergence of the idea of the
principal as an instructional leader. The goal of observation became to provide feedback to
teachers to enhance the quality of teaching and thereby support student learning.
An effective principal is a “helper” who observes teaching and provides teachers with
constructive feedback to improve classroom instruction (Bridges, 1967). However,
researchers have indicated that many principals either rarely or never observe teaching to
provide feedback to teachers (Seyfarth and Nowinski, 1987). The United States educational
reform of 2011 that adopted a more strategic approach to teacher evaluation put
unprecedented pressure on school principals to conduct regular classroom observations
(Derrington and Campbell, 2018). Over time, policymakers have increasingly recognized the
importance of school principals’ observations for the development of teacher professional
learning and effective teaching practices, with many nations now encouraging principals to
visit classrooms and provide teachers with constructive feedback after their visits for either
summative or formative evaluation purposes (Martinez et al., 2016). School principals are,

Furthermore, while little research has been conducted on the relationship between feedback
from principals and teacher self-efficacy, the few existing studies indicate that such feedback
has a positive influence on teachers’ self-efficacy beliefs. Based on interviews with 28
teachers, Mireles-Rios and Becchio (2018) suggest that principal-teacher interaction,
particularly during the post-observation phase of classroom observation, has an impact on
teachers’ sense of efficacy. There is also strong evidence that principals’ instructional
leadership—a style of educational leadership that heavily emphasizes observation
feedback—is associated with teachers’ sense of efficacy (Duyar et al., 2013; Bellibas and
Liu, 2017; Liu and Hallinger, 2018). For instance, Bellibas and Liu (2017) found that teachers
have a stronger belief in their instructional practices, student engagement and classroom
management at schools where the principal employs instructional leadership practices such
as supervising teaching, providing feedback and creating a positive learning environment.

Principals’ observation feedback is positively related to teachers’ belief in their abilities as
a teacher in general (H6), as well as more specifically in terms of teaching (H7), engaging
students (H8) and classroom management (H9).
Finally, when H1 (the positive relationship between self-efficacy and teacher practice) and
H6 (the positive association between principal observation feedback and teacher self-efficacy)
are confirmed, an additional hypothesis can also be established:
H10. There will be an indirect correlation between principal observation feedback and
teacher instructional practices through the mediating role of teacher self-efficacy.
Method
The present research is based on a cross-sectional survey design following quantitative
research methods, using teacher survey responses from the TALIS 2018 data source.
Data source
This study uses a secondary international data set from the TALIS, organized and
administered in 2018 by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development
(OECD). The TALIS is an important international survey that aims to collect a large set of
information on schools, principals, and teachers from both OECD and non-OECD nations

to provide an opportunity for policymakers to compare the effectiveness of different nations’
education and school systems. Three cycles of the TALIS have been conducted so far: the first
cycle in 2008, with the participation of 24 nations or regions; the second cycle in 2013, with 34
participating regions; and the third in 2018, with a total of 48 countries or regions taking part
in the survey. The TALIS is comprised of two main data sets at the school- and teacher-level.
The school data set includes rich information regarding school demographics, as well as

teachers’ demographics, behaviors and practices, such as self-efficacy, job satisfaction,
teaching practices, innovativeness, collaboration, professional learning and feedback. Given
the scope of this research, only the teacher data set from Turkey was used.

Variables and measures
The main outcome variable used in this study is teachers’ instructional practices (TCP).
In the TALIS, teachers are asked to indicate how often they engage in certain practices as
part of their classroom teaching. The total number of items is 12, each of which is based on a
four-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (“never or almost never”) to 4 (“always”). Factor
analysis of the items provides evidence that they are grouped into three dimensions: clarity
of instruction (CPCLR), cognitive activation (CPCA) and classroom management (CPCM).
The CPCLR dimension comprises four items: “I present a summary of recently learned
content” (TT3G42A), “I set goals at the beginning of instruction” (TT3G42B), “I explain
what I expect the students to learn” (TT3G42C), and “I explain how new and old topics are
related” (TT3G42D). The CPCA dimension encompasses four items: “I present tasks for
which there is no obvious solution” (TT3G42E), “I give tasks that require students to think
critically” (TT3G42F), “I have students work in small groups to come up with a joint
solution to a problem or task” (TT3G42G), and “I ask students to decide on their own
procedures for solving complex tasks” (TT3G42H). Finally, the CPCM dimension includes
four items: “I tell students to follow classroom rules” (TT3G42I), “I tell students to listen to
what I say” (TT3G42J), “I calm students who are disruptive” (TT3G42K), and “When the
lesson begins, I tell students to quieten down quickly” (TT3G42L). As reported by the
OECD (2019), exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)
indicate an overall good fit of the main scale and dimension-based scales in the Turkish
context.
Teacher self-efficacy (TSE) was used as the main mediating variable in this study.
To create the variable in the TALIS, teachers are asked to indicate to what extent they believe
they can demonstrate certain behaviors in their daily teaching. Twelve (12) total number of
items measure TSE in the TALIS, each based on a four-point Likert-type scale ranging from
1 (“not at all”) to 4 (“a lot”). Factor analysis of the items shows that they are clustered into three
dimensions: self-efficacy in classroom management (SECM), self-efficacy in instruction
(SEINS) and self-efficacy in student engagement (SESE). The items for SECM are: “Control
disruptive behavior in the classroom” (TT2G34D), “Make my expectations about student
behavior clear” (TT2G34F), “Get students to follow classroom rules” (TT2G34H) and “Calm a
student who is disruptive or noisy” (TT2G34I). The SEINS dimension comprises the
following four items: “Craft good questions for students” (TT2G34C), “Use a variety of
assessment strategies” (TT2G34J), “Provide an alternative explanation, for example when
students are confused” (TT2G34K) and “Vary instructional strategies in my classroom”
(TT2G34L). Finally, SESE involves four items: “Get students to believe they can do well in
schoolwork” (TT2G34A), “Help students value learning” (TT2G34B), “Motivate students
who show low interest in schoolwork” (TT2G34E) and “Help students think critically”
(TT2G34G). EFA and CFA analysis conducted by the OECD (2019) indicate an overall good
fit of the main scale and dimension-based scales in the Turkish context.
In the present study, “principals’ feedback based on classroom observation (POF)” was
used as the main predictor variable. This variable is a dummy through which teachers
are asked to indicate whether their principal used observations of classroom teaching as a
source of information to provide them with feedback. The responses are “Yes” (1) or “No” (0).
This enables comparison of instructional practices between those who received observation
feedback from their principals and those who did not.
Analytical strategy
Analysis of the data began by providing descriptive (means and standard deviations) and
reliability (alpha) statistics for and correlations among the main variables and associated
factors. Then, a mediation analysis was conducted using structural equation modeling (SEM)

Downloaded from http://www.emerald.com/ijem/article-pdf/37/1/85/794911/ijem-02-2022-0086.pdf by Istanbul Beykent Universitesi user on 24

with Mplus 7 software to analyze the structural relations among variables in the conceptual
model. SEM is a useful tool that allows examination of the relationships among multiple
variables and reveals whether any variable significantly mediates the relationship between
the predictor and outcome variable(s). In this study, two models were created. The first model
examined the direct relationship between POF and TCP, considering the mediating role of
TSE. Then, the second model tested the mediating effect for each dimension of TSE to
identify which dimension had the largest mediation effect. In the analysis of the data, each
survey item describing each of the three dimensions of TSE and TCP was treated as an
observed variable where dimensions and main variables (TSE and TCP) were treated as
latent. Furthermore, the bootstrapping method based on 2,000 bootstrap samples, suggested
by Preacher and Hayes (2008), was used to assess the effect size and obtain confidence
intervals and significance levels for paths among variables. Finally, all estimates were
transformed into standardized values to show comparable effect sizes for the relationships
among variables in both models.

Findings
This section begins by reporting the results of the descriptive and reliability analysis of the
variables used in the measurement model (Table 2). These results indicate that all scales are
above the desired standard for reliability of 0.70, ranging from 0.767 for teacher practice in
cognitive activation (TPCA) to 0.913 for teacher self-efficacy (TSE). The mean values for all
constructs are likewise relatively high as measured on the four-point Likert-type scales: 3.25
for TSE and 2.87 for teacher classroom practice (TCP). In terms of the different dimensions,
the mean values are lowest (2.32) for TPCA and highest (3.31) for teacher self-efficacy in
classroom management (SECM). All correlations among variables and dimensions in the
measurement model are significant at 0.01 level, except for the correlation between principal
feedback (POF) and teacher practice in classroom management (TPCM). The correlation
between TSE and TCP (r 5 0.493) is larger than the correlation between POF and TSE
(r 5 0.076) and the correlation between POF and TCP (r 5 0.073).

Discussion
This study explored possible relationships between principals’ observation feedback and
teachers’ self-efficacy and classroom practices. Two SEM models were generated to examine
both direct and indirect associations between feedback and teaching, considering the
mediating role of teacher self-efficacy in general and of their efficacy in teaching, student
engagement and classroom management. The results showed that self-efficacy was an
essential factor for better instructional practices as perceived by teachers. More specifically,
teachers’ belief in their ability to do better in terms of instruction and student engagement
was related to overall better teaching practices. This supports the theory that individuals’
belief in their capability to accomplish something can influence their actions and practices
(Bandura, 1977) and the positive relationship between self-efficacy and teaching identified in
an earlier empirical study (Poulou et al., 2019). Findings suggested that teachers’ belief in their
ability to deal with problems related to teaching and learning and to improve their instruction
and student engagement is likely to influence their actions during lessons and help them
implement the type of instruction conducive to student learning. However, there was no
correlation between high self-efficacy in classroom management and high-quality teaching
practices. This could be due to the nature of classroom management, with its emphasis on
controlling or preventing certain student behaviors, such as getting students to follow rules
and calming disruptive or noisy students (Tschannen-Moran and Hoy, 2001). Meanwhile,
most descriptions of high-quality instruction concern activities supporting student
engagement and activation (F€ortsch et al., 2017).
The results indicated that principals’ feedback based on classroom observation is a
significant and positive predictor of teachers’ classroom practices. This means that teachers
generally performed better when their principal provided feedback after conducting
classroom observations compared to teachers who did not receive any feedback. However,
the size of this relationship is very small, suggesting that principals’ feedback only results in
minor changes; nevertheless, such changes are educationally significant. When observations
are conducted and feedback provided systematically, the principal, as the formal leader of the
school, can hereby encourage teachers to reflect on their instruction practices and make
changes to improve the quality of these practices (Supovitz and Buckley, 2008). This supports
the argument that principals should be encouraged to engage in classroom observations and
provide feedback based on data gathered during these observations to help teachers enhance
their teaching (Wahlstrom and Louis, 2008).
The relationship between principals’ observation feedback and teachers’ instructional
practices is mediated by teachers’ sense of efficacy in teaching. The study’s findings
indicated that principals’ feedback has a positive impact on all dimensions of teachers’
self-efficacy – in student engagement, classroom management and instruction – in line with
previous studies (Bellibas and Liu, 2017; Liu and Hallinger, 2018). However, the findings
showed that this does not always translate into real-world changes to teaching practices.
Only teachers’ self-efficacy in instruction was a significant mediator between feedback and
teaching practices. This means that principals’ feedback can improve teaching both directly
and indirectly, particularly by influencing teachers’ sense of efficacy in teaching. The reason
only self-efficacy in teaching mediates the link between feedback and teaching is mainly
attributable to the strong relationship between self-efficacy in teaching and actual teaching
practice. While effect sizes for the association between feedback and all dimensions of

Limitations and future research
When interpreting the study’s findings, several limitations should be considered regarding
the variables and analysis. First, the quantity and quality of the feedback provided to
teachers likely makes a difference in the impact on their self-efficacy in teaching and actual
teaching practice. However, principals’ observation feedback functioned as a dummy
variable in this study, as the TALIS asks teachers to indicate whether their principal
provided feedback after observing their teaching. As such, the present study does not
provide any indication of the relationship between the frequency or quality of observation
feedback and teachers’ beliefs and practices. Future research on the frequency and quality
of such feedback could help develop a better understanding of whether and to what extent
principals’ observation feedback is beneficial for the quality of teaching and learning
activities.
Second, the results of this study are based on a cross-sectional survey design using a
nationally representative secondary data set compiled by the OECD. Cross-sectional survey
designs help make sense of the relationship between variables of interest but cannot be used
to make causal inferences about the relationship between variables since the data represent a
snapshot of a given point in time. While this study indicates that teachers have a more
positive assessment of their teaching practices and self-efficacy when their principal
observes their teaching and offers them feedback based on the observations, it does not mean
that feedback causes better teaching practices as an interpretation of findings. However, the
results presented in this paper should encourage researchers to conduct longitudinal or
experimental studies exploring causal effects of principals’ observation feedback on teachers’
instructional practices over time.
Third, the scale measuring instructional practice was developed based on teachers’
self-assessment, which could raise concerns about the objectivity of the data due to the issue
of social desirability effects. Teachers are likely to report what they (believe they) should do
instead of what they actually do. This is a particularly serious issue when the scale asks
respondents to indicate their level of agreement (ranging from totally agree to totally
disagree). To avoid or at least reduce the likelihood of social desirability effects, the OECD
(2018) used a frequency response scale, asking teachers to indicate how often a specific
instructional practice occurs during their lessons. Therefore, the instructional practice scale
does not assess the quality of instruction; instead, it measures the frequency of a specific
practice during teaching. Quantitative studies incorporating classroom observations to
quantify the frequency of instructional practices might provide a more reliable data set to
assess the quality of teaching practice (Cohen and Goldhaber, 2016).
Conclusions
While there has been much debate regarding the recent policy shift in Turkey towards
encouraging principals to conduct classroom observations and provide teachers with
feedback to improve the quality of teaching, little research has been conducted exploring
whether or to what extent principals’ observation feedback matters. This study sought to
understand of the relationship between principals’ observation feedback and teachers’

classroom practices, while considering the potential mediating role of teachers’
self-efficacy, using a nationally representative dataset (TALIS 2018) made available by
the OECD. The study’s findings provide evidence that principals’ observation feedback has
a positive impact on the quality of instructional practices. Overall, teachers who indicated
that they received feedback from their principal also reported greater self-efficacy, as well
as more frequent use of high-quality instructional practices. That being said, it is important
to highlight that the effect size of this impact is not very large. Based on the results, it can be
concluded that principals’ observation feedback had a marginal but educationally
important impact on teaching practice. The extent of changes in teaching practice is
likely to vary depending on the quality of the feedback provided by principals. When
principals are better prepared for instructional supervision and feedback, they may exert a
greater influence on teaching. Policymakers should therefore consider incorporating
effective strategies for instructional supervision as part of pre-service and in-service
training programs for principals organized by both universities and the ministry.
Furthermore, the results of this paper encourage any current school principals who may
have had misgivings to conduct regular observations of teaching and provide constructive
feedback to teachers at their schools. This feedback can help teachers to reflect on and
identify the strengths and weaknesses in their teaching and encourage them to adapt their
practices accordingly.
