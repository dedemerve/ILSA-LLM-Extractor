This study analyzed the direct and indirect effects of teacher collaboration on the clarity of instruction through
teacher self-efficacy in instruction and tested whether this relationship varied by organizational commitment to
schools. We utilized a moderated mediation analysis using cross-sectional survey data from the U.S. TALIS 2018.
We found that teacher collaboration has both direct and indirect effects on the clarity of instruction and that this
positive relationship is stronger when teachers have a higher level of organizational commitment. This study
provides theoretical and practical implications for school leaders, educators, and researchers to enhance teaching
practices through collaboration.

Teacher collaboration is critical in developing effective teaching
practices and enhancing school effectiveness (Day et al., 2016). Globally, educational institutions have established school-based teacher
collaborative practices to promote opportunities for teachers to collaborate with their colleagues (Bush & Grotjohann, 2020; Doppenberg
et al., 2012). This facilitates the exchange of ideas, knowledge, perspectives, and strategies (Stoll et al., 2006). The rationale for collaboration among teachers lies in its potential to improve instructional
practices (Ronfeldt et al., 2015) and foster a sense of belonging within
the organization (Reeves et al., 2017), thereby enhancing student
learning outcomes (Bryk et al., 1999; Darling-Hammond & Richardson,
2009). Studies have consistently demonstrated the positive role of
teacher collaboration in teaching school environments. This can
encourage innovative pedagogies, improve classroom communication,
and enhance student academic achievement (Pan et al., 2024; Vangrieken et al., 2015).
In addition to such positive roles, teacher collaboration significantly
enhances teacher efficacy (Sehgal et al., 2017). Research indicates a
positive relationship between collaborative work and teacher efficacy
(Tschannen-Moran & Barr, 2004; Xie et al., 2023). Specifically, as
teachers participate in more collaborative activities, their sense of efficacy tends to increase. As a powerful motivator in teaching, higher efficacy prompts teachers to exert their utmost efforts in their work,

which, in turn, positively influences instructional practices (Nie et al.,
2013; Ross & Gray, 2006). Therefore, teacher efficacy may positively
mediate the relationship between collaboration and instructional
practices.
While previous research has established a positive link between
teacher collaboration and instructional practices, it has not yet explored
the impact of collaboration on a specific aspect of teaching—namely,
clarity of instruction. Instructional practices encompass various skills,
including the ability to provide clear instructions, manage classrooms,
and plan lessons. Among these skills, clarity of instruction is particularly
crucial for student achievement (Bardach et al., 2018; Chen & Lu, 2022).
Multiple teaching assessment instruments, including the Classroom
Assessment Scoring System (CLASS; Pianta & Hamre, 2009; Pianta et al.,
2008, p. 112) and the Framework for Teaching Evaluation Instrument
(Danielson, 2013), have highlighted the importance of instructional
clarity. Understanding how teacher collaboration contributes to
instructional clarity can provide valuable insights for school leaders
seeking to support teachers and enhance student learning.
Another important factor in the discourse on teacher collaboration is
school commitment, which is defined as the sense of attachment
teachers feel toward their school (Kiral & Kacar, 2016). Scholars have
mentioned that teachers’ commitment to schools positively relates to
both collaboration and teaching practices. In other words, teachers who

1.2. Teacher collaboration and clarity of Instruction
Instructional clarity is a critical component of effective teaching and
has been identified as a significant factor in student learning (Maulana &
Helms-Lorenz, 2016). Scholars generally define instructional clarity as a
teacher’s ability to deliver classroom instruction concisely and clearly
(Chen & Lu, 2022; Maulana et al., 2016; Metcalf, 1992). In alignment
with these definitions, this study refers to clarity of instruction as an
instructional practice that is easily understandable regarding course
content, goal setting, and learning expectations. Additionally, it encompasses the ability to articulate the relationship between current and
previously taught material.
Clarity of instruction has been linked to student learning and academic achievement. For instance, Titsworth et al. (2015) conducted two
meta-analyses that demonstrated a positive relationship between
instructional clarity and student achievement, both at individual and
class levels. Utilizing the TIMSS 2019 data, Chen and Lu (2022)
demonstrated a positive relationship between instructional clarity and
students’ self-concept in learning. Using the same data, Gao and Lv
(2024) similarly demonstrated a positive correlation between instructional clarity and students’ mathematics achievement, mediated by their
math self-concept.
Moreover, nearly every prominent instrument for teaching assessment, including the Classroom Assessment Scoring System (CLASS;
Pianta & Hamre, 2009; Pianta et al., 2008, p. 112) and the Framework
for Teaching Evaluation Instrument (Danielson, 2013), emphasizes that
clarity of instruction is a crucial component. Therefore, providing clear
learning instruction and goals is essential for teachers to effectively meet
their students’ needs.
Despite a variety of empirical evidence on the relationship between
instructional clarity and student learning, there has been limited
research exploring the factors that contribute to instructional clarity. To
the best of our knowledge, only one study has explicitly examined the
relationship between instructional clarity and teacher collaboration.
Using data from TALIS 2018, Xie et al. (2023) found that there was no
direct relationship between professional collaboration and the clarity of
instruction in China and the United Kingdom. However, they identified a
significant indirect relationship, with self-efficacy serving as a mediator.
Further, they discovered that exchange and coordination among
teachers directly affected the clarity of instruction. Nevertheless, no
study has investigated how this relationship may vary based on teachers’
attachment to their workplace using large-scale data. Exploring this
issue not only provides evidence regarding whether the relationship
between collaboration and clarity of instruction varies across different
regions and education systems, but it also enriches the literature by
integrating a new perspective into the conceptual and analytical model
(Huang & Huang, 2024).

1. Literature review
1.1. Teacher collaboration
Teacher collaboration has gained significant global attention for its
role in enhancing teaching practices, fostering professional development, and facilitating organizational learning (Hargreaves, 2019).
While a universally accepted definition of teacher collaboration is
lacking, it is generally described as “joint interaction in the group in all
activities that are needed to perform a shared task” (Vangrieken et al.,
2015, p. 23). Through this social network, teachers exchange knowledge
and resources both formally and informally with colleagues to improve
their teaching practices (Baker-Doyle & Yoon, 2011). Given that this
network operates within specific work contexts, teachers’ perceptions of
their workplace and colleagues are crucial for understanding the quality
and effectiveness of teacher collaboration (Avalos-Bevan & Bascopé,
2017). Specifically, teacher collaboration can be classified into two
categories: formal and informal. Formal teacher collaboration occurs in
professional learning communities, while informal teacher collaboration
typically takes place in school-based groups focused on enhancing
teaching practices.
Moreover, collaboration can take various forms, including
exchanging ideas on instructional practices (Hindin et al., 2007), planning teaching activities and content, discussing daily challenges,
participating in professional development (Burbank & Kauchak, 2003),
and engaging in other teaching-related tasks together (Voogt et al.,
2016). In this study, we utilized the metric of teacher collaboration from
TALIS, which encompasses a wide range of activities in which teachers
participate to improve teaching and learning.
Previous studies have demonstrated that teachers’ engagement in
collaborative activities tends to positively impact student achievement
(Goddard et al., 2007) and school development (Goddard & Kim, 2018).
Collaborative work enables them to acquire knowledge and insights
from their colleagues (Darling-Hammond & Richardson, 2009), share
effective teaching strategies (Gregson & Sturko, 2007), and set objectives for their students (Martin-Beltran & Peercy, 2014). Ultimately, this
results in improved instructional methods.
Based on survey data from elementary school teachers in a large
urban school district, Goddard et al. (2007) discovered that in schools
characterized by higher levels of collaboration, students tend to achieve
better outcomes in math and reading. Similarly, Ronfeldt et al. (2015)
validated that teacher collaboration enhances student learning and

Moreover, researchers have established that commitment and

Previous research has established the relationship between
commitment and collaboration, and commitment and teacher selfefficacy separately. However, limited knowledge has been revealed
regarding the extent to which school commitment influences the relationship among all these factors. Based on the valuable insights from
previous studies, we posited that commitment would serve as a
moderator in examining the relationships among teacher collaboration,
teacher self-efficacy, and clarity of instruction.
This study aims to advance our knowledge of how teacher collaboration impacts a specific instructional practice—clarity of instruction
through teacher self-efficacy and school commitment. Scholars have
individually analyzed some of these factors. However, we have not come
across any study that has utilized nationwide represented data in the U.
S. to examine the extent to which collaboration relates to clarity of instruction and the paths of such relationship. Despite the widespread calls
for collaboration in the U.S., few large-scale studies have used nationwide data to investigate its relationship with instructional practices.
Moreover, by incorporating the factor of school commitment into our
model, our study will advance Xie et al.’s (2023) knowledge on whether
commitment facilitates or impedes teacher collaboration practices. In
other words, we build upon previous research by examining an extended
model that investigates how the relationship between collaboration and
teaching practices, mediated by teacher self-efficacy, is moderated by
teachers’ commitment to their respective schools (Huang & Huang,
2024). This study’s findings will provide insights for stakeholders and
policymakers regarding how to enhance teachers’ instructional practices. These practices exhibit a strong correlation with student academic
achievement and overall school improvement (Goddard et al., 2007;
Weddle, 2022). This study aims to address the following research
questions.

1.3. Teacher self-efficacy in Instruction, teacher collaboration, and clarity
of Instruction
Teacher self-efficacy, defined as teachers’ beliefs in their ability to
complete specific tasks to achieve educational goals (Granziera & Perera, 2019), has garnered significant attention in educational research.
Scholars have proposed that higher teacher self-efficacy is associated
with increased dedication to instruction by teachers, which subsequently positively influences student outcomes. This positive relationship will have a reciprocal influence on the development of higher
teacher efficacy beliefs (Shahzad & Naureen, 2017). Empirical research
has confirmed that teachers with a higher level of teaching efficacy
exhibit greater confidence in their teaching skills and are more inclined
to explore novel teaching approaches (Nie et al., 2013; Ross & Gray,
2006).
Furthermore, research has indicated that teacher collaboration
positively correlates with teacher self-efficacy (Goddard et al., 2015;
Kazemzadeh et al., 2017). Duyar et al. (2013) utilized TALIS data and
discovered that teachers’ professional collaboration positively correlates
with teacher self-efficacy and satisfaction. Goddard et al. (2015)
consistently demonstrated that teachers’ collective efficacy was influenced by teacher collaboration, which positively correlates with student
learning. Similarly, Kazemzadeh et al. (2017) found that teachers’ perceptions and collaborative practices positively correlate with their
self-efficacy in teaching. Recently, Liu et al. (2021) used TALIS 2013
data to demonstrate a positive relationship between teacher collaboration and teachers’ teaching efficacy. Despite findings regarding the
direct effect of teacher self-efficacy on student learning, scholars have
investigated the indirect effects of teacher self-efficacy in teaching and
learning. These studies have revealed that self-efficacy serves as a
mediating variable between the school environment and teachers’
practices (Han et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2019). Xie et al. (2023) have also
used teacher self-efficacy as a mediator when examining teacher
collaboration and instructional practices in China and the U.K.
Based on previous research, this study hypothesizes that teacher selfefficacy would mediate the relationship between teacher collaboration
and clarity of instruction in the U.S. Teacher efficacy encompasses
multiple aspects, such as efficacy in student engagement, classroom
management, and instructional strategies (Tschannen-Moran & Hoy,
2001); however, this study utilized the self-efficacy measure from TALIS
2018, which specifically focused on teachers’ reported efficacy in relation to instruction.
1.4. Role of organizational commitment
Commitment is a factor that has been extensively discussed in the
scholarly literature on organization improvement (Vangrieken et al.,
2015). Commitment refers to an individual’s affective attachment to a
particular organization (Mowday et al., 1979). According to Mowday
et al. (1982), organizational commitment refers to an individual’s
commitment to an organization, characterized by a strong confidence in
achieving the organization’s goals and a willingness to exert considerable effort. In other words, organizational commitment is the strong
connection that teachers experience with the school where they are
employed (Kiral & Kacar, 2016). Moreover, teachers who develop a
sense of commitment to the school are more likely to effectively fulfill
their responsibilities (Eren, 2001). Commitment is a significant predictor for various outcomes in schools, such as teacher retention
(Mclnerney et al., 2015), self-efficacy (Canrinus et al., 2012), dedication, and improved performance in instruction (Dee & Wyckoff, 2015).
Research has highlighted the interaction between teacher collaboration and teachers’ commitment (Vangrieken et al., 2015). Teachers
who are committed and have a sense of belonging in their work environment are more inclined to actively participate in collaborative efforts, such as professional learning communities or engaging in
cooperative work (Vangrieken et al., 2017). This collaboration among

Little’s (1990) research emphasized the importance of establishing a
professional community in schools where teachers can engage in
collaborative practices. Little (1990) argued that collegial interactions,
such as collaborative planning and joint working, result in improved
teaching practices and superior student outcomes. While acknowledging
the significance of collaboration and interdependence, Little (1990)
highlighted that teacher collaboration should extend beyond mere
“getting along” or “working well together.” Moreover, research on
teacher collaboration should focus not on evaluating teacher competence or performance but rather on examining how teachers are influenced by their colleagues regarding their teaching practices and

commitment. Hargreaves (2019) stated in his review article that
teachers who receive extensive collaborative professional training are

Drawing upon Little’s (1990) assertion regarding teacher collaboration
and previous research, this study shifts its focus from evaluating the
collaborative level of teachers in schools to examining the impact of
teacher collaboration on teaching practices.
Our study also considers teacher self-efficacy in instruction as a
mediator and school commitment as a moderator in the relationship
between teacher collaboration and teaching practices. The concept of
self-efficacy is derived from Bandura’s social-cognitive theory of
behavioral change (Bandura, 1977). It refers to a teacher’s belief in their
ability to effectively cope with tasks in school. Previous studies have
demonstrated that teacher efficacy is essential in predicting teacher
practices (Depaepe & König, 2018; Poulou et al., 2019). Based on
empirical findings and the social-cognitive theory on self-efficacy, this
study posits that efficacy mediates the relationship between teacher
collaboration and teaching practices. Similarly, school commitment is
closely related to both teachers’ participation in collaborative work
(Little, 1987) and work efficacy (Chesnut & Burley, 2015). Hence, this
study also examines school commitment as a moderator and its role in
affecting the relationship between collaboration, efficacy, and teaching
practices.
Given that current school reform initiatives, such as shared decisionmaking, require teachers to collaborate and that teacher collaboration
can improve student learning outcomes (Little, 1982), this study’s
findings will provide insights into the dynamic interplay among teacher
collaboration, teacher self-efficacy, organizational commitment, and
clarity of instruction.
To further explore the nature of this relationship between teacher
collaboration and clarity of instruction, we utilize a sample of USA lower
secondary school teachers to investigate our conceptual model (Fig. 1).
The study examines a set of hypotheses outlined as follows.

3.1. Data and analytical sample
This study used nationally representative U.S. data from lower secondary schools (grades 7–9 in the United States) participating in TALIS
2018. It was designed by the OECD with the participation of 48 countries
and economies (OECD, 2019). TALIS employed a stratified, two-stage
cluster sampling methodology in each country: approximately 200
schools in each country were selected with a probability proportional to
their size in the first stage. In the second stage, around 20 teachers were
randomly picked from the selected schools.
We used teacher surveys in which 2560 teachers in 165 lower secondary schools participated. For this study, teachers who had exclusively or primarily taught special needs students in the target class were
removed. It restricted the analytic sample to 2004 teachers in 165
schools, resulting in a reduction of approximately 21.7%. In addition,
schools with fewer than three teachers were also excluded to reduce
biased estimates in the multilevel data structure (Maas & Hox, 2005).
The percentage of missing data used in the analyses ranged from less
than 1% (female, years, advanced degree, subject, and school location)
to no more than 4% (ESES and school size). The results of Little’s (1988)
missing completely at random test indicated that the missing data was
not missing completely at random, x2 (1138) = 1357.058, p < 0.001. To
rectify the absence of this data, we utilized the Full Information
Maximum Likelihood Estimation (FIML) technique (Graham, 2009).
Using FIML generates less biased estimates than other methods like
listwise deletion, pairwise deletion, and similar response pattern
imputation (Enders & Bandalos, 2001). Consequently, the analytic
sample was restricted to 1897 teachers across 156 schools.

Clarity of Instruction Clarity of instruction, in general, refers to clear
and comprehensive instruction and learning goals, the ability to connect
new and old topics, and providing students with a summary of the lesson
at its end, which are important influences on student learning (Chen &
Lu, 2022; OECD, 2019; Titsworth et al., 2015). In this study, aligned
with previous researchers, we used the clarity of instruction in TALIS
2018 as the dependent variable in the analysis (Xie et al., 2023).
Teachers reported on their own use of organized and planned teaching
practices within the classroom (e.g., I present a summary of recently
learned content). This was assessed on a four-point scale, ranging from 1
(never or almost never) to 4 (always). The clarity of instruction was
determined by four indicators (see Table 2 for additional information).
These four indicators were employed to assess the clarity of instruction, with the factor model achieving a satisfactory fit: X2 (2) =
20.119, p < 0.001, SRMR = 0.032; CFI = 0.971; TLI = 0.914; RMSEA =
0.068 (Hu & Bentler, 1999). All the factor loadings exhibited statistical
significance (p < 0.001, with values ranging from 0.557 to 0.785). The
Omega reliability coefficient (ω) was 0.764, the AVE (average variance

extracted) value was 0.457, and the CR (composite reliability) value was
0.768 (Flora, 2020; Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Therefore, the latent
variable of clarity of instruction was considered acceptable (see
Table 2).

3.3. Analytical strategy
The data used in the present study have a nested structure in which
teachers were nested within schools (Choi, 2023). To handle the nested
data structure, this study used multilevel structural equation modeling
(hereafter, MSEM). This approach allows researchers to control for
measurement error at each level due to the sampling of items, enables
the simultaneous testing of multiple paths between latent factors, and
allows for the assessment of model fit indices (Krull & MacKinnon, 2001;
Preacher et al., 2010).
All items measuring the latent variables in this study were answered
by teachers and focused on individual perceptions of each concept using
the “I” statement. Therefore, this study employed a 1-1-1 multilevel
design in which the main predictor, mediator, and outcome were constructed at the individual level (Krull & MacKinnon, 2001; Preacher
et al., 2010). This approach is justified because the design of the items
and the concepts of the constructs refer to individual-level features
rather than group-referenced capabilities. For robustness, however, we
conducted additional models where both individual and group-level
constructs were specified. The final model will be determined based
on the results of various model fit indices.
The analytical procedures were categorized into three key stages.
The first stage was to examine the construct validity of our analytic
model using multilevel confirmatory factor analysis (MCFA). Following
the construct modification and validity test, we proposed a 1-1-1 MSEM
in the second phase, based upon the MCFA model evaluated in the first
stage. Lastly, based on the 1-1-1 multilevel mediation model, we

We utilized Preacher et al.’s (2007) method for conducting moderated mediation analysis and the three-step process for latent moderated
structural equation modeling (LMS) proposed by Cheung and Lau
(2017). LMS provides more accurate parameter estimates compared to
multiple regression and allows the modeling of second-order factors
with multiple dimensions (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). Because LMS does
not provide traditional indices to check how well a model fits the whole
data set (Cheung & Lau, 2017), we first used CFA and MCFA to ensure
that the main-effect-only models did a good job of fitting the data. We
used Hu and Bentler’s (1999) suggested indices like SRMR, CFI, TLI, and
RMSEA. We then tested the mediated effects of teacher self-efficacy in
instruction on the relationship between teacher collaboration and clarity
of instruction through the MSEM procedure.
We evaluated a model with latent interaction between teacher
collaboration and organizational commitment, as well as the estimation
of the path from this latent interaction to teacher self-efficacy in instruction and clarity of instruction (Cheung & Lau, 2017). We focused on
how the relationships between teacher collaboration and both teacher
self-efficacy in instruction and clarity of instruction were moderated by
varying levels of organizational commitment. We conducted tests on
three coefficient values at low (− 1 standard deviation), medium (mean),
and high (+1 standard deviation) to estimate the moderated mediation
effects, setting the mean value of our moderator variable to zero. Monte
Carlo bootstrapping with 20,000 repetitions was used to obtain confidence intervals (CIs) around the product of coefficients for the respective
paths (Mackinnon et al., 2004). The approach allowed us to quantify the
moderated mediation effects in a single holistic model step, compared to
a piecemeal approach in moderated mediation (Preacher et al., 2007).
Data preparation was conducted using STATA 18.0, and Mplus 8.10
was used for all data analysis (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). We used the
maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors and
adjusted chi-square statistics (MLR) method to estimate CFA, MCFA,
MSEM, and multi-level LMS models. This corrected the standard errors
and model fit indices, making the results robust to data normality violations (Padgett & Morgan, 2021). Moreover, teacher-level and
school-level weights (tchwgt, schwgt respectively) from the TALIS 2018
dataset were used in the multilevel modeling at the corresponding level,
respectively (OECD, 2019). Each teacher and school were assigned
weights to account for unequal probabilities of selection and nonresponse (Asparouhov, 2006). All control variables, at both teacher and
school levels (except for dummy variables), were grand mean centered
to achieve an interpretable intercept and assist with convergence issues
(Enders, 2013).

5. Discussion and implications
This study finds that teacher collaboration is positively associated
with clarity of instruction. This correlation exists both directly and
indirectly through teacher self-efficacy in instruction. It further reveals
that the effect of teacher collaboration on the clarity of instruction is
amplified when teachers possess a higher level of organizational
commitment to their schools. These findings provide a holistic
perspective on the relationship between teacher collaboration and the
clarity of instruction.

Our study enriches our understanding of how teacher collaboration
links to the clarity of instruction, with a particular emphasis on the role
of teacher self-efficacy in instruction. As indicated by social cognitive
theory, self-efficacy is connected to one’s belief in completing professional tasks. Our study provided empirical evidence on the importance
of self-efficacy in teachers’ achievement of educational objectives.
Specifically, our results indicate that teacher self-efficacy acts as a partial mediator between teacher collaboration and clarity of instruction,
accounting for approximately 49% of the overall impact of teacher
collaboration. Given the strong link (β = 0.500) between teacher selfefficacy in instruction and clarity of instruction, our results suggest
that teacher collaboration strategies—when customized to boost selfefficacy—will increase their overall influence on clarity of instruction.
For instance, teacher leaders can contribute by setting common goals
and expectations in collaborative work or professional development that
have an impact on teacher efficacy, which consequently enhances
instructional practices toward students.
Moreover, the exploration of this indirect pathway provides a theoretical comprehension of how teacher collaboration contributes to the
development of instructional practices. While collaboration itself aids
teachers in generating clarity of instruction, the results reveal that it also
inspires them to reflect on their own beliefs about their instructional
abilities and capacities, subsequently improving classroom teaching
practices. Therefore, it is imperative for school leaders to ensure that
teachers are afforded sufficient time and opportunities to acquire the
benefits of collaborative activities (Goddard & Kim, 2018). This will
allow them to reflect on their instructional practices, leading to a more
positive impact on the clarity of instruction.
Our study also reveals that the direct and indirect impact of teacher
collaboration on instructional clarity is amplified when teachers
demonstrate a high level of commitment to their schools. Teachers

7. Conclusions
This study explored the relationship between teacher collaboration
and self-efficacy in instruction, as well as the clarity of instruction in the
classroom. It recognized the importance of teacher collaboration in
enhancing instructional practices, teacher development, and organizational learning (Hargreaves, 2019). Moreover, acknowledging the
interplay of teacher collaboration with organizational commitment
(Vangrieken et al., 2015, 2017), we discovered its moderating effects on
the relationships between teacher collaboration, self-efficacy in instruction, and clarity of instruction.
These findings offer both practical and theoretical insights for a
deeper understanding of the role of teacher collaboration in teaching
practices. Given these findings, we recommend that school and district
leaders implement leadership practices and provide sufficient support to
assist teachers in effectively engaging in collaborative work (Choi, 2023;
Goddard & Kim, 2018; Goddard et al., 2010). Such efforts are likely to
improve self-efficacy in instruction and enhance the clarity of instruction. This study also contributes to the conceptual understanding of how
teacher collaboration improves teaching practices and the conditions
that amplify these positive mechanisms.

6. Limitation and future directions
Despite the contributions, our study has some limitations, and future
research would advance our knowledge about teacher collaboration,
clarity of instruction, teacher self-efficacy, and organizational
commitment.
First, our study relied on teacher self-reports to quantify the variables
used in this study. However, scholars have raised concerns about the
objectivity of such data (Xie et al., 2023), noting the potential for
teachers to overestimate their instructional practices or introduce bias in
assessing their efficacy. We acknowledge that discrepancies may exist
between teachers’ self-reports and their actual teaching practices, as
well as collaborative practices. To address this limitation, future
research could employ additional methods, such as classroom observations and the collection of lesson artifacts, to measure teaching practices
more accurately. Furthermore, incorporating perceptions from other
stakeholders, such as school principals and parents, could provide a
more comprehensive assessment of teaching practices.
Second, due to the nature of cross-sectional data, we caution readers
against interpreting our findings as having a causal inference. In addition to the main analysis utilizing MSEM to provide less biased estimates
(Maxwell & Cole, 2007), we ran multiple competing models (e.g., clarity
of instruction-teacher efficacy-teacher collaboration, in order) to test
whether the relationships analyzed in this study are subject to reverse
causality (see Appendix A). According to chi-square difference tests used
to compare the current model with competing models (Kline, 2023;
Werner & Schermelleh-Engel, 2010), the current model fits the data
better compared to the competing models, maintaining it as the model
