How can an innovative teaching force be developed? The
empowering role of distributed leadership
Yuan Teng , Rui Pu *, Yipu Hao
Faculty of Education, Central China Normal University, 152 Luoyu Road, Hongshan District, Wuhan, 430079, China

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Teacher innovation
Distributed leadership
TALIS 2018
Teacher collaboration
Self-efficacy

The development of an innovative teaching force is highly related to talent cultivation and
educational modernization. However, few studies have explored how to build an innovative
teaching team by empowering teachers through distributed leadership. Based on TALIS 2018 data
for teachers in Shanghai, China, this study explores the influence of distributed leadership on
teacher innovation in an Asian context. The results show that distributed leadership can significantly promote both team and individual innovation by transforming the school structure and
teacher agency as well as structure-agency interaction. Namely, teacher collaboration and selfefficacy partially mediate the relationship between distributed leadership and teacher innovation, and collaboration-teacher self-efficacy has a chain mediating effect on this relationship.
Such effects of distributed leadership on teacher innovation have also been verified in South
Korea and Singapore. Therefore, to develop an innovative teaching force in Asian contexts, it is
necessary to enhance principals’ distributed leadership and further create a collaborative climate
under distributed leadership.

1. Introduction
Teacher innovation has become a crucial factor in the success of an educational system around the world (OECD, 2013; Rubenstein,
McCoach & Siegle, 2013), as it is closely related to the cultivation of quality talent and the adaptation of the educational system in the
reform era (Fullan, 2009; Hargreaves & Fullan, 2012). The Chinese government has clearly expressed its decision to improve teacher
innovation in a series of national policies, such as the Opinions on Comprehensively Deepening the Reform of the Development of a Teaching
Force in the New Era implemented in 2018, China Educational Modernization 2035 issued in 2019, and the Plan for Strengthening the
Teaching Staff in Basic Education in the New Era promulgated in 2022. In practice, to encourage teachers to innovate, the government has
adopted various strategies, such as a performance-based salary system, regular training, and collective teaching and research. However, primary and secondary school teachers still show insufficient awareness of classroom innovation (Zhang & Yang, 2011), inadequate preparation for innovation, and a low frequency of teaching innovative practice (Wu, 2021), indicating that teachers’
innovative capability and practices should be further improved.
Teacher innovation means that “teachers can spontaneously and purposefully generate and develop new ideas to improve their
work and school” (Janssen, 2003). It includes innovation at both the team and individual levels. Innovation at the team level influences
the practice of the entire school organization, while innovation at the individual level is more conducive to work improvements on a

smaller scale (Kundu & Roy, 2016). In reality, the school context is vital for improving the awareness and capacity of innovation in the
teaching force. Contextual factors such as school management and principal leadership, especially transformational leadership, that
are related to teacher innovation have received attention in previous research (Cai & Tang, 2022; Davies et al., 2014; Ekvall, 1996; Hall
& Hord, 2011; Zhu, Yao & Zhang, 2019). Previous studies have noted that teacher innovation depends on teachers’ autonomy and
spontaneity, and excessive control or external pressure reduces teachers’ professional autonomy (Reeve, 2009), which is detrimental
for teacher innovation. In reality, distributed leadership emphasises the sharing of leadership responsibilities among staff, and encourages the participation of all members in decision-making regarding school affairs (Harris, 2008; Hartley, 2007). These research
findings imply, to some extent, that teachers can be empowered by having fuller professional autonomy under distributed leadership,
which is beneficial for teacher’s agency in the context of innovation. Moreover, due to the difficulty of innovation, a supportive school
structure such as a collaborative team is also crucial in collectivistic societies (Davies et al., 2014). Distributed leadership that promotes a collaborative and shared working structure might also contribute in this respect. Therefore, it is highly likely that distributed
leadership might facilitate teacher innovation by influencing teacher agency and school structure.
However, existing studies have failed to analyze the relationship between distributed leadership and teacher innovation, especially
in China and other Asian contexts (e.g., Singapore, South Korea, etc.), which are characterised by hierarchy, large power distance and
collectivism (Hairon & Dimmock, 2012; Hofstede, 2010). It is worth considering the extent to which and how the distributed leadership strategies that have been developed in Western countries may apply to teacher innovation in Asian societies. The mechanism
underlying the impact of leadership practice on teacher innovation has not been clearly explicated, as previous studies have failed to
explain the dynamic interactions between structural change and active agents under certain leadership approaches. Additionally, most
studies have focused on teacher innovation at the individual level while ignoring innovation at the team level. Improvements in
teachers’ individual innovative capability mostly affect the students in the classes they teach, but the innovation of the teaching force
as a whole benefits all students and the whole school. As China and many Asian countries are often considered to exhibit a collectivist
culture, team innovation is of great significance in such cultural background. Thus, teacher innovation at the team level should not be
neglected. Therefore, this study focuses on the innovation of both individual teachers and teams, explores the influence of principals’
distributed leadership on individual teacher innovation and team innovation in school contexts in Asian countries, and further analyses
the mechanism of this influence (especially the transformation of both structure and agency) to provide a reference for developing an
innovative teaching force.
2. Literature review
2.1. Teacher innovation and distributed leadership
Previous studies have indicated that teacher innovation is influenced by individual characteristics and school contexts. In terms of
individual characteristics, factors such as teachers’ demographic features (e.g., years of teaching, education level, and professional
title) (Tang, Wang & Xie, 2019; Thurlings, Evers & Vermeulen, 2015), willingness to innovate and attitudes toward innovation (Davies
et al., 2014; Huang, Lee & Dong, 2019), creative self-efficacy (Huang, Lee & Yang, 2019) and basic psychological needs (Cai & Tang,
2022) significantly influence the behaviours associated with teacher innovation. Contextual school factors such as principal leadership
style and behavior (Ekvall, 1996; Hall & Hord, 2011; Zhu et al., 2019), supportive school conditions (Cai & Tang, 2021; Davies et al.,
2014), professional learning communities (Liu, Lu & Yin, 2022) and school innovation climate (Chou, Shen, Hsiao & Shen, 2019; Wu,
2021) are key factors influencing teachers’ innovation behavior and often play an indirect role by affecting factors such as teachers’
autonomy (Cai & Tang, 2022; Wang & Cai, 2016) and self-efficacy (Cai & Tang, 2021).
In terms of school contexts, principal leadership style and behavior are vital contributors. Existing studies in corporate contexts
have shown that leaders and their leadership behaviours are significant for promoting employee and organizational innovation (Jia,
Chen, Mei & Wu, 2018; Le & Lei, 2019). Research in school contexts has focused more on the role of transactional leadership and
transformational leadership and found that both significantly promote teachers’ teaching innovation (Hou, 2018; Howell & Hall-Merenda, 1999). Messmann, Evers and Kreijns (2022)) explained that transformational leadership influences teacher’s innovative
behavior through teacher’s basic psychological needs satisfaction. Similarly, empowering leadership can also improve teachers’
innovative behavior by increasing their psychological empowerment (Zhu et al., 2019). However, authoritarian leadership largely
impedes teacher innovation by decreasing their autonomous motivation (Wang & Cai, 2016). Apparently, previous studies have
confirmed the important role of leadership in teaching innovation, but most such studies have focused on transformational leadership
rather than distributed leadership. In addition, the mechanism underlying the effects of leadership on teacher innovation has not been
fully uncovered, as the literature has mainly explained such effects from the perspective of the teacher’s agency (such as in terms of
psychological needs or motivation) while ignoring the transformation of structure and the structure-agency relations under certain
leadership practices.
The global trend of decentralization has called for a transformation from the traditional centralized leadership model to a more
decentralized model; thus, distributed leadership has gradually become prevalent in both educational practice and research (Hartley,
2007). Although no one definite concept of distributed leadership has been produced, the definitions provided by Gronn, Spillance and
Harris have been widely recognized. According to Gronn (2002), leadership is distributed when individuals pool their expertise to
achieve common goals through concerted action. Under distributed leadership, school staff, parents and students can participate in
school decision-making (Harris, 2008). Spillance (2006) defined distributed leadership from the perspective of a practice-centered
theory, and highlighted the fact that distributed leadership is a shared leadership style that involves an interactive process among
leaders, subordinates and situations. It emphasises full empowerment and distributed responsibility (Spillane, 2006). Accordingly,

under conditions of strong distributed leadership, a group of members are included in school decision-making processes; schools
establish an open organizational culture and collaborative climate (Chen, 2018; Liu, Bellibaş & Gümüş, 2021) and provide a favourable
environment in which stakeholders can participate actively (Leithwood et al., 2007).
A large number of studies conducted in Western and cross-national contexts have shown that distributed leadership improves
teachers’ work. When schools have strong distributed leadership, teachers’ self-efficacy (Liu et al., 2021; Sun & Xia, 2018), organizational commitment (Hulpia & Devos, 2010), agency (Hilal, Hammad & Polatcan, 2022), participation (Muijs, Harris, Chapman, Stoll
& Russ, 2004), collaboration (Amels, Krüger, Suhre & van Veen, 2020; Özdemir, Kılınç, Polatcan, Turan & Bellibaş, 2023), job
satisfaction (Hulpia, Devos & Rosseel, 2009) and teaching quality (Bryk, Sebring & Allensworth, 2010) can all be significantly
improved. The positive effects of distributed leadership on teachers have been explained in terms of improved decisional capital,
enhanced teacher empowerment as well as the establishment of a shared and collaborative school environment (Brown, MacGregor &
Flood, 2020; Holdsworth & Maynes, 2017; Torres, 2019). Several scholars have noted that when leadership is distributed in a
disorderly manner, this situation can result in negative outcomes (Leithwood et al., 2007). Evidently, the positive effects of distributed
leadership on teachers and their work have been widely proven in Western and cross-national context, but research examining how
distributed leadership influences teacher innovation in the Asian context remains rare.
In reality, the specific features and effects of distributed leadership might vary with cultural backgrounds (Hairon & Goh, 2015;
Liu, 2020b; Liu, Bellibas & Printy, 2018). For example, Liu (2020) demonstrated the alignment of distributed leadership patterns with
cultural clusters. Tan (2022) suggested that power distance and other Asian values result in a nuanced implementation of distributed
leadership in schools in Singapore. Findings concerning the effects of leadership in different cultural backgrounds have been inconsistent. Some studies have argued that the impact of the same leadership approach varies across different contextual backgrounds (Ho
& Tikly, 2012; Liu & Hallinger, 2021). Karadag (2020) reported that educational leadership has a stronger effect on student learning in
vertical-collectivist cultures than in horizontal-individualistic cultures. However, Jönsson, Muhonen, Denti and Chen (2015) found a
similar positive effect of empowering leadership on employee learning in China and Sweden, which indicated different levels of power
distance culture. Given these inconclusive results, it is necessary to ground the study of distributed leadership in a specific context.
Recent empirical studies in China have indicated that distributed leadership has a positive impact on teachers’ job satisfaction
(Gong, 2018; Liu, Keeley, Sui & Sang, 2021) and commitment to change (Liu, 2020a). China and many other Asian countries have been
characterised as exhibiting a hierarchal and collective culture. In a bureaucratic school organization, power and authority are largely
centralized, and teachers, who are the professionals responsible for teaching, usually lack the authority necessary to participate in
decision-making, greatly hindering teachers’ working motivation (Wang, 2013) and further impeding their teaching innovation. Thus,
distributed leadership is particularly crucial in such a hierarchal school structure since it delegates decision-making authority to
teachers (Short & Greer, 1997) and empowers teachers to exercise their professional knowledge to innovate. Moreover, people in
collectivistic societies are more likely to work collaboratively and share responsibility (Hofstede, 2010; Lu & Smith, 2022). Collaboration is also common in teaching practices in many Asian countries, for example, in professional learning communities in China
(Wang, Wang, Li & Li, 2017), South Korea (Yoo & Jang, 2022), and Singapore (Hairon & Tan, 2017). Distributed leadership promotes a
shared and collaborative school environment (Chen, 2018; Liu et al., 2021), which coincides with teacher’s work requirements in a
collectivist culture. Therefore, this study posits that distributed leadership has a positive effect on teacher innovation in China and
proposes the following Hypotheses H1a and H1b:
H1a: Distributed leadership has a significant positive effect on team innovation.
H1b: Distributed leadership has a significant positive effect on individual teacher innovation.
2.2. The mediating role of teacher self-efficacy and collaboration
Previous studies regarding the effects of distributed leadership on teachers (in which context teacher innovation has remained
understudied) have focused either on teacher agency or the changed school environment while ignoring the dynamic interaction
between agency and structure. The promotion of teacher innovation depends on the stimulation of teacher agency, the support of
school structure and the structure-agency interaction. It would be more difficult for individual teacher to innovate within the context of
a hierarchical educational system (Pan & Wiens, 2023), in which case the adjustment of the power structure becomes especially
important. Distributed leadership would contribute in this respect. A distributed model of leadership in the Asian context is highly
likely to facilitate teacher innovation through three paths, namely, the transformation of agency and structure as well as the interaction
between these factors.
First, distributed leadership might promote teacher innovation by enhancing teacher’s agency. According to Bandura’s (1982,
1997) social cognitive theory, self-efficacy is central and fundamental for personal agency. Teacher self-efficacy reflects teachers’
perceptions of their capacity to do well in teaching and to cope with challenges in their work (Tschannen-Moran, Hoy & Hoy, 1998).
Scholars have demonstrated that teacher self-efficacy plays a critical role in teachers’ work and performance (Caprara, Barbaranelli,
Borgogni & Steca, 2003; Klassen, Tze, Betts & Gordon, 2011), as self-efficacy is the basis of human agency and regulates people’s
thoughts and behavior. When individuals have low confidence about their capacity to complete relevant work tasks, they will not be
fully engaged, while those with high self-efficacy are more likely to try new approaches and constantly challenge themselves (Tierney
& Farmer, 2004). The study of enterprises has shown that employees’ self-efficacy is a significant predictor of their innovative behavior
(Tierney & Farmer, 2002). In schools, teacher self-efficacy has also been shown to exert a critical effect on teacher innovation (Cai &
Tang, 2021; Cayirdag, 2017). Namely, teachers with high self-efficacy show greater innovation in teaching.
Additionally, teacher self-efficacy is influenced by school contexts, especially school leadership. Previous studies have identified

principal leadership as an important factor promoting teacher self-efficacy (Bellibas & Liu, 2017; Zheng, Li, Chen & Loeb, 2017).
Bandura (1997) argued that support from leaders is a key motivator for employees’ self-efficacy. According to Bellibas and Liu (2017),
principals’ teaching leadership has a significant positive effect on teacher self-efficacy. Zheng et al. (2017)) found that the involvement
of principals in school activities could improve teachers’ teaching efficacy. A few scholars have explored the relationship between
distributed leadership and teacher self-efficacy. For example, based on international data, Sun and Xia (2018) and Liu et al. (2021)
both confirmed that the stronger the distributed leadership perceived by teachers is, the higher their self-efficacy. In the hierarchical
and bureaucratic educational structure found in China and many other Asian countries, educational administrators and school
principals have positional authority, while teachers are largely excluded from decision-making and usually lack the power even in their
own professional areas (Lu & Smith, 2022; Wang, 2013).To improve teacher self-efficacy in such contexts, it is more important for
teachers to be given authority through distributed leadership. Therefore, distributed leadership could influence teacher innovation in
China through the effect of teacher self-efficacy, and we formulate Hypotheses H2a and H2b as follows:
H2a: Teacher self-efficacy plays a mediating role in the relationship between distributed leadership and team innovation.
H2b: Teacher self-efficacy plays a mediating role in the relationship between distributed leadership and individual teacher innovation.
Second, with regard to teacher innovation in a hierarchal school, individual agency alone is insufficient to encourage the whole
team to be innovative. The transformation of the school structure is vital. In the collectivistic context prevalent in China and other
Asian countries, teams featuring deep collaboration and shared responsibility are central to the task of loosening or even transforming
the strict bureaucratic structure, as teachers in such societies tend to engage in collective actions (Hofstede, 2010). In reality,
collaboration and reciprocal sharing among teachers are vital for improving the work of the overall teaching force. The teaching
profession has traditionally been perceived as one where teachers rely on individual expertise to perform; however, in recent years,
researchers and educational practitioners have gradually realized the importance of collaboration in improving teachers’ professional
learning and teaching quality (Ainley & Carstens, 2018). Both the International Summit on the Teaching Profession and the OECD
emphasised the crucial role of teamwork in teachers’ work (OECD, 2013; Schleicher, 2015) and mentioned that collaboration was
crucial for teachers’ instruction and professional development (Chong & Kong, 2012).
Empirical studies have also found that teacher collaboration is effective in improving teachers’ performance (Duyar, Gumus &
Sukru Bellibas, 2013; Ronfeldt, Farmer, McQueen & Grissom, 2015). For example, schools with a higher level of collaboration displayed higher teacher job satisfaction (Torres, 2019). Collaboration is even more important for teaching in Asian countries that
emphasize collectivism than in Western countries, as proved by Lu and Wang (2021). In addition, teachers in China are familiar with
the tradition of working collaboratively in teaching and research groups as well as grade groups (Lu & Smith, 2022; Wang et al., 2017).
Thus, it can be predicted that knowledge sharing and mutual support in collaboration, which facilitate the generation of collective
wisdom, are very likely to stimulate innovation, which requires a collegial workplace culture.
However, teacher collaboration depends heavily on the leadership style of the organization. Wang (2013) found through an
ethnographic study that autocratic principal leadership causes teachers to retreat from collective work to the private sphere of individuals. The study by Gumus, Bulut and Bellibas (2013)) confirmed the correlation between principal leadership and teacher
collaboration. Based on the analysis of data from a large-scale international survey, Liu and Werblow (2019) showed that distributed
leadership can significantly improve professional collaboration among teachers. Principal distributed leadership helps to establish a
democratic school climate, empower each teacher and provide teachers with more opportunities to freely express themselves (Gumus
et al., 2013; Liu & Werblow, 2019). All of these factors are conducive to unreserved exchanges and communication among teachers,
thereby enhancing teacher collaboration. Thus, this study proposes the following research hypotheses:
H3a: Teacher collaboration plays a mediating role in the relationship between distributed leadership and team innovation.
H3b: Teacher collaboration plays a mediating role in the relationship between distributed leadership and individual teacher innovation.
Third, structural change also influences teacher agency, resulting in active structure-agency interaction. Specifically, in addition to
transforming the hierarchal organizational structure to establish a more democratic and collaborative organization, the collective
strength formed by deep teamwork can reduce the uncertainty associated with employees’ work, enhance their self-confidence and
self-efficacy, and ultimately promote teacher’s agency. Social cognitive theory suggests that teacher self-efficacy is based on four
sources: mastery experiences, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological and affective states (Bandura, 1997).
Accordingly, through collaboration with others in teaching and administration, teachers can not only develop their mastery and
vicarious experience but also gain verbal persuasion and affective states, thereby increasing self-efficacy. For Asian contexts that
exhibit a collectivism orientation in particular, deep collaboration not only supports teacher’s work but can largely enhance their sense
of security and confidence. Both Xie’s (2022) China-based study and Chong’s (2012) Singapore-based study showed that collaboration
can significantly improve teacher self-efficacy. Thus, we propose the following research hypotheses in the Chinese context:
H4a: “Teacher collaboration—teacher self-efficacy” has a chain mediating effect on the relationship between distributed leadership and
team innovation; and
H4b: “Teacher collaboration—teacher self-efficacy” has a chain mediating effect on the relationship between distributed leadership and
individual teacher innovation.

3. Methodology
3.1. Data
The data for this study were derived from the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018 conducted by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). TALIS is a survey of school teachers and principals conducted every
five years. The 2018 survey involved primary and secondary school teachers and principals from 31 countries and regions. TALIS
applied stratified two-stage probability sampling: 200 schools in each country/region were first sampled, and then no fewer than 20
teachers and one principal in each school were selected. For TALIS 2018, 197 principals and 3976 teachers from 198 junior high
schools in Shanghai, China, were included in the survey.
This study mainly uses data for teachers from Shanghai, China, with data for teachers from South Korea and Singapore further
being analysed to verify the research findings. Samples with missing data on dependent variables were eliminated, while the missing
data on independent variables were replaced with the school mean. Finally, for the Shanghai sample, 3936 teacher samples were used
to analyze team innovation, and 3237 were used to analyze individual innovation. Male and female teachers accounted for approximately 26% and 74% of the participants, respectively; the sampled teachers had approximately 17 years of teaching experience on
average, and approximately 73% of the teachers had 10 years or more teaching experience; approximately 13% of the teachers
participating in the survey had postgraduate degrees; 87% of the teachers reported that teaching was their first choice as a career; 74%
were urban teachers while 26% were rural teachers; and 85% of the teachers were from public schools.
3.2. Measures
The dependent variable of this study is teacher innovation, including team innovation and individual innovation. Specifically, team
innovation emphasises the innovation attitudes of the group of teachers in each school and was measured by four items, for example,
“Most teachers in this school strive to develop new ideas for teaching and learning” and “Most teachers in this school are open to
change”. The higher the score was, the stronger the willingness of the teaching team in the school to innovate. Individual innovation
refers to the activities that individual teachers conduct in their daily teaching to develop students’ innovation capabilities and was
measured by four items, such as “I present tasks for which there is no obvious solution” and “I give tasks that require students to think
critically”. The higher the score was, the stronger the practice of individual teacher innovation. The use of such items to measure team
and individual innovation has also been emphasised by OECD (2013, 2019) and other scholars (Blömeke, Nilsen & Scherer, 2021; Wu,
2021). The Cronbach α coefficients of the two variables were 0.948 and 0.737, respectively, indicating the good reliability of the two
scales. The confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) results for team innovation (χ2/df =33.707, RMSEA=0.091, CFI=0.996, SRMR= 0.006)
and individual innovation (χ2/df = 5.596, RMSEA= 0.038, CFI= 0.997, SRMR= 0.009) showed that the models exhibited an
acceptable data fit.
Distributed leadership is the key independent variable of this study and refers to the level of distributed leadership in the school
perceived by teachers. This variable was measured using five items, such as “This school provides staff with opportunities to actively
participate in school decisions”, “This school has a culture of shared responsibility for school issues” and “There is a collaborative
school culture”, with higher scores indicating higher levels of distributed leadership in the school. The Cronbach α coefficient of this
variable was 0.941, which indicated that the scale has good reliability. The CFA results showed that the model fit the data well (χ2/df
= 12.757, RMSEA=0.059, CFI=0.935, SRMR=0.047).

Teacher collaboration and teacher self-efficacy are the mediating variables in this study. Specifically, teacher collaboration was
measured using a multidimensional composite scale that includes two subdimensions, namely, exchange and cooperation (four items)
and professional collaboration (four items), with a total of eight items, for example, “Teaching jointly as a team in the same class” and
“Attending team conferences”. The higher the score was, the stronger the collaboration among teachers. Teacher self-efficacy was
measured using a multidimensional composite scale that was divided into three dimensions, namely, self-efficacy in classroom
management (four items), self-efficacy in instruction (four items), and self-efficacy in student engagement (four items), with a total of
12 items, for example, “In your teaching, to what extent can you control disruptive behavior in the classroom”. Higher scores indicate
higher teacher self-efficacy. The Cronbach α coefficients of teacher collaboration and teacher self-efficacy were 0.851 and 0.962,
respectively, indicating that the scales have good reliability. The CFA results regarding teacher collaboration (χ2/df = 26.293,
RMSEA=0.080, CFI=0.967, and SRMR= 0.030) and teacher self-efficacy (χ2/df =25.409, RMSEA=0.079, CFI=0.966, SRMR=0.029)
showed that the models fit the data well.
Control variables include teacher gender, years of teaching, educational level, school type (public or private), school location
(urban or rural), whether teaching was their first choice as a career, and the teachers’ perception of whether the school has an effective
professional development because previous studies have indicated that teacher’s demographic information as well as professional
learning communities (Liu et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2019; Thurlings et al., 2015) are crucial factors with regard to teacher innovation.
3.3. Data analysis
This study first utilized Stata 17.0 software for data cleaning, descriptive analysis and regression analysis. Then, a structural
equation model was constructed using the software Mplus 8.3 to further explore the relationships of distributed leadership, teacher
self-efficacy, and teacher collaboration with teacher innovation. Then, the bootstrapping technique was applied to test the independent and chain mediating effects of teacher self-efficacy and teacher collaboration.
4. Results
4.1. Descriptive analysis
Table 1 displays the values of teacher innovation (both team and individual innovation), teacher collaboration, teacher selfefficacy, and distributed school leadership for Shanghai, China and compares the data for Shanghai, China, with data from other
countries, thereby indicating significant differences among these countries. Further post hoc tests indicated that team innovation in
schools in Shanghai was significantly higher than the international average (t = 20.607, p<0.001), while individual teacher innovation
in Shanghai was not significantly different from the international average (t=− 1.020, p>0.05). These findings suggest that teachers in
Shanghai exhibit a strong willingness to innovate as a team. Moreover, teacher collaboration, teacher self-efficacy, and the distributed
leadership perceived by teachers in Shanghai schools were all slightly lower than the international average, but such differences were
not significant.
Table 2 shows the correlations among variables. Distributed leadership is positively correlated with teacher collaboration, teacher
self-efficacy, and team and individual innovation. Both teacher self-efficacy and teacher collaboration are positively related to team
and individual innovation. Furthermore, there is also a significant correlation between teacher collaboration and teacher self-efficacy.
These results provide initial support for the research hypotheses.
4.2. The relationship between distributed leadership and teacher innovation
A stepwise regression based on the ordinary least squares method was applied to initially examine the relationship between
distributed leadership and teacher innovation while controlling for background variables and to examine the possible mediating role of
teacher collaboration and teacher self-efficacy in this relation. The results are shown in Table 3. Models 1 and 5 explore the effect of
distributed leadership on teacher innovation when only control variables are included, with team innovation and individual innovation being dependent variables, respectively. Models 2 and 6 and Models 3 and 7 investigate the influence of distributed leadership
on the two mediating variables (teacher collaboration and self-efficacy), with teacher collaboration and self-efficacy as the dependent
variables, respectively. Models 4 and 8 are the final full models, i.e., they analyze the influence of distributed leadership on team
innovation and individual innovation after the inclusion of mediating and control variables.

4.3. Test of the mediating effects by structural equation models
To compensate for the shortcomings of stepwise regression for mediation tests and to better control the measurement errors, this
study further adopted a structural equation model based on the bootstrap method to further verify the direct effect of distributed
leadership on teacher innovation, the separate mediating effects of collaboration and self-efficacy, and the chain mediating effect. The

model fit indicators with team innovation and individual innovation as dependent variables were χ2/df=14.061, RMSEA=0.058,

Distributed leadership had a direct or indirect influence on the two dimensions of teacher innovation through four paths (see

teacher innovation; indirect path 2, distributed leadership → teacher self-efficacy → teacher innovation; and indirect path 3,
distributed leadership → teacher collaboration → teacher self-efficacy → teacher innovation. The values (unstandardized) for the
direct effect of distributed leadership on team innovation and individual innovation were 0.314 and 0.068, respectively, which were
significant at the p<0.001 level; therefore, H1a and H1b were accepted. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the three indirect paths
did not contain zero; therefore, H2a and H2b, H3a and H3b, and H4a and H4b were accepted. The path diagrams are shown in Fig. 2.
As shown in Table 4, distributed leadership influenced team innovation more strongly through the direct path (accounting for
79.49% of the total effect), while the mediating effect accounted for only 20.51% of the total effect; significant differences were found
between the three mediation paths, among which “distributed leadership → teacher collaboration → team innovation” showed the
greatest mediating effect. For individual teacher innovation, distributed leadership promoted individual teachers’ innovative practice
more strongly through the mediation paths (accounting for 69.37% of the total effect); significant differences were found between
different mediation paths, among which “distributed leadership → teacher self-efficacy → individual innovation” had the largest
mediating effect.
4.4. Verification of the model in South Korea and Singapore
To verify the relationship between distributed leadership and teacher innovation in Asian contexts, this study applied data from
two other Asian countries (South Korea and Singapore) for testing, as these two countries are typical Asian countries that feature a
hierarchical educational structure and collectivist cultural values (Hairon & Tan, 2017; Yoo & Jang, 2022). The indicators of the model
for the Singapore sample were χ2/df=12.160, RMSEA=0.059, CFI=0.935, and SRMR=0.047 (with team innovation as the dependent
variable) and χ2/df= 9.339, RMSEA= 0.052, CFI= 0.929, and SRMR=0.054 (with individual innovation as the dependent variable).
The indicators for the model for the South Korea sample were χ2/df=9.663, RMSEA=0.055, CFI=0.953, and SRMR=0.040 (with team
innovation as the dependent variable) and χ2/df=10.404, RMSEA=0.060, CFI=0.930, and SRMR=0.044 (with individual innovation
as the dependent variable), indicating good model fit for both countries.
As shown in Fig. 3, for team innovation, all path coefficients reached a significant level (p<0.001), indicating that in South Korea
and Singapore, distributed leadership had a positive effect on team innovation and that teacher collaboration and self-efficacy played
mediating roles and teacher collaboration—self-efficacy acted as a chain mediating effect on this relationship. For individual teacher
innovation, the direct effect of distributed leadership on individual innovation was not significant in either country, but the effects of
teacher collaboration and self-efficacy were significant as an independent mediator or a chain mediator. In general, the model of the
effect of distributed leadership on teacher innovation constructed in this study largely holds in the cases of South Korea and Singapore,
further confirming the vital role of distributed leadership in teacher innovation.

5. Discussion
In the current transformational era, a teaching force with high innovative capability and practice is vital for talent cultivation and
national development. To improve teacher innovation, previous research has mainly focused on individual teachers’ psychological
characteristics, such as self-efficacy and willingness to innovate (Davies et al., 2014; Huang et al., 2019), and the greater school
environment, such as schools’ innovative climate and supporting conditions (Cai & Tang, 2021; Chou et al., 2019). Although several
scholars have recognized the importance of principal leadership in promoting teacher innovation (Hou, 2018; Howell & Hall-Merenda, 1999), the underlying mechanism, especially the dynamic interactions between structural change and active agents, has not been
fully explained, and the role of distributed leadership in schools, especially in Asian contexts, has been neglected by most studies.
Distributed leadership helps to decentralize power to teachers, thus empowering each teacher in the school and transforming the
organizational structure (Spillane, 2006). As innovation largely depends on the initiative of teachers and on structural support
(Janssen, 2003), an empowered teacher who is supported by the school structure under conditions of distributed leadership is more
likely to innovate. This study contributes in this respect by taking the context, structure and agency into account in the study of

distributed leadership and teacher innovation. Namely, the current research offers empirical evidence concerning how distributed
leadership contributes teacher innovation at both the team and individual levels by transforming the school structure and teacher

First, this study shows that the willingness of teaching teams in Shanghai junior high schools to innovate is stronger than the
international average, while the innovation practices of individual teachers are similar to the international average. The high level of
team innovation in Shanghai is closely related to both the collectivistic culture and collaborative teaching practices prevalent in China.
Collectivism has been identified as a core Chinese cultural value (Bush & Haiyan, 2000; Hofstede, 2010). In China, the group approach
is a dominating factor influencing social and working life, with collaborative working and group cohesiveness exhibiting significant
importance (Bush & Haiyan, 2000). In such a cultural context, teamwork (as opposed to individual effort) has been widely emphasised

(Hofstede, 2010), a claim which also holds true for teacher innovation.
This collectivist ideology is evident in education in the form of collective work among teachers in China based on the structured
form of teaching and research groups (jianyan zu), lesson preparation groups (baike zu) and grade groups (nianji zu). Since the late
1950s, the Ministry of Education has required each secondary school to establish a teaching and research group for each subject; such
practices have persisted until the present, with teachers of the same subject establishing a teaching and research group and teachers
focusing on the same subject in the same grade establishing a lesson preparation group (Hairon & Tan, 2017). Within such groups,
teachers work collectively to prepare course materials, discuss questions, plan and observe public lessons, and perform other teaching
and research activities on a regular basis (Wang et al., 2017). The grade group is composed of all teachers working in the same grade,
and group members work together in this group to address daily student management at the grade level. The teaching and research
groups, the grade groups and the collective teaching and research system have laid an organizational and institutional foundation for
team innovation by teachers. In the daily collective teaching and research process, the team can integrate the wisdom and strength of
each teacher to develop innovative strategies to solve problems in teaching and management.
Second, the current research finds that distributed leadership plays an important empowering role in teacher innovation, significantly promoting team and individual innovation by teachers. The higher the level of distributed leadership perceived by teachers, the
stronger their willingness to innovate as a team will be and the higher the degree of individual innovative teaching practice will be.
This effect was also statistically significant in Singapore and South Korea. The positive impact of distributed leadership on teacher
innovation does not vary with the national context, at least in the sampled Asian countries.
On the one hand, distributed leadership equally shares the power, responsibility, and benefits of leadership with school teachers,
empowering each teacher to participate in school affairs (Gronn, 2002; Leithwood et al., 2007). This point is especially important for
teachers working in a hierarchical educational structure in Asian contexts. Ironically, teachers who possess the professional capital
necessary for teaching have been placed at the lowest level of the hierarchical educational structure (Hairon & Tan, 2017; Wang,
2013). In such contexts, teachers usually lack the authority and power necessary to engage in school decision-making, which impedes
the ability to make wise and appropriate decisions for teaching and innovation improvement. However, by employing a distributed
model of leadership, the degree of external control over teachers can be reduced, while teachers’ professionalism and autonomy can be
fully respected. Teachers’ control over their own work can thus be enhanced, contributing to a less alienated and more harmonious
relationship between teachers and their work. As a result, teachers’ working motivation and initiative are improved, which is
conducive to individual teachers’ innovative practices. On the other hand, distributed leadership leads to a more collaborative and
democratic organizational structure (Leithwood et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2017), which is a preferred working environment for people
in collectivistic societies such as China. In such a school environment, teachers with a collectivism orientation are more likely to
engage in close collaboration, which helps increase the willingness to innovate and innovative practices exhibited by teaching teams
and individuals.
Third, the results further suggest that distributed leadership facilitates teacher innovation by transforming the work structure
(collaboration) and teacher agency (teacher self-efficacy) as well as by promoting dynamic interaction between structure and agency.
Specifically, both teacher collaboration (referring to a collaborative school structure) and self-efficacy (focusing on teachers’ agency)
have partial mediating effects, and teacher collaboration–self-efficacy has a chain mediating effect on the relationship between
distributed leadership and teacher innovation; additionally, these mediating effects were further verified for Singapore and South
Korea. These findings illustrate that distributed leadership not only affects teacher innovation directly but also plays an indirect role
through teacher collaboration and teacher self-efficacy.
One indirect path by which distributed leadership promotes teacher innovation is by activating teachers’ own agency. Working in a
hierarchical and bureaucratic educational structure, teachers in China and some other Asian countries suffer greatly from a lack of
authority even in their own professional areas (Lu & Smith, 2022; Wang, 2013), which reduces their self-efficacy and ultimately
hinders teaching innovation. Thus, the delegation of authority becomes vital. School leaders who employ distributed leadership
delegate great authority to teachers. As a result, teachers can be respected, empowered, and supported, and their self-efficacy can be
enhanced. The stronger teachers’ self-efficacy is, the more likely they are to engage in team and individual innovation (Cai & Tang,
2021; Cayirdag, 2017). Another indirect path focuses on transforming the hierarchical school structure into a collaborative working
environment. Distributed leadership with shared decision-making can facilitate the establishment of a democratic and supportive team
climate and promote sincere collaboration among teachers. As deep collaboration is widely emphasised in collectivistic societies,
enhanced teacher collaboration significantly contributes to team innovation. The innovation of individual teachers also benefits from
collaboration. Through collaborative learning and research, individual teachers’ teaching capacities improve. This can increase the
innovative capability of individual teachers and lay the foundation for their individual teaching innovation. The final indirect path
involves promoting structure-agency interaction, such as through the establishment of a collaborative structure to facilitate teacher
agency. A collaborative structure not only meets the needs of teachers with a collectivistic cultural orientation but also improves their
teaching capacity through teamwork. Hence, teachers themselves would feel more confident and enjoy better self-efficacy, through
which teachers’ team and individual willingness to innovate and innovative practices can be further promoted.
It should also be noted that team innovation is more influenced by the direct effect of distributed leadership, while individual
innovation is more affected by the indirect effect. This difference might be related to the scope and features of distributed leadership as
well as the Asian contexts. In reality, distributed leadership initially functions at the wider school level, as it address participation in
decision-making and the presence of a shared and collaborative culture in schools (Harris, 2008; Leithwood et al., 2007; Spillance,
2006). Team innovation, which refers to the innovation attitude of the group, depends heavily on the school environment. Driven by
the collectivistic culture and collaborative teaching practices, distributed leadership can directly provide school-level support for
group work in the context of teaching innovation. In comparison, individual innovation emphasises the innovative behaviours of

individual teachers. The ultimate ability to promote such innovation relies more on team guidance and changes in individual psychology (Huang et al., 2019; Schleicher, 2015), neither of which is a direct element of distributed leadership. Thus, the distribution of
leadership within the school tends to influence individual innovation more strongly through its indirect effect on teacher self-efficacy
and collaboration while facilitating team innovation via a more direct path.
Evidently, in an Asian context, distributed leadership influences teacher innovation by changing the school structure and teacher
agency as well as the interaction of these two factors since the distributed model of leadership can not only improve teachers’ selfefficacy but also create a democratic and supportive collaborative climate. With the team’s collective efforts, both team and individual innovation would be significantly enhanced.
6. Implications and limitations
This study found significant effects of distributed leadership on teacher innovation at both the team and individual levels, and
explored the mechanism underlying this effect in Asian contexts. Distributed leadership promotes teacher innovation by transforming
the school structure and teachers’ agency, namely, through the mediating effects of teacher collaboration and self-efficacy. These
findings provide important implications for educational administrators and school leaders to develop an innovative teaching force.
To promote teacher innovation, principal distributed leadership should be improved to better empower teachers. The school
principal should consciously update his or her leadership style, decentralize leadership power, and delegate responsibility to every
teacher. Educational administrators can help clarify the basic rules and strategies of conducting distributed leadership for the reference
of school principals. Systematic training in distributed leadership is also necessary to provide guidance for school leaders. In addition,
as the empowering role of distributed leadership on teacher innovation functions through teacher collaboration (including exchange
and cooperation and professional collaboration), a collaborative climate and a democratic and supportive teaching team should be
built. Under distributed leadership, a collaborative team and climate should be and can be more easily created. The improved
organizational structure and institutional system for teacher collaboration can contribute to achieving this goal. It should be noted that
collaboration is not only performed for its own sake; rather it should also aim to improve teachers’ agency through collaborative work
and ultimately promote teaching innovation. Moreover, innovation at both the team and individual levels should be emphasised, and
their mutual promotion must be facilitated. On the one hand, it is necessary to enhance individual teachers’ innovation capacity and
practice through training, instruction and encouragement. On the other hand, the team of teachers should consider innovation as its
important aim and achieve the innovation goal through a collaborative effort.
This study presents important findings and significant implications regarding teacher innovation; however, there are still several
limitations. First, it offers only a quantitative reflection of the relationships among distributed leadership, teacher collaboration, selfefficacy and teacher innovation. Further qualitative and mixed research is needed to generate more accurate and detailed overall
explanations. Second, due to a lack of longitudinal data and a quasi-experiment, this study is unable to further verify the accurate
causal relationship between the variables studied in this research. Future research can include a follow-up study and collect longitudinal data to obtain a more accurate causal effect of distributed leadership on teacher innovation. Third, due to data limitations, the
measurement of individual innovation can be further improved, although the current measurement is acceptable. Finally, this study
analysed data drawn from only three Asian countries, i.e., China, Singapore and South Korea; thus, the conclusions and implications of
this study might be limited to Asian countries that feature similar cultural and educational backgrounds. In the future, the research
sample can be expanded to investigate the validity of this model in other Asian countries or in Western countries.
