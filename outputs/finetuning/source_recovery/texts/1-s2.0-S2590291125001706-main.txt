A B S T R A C T
This research explores the impact of teachers’ teaching strategies on students’ learning strategies, addressing the 
common oversight of focusing on "what" to study rather than "how" to study effectively. Using qualitative 
methods, the study involved 12 student-teachers enrolled in a B.Ed. program at a higher education institute in 
Pakistan. Data were gathered through semi-structured interviews and classroom observations. To ensure credi­
bility and minimize bias, member checking and reflexivity were employed. The findings highlight a strong 
correlation between educators’ teaching strategies and students’ learning approaches, demonstrating that stu­
dents tend to adopt learning strategies that align with teaching methods and assessment practices. The study 
discusses these strategies in detail, emphasizing their implications for educators and teacher education institutes. 
It underscores the importance of considering students’ learning strategies when adopting different teaching 
approaches to foster deeper learning. Additionally, teacher education institutes should critically assess their 
teacher preparation programs to equip future educators with effective teaching strategies that promote mean­
ingful and lasting learning.
1. Introduction
Classrooms, as multi-dimensional units, encompass numerous fac­
tors that significantly impact students’ academic achievements 
(Abazao˘glu & Aztekin, 2016). Among these factors, the quality of 
teacher has consistently been identified as a major factor in determining 
the quality of learning and, consequently, the level of student achieve­
ment (Darling-Hammond, 2000; Desimone, 2009; Guskey, 2002). The 
quality of teachers, on the other hand, depends on the instructional 
strategies they use (Hattie, 2008; Marzano, 2003). In this backdrop, 
educational research has consistently sought ways to enhance students’ 
learning experiences by gauging the effectiveness of teaching strategies. 
The need to improve teaching strategies underscores the need to identify 
and implement effective teaching strategies.
A prevalent issue with teaching strategies identified through 
research is the tendency to focus on instructing students on ‘what’ to 
study, with a focus on learning outcomes neglecting the equally crucial 
aspect of teaching them ‘how’ to study (McGuire & McGuire, 2018). This 
neglect frequently leads to students depending only on cursory strategies 
of learning, which impairs their performance in a variety of courses. 
Learners can take on a "study-to-pass" mindset, which would hinder the 
growth of a sincere interest and thorough comprehension of the mate­
rial. Such superficial techniques have consequences that go beyond a 
student’s individual academic achievement. They also interfere with 
students’ overall progress and lead to an imbalance in the classroom 
dynamic (Herrmann et al., 2017). Consequently, teachers face diffi­
culties to achieve learning goals owing to such difference in under­
standing level.
In addition, educational literature is abundant with studies con­
ducted to associate teaching strategies to students’ learning outcomes. 
These studies (see, for example, Zhu & Kaiser, 2022) show a close 
relationship revealing that the more effective the teaching strategies the 
higher the learning outcomes. Limited attention has, however, been 
given to how teaching strategies influence students’ learning strategies.
In addition, teaching and learning strategies are also context- 
dependent and research on teaching strategies in different context is 
required to facilitate evidence-based practice (Hattie, 2008; Slavin, 
2012).

In this background, the main purpose of this research is to highlight

2. Literature review
Teaching skill combines behavioral, social and academic facets to 
create a learning environment that accommodates all students. Effective 
teaching is more than a simple set of teaching strategies. It transcends ‘fit 
for all’ approaches and captures a composite of methodologies that are 
unique to individual learners as well as subjects (Branch-Mueller & 
Rodger, 2022). A holistic view of effective teaching strategies has been 
presented by The Global Teaching Insights (GTI) initiative by including 
six major domains, namely, content quality, emotional support, social 
interactions, classroom management, students’ involvement, and 
assessment and feedback (Zhu & Kaiser, 2022). Each of these domains 
signifies a crucial component of effective teaching. Content quality is the 
quality of the materials and the way they are made understandable to 
the learners. Social interaction is providing students with the opportu­
nities to interact with their peers. This dimension and the emotional 
support ensure inclusive and safe learning environment. Classroom 
management facilitates those conditions that make instructions and 
learning happen smoothly. Students’ involvement shows and ensures 
student learning. Assessment and feedback guide the teaching and 
learning strategies by identifying areas for improvement and identifying 
specific strategies to address the improvement areas. Thus, effective 
teaching is a carefully managed process.
Literature suggests that the success of a teacher at classroom depends 
upon the quality of instructional strategies she uses. Poor instructional 
strategies result in failure at classroom. On the basis of rigorous analysis 
of teaching, Marzano (2003) has come to the conclusion that the “expert 
teacher has more strategies at her disposal than the ineffective teacher” 
(p.87). Effective teaching strategies such as clarity of instruction, 
cooperative learning opportunities, feedback and formative assessment 
result in enhanced student learning (Hattie, 2008). On the other hand, 
vague objectives, inconsistent feedback and poor classroom manage­
ment make teaching less successful with students ultimately leading to 
reduced student motivation and learning (Marzano, 2003).
2.1. Learning strategies
Learning strategies, the tools wielded by learners to acquire knowl­
edge, shape their educational journeys profoundly. Rooted in founda­
tional work by Marton and Saljo, the distinction between deep and 
surface learning strategies, along with the achieving approach, lays the 
groundwork for understanding how students engage with academic 
material (Phan, 2006; Yang, 2016). High-achieving students exhibit a 
versatile repertoire of learning strategies compared to their 
low-achieving peers (Dembo & Seli, 2016). They adeptly manage time, 
monitor progress, adapt their environment, and address comprehension 
gaps—demonstrating metacognitive tendencies. Self-regulated learning 
(SRL) encompasses cognitive, contextual, affective, and motivational 
factors, enabling learners to navigate their educational pathways more 
effectively (Heikkil¨a & Lonka, 2006).
However, a significant instructional challenge arises from the di­
versity of understanding levels among students within a classroom (Du 
Plessis, 2019). This diversity stems from varying backgrounds and ne­
cessitates personalized instruction, posing a considerable obstacle for 
teachers accustomed to rigid and traditional methods. Addressing these 
discrepancies demands strategies to minimize gaps in understanding 
levels, fostering self-regulated learning skills essential for students 
navigating a rapidly evolving world (Al-Bahrani, 2022). In the 
contemporary landscape, cultivating 21st-century skills becomes 
imperative for every student. Hence, students lacking in self-regulation 
and independent learning skills risk falling behind (Gorgoz & Tican, 
2020). Educators, therefore, should refine their teaching strategies using 
evidence-based outcomes to support students’ learning effectively.
2.1.1. Factors affecting learning strategies
Educational research presents three overarching categories influ­
encing students’ learning strategies: institutional, home, and student- 
related factors (Rosemala, 2008). Institutional factors encompass 
physical 
elements—teachers, 
classrooms, 
management, 
tech­
nology—and social dimensions—school ethos, fear of failure, peer in­
fluence and societal pressures. Home factors encompass financial or 
parental support, while student factors encompass gender, motivation, 
skills, interests, and personality traits (Ehrman & Oxford, 1989; Gha­
dessy, 1998; Green & Oxford, 1995).
Studies highlight the significance of various elements—subject ma­
terial, grades, teaching strategies, and notably, teachers’ feedback—in 
shaping learners’ choice and utilization of learning strategies (Rahimi, 
2008). Among these factors, the teacher’s role emerges as pivotal in 
molding students’ learning strategies (Rosemala, 2008). Teachers’ 
pedagogical content knowledge correlates significantly with students’ 
success. Moreover, teachers’ motivation and enthusiastic behaviors 
profoundly influence students’ learning outcomes (Keller et al., 2017; 
Kunter et al., 2013). Highly motivated teachers adopt effective teaching 
strategies that fosters similar motivation in their students (Kalyar et al., 
2018; Rashid & Akram, 2019).

3. Methodology
The present study is rooted in a qualitative research orientation, 
aiming to capture data in the form of words, phrases, visual represen­
tations, and thematic insights (Baxter & Jack, 2015). Adopting an 
exploratory case study approach, this research seeks to draw conclusions 
from a naturalistic inquiry, aligning with the research’s overarching 
aim. The selection of this particular case study serves the purpose of 
social research and is facilitated by the ease of acquiring willing par­
ticipants, as an instrumental case need not inherently defend its typi­
cality (Tellis, 1997). This investigation centers around a pivotal 
phenomenon: the teaching strategies that influence students’ learning 
strategies.
Table 1 represents the overall design and steps carried out during this 
research.
The context of this study was the department of education of a higher 
education institute located in Northern Sindh, Pakistan. This depart­
ment offers advanced academic programs, including BEd, MPhil, and 
PhD degrees, catering primarily to female students in a relatively 
backward region of Pakistan. The research cohort comprised 12 student- 
teachers enrolled in the second year of the B.Ed. (Hons.) program. An 
email stating the purpose and procedures of the study was sent to the 
target group. Out of around 45 students, 18 showed willingness to 
participate. Considering the manageability of data and time constraints, 
12 students who responded first were taken as final participants. We 
thanked the remaining seven students for their willingness and informed 
them of our selection criteria. In addition, two teachers teaching English 
to this cohort were also invited to participate in order to facilitate 
observations in the classroom. We obtained permission and consent 
from these teachers in order to observe their classes.
Two random groups were formed, each comprising six students. The 
major technique for gathering data from these groups were semi- 
structured interviews. An effective method for obtaining people’s com­
plex viewpoints and ideas is qualitative interviewing (Roshaidai & 
Arifin, 2018). Face to face semi-structured group interviews were used 
to promote in-depth conversations and give interviewers and in­
terviewees the opportunity to thoroughly examine the topic (McQueen 
et al., 2013). Initially, some guided semi-structured interview questions 
were framed around the domains of the conceptual framework such as 
the dominant teaching strategies the teachers use and the learning 
strategies the students use. On the basis of their responses, we probed 
deeper to elicit particular teaching strategies and learning strategies. 
Each group was interviewed once, each interview lasting 30–40 min. 
However, some members of one group were approached again as some 
of their views required further elaboration and clarification. The in­
terviews were audio taped for transcription purposes with the partici­
pants’ permission. Considering the convenience of the participants, the 
interviews were conducted in a safe and peaceful space at the university.
In addition, five semi-structured observations were conducted in 
order to understand the teaching strategies in the classroom, which 
validated and enhanced the information obtained from the participants. 
Like taking textual photographs with senses, this observational method 
sought to carefully record occurrences, behaviours, and activities in a 
social setting (Kawulich, 2005). The semi-structured observations 
mainly focused on the teaching strategies the teachers used and those 
learning strategies of students that were observable. One dominant 
learning strategy the students used, for example, was note taking. In this 
way, observations allowed the researchers to validate findings emerging 
from interviews in relation to the teaching and learning strategies.
The field notes that resulted from these observations were precisely 
converted into reflective records that showed how teaching strategies 
affected learning strategies. The interviews were transcribed when the 
data collection phase was over. All data sets, including reflections and 
interview transcripts, were systematically arranged and thoroughly 
examined using a thematic analysis method (Tellis, 1997). Initially both 
the authors coded some part of one of the interviews to ensure coding 
reliability. Then the first author led the coding. Once the coding process 
was over, the authors again get together to ensure the codes accurately 
represent the views of the participants. Revisions were made wherever 
necessary. Then the codes were thoroughly examined to look for reoc­
curring patterns and trends (Braun & Clarke, 2006). The identified 
patterns were categorized and labelled. Collaborative learning, 
hands-on activities, group discussions and random questioning were, for 
example, frequently emerging patterns. These patterns were revisited to 
classify them into broader and overarching themes. Activity-based

4. Findings
The primary aim of this research was to examine the impact of 
teachers’ teaching strategies on the learning strategies of students. The 
study commenced by exploring the predominant teaching strategies 
through interviews and observations. Subsequently, it revealed the 
learning strategies adopted by students in response to their educators’ 
teaching strategies. Moreover, the investigation delved deeper into un­
derstanding the teaching factors that significantly influence students’ 
strategies of learning, thus, identifying those teaching strategies that 
employ either a positive or negative influence on learners’ strategies of 
learning. This section starts with the dominant teaching strategies the 
teachers use. Then the learning strategies influenced by the teaching 
strategies are presented. The section ends with documenting the factors 
that influence the learning strategies. Fig. 2 below represents the 
emerging themes and the organization of this section.
4.1. Activity-based teaching strategies
The data uncovered a prevalent use of learner-centered approaches 
within the educational sphere. Group work, discussions, random ques­
tioning, and other active learning strategies were evident, alongside a 
recurring emphasis on recapitulation within classroom practices.
Group Work and Discussions. Group work and discussions were the 
dominant teaching strategies that the students experienced and articu­
lated. As a participant revealed, “I experience group work and discus­
sions as dominant in most of my classes." It sounds like the participants 
perceives group work as a prevalent or significant aspect of their aca­
demic experience across various classes. The statement also suggests 
that discussions, or assignments involving teamwork are frequently 
emphasized or used within their coursework. This could imply that 
students are engaged in collaborative efforts with peers, where learning 
and problem-solving are achieved through shared efforts and in­
teractions within a group setting. The classroom observations also 
revealed the frequent use of group work and discussions. Whenever the 
researchers visited the target classes for observations, students are 
mainly engaged in group work or discussing some topics.
Practical Work. Another participant stated that “Our teacher prefers 
practical work. For example, we were assigned a task to practically 
demonstrate our response to a situation. When we practise, we get 
involved practically in the task”. Building on this, another participant 
revealed that: 
The teacher uses hands-on teaching strategies in our class. Practically 
speaking, we engage in practical actions. Furthermore, our in­
structors assign us other repetitive tasks in which we must edit or 
rework on our completed work. This makes it possible for us to gain 
deeper knowledge.
These illustrative quotations highlight the widespread application of 
learner-centered strategies. There is a shift from traditional didactic 
teaching strategies and towards more interactive and engaging ap­
proaches, as evidenced by the emphasis on group work, collaborative 
learning, discussions, and hands-on activities. The students’ recognition 
of their practical work indicates how well hands-on activities improve 
their conceptual comprehension. It reflects a dynamic learning envi­
ronment in which students are deeply engaged with real-world appli­
cations, leading to a greater understanding of the material.
Repetition. Repetition is mentioned as a way to ensure

and interactive. However, there were also references to certain lecture- 
based teaching strategies. As one of the participants stated, “most of the 
time our teachers engage us in activities, be it presentation, discussion or 
other group activity. However, occasionally they also use lectures or 
explain concepts through slides”. This view was supported by observa­
tion as some teachers were observed lecturing or giving presentations. It 
may be because some concepts require input from teachers before stu­

strategy all the time, it may become boring for students.
4.1.1. Notes taking and discussion as dominant learning strategies
Participants demonstrated a dependence on taking notes in response 
to these instructional tactics, especially in courses requiring theoretical 
knowledge. They employed a variety of tactics, which changed ac­
cording to the subject and the teaching strategy.
Taking Notes while Defining a Topic. Students reported taking notes 
while trying to define and comprehend a topic. As one of the participants 
said, “We try to comprehend and define the topic first and therefore, we 
frequently take notes during this process for future reference”. By using 
notes-taking as a personalized study tool, students revisit and strengthen 
their grasp of the content at their own pace. The emphasis on note taking 
to consolidate knowledge suggests that this method serves as a means to 
reinforce learning and recall information in the form of study guides 
during assignments or assessments. As a student stated, “notes-taking 
makes us give more attention to what is being done or discussed in the 
class”. In this sense, engaging in active notes-taking during class en­
hances students’ capacity for comprehension.
Taking Notes during Presentation. It was found that before engaging 
students in any discussion activity, students were required to attend an 
instructive presentation. As observed in a class, students were actively 
taking notes during the presentation. A student shared that “taking notes 
enables us to give more input during the discussions in which our 
teacher engages us after the lecture”. Thus, notes-taking serves to foster 
active listening, facilitate retention, and augment the overall compre­
hension of the topic under discussion. During class discussions, students 
are encouraged to share their knowledge and explain the material in 
their own words. This method helps students process the course material 
actively rather than just absorbing it.
Discussion as a Learning Strategy. When it comes to the subjects 
that are more theoretical, students prefer and use discussion as a 
learning strategy. Highlighting the importance of this strategy, one of 
the participants stated that, "I prefer discussions in theoretical subjects 
and it allows me to share my understanding with others". This suggests 
that conversations allow interactions with others and sharing of ideas 
that foster a deeper understanding and internationalization of the con­
cepts. “Many of our confusions are clarified when we discuss the con­
cepts in groups or in pairs”, a student revealed. The participants believed 
that when they converse, they listen to different point of views, over­
come their confusions and collectively create sense of the material. 
Coupled with enhancing understanding of the concepts, as students 
engage in conversations, they process and synthesize information that 
leads to developing critical thinking skills. As observed during the 
whole-class discussion, students were actively sharing their views and 
trying to overcome the confusions related to the concept under discus­
sion. Consequently, discussions provide the students with the opportu­
nity and environment to develop a deeper understanding of the 
concepts. Majority of the students interviewed for this research were 
found realizing the importance of discussion in the process of teaching 
and learning.
Students relying on notes taking in response to the teaching strate­
gies of the teachers suggest that students use different approaches of 
learning in response to the different strategies of teaching. When the 
students are expected to demonstrate their learning in some situation 
such as presentation, discussion and responding to questions posed by 
teachers, they depend on note taking to be prepared for such situations. 
They prefer discussion in theoretical subjects as it helps them overcome 
their conceptual confusions through conversing with others. These 
adaptive behaviours show that students use learning strategies on the 
basis of the teaching strategies. In the following section, we provide 
further details on the factors affecting students’ learning strategies.
4.1.2. Factors affecting learning strategies
Students identified a variety of factors that affect their learning 
strategies. These strategies include teaching strategies, assessment 
practices, teachers’ cooperation and flexibility. These factors determine 
students learning strategies.
Lectures. Although, there were limited reference to lectures, even 
though they were educational, they occasionally made students bored or 
uninterested. "I have so many confusions and questions to clarify. Lec­
tures do not give me opportunity to clarify these. When my questions go 
unanswered, I start feeling anxious and disinterested," a participant 
stated. “I study less as a result of this misunderstanding, which hinders 
my chances of passing the course”, she added. This representative quote 
implies that typical lectures led by teachers are ineffective to clarify 
confusions and to encourage participation and engagement from the 
students. However, some teachers encourage participation during lec­
tures through questioning technique. More students were engaged when 
lectures were interactive and involved questions by the teachers. As a 
participant stated, "Questions encourage active participation, which 
improves our learning, while interactive lectures offer in-depth knowl­
edge." Thus, lectures are helpful if they are brief and involve 
questioning.
Assessment. Some students reported using notes taking strategy 
during lecture provided it involved some follow up activity or if the 
conceptual understanding of students were assessed in the examina­
tions. “Taking notes increases exam scores since lecture material is 
frequently the basis for exam questions”, a participant stated. When the 
assessment practices involve conceptual understanding, students still 
depend on note taking as these notes help them to revise the concepts 
and to present them in the examination. If the examinations are memory 
driven, students barely take notes during lectures. It suggests that 
assessment practices also influence students learning strategies.
Cooperation of teachers. Students were more interested in cooper­
ative strategies that adjusts to the learning needs and level of the stu­
dents. As one of the participants stated, “Our teacher always cooperates 
with us and also realizes that we have different learning styles. He gives 
us proper time and attention”. As this representative quote shows, stu­
dents consider those teachers more influential and motivator who are

students. These are the teachers who accommodate students’ differences 
and learning styles and thus make them feel more supported. Conse­

Multimodal Teaching Strategies. Multimodal teaching strategies are 
found to be another significant motivator for effective learning. Students 
were of the view that teachers create more conducive learning envi­

and multimedia presentations. A representative quote is taken from the 
interview of a student who said, “Using just one teaching strategy such 
as lecture or reading tasks is less interesting for us. When a teacher uses a 
variety of strategies including visuals, we pay greater attention”. 
Classroom observations supported this finding as students were found 
paying greater attention and showing more interest when any of the 
above mentioned techniques were used by the teachers. It could be 
because these techniques possess greater potential to meet the learning 
needs and styles of a wide range of students. These strategies also 
encouraged students’ active participation resulting in a more interactive 
teaching and learning processes. The positive influence of using multi­
modal teaching strategies confirms the critical role of stimulating and 
engaging learning environment for students. This finding also un­
derscores the need and importance of diversifying teaching considering 
the diversity of students in today’s classrooms.
Ignoring Students’ Questions. Coupled with the absence of the 
multimodal teaching strategies, ignoring the questions that arise from 
their confusions result in their demotivation. “I always feel bad when my 
confusions are not clarified” a student showed her concern. Paying 
attention to the questions and concerns are, thus, very crucial to keep 
student interested and engaged. A student revealed that “when my 
teacher ignores my question, I feel that I have no value in front of that 
teacher”. Thus, when students are neglected or their questions are not 
properly answered, it lowers their interest and motivation for learning. 
Students prefer good relationship and effective communication in the 
classroom. “I want that my teacher should value me and communicate 
actively with me”, a student said. Effective communication for them was 
carefully listening to them, responding to their questions, acknowl­
edging their contributions and sharing feedback. Thus, when students 
feel encouraged and heard, they develop trust on teacher.
These findings validate the significant influence of teaching strate­
gies on students’ engagement. When students feel disconnected with the 
learning process, their ability to understand the given material is con­
strained. This highlights the importance of using a variety of instruc­
tional strategies considering the interest, level and needs of every 
student. Participants’ preferences for specific learning strategies are 
strongly correlated with the instructors’ pedagogical techniques, and 
what is expected of the students during exams. Boredom-induced 
cramming and disengagement are two examples of passive learning 
behaviours that students demonstrate when exposed to teacher-centered 
lectures that lack interactive features. The uneasiness and lack of in­
terest that students feel when their questions go unanswered in lectures 
emphasises how important teacher-student communication and support 
are to preserving students’ motivation and engagement.
4.1.3. Metacognitive strategies to learning
It was encouraging to reveal that the research participants used 
metacognitive skills while adapting to the changing instructional con­
ditions. A representative quote taken from the interview of a partici­
pants reflects their adaptive skills. She stated: 
I used to rely on the strategy of cramming that enabled me achieve 
good scores. But this strategy didn’t work for the conceptual ques­
tions posed at the final term exam. When I experienced this, I paid 
more attention during our class to develop a deeper understanding of 
concepts. Thus, I had to rely more on collaborative work rather than 
cramming individually.
This representative quote shows the development of metacognitive 
skills. Their effort to develop metacognition is reflected in the shift from 
cramming to deep learning. This process demonstrates that students not 
only realize their learning approaches but also adapt their approaches to 
the shifting conditions. This self-directed approach to learning is more 
effective and thoughtful. Learners having this skill and strategy are more 
likely to adapt to and survive in any challenging context especially in the 
21st century.
The research participants further highlighted that their learning 
strategies at school level depended on the teaching and assessment 
practices of the then teachers. “They expected us to cram and to 
reproduce them in the exam and therefore, we were doing accordingly”, 
another student shared her school experience. She was of the view that 
now they are no more expected to reproduce notes in the test. She 
continued: 
Now the questions are more of application and evaluation level. 
Teachers use those teaching strategies in classroom through which 
we practice application and evaluation levels of different concepts. 
Thus, we try to align our learning strategies to the teaching and 
assessment practices used by the teachers.
Thus, the data shows a close relationship between the teaching 
strategies and learning strategies. The focus on the higher order learning 
in the Blooms taxonomy by the teachers leads to deeper learning stra­
tegies by the students and vice versa. If given the opportunity, students 
possess metacognitive skills that they use to adapt to any changing and 
challenging conditions. As revealed by the participants, they used 
learning strategies on the basis of the teaching and assessment strate­
gies. During the classroom observations, it was evident that students 
were actively involved in interactive activities; listening to others 
attentively, sharing their ideas, asking questions and showing interest in 
those activities that involved problem-solving. “These are the opportu­
nities to develop a sound understanding of the concepts so that we will 
attempt our tests according to the expectations of our teachers”, a stu­
dent stated while responding to a post-observation question. The ability 
to recognize the distinctive prerequisite of an educational setting and 
modifying approaches accordingly indicates a high level of self-control 
and metacognitive skills.
This finding also highlights the importance of metacognitive skills 
for students. On the basis of the above evidence, it is safe to argue that an 
effective teaching is the one that develops metacognitive skills of stu­
dents so that they will adapt to the constantly changing and challenging 
scenarios, not only in educational context but also in their future career. 
Using a variety of teaching and assessment strategies enables students to 
reflect on their learning strategies to adapt to the variety of teaching 
strategies and thus to develop their metacognitive skills. This finding has 
a significant implication for teachers and teacher education programs.
5. Discussion
Teachers exert considerable influence over students’ learning out­
comes, a relationship extensively investigated in research (Hattie, 
2012). However, existing literature has not precisely identified which 
teaching practice domains directly impact learners’ learning strategies. 
Addressing the gap, this study revealed the teaching strategies affecting 
learning strategies highlighting how teachers can enhance their prac­
tices to better support students’ learning strategies, ultimately 
improving educational outcomes.
Participants emphasized the dependency of their learning strategies 
on the teaching strategies. In this regard, previous studies have cited 
various influential factors, such as subject material, grading, teaching 
strategy, teacher attitude, and notably, teachers’ feedback (Jena, 2024; 
Rahimi, 2008). This study supported the previous studies that the 
teaching strategies play a crucial role in shaping the learning strategies 
that students employ (Biggs et al., 2022). When teachers use active 
teaching strategies that require student participation, such as

discussions, problem-solving tasks, or group work, they foster an envi­
ronment where students are encouraged to adopt similar active learning 
strategies. This multifaceted approach also helps minimize the gap in 
understanding levels among students (Hattie, 2012). When students are 
expected to produce or participate in specific learning activities, they 
often engage in preparatory tasks such as note-taking (Voyer, et al., 
2022). Note-taking helps students organize and retain information, 
which is crucial for active engagement. By collecting and structuring 
ideas through note-taking, students are better prepared to participate in 
discussions and apply their knowledge in practical situations.
In contrast, traditional teaching methods, which typically involve 
lectures and passive absorption of information, often result in students 
remaining passive. The passive teaching methods, such as lecturing 
without interactive elements, are less effective in promoting active 
student engagement and deep learning. Students are less likely to 
participate actively if they are not given clear expectations or opportu­
nities to engage with the material. Their motivation wanes when 
teachers overlook queries and feedback, resulting in a superficial 
approach to learning focused solely on passing exams. In addition, 
employing a single method widens the gap among students’ learning 
levels (Robinson & Aronica, 2009).
The need and ability to adapt to the changing instructional and 
assessment practices is another interesting and important insight 
emerging from this study. Students reflect on their learning strategies 
vis-`a-vis the teaching strategies, and align their learning approaches to 
the dominant teaching strategies. This insight highlights the importance 
of metacognitive skills – knowledge of one’s own cognitive process or 
thinking about thinking. The more one is good at metacognitive skills 
the more one can adapt to any changing and challenging scenarios 
(Stanton et al., 2021). This skill is particularly important for the learners 
of the 21st century where the only certainty is its uncertainty. Only those 
individuals who possess metacognitive skills can survive and succeed in 
the uncertain times and situations. Focusing more on the development of 
metacognitive skills, therefore, seems a timely strategy to prepare our 
students for the uncertain times. It is surprising, however, to note that 
the existing literature is remarkably silent about the role of metacog­
nition in students’ learning strategies. Attending to this area would be of 
significant benefit to teachers and students for its potential contribution 
to self-organization skills and enhanced learning outcomes.
Thus, these findings and insights have significant implications for 
educators and teacher education institutes. Educators must realize that 
the learning strategies of students depend upon the teaching strategies 
the educators use. To facilitate learning strategies having potential for 
deeper and visible learning, educators have to use more engaging ac­
tivities. Since learning is a life-long phenomenon, the learning strategies 
students develop during their studies will have longer impact on their 
life and career. Developing metacognitive strategies to learning, there­
fore, is one of the most important aspects of effective teaching. This skill, 
if developed during formal studies, will enable students to learn on an 
ongoing basis using self-directed learning approaches. In addition, 
teacher education institute should consider the influence of teaching 
strategies on not only learning outcomes but also learning strategies of 
students. Teacher education curricula, therefore, should promote active 
and engaging teaching strategies considering the learning strategies of 
students. Lastly, assessment practices, as evident from this research, also 
drive learning strategies of students. Assessment practices that assess 
deeper learning will lead to more effective learning strategies compared 
to the practices that check memorization. Educators and teacher edu­
cation institutes must also consider this important factor in their 
practices.
Thus, the study adds significant insights to the existing literature on 
how teaching strategies develop students’ metacognitive skills, an 
underexplored area in the context of higher education. By focusing on 
relatively backward region, it provides critical insights into the unique 
approaches of teachers and learners. The study emphasises the interplay 
between teaching and learning, showcasing how effective pedagogical 
methods can shape and enhance students’ approaches to learning. These 
insights are gained through semi-structured interviews and classroom 
observations, which provide rich, real-world data that is often over­
looked in quantitative studies. This method particularly helped to 
investigate the dynamics of teaching and learning in the real context. 
Beyond its empirical contributions, the study enriches theoretical 
frameworks by highlighting those teaching strategies that contribute 
more to adopting effective learning strategies. Moreover, its findings can 
directly inform teacher training programs, policy reforms, and resource 
allocation, empowering educators to adopt impactful strategies tailored 
to the levels and needs of learners in the contemporary world. Conse­
quently, the research is both unique and significant in relation to its 
contribution to the existing field of teaching and learning processes in 
higher education.
6. Conclusion
This study highlights the substantial impact of teaching strategies on 
students’ learning strategies. Active teaching strategies, such as discus­
sions, problem-solving tasks, and group work, create an environment 
where students are encouraged to engage actively with the material. 
This active engagement is further supported by preparatory tasks like 
note-taking, which helps students organize and retain information, 
thereby enhancing their participation in discussions and practical ap­
plications. Clear expectations set by educators play a crucial role in 
influencing students’ approach to learning. When students are expected 
to actively participate and are given interactive tasks, they are more 
likely to adopt effective learning strategies. Conversely, traditional 
teaching methods that rely heavily on passive lectures without inter­
active components tend to result in less student engagement and 
learning. Therefore, integrating active teaching strategies and setting 
high expectations for student involvement can significantly improve 
learning outcomes and foster a more engaging educational experience.
Since these findings are based on the experience of only 12 BEd level 
students in one of the higher education institutes in Pakistan, they may 
not be generalized to other systems within and beyond the country. In 
addition, similar to other qualitative research, there were certain other 
limitations and challenges which might have affected our data collection 
and interpretation. Since qualitative research carefully elicit meaning 
from each words the participants use, it was quite challenging to 
interpret some words. For example, participants were using the words 
‘strategies’, ‘practices’ and ‘pedagogies’ interchangeability which 
created some confusion for the researchers mainly because, in educa­
tional literature, these words connote different meanings. In addition, 
student-teachers were very reluctant to openly comment on the teaching 
strategies of their teachers especially when such strategies were not that 
much helpful to enhance their learning. The researchers also acknowl­
edge the possible biasness in interview questions and especially in the 
probing questions which might have been influenced by researchers’ 
preference to teaching strategies and learning strategies. Lastly, the in­
terviews were supported by only five observations of classroom teaching 
and learning processes. Greater number of observations could have 
provided deeper insights into the phenomenon.
On the basis of these limitation, future studies may be conducted on a 
larger scale preferably supported by quantitative data to understand and 
validate the dominant teaching strategies in other higher education in­
stitutes and how those strategies affect the learning strategies of students 
studying in those institutes. In this way, more wide-ranging evidence- 
based findings can be revealed and publicized for the consideration of 
policy makers and teacher education institutes.
