The OECD Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) is the largest international survey of 
teachers and principals. By collecting internationally comparable information, it ensures their voices are 
represented in education policy making. 
TALIS relies solely on self-reports, which reflect perceptions but may be shaped by social and cultural 
context. Therefore, cross-country comparisons should be interpreted with caution. 
This note presents findings based on responses collected in 2024 from lower secondary teachers and 
principals. It only comments on statistically significant differences or changes.

• 
In Slovenia, 36% of teachers work in schools where the share of students whose first language is

• 
The share of teachers who feel they can adapt their teaching to the cultural diversity of students 
“quite a bit” or “a lot” is 42% (lower than the OECD average: 63%), and the share of those who can 
ensure that students with different cultural or ethnic backgrounds work together “quite a bit” or “a 
lot” is 77% (higher than the OECD average: 74%). 
Special education needs 
• 
The share of teachers who teach in schools where more than 10% of students have special 
education needs is 48% (similar to the OECD average: 46%). The share of teachers in these 
schools increased by 21 percentage points between 2018 and 2024. To note, in certain countries, 
the term has shifted from special education needs to learning support needs. This needs to be 
considered when examining trend data on special education needs in schools. 
• 
The share of teachers who feel they can design learning tasks to accommodate students with 
special education needs “quite a bit” or “a lot” is 61% (similar to the OECD average: 62%), while 
the share of those reporting that they can work jointly with other professionals and staff to teach 
students with special education needs in the classroom “quite a bit” or “a lot” is 72% (similar to the 
OECD average: 72%). 
Social and emotional skills 
• 
Social and emotional skills are vital for academic, professional, health, and societal outcomes, 
making it essential to understand teachers’ competencies and confidence in teaching them. In 
Slovenia, 76% of teachers feel they can support students’ social and emotional learning “quite a 
bit” or “a lot” (higher than the OECD average: 73%), and 91% report being comfortable providing 
instruction on social and emotional skills to students (higher than the OECD average: 86%). 
• 
67% of teachers report “frequently” or “always” developing students’ skills in understanding their 
own emotions, thoughts or behaviour (similar to the OECD average: 68%), and 74% report that 
they “frequently” or “always” focus on developing student skills in empathising with others (lower 
than the OECD average: 82%). 
Technology 
• 
Many education systems were forced to use online or hybrid learning during the COVID-19 
pandemic, and some systems have maintained those methods. In Slovenia, 10% of teachers work 
in schools where at least one lesson was taught as hybrid or on line in the last month (lower than 
the OECD average: 16%). 
• 
35% of teachers report having used artificial intelligence (AI) in their work (similar to the OECD 
average: 36%). Teachers tend to use AI to efficiently learn about and summarise a topic (75%), 
generate lesson plans or activities (65%), and help students practice new skills in real-life scenarios 
(48%). The least frequent use of AI is to support students with special education needs (26%), 
automatically adjust the difficulty of lesson materials according to students’ learning needs (25%), 
and review data on student participation or performance (23%). 
• 
Among teachers who report that they have not used AI in their teaching in the 12 months prior to 
the survey, 69% report that they do not have the knowledge and skills to teach using AI (lower than 
the OECD average: 75%) and 20% say that their schools lack the infrastructure to use AI (lower 
than the OECD average: 37%).

Initial teacher education 
• 
In Slovenia, 62% of recent graduates (teachers who have completed their initial teacher education 
in the five years prior to the survey) “agree” or “strongly agree” that the quality of their initial 
education was high overall (lower than the OECD average: 75%). 
• 
The following shares of recent graduates report that their initial teacher education prepared them 
well for: subject content (66%), general pedagogy (38%), teaching in a multicultural or multilingual 
setting (11%), using digital resources and tools for teaching (48%), and supporting students’ social 
and emotional development (24%). In 2024, the share of recent graduates feeling prepared “quite 
a bit” or “a lot” for subject content was 25 percentage points lower compared to 2018. For general 
pedagogy, it was 39 percentage points lower compared to 2018. 
Induction and mentoring 
• 
Among teachers who have recently joined their current school (within five years prior to the survey), 
66% report having taken part in either formal or informal induction in their school (lower than the 
OECD average: 72%). Rates of participation in any induction programme (whether formal or 
informal) have increased by 29 percentage points between 2018 and 2024 (the OECD average 
increased by 31 percentage points). 
• 
13% of novice teachers (with up to five years of teaching experience) have an assigned mentor 
(lower than the OECD average: 26%), and 83% work in schools offering some mentoring 
programmes (similar to the OECD average: 81%). Between 2018 and 2024, the share of novice 
teachers with an assigned mentor increased by 7 percentage points.

Continuous professional learning 
• 
43% of teachers report that the professional learning activities they participated in during the 12 
months preceding the survey had a positive impact on their teaching (lower than the OECD 
average: 55%). The share of novice teachers who find professional learning impactful is similar to 
what experienced teachers report. 
• 
The areas for which teachers most commonly report high level of professional learning needs 
include: classroom management for student behaviour (24%), methods for supporting students’ 
social and emotional learning (21%), and student assessment practices (19%). The areas most 
commonly indicated by novice teachers in relation to high level of professional learning needs are: 
classroom management for student behaviour (30%), student assessment practices (23%), and 
methods for supporting students’ social and emotional learning (23%). 
• 
Teachers most commonly identify the following barriers to engaging in professional learning: 
professional learning conflicts with work schedule (67%), not having time due to other commitments 
or responsibilities (64%), and professional learning is too expensive (59%). The barriers that novice 
teachers are most likely to report as preventing them from participating in professional learning 
include: not having time due to other commitments or responsibilities (77%), professional learning 
conflicts with work schedule (62%), and professional learning is too expensive (60%).

collaborative professional learning did not change.

Contracts and hours 
• 
In Slovenia, 88% of teachers have a permanent contract (higher than the OECD average: 81%). 
Between 2018 and 2024, the share of teachers with a permanent contract decreased by 
3 percentage points. Novice teachers are more likely to have a fixed-term contract than their 
experienced peers. The share of teachers who are employed on a fixed-term contract while 
reporting job security as highly important is 5% (lower than the OECD average: 9%). 
• 
9% of teachers are employed part-time (working up to 90% of full-time hours) (lower than the OECD 
average: 19%). Since 2018, the share of teachers working part-time has not changed. Novice 
teachers are more likely to be employed part-time than their experienced colleagues. The share of 
teachers who work part-time but do not consider job flexibility highly important is 3% (lower than 
the OECD average: 7%). 
• 
73% of teachers “agree” or “strongly agree” that they are satisfied with their terms of employment 
(barring salaries) (higher than the OECD average: 68%). Teachers’ satisfaction with their terms of 
employment (barring salaries) has decreased by 4 percentage points since 2018.

Salary 
• 
33% of teachers “agree” or “strongly agree” that they are satisfied with their salaries (lower than

not change.

Sources of stress 
• 
In Slovenia, the most commonly reported sources of stress are: having too much administrative 
work to do (63%), addressing parent or guardian concerns (51%), and maintaining classroom

Working hours 
• 
Full-time teachers report that their total working hours per week is 40.5 hours (similar to the OECD 
average: 41). Teachers’ total working hours have not changed since 2018. 
• 
Full-time teachers report spending 23 hours per week on teaching (similar to the OECD average: 
22.7). Teachers’ time spent on teaching has increased by 3 hours since 2018. 
• 
Full-time teachers report spending 9.3 hours per week on preparing lessons (higher than the OECD 
average: 7.4). The time spent on preparing lessons has not changed since 2018. 
• 
Full-time teachers report spending 4.8 hours per week on marking and correcting of student work 
(similar to the OECD average: 4.6). Teachers’ time spent on marking and correcting of student 
work has increased by 1.2 hours since 2018.

Full-time teachers report spending 4.5 hours per week on administrative work (higher than the

Classroom management and student behaviour 
• 
86% of teachers report that in a randomly chosen class the share of students is at least 1% in more 
than one of the following categories: students with behavioural problems; students with difficulties 
understanding the language of instruction; students with special education needs (higher than the 
OECD average: 77%). Novice teachers are equally likely to report teaching such diverse classes 
as their experienced peers. 
• 
18% of teachers report losing quite a lot of time because students interrupt the lessons (similar to 
the OECD average: 18%) and spending 14% of class time on keeping order in the classroom (lower 
than the OECD average 15%). Between 2018 and 2024, the share of class time spent on keeping 
order increased by 3 percentage points (the OECD average increased by 2 percentage points). 
Appraisal 
• 
The most commonly reported appraisal methods (as reported by principals) are: observations of 
classroom teaching (100%), students’ external results (90%), and school-based and classroom-
based results (88%). 
• 
The most frequently reported consequences of appraisal include: discussing with the teacher 
measures to remedy any weaknesses in teaching (82%), developing a development/training plan 
(51%), and appointing a mentor to help the teacher improve his/her teaching (19%).

Achieving lesson aims 
• 
In Slovenia, 38% of teachers report meeting their lesson aims “quite a bit” or “a lot” across all seven 
areas – teaching clarity, cognitive activation, feedback, support for consolidation, adapting teaching 
to the different needs of students, supporting students’ social and emotional learning, classroom 
management – covered by TALIS (lower than the OECD average: 44%). Among teachers’ lesson 
goals, helping students to manage their own emotions as well as their thoughts and behaviour 
(61%) is the least likely to be achieved. 
• 
The share of novice teachers (with up to five years of teaching experience) who report fulfilling 
lesson aims across all seven areas covered by TALIS (28%) is 14 percentage points lower 
compared to that of experienced teachers (41%). 
Well-being 
• 
19% of teachers experience stress “a lot” in their work (similar to the OECD average: 19%); 8% 
say their job negatively impacts their mental health “a lot” (lower than the OECD average: 10%), 
and 7% report that it negatively impacts their physical health “a lot” (similar to the OECD average: 
8%). Between 2018 and 2024, the share of teachers experiencing stress “a lot” in their work did 
not change. 
• 
Teachers under age 30 are equally likely to report feeling stressed “a lot” as their colleagues aged 
50 and above. 
Satisfaction and retention 
• 
The share of teachers who report that, all in all, they are satisfied with their jobs is 85% (lower than 
the OECD average: 89%). The share of teachers satisfied with their jobs has decreased by 
5 percentage points since 2018. 
• 
Teachers in rural schools tend to be equally satisfied with their jobs as their peers in urban schools. 
• 
16% of teachers under age 30 express their intention of leaving teaching within the next five years 
(similar to the OECD average: 20%). This share has not changed since 2018.
