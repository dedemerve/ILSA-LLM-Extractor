A B S T R A C T
Dialogic teaching is an important pedagogical approach in which science and mathematics preservice teachers 
(PSTs) should develop competency and fluency. Generative artificial intelligence (AI)-powered virtual reality 
simulation holds the potential to increase dialogic teaching practice for PSTs. This multi-case study involved 34 
PSTs participating in an AI-powered practice of inquiry-based approach in simulated teaching, coupled with field 
observations and semi-structured interviews to gather PSTs’ perceptions and experiences of the learning process. 
The findings of the study illuminate the potential of AI-powered simulation for teacher education and generate 
insights that inform the design of AI-powered virtual students to support and facilitate interactive teacher 
learning.
1. Introduction
Preparing teachers, particularly preservice teachers (PSTs), to prac­
tice dialogic teaching is a major goal for contemporary science and 
mathematics teacher education (McNeill & Pimentel, 2010; Xie & Lin, 
2025). Effective science and math teaching centers on engaging students 
in discussion and argumentation beyond disseminating an established 
body of knowledge (NCTM, 2000; NGSS Lead States, 2013; NRC, 2012). 
Scaffolding and orchestrating productive talk through questioning and 
negotiation, or dialogic teaching, will prompt students to explicate their 
reasoning, learn from and critique others’ thinking, and develop their 
critical thinking skills (Alexander, 2008; Mercer & Littleton, 2007; 
Soysal, 2023). Participating in productive discourse in the classroom 
enhances students’ explanatory rigor, conceptual understanding, and 
learning outcomes (Colley & Windschitl, 2016; Grinath & Southerland, 
2019; Tao & Chen, 2024; Xie & Lin, 2025). For example, a recent study 
(Soysal, 2023) found that students’ talk productivity increases when 
teachers invite them to discuss or volunteer their ideas and ask 
open-ended questions. Despite the documented benefits, practicing 
dialogic teaching is complex, challenging, and rarely observed in 
classrooms (Banilower et al., 2018; Edmondson & Choudhry, 2018; 
Sedova et al., 2014). PSTs, whose learning experiences as students are 
mostly teacher-centered and authoritative, struggle to learn and practice 
this approach (Kang & Windschitl, 2018; Yigit-Gencten et al., 2024). As 
such, research is needed to explore how PSTs can gain confidence and 
mastery in practicing dialogic teaching.
Virtual simulation, which involves imitating and representing real- 
life experiences in virtual environments, is a widely used technique 
for skill learning and development across disciplines (Pan et al., 2006). 
Virtual simulation-based learning has seen increasing adoption in pre­
service teacher education as it provides a low-stakes environment for 
preservice professionals to practice teaching (Dalinger et al., 2020; Ke 
et al., 2020). Practicing with simulated environments empowers PSTs to 
act on their thoughts and practice pedagogical approaches without 
real-life consequences (Cobb, 2007; Muir et al., 2013). Role-playing 
with virtual students, either peer- or actor-puppeteered, enables PSTs 
to practice microteaching and discussion orchestration in a contextual­
ized and experiential setting, supporting the transition between active 
participation and reflection—processes found to be essential for teacher 
learning (Dalinger et al., 2020; Dieker et al., 2014; Ke & Xu, 2020; 
Rhemer et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025a).
The advent of generative AI-powered virtual students, enabled by 
recent advancements in large language models, has introduced a new 
dimension to teacher preparation by providing more authentic and

Importantly, PSTs’ experiences and learning gains are strongly 
correlated with the perceived usefulness of the proposed learning 
experience. Their perceptions and experiences influence how the 
introduced technology is used and, consequently, the effectiveness of the 
technology-supported learning program (Mikeska et al., 2023; Parr,

crucial for developing effective and personalized professional learning, 
and is fundamental to optimizing AI-powered teacher learning appli­

teaching simulations, there is limited understanding of how PSTs 
perceive and make sense of emergent, AI-powered teaching simulations, 
and how AI-powered virtual students shape PSTs’ practice experiences. 
To bridge the gap, the aim of the study is to delve into PSTs’ experiences 
of teaching AI-powered virtual students in a virtual simulation, and to 
yield implications and lessons learned for both teacher education and 
AI-powered learning.
Specifically, this study is guided by the following research question: 
What are science and mathematics PSTs’ perceptions and experiences of 
practicing dialogic teaching with AI-powered virtual students in the 
virtual simulation?
2. Literature review
2.1. Dialogic teaching in science and mathematics classrooms
Classroom talk in traditional science and mathematics classrooms 
has at times been characterized by a monologic pattern, where teachers 
transmit knowledge through lectures, and students are passive re­
cipients (Banilower et al., 2018). While efficient in content delivery, 
such a monologic pattern serves to uphold teachers’ authority in the 
classroom with a focus on teacher-led talk and limits students’ mean­
ingful contributions (Lemke, 1990). Recognizing the importance of 
student talk for cognitive development, there has long been a call for 
students’ active participation in discussion and argumentation in 
learning science and mathematics concepts and practices (Colley & 
Windschitl, 2016; Mercer & Littleton, 2007; Windschitl et al., 2012). 
Dialogic teaching, correspondingly, arises as a pedagogical approach 
that actively elicits students’ ideas, presses for evidence-based expla­
nations, and engages students in discourse-rich sensemaking activities 
(Alexander, 2020; McNeill & Pimentel, 2010). There is evidence that 
dialogic teaching promotes not only disciplinary learning but also 
learner adaptation and inclusion (Alexander, 2020; Grinath & South­
erland, 2019; Miller et al., 2018; Tao & Chen, 2024). Specifically, dia­
logic teaching is characterized by teachers’ constant use of open-ended 
questions and follow-up prompts, which encourage and support students 
to elaborate and evolve their ideas (Colley & Windschitl, 2016). Dialogic 
talk moves, therefore, transfer authority to students, engaging them in 
perspective-taking and intellectual talk with peers, which helps to 
improve rigor in their discourse (Colley & Windschitl, 2016; Grinath & 
Southerland, 2019; Soysal, 2023). Recent meta-analyses have found a 
significant association between teachers’ dialogic talk with students’ 
engagement and learning outcomes (Tao & Chen, 2024; Xie & Lin, 
2025). Meanwhile, teachers who practice dialogic teaching seek to 
create a supportive and inclusive environment where everyone is 
encouraged to participate, and their contribution is valued––including 
those at risk or disadvantaged (Hennessy et al., 2023; Miller et al., 
2018).
Teachers play pivotal roles in creating such a discourse-rich learning 
environment: they must allow space for and be supportive of student 
talk and employ productive discourse such as probing questions to 
maintain meaningful dialogue (Sedova, 2014). However, novice teach­
ers often struggle to notice, elicit, analyze, and build on student talk that 
emerges in diverse classrooms (Edmondson & Choudhry, 2018; Sedova 
et al., 2014; Teo, 2016). Research has found that a significant shift from 
traditional to dialogic teaching approaches is challenging (Martin & 
Hand, 2009). Novice teachers need opportunities to systematically and 
iteratively experiment with this new approach and build both a model of 
expertise and fluency that transforms dialogic teaching into an auto­
matic practice (Alexander, 2020; Martin & Hand, 2009; Osborne et al., 
2013).
2.2. Simulation-based, experiential teaching practice
Experiential learning theory (Kolb, 1984) posits that learning is 
constructed through active experimentation and a repeated cycle of 
progression. Through the lens of experiential learning, learning is 
viewed as a process where learning is negotiated and re-negotiated 
through experience (Kolb, 1984). Learners engage in concrete experi­
ences, such as clinical simulations and experiments, that support their 
observation, reflection, and discussion, as well as abstract conceptuali­
zation (Kolb, 1984). These iterative, experiential learning processes 
promote students’ engagement and motivation (Kong, 2021), while 
promoting student-centeredness and autonomy (Passarelli & Kolb, 
2023). In alignment, several studies on teacher education and 
simulation-based learning have built on the theory of experiential 
learning (e.g., Ke & Xu, 2020; Levin, 2024).
Teaching is a complex practice that is learned purposefully and 
intentionally. Effective teaching involves not only subject knowledge 
but also the skills of orchestrating instructional activities and making 
interactive decisions (Grossman et al., 2009). Teaching skills cannot be 
acquired solely from textbooks or transmitted by veteran teachers 
(Labaree, 2000); rather, they are socially constructed through experi­
ential exploration of variables in specific teaching contexts, including 
the learner and the learning environment (Rodriguez, 2012). As such, 
there has been a call to shift from a traditional emphasis on preparing 
teachers to be knowledgeable about teaching to a focus on preparing 
them to excel in the execution of the work of teaching (Ball & Forzani, 
2009). Engaging PSTs in practice-based teacher learning arises as an 
important teacher preparation approach that engages novice teachers in 
the enactment of decomposed teaching skills, emphasizing the oppor­
tunities for novice teachers to carry out the interactive work of teaching 
and critically reflect on the outcomes iteratively (Ball & Forzani, 2009; 
Grossman et al., 2009).
Virtual simulations provide opportunities for PSTs to explore 
different teaching scenarios in a recursive manner, practices that can be 
a challenge in traditional teacher preparation pedagogies with limited 
resources (Dieker et al., 2014; Ke et al., 2020). Virtual simulations have 
been developed specifically for teacher education, such as simSchool 
(Christensen et al., 2011), TeachLivE (Dieker et al., 2017), Teacher 
Moments (Thompson et al., 2018), and simulations built in Second Life 
or OpenSimulator (Ke et al., 2023). For example, TeachLivE simulated a 
classroom using a mixed-reality environment that combined a virtual 
simulation environment (i.e., a screen display) and a physical classroom. 
Teachers joined the physical room with a speaker to interact with virtual 
students and practiced classroom management or designated skills in 
customized scenarios (Dieker et al., 2017). The previous virtual simu­
lation tools have been applied to train various skills with PSTs, such as 
scientific discourse skills (Lee et al., 2024), interpersonal skills (Theelen 
et al., 2019), and general teaching skills (Ke et al., 2016). The simula­
tions support PSTs to develop proficiency in skills important in teaching 
in an in-situ and safe sandbox, and have the potential to address the

2.3. Virtual students in teaching simulations
Virtual students compose an important component in simulation- 
based learning by enabling interactions and facilitating constructive 
learning (Dai et al., 2023; Dai & Ke, 2022; Graesser, 2016). Virtual 
students generally fall into two types in teacher education. The first one 
is the virtual agents, powered by computer scripts or role-played by 
students or professional actors. Specifically, virtual agents can be pup­
peteered by peer PSTs (Rayner & Fluck, 2014), simulation specialists 
(Lee et al., 2024), and trained human interactors (Mikeska et al., 2023). 
For example, Mikeska and colleagues (2023) examined the impact of 
integrating virtual simulations in a teaching method course on PSTs’ 
ability to facilitate argumentation-based discussion. The PSTs completed 
several performance tasks when interacting with virtual students 
role-played by a human interactor. They reported that incorporating 
virtual students into the virtual simulation had a positive impact on 
PSTs’ core teaching practices. In other research approaches, virtual 
agents are computer-controlled and scripted to generate simple re­
sponses. For example, Ke et al. (2020) explored the educational affor­
dances of using simulation with scripted virtual students for teacher 
learning with graduate teaching assistants. It was found that virtual 
student-enabled simulation can engage novice teachers in the 
task-specific practice of teaching, where their performance of demon­
strative instruction and interactive classroom discourse was improved.
Leveraging emerging AI technologies (such as large language 
models), virtual students can be developed to generate diverse and 
semantically meaningful responses in context. Research on developing 
AI-powered virtual agents for preservice teacher education is just 
emerging (Dai et al., 2024; Demszky et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). For 
example, Demszky et al. (2023) designed and tested generative AI to 
improve teachers’ uptake of students’ ideas. The generative AI infused in 
teaching practices was able to create interaction opportunities that help 
teachers improve their abilities to focus on students’ contributions in 
classroom discourse and embrace student-centered pedagogy. Recently, 
Dai and colleagues (2024) deployed virtual conversational agents for 
teacher learning in a virtual simulation. They found that PSTs’ inter­
personal skills and teaching practices were facilitated by virtual agents’ 
computational humor and real-time conversations. Zhang et al. (2024)
explored the promises of leveraging AI-powered virtual students in 
improving PSTs’ responsive teaching talk moves. It was found that vir­
tual students can bring nascent resources that support PSTs to notice 
student ideas and practice dialogic talk moves.
Additionally, prior explorations of teaching simulations rely on 
either verbal or text-based interaction with virtual students, both 
yielding promising results. Verbal interaction is accomplished by human 
actors or trained specialists who wear voice modulation equipment 
behind the screen to simulate student-like voices and interaction (e.g., 
Dalinger et al., 2020; Mikeska et al., 2023). These actors receive 
extensive training so that they can provide standardized teaching sce­
narios that mirror the real-world ones. Scholars have found that teachers 
using actor-supported, verbal environments have strategic practice op­
portunities and improved self-efficacy and dialogic skills after training 
(Dalinger et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2024; Mikeska et al., 2023). Text-based 
interactions are another salient strategy for teaching simulations (e.g., 
Christensen et al., 2011; Lim et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a, 2025b). 
Similar to simulations that adopt verbal interaction, text-based in­
teractions also provide iterative opportunities for practicing diverse 
teaching skills and demonstrate the potential to improve teachers’ 
teaching competency (Zhang et al., 2025a; Ke et al., 2020). Moreover, 
participants have reported a sense of authenticity in both verbal and 
text-based interaction within teaching simulations (e.g., Dalinger et al., 
2020; Ke et al., 2020), demonstrating their potential as a useful tool for 
teacher preparation. In the current study, we employed text-based 
interactions as an intermediary interface consisting of text input and 
chat log panels (see more in section 3.2). This learning-interaction 
interface was designed to elicit active user input while necessitating 
performance of content-related thinking, which helps to scaffold 
cognitive engagement (Ke, 2017). Through the use of text input and chat 
log panels, participants can pause to think, reason with student data, and 
engage in reflexive practices in the moment, essential components for 
PSTs to practice dialogic teaching (Alexander, 2018; Sanchez & Atha­
nases, 2023).
2.4. Teachers’ perceptions and experiences of teaching simulations
Prior research has actively investigated PSTs’ perceptions and ex­
periences of teaching virtual students in virtual simulations, with most 
of them being scripted or human-puppeteered virtual agents. Existing 
research on teachers’ practicing teaching with virtual students presents 
a range of perspectives, highlighting both positive and negative ones. 
Reported positive perspectives included opportunities for skill practice, 
increased preparedness and confidence, and enhanced immersion and 
presence (Lee et al., 2024; Rayner & Fluck, 2014; Zhang et al., 2025b). 
Conversely, negative perspectives ranged from constrained and inau­
thentic interaction, and low perceived usefulness, to pressure from the 
presence of peers (Dalinger et al., 2020; Ke et al., 2016; Rayner & Fluck, 
2014). For example, Rayner and Fluck (2014) examined PSTs’ percep­
tions of teaching scripted virtual students for teacher preparation. The 
findings suggested that the experience helped the participants learn the 
diverse needs of the students, acquire classroom management skills, and 
become better prepared for future professional placement. However, the 
PSTs in the study were skeptical about the usefulness of the simulation 
for practicing teaching, as it was not the same as real-world teaching.
Additional research reported that teaching virtual students, 
compared to role playing with peers, positively facilitates PSTs’ 
emotional states. For example, Lee et al. (2024) explored teacher can­
didates’ experience of teaching virtual agents controlled by a simulation 
specialist, compared to the experience of traditional rehearsals (peer to 
peer). It was found that teacher candidates perceived teaching virtual 
agents as more engaging, and enacted more prompts to pursue students’ 
ideas. On the other hand, Dalinger et al. (2020) reported that despite the 
benefits of increased practice opportunities and perceived transfer of 
learning, there were also reported challenges associated with practicing 
teaching with human-puppeteered virtual students, including the pres­
sure of teaching with peers, inadequate preparation, and limited 
teaching scenarios available. In some studies, PSTs expressed doubt 
about the ability of scripted agents to facilitate meaningful conversa­
tions. For example, Ke and colleagues (2016) revealed that, while 
teaching virtual agents appeared to be authentic and improved their 
sense of presence, PSTs complained that teaching scripted virtual stu­
dents lacked reciprocity and context in the interaction.
To address the aforementioned challenges, a small number of studies 
have begun to explore the design and implementation of AI-powered 
virtual students for teacher education (e.g., Barrett et al., 2025a; 
Zhang et al., 2025a). However, PSTs’ perceptions and experiences of 
teaching virtual students, especially AI-powered virtual students to 
practice dialogic teaching, are largely underexplored.
3. Method
In this study, we employed a qualitative multi-case study design (Yin, 
2009) to gain fine-grained insights into PSTs’ contextualized experi­
ences with practicing teaching with AI-powered virtual students in a 
virtual simulation. Each participant’s interactions and perceived expe­
riences with teaching AI-powered virtual students, bounded by time and 
space, were considered a unique case. Detailed and in-depth data were 
collected from multiple sources of information, including recorded 
videos, observations (field notes), dialogue logs, and semi-structured 
interviews.

3.1. Participants
The participants consist of a purposive sample of 34 preservice sec­
ondary teachers recruited from multiple teacher education programs at 
a large research university in the Southeastern U.S. The participants 
were recruited based on the criteria of being PSTs in science and 
mathematics-related areas, being currently enrolled in teacher prepa­
ration programs, and being over 18 years old. In this study, we define 
PSTs as individuals who are enrolled in teacher preparation programs 
and have not yet assumed full-time, independent teaching roles. Among 
the participants, nineteen were Caucasian, five were Asian, four were 
African American, four were Hispanic, and two chose not to disclose. 
The majority of the participants were female (88 %), with nineteen 
being undergraduate students and fifteen graduate students. Sixteen 
participants’ self-reported focused subjects were Biology, nine were 
Math, seven were Science (Environmental science = 2, Earth space 
science = 2, Middle school science = 2, Neuroscience = 1), and two were 
STEM education. Seventeen had teaching-related experience (e.g., pre­
paring teaching materials and participating in practicum-based course­
work) of less than two years, four had more than two years, and twelve 
had no prior experience.
3.2. EVETeach: Simulation-based learning environment
We designed and developed Enactive Virtual Environment for 
Teaching (EVETeach) (Ke et al., 2023) with the purpose of developing 
PSTs’ ability to employ dialogic teaching practices. Drawing from the 
framework of ambitious science teaching (core discourse practices; 
Windschitl et al., 2012) and 5E inquiry-based instructional model 
(engage, explore, explain, elaborate, and evaluate; Bybee, 1990), the 
teaching tasks in the current study were built on two stages of “eliciting 
student ideas” and “building consensus” where PSTs are expected to 
actively elicit virtual students’ ideas, orchestrate discussion, and engage 
the students in scientific and mathematical reasoning and argumenta­
tion. Three virtual classrooms of Mathematics, Life Science, and Physical 
Science were designed and built in OpenSimulator, a desktop-based, 
open-source, and multiuser virtual reality platform. The virtual class­
rooms were designed to resemble a real classroom, equipped with 
interactive teaching aids and AI-powered virtual students (see Fig. 1). 
Six AI-powered virtual students were integrated into the virtual class­
room (named Karen, Linda, Thomas, Robert, John, and Michael).
EVETeach (see the workflow in Fig. 2) was developed using 
Generative Pre-trained Transformer-2 (GPT-2), a deep neural network 
model developed by OpenAI (Radford et al., 2019). EVETeach’s locali­
zation was accomplished with real classroom interactions gathered and 
transcribed from classroom recordings sourced from TIMSS (https://

EVETeach was purposefully designed to help teachers, especially 
PSTs, practice classroom dialogue and the pedagogical reasoning un­
derlying the dialogue. First, the interaction between PSTs and virtual 
students was designed to be reciprocal, which required PSTs to respond 
to student discourse back and forth rather than to engage in a didactic 
monologue (Alexander, 2008; Skidmore, 2019). That is, PST only 
received student responses when they queried students. Second, PSTs 
were expected to construct responses through the reasoning of student 
discourses and pedagogical decision-making instead of choosing from 
close-ended options from the menu, which offers personalized and 
authentic interactions (Lee & Yeo, 2022). Third, the virtual students 
were trained from real classroom data and further fine-tuned to generate 
responses that show varying levels of engagement and knowledge. The 
diversity offers possibilities to experiment with different teaching stra­
tegies and practice different talk moves (Zhang et al., 2025b; see also 
student discourse examples in Appendix A and dialogue transcript in 
Appendix C).

3.3. Procedure and data collection
Participants joined the 2-h session individually from their own 
computer with one trained human facilitator. The session was held on 
Zoom, the facilitator set up the virtual teaching simulation on their 
computers, shared their screen, and granted participants the remote 
control so that they could interact with the virtual simulation and the 
students. The session was structured in three main phases: i) orientation 
and training, ii) teaching virtual students, and iii) interviews. During 
orientation and training, the facilitator guided participants through 
several introductory videos covering the procedure, the purpose of the 
learning, and expectations. After a brief training on how to interact with 
the virtual students (e.g., notice evidence in student thinking, employ 
alternative strategies such as orchestration), participants first tested out 
teaching with one sample virtual student to familiarize themselves with 
the format. In the second phase, PSTs discussed with the facilitator the 
topic, learning goals, and potential strategies before they practiced 
teaching with the whole class. They would choose from one of the 
following topics: life science (fungi, ecosystems/life cycle), physical 
science (gas laws, pressure and density), and math (linear equation, 
graphing, and border problem). They then practiced teaching six virtual 
students in the simulation. Participants selected one focal topic for their 
teaching session first. However, if participants felt that their virtual 
students had reached a consensus on the initial topic, they could proceed 
with a second topic. In particular, participants practiced engaging the 
students and building consensus on the chosen content topics. In this 
study, the “consensus” refers to the instructional objective that PSTs 
anticipated achieving with the students for a given discussion topic (e.g., 
characteristics of fungi’s living environments and the steps involved in 
solving a quadratic equation). For example, when discussing the char­
acteristics of fungi’s living environment, PSTs guided virtual students to 
reason about features such as moisture and temperature, supporting the 
group in reaching agreement on these key aspects. The interaction be­
tween PSTs and virtual students was text-based. Participants were 
encouraged to do unprompted think aloud to externalize their thinking 
and decision-making process (Van Someren et al., 1994). The facilitator 
asked questions only occasionally probing into their thinking.
Following the practice sessions, participants received a semi- 
structured interview in the third phase, where the facilitator posed 
questions on 1) their experiences of teaching virtual students in the 
simulation, 2) their perceptions of virtual students, 3) their perceptions 
of the simulation, and 4) their feedback and suggestions for improving 
the overall experience. The facilitator asked follow-up questions to elicit 
additional details and clarify interesting discussion points during the 
interview. The sessions were audio- and video-recorded and transcribed 
verbatim. Field (observation) notes on participants’ experiences were 
recorded. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. A 
total of 34 sessions were recorded.
3.4. Data analysis
A qualitative, inductive coding process was employed to capture the 
varied aspects of PSTs’ perspectives (Blandford et al., 2016). We trian­
gulated the session transcripts, field notes, and semi-structured in­
terviews to achieve a comprehensive and nuanced depiction of those 
perspectives as well as establish trustworthiness (Patton, 1999). We first 
conducted a within-case thematic analysis, identifying salient codes per 
case (i.e., the participants), then compared cases to identify common 
patterns and variations. These cross-case comparisons informed the final 
set of thematic categories presented in the findings. For each case, we 
treated the interview, session transcripts, and field notes as interrelated 
data sources within a single case. As suggested by Patton (1999), data 
from different sources might yield different results as “different types of 
inquiry are sensitive to different real-world nuances” (p. 1193). There­
fore, codes for a case were considered present if they emerged in any 
source, and we used cross-source comparisons to deepen our 
interpretation, not to acquire code repetition across sources. This 
approach reflects our aim to build a deep insight into each case, as 
triangulation has been suggested as an important approach to 
strengthen validity (Yin, 2013). The first and third authors immersed 
themselves in the data and met regularly throughout the coding process 
to debrief and validate the findings for credibility. Specifically, sys­
tematic coding and thematic analysis were conducted. We first 
employed open coding to generate initial codes. Open coding was con­
ducted at the phrase, sentence, or paragraph level, depending on the 
associated semantic meanings and how participants conveyed their 
ideas. We labelled the data with initial codes and then collected the 
excerpts for all codes into a data table to better visualize the salient 
patterns and variations. We then conducted an axial coding process to 
further refine, align, and synthesize initial codes across cases into 
emergent themes. Finally, a selective coding procedure was performed 
to ensure the most meaningful and salient themes were selected and 
integrated (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). The final themes were identified 
based on relevance to our research question, the richness and depth of 
supporting evidence, and, where applicable, their prevalence across 
participants. To ensure consistency in qualitative coding, two re­
searchers independently coded 15 % of the dataset (n = 5). Initial 
interrater reliability was calculated using Cohen’s kappa, which yielded 
a value of κ = 0.82, indicating satisfactory interrater reliability (Landis 
& Koch, 1977). Throughout the coding process, we regularly engaged in 
peer debriefing meetings to provide an external check on our analytic 
process, where the emerging codes, themes, and interpretations were 
discussed and refined to achieve 100 % interrater agreement. The coding 
framework and examples are in Appendix B. For data reporting in the 
following section, data excerpts from think aloud, interviews, field 
notes, and dialogue logs are indicated by the parenthetical marker such 
as “(thinking aloud in teaching simulation).” For example, “(thinking 
aloud in the teaching simulation)” signifies that PSTs were verbalizing 
their thinking process during the teaching session, and the marker 
“(turning to a particular student)” or “(typing to the whole class)” sig­
nifies that the PST was typing their discourse to this student. Also, the 
number and percentage used represent the number of participants and 
the percentage of participants who shared the same code (e.g., n = 10, 
10/34 = 29 %).
4. Findings
Thematic analysis revealed four qualitative themes regarding PSTs’ 
perceptions and experiences in the virtual simulation: 1) Virtual teach­
ing simulation facilitates differing dynamics of engagement in core 
teaching practices, 2) PSTs navigate authenticities and variances in 
teaching AI-powered virtual students, 3) Virtual teaching simulation 
acts as a pedagogical sandbox, 4) Virtual teaching simulation works as a 
supplement to teacher preparation. The results highlighted the potential 
of AI-powered simulation for practicing dialogic teaching from PSTs’ 
perceptions and experiences (for additional context and illustrative ex­
amples, see also PSTs’ overall dialogic talk moves in Appendix D) and 
shed light on the lessons learned for future AI-powered learning design 
and implementation.
4.1. Virtual teaching simulation facilitates differing dynamics of 
engagement in core teaching practices
As observed, the interaction between PSTs and AI-powered virtual 
students in the simulation unfolded as a learning experience charac­
terized by a blend of satisfaction and frustration that engaged PSTs in 
the fruitful exploration of solving teaching problems in distinct but 
important ways.
More than half of the PSTs (n = 26, 76 %) reported that teaching in 
the simulation was “interesting,” “enjoyable,” and “engaging.” For 
example, one participant praised the back-and-forth interaction with 
virtual students, “It was good. It’s really fun and engaging to talk through it

comment underscored participants’ general positive attitude toward the 
simulation-based learning experience.
Amidst positive perceptions, challenges and frustration were also 
observed and self-reported. Most participants (n = 27, 79 %) noted the 
experience was “challenging,” “difficult,” and “frustrating.” PSTs were 
expected to reason about students’ diverse ideas and build on them, yet 
it was challenging to enact. Here is an example from P14’s class (Gas

P14: (Typing to the whole class) Talking about cooling and water, what

Participants also reported why they felt frustrated, with the lack of 
pedagogical resources being the most prevalent. Many mentioned that 
frustration arose from a lack of pedagogical or epistemic resources to 
make instant decisions when virtual students deviated from the topic (n 
= 11), gave one-word responses or remained silent (n = 6), and provided 
circular responses (n = 1). For example, P3 grappled with challenges 
when students veered off-topic, expressing, “I was getting really frustrated 
in the middle of it. I was like, how do I word this? How do I do that? And 
whenever they would say the complete opposite thing, I would be like, I don’t 
even know how to move on from this point” (thinking aloud in the teaching 
simulation). P12 commented on the challenge of managing silence, “It’s 
kind of hard when nobody speaks because then you don’t know what to do 
next” (thinking aloud in the teaching simulation). Four teachers high­
lighted difficulties in helping virtual students connect or synthesize 
different ideas (n = 4). P15 expressed, “There are some different percep­
tions, different ideas that are separated and should be connected somehow, 
which I can say, I struggle a little bit to help them to make con­
nections”(interview). Two PSTs experienced struggles and attributed 
them to student disengagement (n = 1) or low understanding (n = 1). 
Two others found challenges in the absence of student-to-student 
interaction (n = 1) and the experience of lag (n = 1). One participant 
reported struggling due to limited content knowledge (n = 1). Apart 
from teaching and pedagogy-related challenges, four participants found 
the simulation challenging due to a lack of VR experience or uncertainty 
about what to expect (n = 4).
At the same time, PSTs acknowledged that the challenging situations 
in the simulation were similar to those in real classrooms. “I would say 
it’s hard. And whether or not that’s an artifact of the simulation or just in 
general, it’s hard to get students to participate in the way that you want. But 
that is accurate of what happens in a classroom because you’ll get students 
who don’t pay attention” (P11, interview). P11’s comment was echoed by 
P34, who remarked, “I spent a lot of time, you know, trying to get them 
engaged first off, and so that was a challenge for me. And that’s a challenge 
for most teachers, you know, trying to start off teaching a subject and trying to 
get them engaged” (P34, interview).
As observed, frustration experienced by participants did not detract 
from their engagement; rather, it served as a catalyst for cognitive 
engagement and problem solving in teaching practices. For example, 
P26 was observed to ask different questions, and to different students to 
elicit ideas (Gas Laws, dialogue logs): 
P26: Why do you think the tank imploded after the worker let out the 
steam?
Karen: Because the steam got cold.
P26: Good job, Karen. Why did the steam get cold?
Karen: Because it froze. Because it froze inside the tanker.
P26: Who can tell me why the air froze in the tanker?
Thomas: Because it froze in.
P26: (Thinking aloud in the teaching simulation) So, well, I mean, so 
the students didn’t quite get it right? I don’t know what I’m supposed to

(Turning to Linda) Linda, why do you think that the tank imploded?
Linda: Because the steam got cold.
P26: Why did the steam get cold?
Linda: Because the steam got cold.
P26: Can anyone tell us why the steam got cold?
Thomas: Because it was cold outside.
P26: Good answer, Thomas. How does the cold temperature outside cause 
the tanker to collapse?
Later in the interview, P26 noted she was motivated to engage more 
when frustrated, “I just feel like I was frustrated because I felt like they 
weren’t understanding what I was saying. But it made me want to keep 
trying” (interview). Similarly, P33 was observed to keep asking different 
questions to elicit students’ ideas. He shared, “[I feel it’s] kind of stuck, 
but I’m trying to still steer the class in the right direction.”
However, it was also observed that PSTs tended to be less dialogic 
when they were frustrated or did not know how to respond. This was 
observed when students were confused or did not provide the “correct 
answer.” As P2 commented, “But once I saw that, we weren’t really getting 
anywhere. I thought it would be good to start being more direct with them 
[lecturing]” (interview). Lecture-based teaching was also observed 
when students were silent or gave short answers to PSTs’ questions. Here 
is an example from P29’s class (Fungi, dialogue logs), 
P29: Most fungi are found in moist areas that have little to no light. Would 
you say mushrooms are fungi?
Linda: No.

Michael: (silent)
P29: That is incorrect. Mushrooms are actually the most common form of 
fungi that are visible to the human eye.

and closely resembling real-life teaching, nuanced differences between 
AI-empowered and real-world teaching were highlighted. Most partici­
pants (n = 26, 76 %) viewed teaching virtual students as authentic or 
realistic, as it is similar to teaching real students. As P4 expressed in the 
interview, “I like its human-like and everything. For sure, they have their 
own opinions. They ask all sorts of weird questions. Sometimes they get off 
topic, and that’s okay, like they kind of exhibit the same kind of personality

PSTs’ active engagement. They were observed to be excited about stu­

appraisal, which demonstrated PSTs’ efforts to create a supportive and 
intellectually safe environment. Here is an example from P3’s class (Gas

Participants observed differences among virtual students in terms of 
understanding levels (n = 12), learning pace (n = 2), and ways of

In contrast, five participants regarded practicing teaching with vir­
tual students as lacking realism (n = 5). Their concerns stem from per­
ceptions of inauthentic interaction (n = 4) and low visual quality (n =
1). Notably, three participants pointed out that, while the virtual stu­
dents could engage in disciplinary discussion, they lacked common

have you ever blown up a balloon?” Thomas said “no.” P20 found this

Participants proposed ways to enhance realism, suggesting 
increasing interactivity by allowing multiple responses at a time and 
student-to-student interaction. P11 expressed her preference to practice 
“wait time,” an important feature of dialogic teaching, stating, “I would, 
at this point, be silent and just wait for another student to respond. I probably 
wouldn’t say anything. Why? Because I would want the students to talk 
rather than me talking” (thinking aloud in the teaching simulation). Three 
participants complained that typing responses felt unnatural and 
preferred verbal communication with virtual students. Lastly, three 
participants requested more visual cues, like reading facial expressions, 
to gauge students’ engagement.
4.3. Virtual teaching simulation acts as a pedagogical sandbox
It was observed that AI-powered simulation creates a low-pressure 
environment wherein PSTs practice and experiment with diverse 
teaching strategies without real-life consequences. For example, P2 
expressed her feelings about teaching virtual students in such an envi­
ronment, “It definitely didn’t make me nervous, the same way, like a real 
classroom. I remember my first few times going into the classroom, I was 
really nervous, and this did not make me nervous. It’s low stake, so you’re not 
worried about ruining a child’s education (P2, interview).”
In such an environment, PSTs were allowed thinking time to reason 
about student responses before responding to them, tending to affirm 
student ideas by building on their thinking or using their language. P21 
emphasized how the simulation afforded time for deeper analysis, 
explaining, “The ability to think creatively as a teacher is important, and 
problem-solving in the moment. Like teachers have to do this within 
milliseconds in the normal class, they don’t have the luxury of analyzing 
every exchange like this (interview).” As an example, P17 closely read 
students’ responses and used the words (e.g., eat, nutrients) that stu­
dents used to help them make connections (Fungi, dialogue logs), 
P17: Right! We eat, but do fungi eat? Food keeps us alive, but what keeps 
the fungi alive?
John: The fungi eat it.
P17: What do fungi need to eat like we do?
Robert: To gain some of the nutrients it might need.
P17: Did everyone hear what Robert said about nutrients? Where do you 
think fungi get nutrients from?
Virtual students’ responses served as naturalistic stimuli that 
prompted PSTs to engage in self-paced rehearsals that involved a 
repeated cycle of interpreting, responding, and reflecting. During teaching 
sessions, participants actively interpreted or analyzed students’ input to 
identify their current understanding. Consider this example (Gas Laws, 
dialogue logs), 
John: I think the steam is trying to escape.
P21: How does the steam try to escape?
John: How does it crush when you heat it?
P21: (Thinking aloud in the teaching simulation) I don’t think he 
understands the process yet, and so he’s asking a question, and then the 
question is about the container itself, maybe not the steam, but about the 
container that he can see. Because the team is something you can’t see, so 
…
This excerpt illustrates that students’ responses prompted P21 to 
engage in real-time interpretation of student reasoning, distinguishing 
between observable and unobservable phenomena to guide their peda­
gogical decisions.
PSTs also interpreted virtual students’ learning states (e.g., engaged, 
disengaged, confused) based on their responses. For example, P33’s 
session interpreted students’ responses, saying, “I feel as if she may not 
be engaged due to her silence and then her other off-topic answers to the 
problems.” Similarly, in another session, P18 analyzed a student’s 
reasoning in real time: 
P18: Have you seen fungi too, Robert?

These analytic processes appeared to pave the way for PSTs’ peda­

‘yeah,’ or just like ‘silence,’ I kind of just start thinking, well, what should I do

P17: Can you share what you discussed about tanker car crush?

In this scenario, students demonstrated various levels of 
understanding, where some students held alternative conceptions (e.g., 
“more pressure in the tank”) and some shared emerging ideas on the 
phenomenon (e.g., “explosion” and “dispersed”). P17 was observed to 
elicit ideas from different students, build on these ideas, and redirect the 
conversation to observations when students focused more on abstract 
ideas (e.g., “molecules” and “pressure”).
It was observed that PSTs felt unsure of what decisions to make, yet 
they enjoyed the flexibility to experiment and figure things out. As 
observed in P21’s session, who was trying to help students build up the 
connection between boiled water and steam, “(Thinking aloud in the 
teaching simulation) Okay. So let me ask the question again.” (Turning to

teaching simulation). “I don’t know, boils? Maybe he doesn’t know what

In another teaching session, when the student Karen was disengaged and 
off-topic, P6 reflected, “Maybe I should keep talking to her until she kind of 
gets it, or maybe I’ll say, what do you mean by that, because maybe she has 
the reasoning, I guess?” These episodes illustrate how the virtual envi­
ronment allowed the PST to consider and test multiple possible re­
sponses without the immediate risk of negative consequences, 
encouraging experimentation and reflective decision-making.
Meanwhile, virtual students’ responses acted as naturalistic feed­
back, prompting PSTs to reflect on the impact of their pedagogical de­
cisions. Moments of critical reflection on one’s teaching practices were 
observed. For instance, in P33’s class, Linda, a virtual student, remained 
silent in response to P33’s question; P33 evaluated his talk moves, 
commenting, “Maybe she isn’t accustomed to the vocabulary that I was 
using” (think aloud in the teaching simulation). The facilitator also noted 
in another session, “In the reflection, she [P9] recalled responses such as 
‘what do you think?’ or ‘hotdogs,’ she discussed she just ignored them, she 
thought it was the simulation malfunctions, but when she also thought that it 
was purposefully designed as a challenge, of which ‘I probably didn’t do a 
good job’” (P9, field note). These critical reflections enabled PSTs to 
evaluate and refine their decisions, as well as consider alternative 
approaches.
4.4. Virtual teaching simulation works as a supplement to teacher 
preparation
Nearly all participants (n = 32, 94 %) agreed that the simulation is a 
“useful” or “helpful” learning tool for teachers to practice dialogic 
teaching. Particularly, participants found it valuable for practicing and 
learning dialogic teaching skills (n = 20), as well as fostering patience (n 
= 3). P14 commented on how the experience bridged theory and prac­
tice, “We’ve learned about questioning as a pedagogy, like we’ve talked 
about these practices. And then here. Go in practice, and you don’t even have 
to worry about like talking yourself in circles with time constraint” (P14, 
interview). Another participant highlighted the value of practice in 
facilitating and orchestrating group discussion, “I think this would be 
really helpful in teacher education. I’m so interested in it, I think it’s fasci­
nating. It could be used for this very reason to help preservice teachers un­
derstand how to respond to students and how to facilitate a group discussion” 
(P15, interview). Moreover, eleven participants (n = 11) stated that 
simulation-based teaching practice can boost “confidence” and “pre­
paredness.” “I think it’s very helpful for like boosting your confidence and 
helping you to adjust while you’re teaching and a lot of that stuff that only 
happens with time” (P1, interview).
PSTs were observed practicing dialogic teaching skills or strategies 
for engaging and interacting with diverse students. One participant 
shared her experience, “This simulation is a great opportunity to practice 
what you want to practice. For me, I purposely started this simulation. I 
wanted to try this simulation to test out my responsive, you know, teaching 
moves” (P16, interview). Another PST shared, “It [the experience] taught 
me more about a different perspective in the classroom. Like, I’ve always been 
the student. So it was nice to be on the other side, and like, learn more about 
how teachers have to be quick on their feet and think about stuff. It taught me

a lot of strategies that I could use in the future, like repeating myself and

Most participants (n = 29, 85 %) acknowledged benefiting from the

questioning skills to effectively elicit student ideas. These strategies 
include narrowing or breaking down questions, employing questions as 
assessments, and posing direct questions to individual students. In P8’s 
session, the facilitator observed, “She developed strategies to use more 
specific questions, questioning skills, more than one question in one turn 
taking, and observing students” (P8, field note). P11 learned how to ask 
specific questions, saying, “It’s important to be very specific with what

how you ask the question is very important” (P11, interview). Additionally, 
twelve participants noted the experience heightened their awareness of 
the importance of content knowledge (n = 12), thus motivating them to 
deepen their understanding of the subject matter to more effectively 
scaffold student learning. A few participants showed a trend to be more 
patient (n = 4), and recognized the challenges inherent in dialogic 
teaching (n = 5). For example, P12 noted she learned about the difficulty 
of teaching, saying, “I think this was like a really good experience on, you 
know, starting out how to teach. It shows how hard it is to teach, and how 
hard it can be to ask the right questions” (interview). Participants also 
gained insights into facilitating and scaffolding group discussion (n = 5), 
and adapting teaching to accommodate diverse learners (n = 4). For 
example, P28 shared her experience of adapting to different students, “I 
learned that, with certain of the students, I had to go back and re-question 
them to get them on track.” However, five participants stated that they 
did not learn anything (n = 5). For example, when asked if the experi­
ence helped her learn any new strategies, P1 responded negatively. She 
expressed a preference for interacting with real students, stating, “I think 
that there are some features that could be added to the program, so that it 
could be used. But otherwise, I think that practice with real students is gonna 
prepare someone best for working with real students” (interview).
Despite the potential and benefits reported, participants identified 
areas for improvement to enhance learning outcomes. Ten participants 
advocated for the development or provision of a lesson plan beforehand 
(n = 10). Another ten participants expressed a desire for more support or 
guidance in navigating challenging situations (n = 10). For example, 
P26 proposed providing teachers with a list of questions, saying, “What 
about, and this is just a suggestion … maybe at least, to start at the beginning, 
there are questions the teachers choose from. And you know, it kind of gives 
them different options” (interview). More importantly, appropriate 
guidance and support are crucial to optimizing the effectiveness of uti­
lizing the tool. As the facilitator noted, “The interactions do not ensure that 
teachers learned productive and ambitious practices … with PSTs, they will 
try a more efficient route [lecturing], instead of building knowledge” (P4, 
field notes).
5. Discussion
Employing a qualitative, multi-case study, the purpose of the study 
was to examine PSTs’ perceptions and experiences of practicing dialogic 
teaching with AI-powered virtual students. We found that participants 
generally held positive perceptions towards AI-powered simulated 
teaching. Teaching virtual students with varying knowledge and 
engagement levels and distinct learning states was perceived to be 
authentic and similar to teaching real students. The real-time, diverse 
responses generated by AI-powered virtual students prompted PSTs to 
attend to student thinking, engaging them in an iterative cycle of 
interpretation, decision making, and reflection (or core dialogic teach­
ing practices). Furthermore, AI-powered virtual simulation creates a 
practice-based and safe environment for PSTs to purposefully enact and 
practice core teaching practices, thereby offering opportunities to 
practice and reflect on their pedagogical decision making.
The study findings highlight the complex dynamics of engagement 
and frustration in the context of AI-powered simulation for PSTs. While 
most PSTs found the experience engaging and enjoyable, they also faced 
intermittent challenges and frustration. For most participants, frustra­
tion arose from a scarcity of teaching resources to address different 
student discourses such as off-task comments or silence. On the other 
hand, PSTs found that the challenges they faced in the simulation were 
similar to those encountered in real classrooms. These challenging mo­
ments enable PSTs to manage frustration during problem solving and 
help them recognize the complex nature of dialogic teaching (Sedova 
et al., 2014; Teo, 2016). When teachers transfer authority by encour­
aging more student talk, classroom discussions are likely to take un­
predictable directions (Maskiewicz, 2015). This unpredictability, while 
challenging, mirrors the dynamic nature of real-world teaching sce­
narios. It is worth noting, however, that while frustration may facilitate 
PSTs’ cognitive engagement in practicing teaching, it may also lead to 
monologic teaching. In-situ learning support could be designed and 
implemented to scaffold their learning. Future studies could explore 
different types of learning support and how learning support should be 
implemented to maximize learning outcomes and sustain engagement.
The observed strong engagement in PSTs when teaching AI-powered 
virtual students partly stems from their perceived authenticity and re­
alism of these virtual students. Most PSTs believed that AI-powered 
virtual students are realistic as they demonstrate diverse knowledge, 
engagement and activity levels, personalities, and ways of learning, as 
those in real classrooms. These findings are promising because the level 
of authenticity and realism learners perceive positively impacts their 
learning experience and gains (Theelen et al., 2019). In contrast, some 
other PSTs found the AI-powered virtual students lacking realism, citing 
unnatural interactions, a lack of common sense, and low visual quality. 
Participants suggested potential areas for improvement, including 
enabling student-to-student interaction when one student is silent. 
These findings highlight future research directions.
Similar to prior research (Dalinger et al., 2020; Dieker et al., 2014; Ke 
et al., 2016; Mikeska et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025a), the study found 
that AI-powered simulation provides a low-stakes, purposefully 
designed environment for PSTs, allowing them to practice and experi­
ment with a variety of teaching strategies without consequences. The 
incorporation of AI-powered virtual students, portraying varying levels 
of knowledge and engagement in their verbal interactions, facilitated 
PSTs to adjust their teaching pace, pay attention to student responses, 
and engage in a cyclical process of interpretation, response, and 
reflection, core dialogic teaching practices. The variety of teaching 
scenarios available in the simulation provided opportunities for PSTs to 
engage in in-the-moment pedagogical reasoning, as evidenced through 
their verbal reflections during the think aloud, demonstrating its po­
tential to enhance the autonomy and capacity of PSTs. These findings 
are consistent with prior research suggesting that virtual students 
prompted PSTs to attend more to student thinking, engaging them in 
dynamic pedagogical reasoning and decision making (Ke et al., 2020; 
Mikeska et al., 2023; Rayner & Fluck, 2014; Zhang et al., 2025a). The 
finding suggests that AI-powered virtual students can provide real-time 
and contextualized feedback for PSTs. Such naturalistic feedback en­
courages PSTs to adopt adaptive teaching methods—teaching strategies 
that build on student ideas during instruction (Parsons et al., 2018), key 
characteristics of an effective and dialogic teacher (Darling-Hammond & 
Bransford, 2007; Fairbanks et al., 2010).
Almost all PSTs perceived the simulation with AI-powered virtual

students as a useful learning tool that engaged them in critical in­
teractions to build teaching competency. AI-powered teacher learning 
provided opportunities for PSTs to iteratively practice the interactive 
process of teaching, thereby holding potential for the development and 
refinement of their dialogic teaching skills. Most PSTs reported that they 
practiced and acquired essential dialogic teaching skills, such as posing 
effective questions. This finding is in line with prior research that un­

virtual students and experiment with various pedagogical approaches 
(Dai et al., 2024; Dalgarno et al., 2016; Dieker et al., 2014). It is also 
consistent with the report of previous studies that AI-powered virtual 
students provide interactive, authentic practice opportunities to nurture 
PSTs’ teaching skills (Barrett et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2025b). 
Notably, participants’ emphasis on the simulation’s role in bridging the 
gap between theory and practice resonates with the literature on expe­
riential learning (Kolb, 1984), where learning is conceived as a process 
that unfolds through experience.
However, the current AI-powered teaching simulation does not 
universally appeal to or benefit PSTs. To enhance learning experiences, 
areas for improvement have been identified, including the need for 
deeper content knowledge, the provision of a lesson plan beforehand, 
additional support or guidance in navigating challenging situations, and 
alternative interaction modes in addition to text-based interaction. Four 
future design suggestions were derived. First, PSTs may be challenged to 
a certain degree to engage in cognitive reasoning. Frustration will 
naturally occur in purposeful quests that involve complex cognitive 
processes of problem solving in teaching. However, frustration and 
challenges should be properly managed to sustain PSTs’ engagement. 
Second, authenticity or perceived realism in the teaching simulation is 
important for teacher engagement. Using real classroom interaction data 
to train virtual students will enable knowledge-rich and reciprocal 
conversations, while daily-life knowledge from a commonsense knowl­
edge base is still needed to drive authentic student responses. Third, 
participants should be encouraged to develop a mini lesson plan, out­
lining the concepts and activities in the learning session. Fourth, PSTs 
usually find it challenging to respond to diverse student-generated 
questions or comments in a dialogic way. It is crucial that PSTs 
receive appropriate pretraining and/or adaptive scaffolding to fully 
leverage the affordances of AI-powered simulation because the use of 
simulation alone does not guarantee an effective and fruitful exploration 
of teaching practices. Rigorous empirical studies are warranted to 
further investigate how the simulation-based learning processes can be 
structured to better promote the target teaching skills development. 
Further, while PSTs generally described the teaching experience in AI- 
powered simulation as authentic and low-stakes, some noted that the 
text-based interaction was less intuitive or less favorable than the verbal 
one. Future AI-powered teaching simulations should integrate verbal 
interactions that simulate student heterogeneity in voice profiles and 
communication styles. Future studies can examine how AI-powered, 
multimodal teacher-student interactions impact teachers’ sense of 
authenticity and practices.
5.1. Limitations
Several potential limitations should be noted. First, this study 
employed a qualitative method to explore PSTs’ perceptions and expe­
riences; the findings should be considered as potential trends rather than 
conclusive evidence. Future studies are encouraged to employ a mixed- 
methods or experimental research design to enhance our understanding 
of the impact of AI-powered learning experiences. Second, this study 
only investigated the perceptions and experiences from the perspective 
of PSTs. The perspectives of teacher educators, stakeholders, and in- 
service teachers could also be explored in the future to promote a bet­
ter understanding of designing and using virtual simulation in teacher 
education. Third, our participants consist of predominantly preservice 
science and mathematics teachers. The EVETeach virtual student agent 
was trained and localized with classroom recordings in science and 
mathematics. Therefore, the results might not apply to other subject 
areas. Fourth, while the think-aloud protocol was mainly unprompted 
and offered valuable insight into PSTs’ real-time reasoning and decision- 
making during the simulation, it may have affected their perception of 
the simulation’s realism. Lastly, while we included illustrative examples 
of dialogic teaching moves in appendix, a systematic analysis of these 
moves was beyond the scope of the current study. Future research could 
build on these preliminary observations by systematically coding and 
analyzing PSTs’ use of dialogic strategies, providing deeper insight into 
how interactions with virtual students support the development of dia­
logic teaching skills.
5.2. Implications
This study contributes to a better understanding of how PSTs expe­
rience, perceive, and make sense of AI-powered teaching simulation. 
The findings suggested that generative AI-powered simulation can be a 
valuable tool for PSTs to practice dialogic skills with perceived 
authenticity, presenting diverse teaching scenarios and iterative prac­
tices. Since we aim to provide authentic practice experiences, it is worth 
noting that teaching is complex, and dialogic teaching is “an intellectual 
adventure, full of unexpected challenges” (Fisher, 2007, p. 629). As our 
study has found, PSTs would lecture or tell students answers when they 
were frustrated and did not know how to proceed with appropriate 
dialogic talk moves. Therefore, future research should explore the 
design of in-situ adaptive supports in AI-powered teaching simulations 
that can support PSTs in navigating challenging scenarios and engage 
them productively in an iterative cycle of interpretation, decision 
making, and reflection.
6. Conclusion
Dialogic teaching is an important pedagogical approach for science 
and mathematics education. The study examined science and mathe­
matics PSTs’ perceptions and experiences of practicing dialogic teaching 
with generative AI-powered virtual students. The results indicated that 
the real-time interaction enabled in virtual teaching simulation is both 
engaging and challenging for PSTs, which helped them recognize the 
complex nature of dialogic teaching and pointed to future research 
areas. The virtual teaching simulation simulated a wide range of 
teaching scenarios for PSTs to enact in-the-moment decision making and 
reflection, holding the potential to support them in gaining fluency and 
capacity in dialogic teaching. While most PSTs perceived the experience 
to be authentic, several found the virtual students lacking realism. 
Collectively, the results of the study illuminate the potential of inte­
grating AI-powered virtual teaching simulation in PSTs’ preparation. 
They provide valuable insights that can inform the design of AI-powered 
learning experiences.
