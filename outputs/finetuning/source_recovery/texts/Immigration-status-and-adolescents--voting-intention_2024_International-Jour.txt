This article investigates whether policy efforts to integrate immigrants into society can have
interpretive effects and motivate adolescents of immigrant origin in European countries to engage
in the host country’s political system. We find that countries with more inclusive integration
policies tend to have smaller gaps in voting intention between adolescents of immigrant origin
and those not of immigrant origin, holding constant an array of individual and family politically
relevant resources. Specifically, differences across countries in the inclusiveness of education
policies appear to be a strong predictor of the magnitude of voting intention gaps between firstgeneration and native adolescents. In the case of second-generation vs. native gaps in voting
intention, the inclusiveness of citizenship acquisition policies emerges as a key predictor. In these
times of strong polarization and anti-immigration discourse across Europe, our findings are
particularly relevant for policymakers and researchers in their efforts to address what might be
done to help promote the long-term political integration of immigrants and their descendants.

1. Introduction
As the flow and settlement of immigrants continues across Europe, the study of the political integration of immigrants has gained
attention from European scholars. Discussing the political integration of immigrants, Martiniello (2006) distinguishes four dimensions,
including acquiring rights, subjective identification with the receiving society, adopting democratic values, and eventually political
participation. Based on such dimensions, a growing body of research had begun to address the last aspect, finding that immigrants
engage in various forms of citizen participation ranging from voting to collective mobilization, even in contexts where they do not have
rights to vote (see De Rooij, 2012; Pettinicchio & de Vries, 2017).
As such, prior research has focused on political participation among adult immigrants, but we know less about the extent to which
and the ways in which European adolescents of immigrant origin in earlier stages of life form their political orientations. While in the

2006; Wong et al., 2018), European scholars have recently addressed the issue (Dollmann, 2022; Eckstein et al., 2015; Hochman &

anti-immigration discourse across Europe, a central focus of immigration debates is the willingness of immigrants and their descendants to fully integrate into the host country. Given that the future of the host country significantly relies on how well immigrant
adolescents adapt to their role as citizens, it is essential to explore how young European immigrants develop their orientations to
become politically engaged in the host country’s political sphere during their formative years.
Another limitation of the existing literature is that most research has focused on the determinants of the political engagement of
immigrant adolescents at the individual level and single-nation cases. This lack of a cross-national perspective is in sharp contrast to
the literature on adult immigrants, which has investigated the role of macro contexts (e.g., different conceptions of national identity
and their crystallization in integration and citizenship policies) in shaping immigrants’ political behavior (see Ireland, 1994; Koopmans, Statham (2000)). However, since not all European countries may achieve successful long-term integration of immigrant adolescents into the political sphere, it is important to examine the conditions under which immigrant adolescents become politically
engaged and the role that the host country’s macro context plays in this respect.
In this paper based on and expanding theories of policy feedback (A. L. Campbell, 2003, 2012; Mettler & Soss, 2004), we focus on
voting intention of European adolescents of immigrant origin in a cross-national perspective, and view immigrant adolescents’
engagement in the host country’s political realm as a process that is shaped by the policy contexts surrounding immigrant integration
(Filindra & Manatschal, 2020; Simonsen, 2021). Utilizing the 2009 International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS),
combined with the 2011 Migrant Integration Policy Index (MIPEX), we focus on a sample comprised of 17 European countries and
investigate whether the degree of inclusiveness/exclusiveness of immigrant integration policy is systematically related to
cross-national differences in voting intention between first-generation and native adolescents, and between second-generation and
native adolescents. This study’s focus on voting intention among adolescents of immigrant origin, particularly in comparison to their
native counterparts, is motivated by the connection between voting intention in early adolescence and their actual voting in adulthood
(D. E. Campbell, 2008; Neimi & Junn, 1998). As previous work has identified immigrants’ electoral participation as a key integration
metric (Munro, 2008; Tillie, 2004), differences in voting intention between adolescents of immigrants and native-born parents would
be significant predictors of the long-term prospect of the political integration of children of immigrant families in host countries.
2. Literature review and hypotheses
2.1. Situating immigrant/native adolescent differences in political engagement: A resource perspective
While much scholarly attention has focused on the socioeconomic integration of immigrant adolescents, their political integration
is another important aspect indicating the degree of integration into the host country. Not only does one of the crucial components of
the host country’s future hinge on how well these immigrant adolescents will adapt to their role as citizens, but their entrance into the
political realm creates opportunities for better representation for the previously marginalized groups (Junn, 1999). In this respect,
several US-based studies have examined various aspects of immigrant adolescents’ political engagement, including political knowledge, the likelihood of voting (Torney-Purta et al., 2006; Torney-Purta et al., 2007), political efficacy (Wong et al., 2018), perceptions
of the government’s responsiveness to citizens (Wray-Lake et al., 2008), volunteerism (Ishizawa, 2014), political discussion and
attention to the media (Stepick et al., 2008). This line of research has documented that compared to their native counterparts,
immigrant adolescents show lower levels of political knowledge, efficacy, and intention to participate in electoral politics upon
reaching adulthood. However, immigrant adolescents do not lag behind their native peers in the propensity for attending to political
messages in the environment, such as at home, in the neighborhood and in the media.
Building on a resource model of citizen participation which posits that possessing politically relevant resources is an essential precondition for political engagement (Brady et al., 1995), previous work has identified resource disparities as the most important factor
that explains differences in political engagement between adolescents of immigrant and native-born families. Here, politically relevant
resources include family socioeconomic conditions, language proficiency, educational expectations, civic skills derived from
involvement in non-political institutions, among others. In most countries except for traditional immigrant countries, which select
immigrants on the basis of human capital characteristics to meet their labor market needs, family socioeconomic conditions tend to be
poorer for immigrant adolescents than their native peers (Zimmermann et al., 2000). Additionally, immigrant adolescents encounter
linguistic and cultural barriers that impede their engagement in the host country’s politics (Ramakrishnan & Espenshade, 2001). In this
resource perspective, to the extent to which immigrant adolescents are disadvantaged or advantaged in terms of possessing necessary
resources compared to their native peers, we should expect related political engagement differences between immigrant and native
adolescents.
Contrary to U.S. scholarship, the political engagement of European adolescents of immigrant families has not yet received the
amount of attention it merits. Recently, the notable influx of school-aged immigrants in Europe has produced research on the nature
and determinants of political engagement among European adolescents of immigrant origin. For example, using data from four

2.2. Cross-national variation in immigrant/native adolescent differences in political engagement: a public policy perspective
Past research relying on single-nation cases views the political engagement of immigrant adolescents as the result of individuallevel attributes. Such views, however, offer limited insights into the role of macro contexts conducive or detrimental to immigrant
adolescents’ engagement in the host country’s political system. In this study, we conceptualize immigrant adolescents’ engagement in
the host country’s political system as a multi-level process. Then, we hypothesize that the host country’s ‘context of reception’ (Portes
& Rumbaut, 2001), especially its policy context surrounding immigrant integration, can shape immigrant adolescents’ political
engagement over and above individual-level attributes.
Theories of policy feedback suggest that the design of public policy is critical to people’s sense of belonging and status, with
consequences for the political engagement of differently targeted groups (A. L. Campbell, 2003, 2012; Mettler & Soss, 2004). Specifically, policy feedback scholars argue that although public policy is a redistributive tool that allocates material resources to different
groups of citizens, its effects can also act symbolically as signals of inclusion or exclusion and have interpretive effects on both direct
targets and broader communities that share material and cultural resources (Condon et al., 2016; Filindra et al., 2011). In the case of
immigrants’ political engagement, for example, a redistributive policy that offers politically relevant resources for immigrants and
their families can incentivize them to engage politically in the host country (Simonsen, 2021); at the same time, however, the
interpretive effects of policy may be equally or even more important for shaping immigrants’ political engagement than resource
effects, as, Ingram (2007, p. 246) succinctly put it, public policy “sends powerful messages about who really matters in politics…and
whether groups with certain identities can expect to receive anything positive from government.” In this respect, from inclusive
policies that promote immigrant integration and social cohesion, immigrants and their families may receive a positive message about
their status as citizens in the host country and what they deserve from the government, which, in turn, contributes to their higher levels
of political trust (Rocha, Knoll, & Wrinkle, 2015), political efficacy (Abrego, 2011), and voter turnout (Filindra & Manatschal, 2020).
Based on and expanding policy feedback theories, the current study postulates that the host country’s policy context surrounding
immigrant integration can have interpretive effects that spillover across generations. Defined as “approaches and measures adopted by
state agencies to help immigrants integrate within the host society” (Bourhis et al., 1997, p. 370), immigrant integration policies differ
from immigration policies which concern the granting of access to the territory of a nation-state (Helbling, 2013). Within immigrant
integration policies, Penninx and Garcés-Mascareñas (2016) distinguished between three dimensions, i.e., the legal-political dimension, the socioeconomic dimension, and the cultural-religious dimension. In each of these dimensions, integration policies can range
from exclusive to inclusive. We thus assume that inclusive integration policies whereby immigrants are positively viewed constructed
based on their social value and deservingness can have strong interpretive effects and motivate adolescents of immigrant origin to
engage in the host country’s political system. More precisely, focusing on adolescents’ intention to vote in adulthood among diverse
measures of political engagement, we expect to find that in countries in which immigrants and their families benefit from inclusive
integration policies, adolescents of immigrant origin may be indistinguishable from, or even exceed those not of immigrant origin with
regard to voting intention. For instance, inclusive policies for immigrant political participation may convey the message that immigrants are members of society who can participate fully in its political system (Callens & Meuleman, 2017), increasing the likelihood to
vote among adolescents of immigrant origin. In countries in which every child has an equal right to a quality education regardless of
immigration status, adolescents of immigrant origin can get access to quality schooling, which may translate into greater intention to
vote as educational attainment is a key predictor of political engagement (Brady et al., 1995). Open citizenship acquisition policies and
extensive anti-discrimination laws also make it more likely that adolescents of immigrant origin envision themselves as active participants in the host country’s electoral process, because, in such countries, their immigration status may create only minor institutional and psychological barriers to political participation (Ariely, 2012).

We used data from the 2009 ICCS database. ICCS 2009 is a cross-national survey study, involving 38 countries that participated in

2009 and that aims to assess 8th graders’ political knowledge, attitudes and behavior. It employs a cluster sampling design in which for
each participating country, a sample of schools is randomly selected, one eighth-grade class is then randomly selected from each
school, and efforts are made to assess all students in a given class.
Among the 38 participating countries, we conducted analyses based on a sample comprised of the following 17 European countries:
Austria, Belgium (Flemish), Cyprus, Denmark, England, Estonia, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Latvia, Norway, Spain,
Slovenia, Sweden, and Switzerland. We focus our attention on countries whose datasets include appreciable numbers of immigrant
adolescents that allowed for the comparison of voting intention between adolescents of immigrant origin and those not of immigrant
origin. We set aside countries in which the total number of adolescents of immigrant origin is less than 4% of the total sample for a
given country. This is because a high number of immigrants in the sample also means a high number of immigrants in the country,
which, in turn, correlates with a policy context surrounding immigrant integration. The Netherlands, which did not meet the ICCS
sampling requirements, was also excluded from the analyses.2
3.2. Measures
Dependent Variable. Applying item response theory techniques to the data for all participating countries, the scale Voting Intention
was constructed in ICCS 2009, where higher values reflect higher levels of intention to vote in adulthood. Voting Intention was based on
questions asking respondents whether they would vote in local and national elections, and would obtain information about candidates
before voting in an election upon reaching adulthood. The measure was standardized by ICCS such that its scale is 50 with a standard
deviation of 10.
Individual-level Independent Variables. Adolescents classified as natives are those who were born in the country being surveyed and
who also had at least one parent born in the same country. First-generation adolescents are those who were not born in the country being
surveyed and whose parents were also born elsewhere. Second-generation adolescents are those who were born in the country being
surveyed but whose parents were born elsewhere.
We used the following variables to control for politically relevant resources at the individual-level: (a) parental SES, (b) educational
expectations, (c) home language, (d) religious involvement, and (e) organizational affiliations. Parental SES was measured by a composite
score, i.e., the national index of students’ socioeconomic background (NISB),3 which was scaled to have a mean of 0 and a standard
deviation of 1 among the participating countries. Adolescents’ educational expectations were measured by the approximate years of
further schooling minus the corresponding numbers of years in the target grade, i.e., the 8th grade. Home language was measured using
a dichotomous indicator (1 = using the language of the survey at home; 0 = other). Religious involvement was based on respondents’
self-reports of whether they had ever participated in religious groups, and measured using a three-point scale (1 = I have never done
this; 2 = I have done this but more than a year ago; and 3 = I have done this within the last 12 months). Organizational affiliations were
also based on respondents’ self-reports to indicate whether they had ever been involved (1 = I have never done this; 2 = I have done
this but more than a year ago; and 3 = I have done this within the last 12 months) in certain types of organizations (e.g., youth organizations affiliated with a political party or union; environmental organizations; human rights organizations; a voluntary group for
helping the community; an organization collecting money for a social cause; a cultural organization based on ethnicity; a group of
young people campaigning for an issue). We constructed an additive index of organizational affiliations by averaging adolescents’
responses to seven different organizations. We also controlled for the following two demographic characteristics: (f) family structure
and (g) gender. Growing up with single parents is found to have a negative impact on children’s voter turnout (Sandell & Plutzer, 2005).
While recent studies document the decrease or even reversal of the traditional gender gap in voting in many democracies, this decrease
is found to apply only to some types of elections (Dassonneville & Kostelka, 2021). Family structure (1 = growing up in single-parent
families; 0 = other) and gender (1 = female; 0 = male) were measured using dichotomous indicators.
Country-level Independent Variables. In this study we empirically assess the host country’s immigrant integration policies using the
MIPEX database of 2010, an indicator system developed by the British Council and the Migration Policy Group. The scale for each
index ranges from 0–100, where 0 = critically unfavorable, 1–20 = unfavorable, 21–40 = slightly unfavorable, 41–59 = halfway
favorable, 60–79 = favorable, and 80–100 = most favorable. More precisely, we focus on four policy areas: political participation,
education, citizenship acquisition, and anti-discrimination. These four areas capture the legal-political dimension (political

5. Conclusions and discussion
Motivating this article was the idea that inclusive integration policies whereby immigrants are positively viewed based on their
social value and deservingness can have interpretive, spillover effects and encourage immigrant adolescents to engage in the host
country’s political system. Thus, we expected that intention to vote in adulthood among first- and second-generation adolescents
would increase relative to their native peers when the host country’s immigrant integration policies become more inclusive, holding
constant an array of individual and family politically relevant resources. Our HLM meta-analyses supported this idea. That is, countries
with more inclusive integration policies show a smaller voting intention gap between immigrant and nonimmigrant adolescents, and in
some cases adolescents of immigrant origin are even more likely to vote in adulthood than those not of immigrant origin. Distinguishing between the sub-dimensions of integration policies, we found that a more inclusive integration policy concerning education
is a key predictor of between-country differences in first-generation/native contrasts in voting intention, whereas the inclusiveness of
citizenship acquisition emerges as a key predictor of between-country differences in second-generation/native contrasts in voting
intention. One possible explanation for this finding is that when access to quality schooling—which enables immigrants and their
descendants to become proficient in the host country’s language and acquire a basic understanding of its political system—is easier for
immigrants as a result of inclusive education policy, such policy can act symbolically as signals of inclusion and motivate first-generation adolescents to engage in the host country’s electoral politics. In comparison, since second-generation adolescents tend to be
more acculturated than first-generation adolescents, they may begin to see that receiving full legal recognition is more important to
becoming informed voters in the host country than merely having the knowledge and skills that help in navigating the political world.
Thus, inclusive integration policies for citizenship acquisition would convey a stronger message for second-generation adolescents that
they are members of the host country who can stay permanently and participate fully in its political realm, which, in turn, help envision
themselves as future participants in electoral politics.
However, this study is not without limitations. First, despite the fact that the immigration context in many of the European
countries in our sample has changed substantially over decades, we utilized the ICCS 2009 survey, from which our dependent variable
came. This limitation was due to the fact that there were only 11 European countries whose ICCS 2016 survey, which was a more
recently collected cross-national database on adolescents’ political engagement, included appreciable numbers of adolescents of
immigrant origin. As a result, we were unable to apply our multi-level modeling approach to the ICCS 2016 survey. However, we
believed that this limitation would not be much problematic, given the stability of country rankings in MIPEX scores over the years
(Ham et al., 2017). Next, our study did not further distinguish immigrant adolescents based on their origin countries as approximately
half of the countries in our sample did not provide information on their countries of origin. Given that immigrants differ across
countries of origin with respect to motivations for migration, material and human capital, and prior political experiences (Portes &
Rumbaut, 2006), it is reasonable to expect that the country of origin affects their post-migration political engagement. Although such
country of origin effects may have been partly taken into account in this study by including various resource variables in our within-country analyses, future research would benefit from utilizing more extensive data on immigrants’ countries of origin.
Another promising avenue for future research is to consider other contextual factors that may influence the political engagement of
European adolescents of immigrant origin. Recent studies show that neighborhoods of high immigrant concentrations in many European cities can also be areas of low political participation, because these areas are usually characterized by high residential poverty
and large proportions of noncitizens (Bratsberg et al., 2020). Moreover, in the face of a general trend of an increase in social inequality
and urban segregation across Europe, a growing body of literature also finds that adolescents of immigrant origin tend to reside in
inner-city neighborhoods with segregated schools, and has attributed poor learning outcomes to such segregation (Bonal et al., 2019;
Allison, 2002; Anon, 2012; Hao & Bonstead-Bruns, 1998; Ingram, 2007; Kim & Amnå, 2014; Martiniello, 2006; Penninx %
Garcés-Mascareñas, 2016; Rocha et al., 2015). Thus, it would be worthwhile for future researchers to investigate how neighborhoods’
and schools’ socioeconomic and ethnic compositions shape the expectations to become informed voters among adolescents of
immigrant origin in a number of settlement countries in Europe.
As evidenced by our findings that adolescents of immigrant origin in European countries with more open integration policies seem
more likely to think of themselves as future participants in the host country’s electoral politics, governmental supports in the form of
policy interventions have the potential to boost the political engagement of adolescents of immigrant origin. If adolescents of
immigrant origin view themselves less politically engaged in the host country, it might be less attributable to the type of immigrants
the host country attracts than to the welcome they are given. Especially in these times of strong polarization and anti-immigration
discourses across Europe (Dancygier & Margalit, 2020), our findings are relevant for policymakers and researchers in their efforts to
address what might be done to help promote the long-term political integration of immigrants and their descendants, and ultimately
the various contributions immigrants might make to the settlement country.

Appendix. Two-step approach in multi-level meta analyses
The first step of our approach (i.e., the within-country step) involved obtaining estimates of differences in voting intention (VI)
between first-generation and native adolescents (FGI vs. NI), and between second-generation and native adolescents (SGI vs. NI), for
each country. This step involved fitting a two-level multilevel model to each country’s data (i.e., students (level-1) nested within
schools (level-2)), and essentially yielded key summaries of the adolescents’ data for each country. As such we refer to it as the withincountry step of the analysis. The second step (i.e., the between-country step) entails investigating the variation in contrasts across
countries utilizing models similar to those that are used to investigate the heterogeneity in effect sizes across studies in meta-analyses,
particularly how differences across countries in the inclusiveness of their integration policies relate to differences in the magnitude of
FGI/NI VI contrasts and the magnitude of SGI/NI VI contrasts.

Advantages of the Two-step Approach versus Working with a Three-Level Multilevel Model
Using the two-step approach described above provides numerous advantages in terms of flexibility in model specification, and the
tractability of model estimation, versus employing three-level multilevel models capturing the nesting of adolescents within schools,
which, in turn, are nested in different countries.
First, by estimating FGI/NI and SGI/NI VI contrasts country by country, we are able to capture the fact that the constellation of
student-level relationships may differ appreciably from country to country. To capture this in a single three-level multilevel model
would entail treating each of the coefficients above as varying across countries, resulting in a model containing an intractably large
number of variance and covariance components.
In addition, fitting the model to each country’s data yields an estimate of the student-level and school-level variance components
for each country, thus allowing for differences in the magnitudes of these variance components across countries. Treating these
variance components as varying across countries would further increase the number of variance components that would need to be
estimated in a single three-level model.
Third, given the large number of student-level predictors specified, estimating the FGI/NI and SGI/NI VI contrasts country by
country provides some flexibility in terms of model specification. For example, we may find that certain student-level interaction terms
are not of statistical nor practical significance in a particular country. In such a situation, we could remove the interaction term from
the model for that country, thus resulting in a more parsimonious model.
