This paper analyses how receiving mentorship and serving as a mentor each relate to teacher job
satisfaction using cross-national data from the 2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey
(TALIS). A two-stage approach, employing school fixed effects and meta-analysis across 49 regions, shows a robust positive association for both mentors and mentees. The mentee effect is
consistent across settings, while mentoring effects vary more. Results highlight the importance of
institutional support to ensure mentoring benefits all participants, with implications for teacher
induction, development, and retention efforts.

1. Introduction
Teacher turnover and attrition continue to challenge educational systems worldwide, often undermining school stability and
student achievement. Excessive attrition usually carries high costs, both financially and pedagogically. Research has pointed to job
satisfaction as a central determinant of teachers’ decisions to remain in or leave the profession, highlighting the importance of supportive school environments and professional relationships. Among the various support mechanisms available, mentorship has gained
considerable attention for its potential to increase well-being, reduce attrition, and enrich the professional lives of novice teachers.
Although prior studies underscore the ways in which mentees benefit, such as by gaining self-efficacy and smoother transitions into
the classroom, relatively few have focused on the experiences and advantages of serving as a mentor. Moreover, large-scale quantitative evidence on mentor outcomes, particularly within compulsory education, remains sparse. Understanding both sides of the
mentor–mentee relationship is crucial for informing policy and practice: if mentoring is mutually beneficial, it could be a powerful
lever to improve overall teacher satisfaction and retention. Conversely, if the burden of mentoring falls unevenly on more experienced
teachers without adequate institutional support, intended benefits may fail to materialise.
This paper addresses these gaps by using data from the 2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS), a large-scale,
cross-national dataset including over 120,000 teachers across 54 regions. Focusing on lower secondary education (ISCED level 2),
we employ a two-stage econometric approach to estimate the effects of both having a mentor and being a mentor on teacher job
satisfaction. We further analyse regional differences through a meta-analytic framework, providing insight into how local contexts can
shape the association between mentorship and job satisfaction. By examining teacher mentorship from both sides, this study seeks to
inform teacher development policies and contribute to broader efforts aimed at sustaining a stable, high-quality teaching workforce.

1.1. Job satisfaction
Teacher attrition is a persistent challenge that varies considerably across countries and subgroups (European Commission/EACEA/Eurydice, 2021; OECD, 2021; UNESCO, 2024). A substantial body of research identifies job satisfaction as a primary antecedent
of teacher turnover. Seminal work by Ingersoll (2001) and subsequent studies (Aydogdu & Asikgil, 2011; Blömeke et al., 2017; Klassen
& Chiu, 2011; Liu & Bellibas, 2018; Renbarger & Davis, 2019; Renzulli et al., 2011; Sims, 2017; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2011; Toropova
et al., 2021) support the notion that higher job satisfaction decreases turnover rates. Accordingly, understanding the determinants of
job satisfaction is crucial from both a policy and research perspective.
Empirical research consistently highlights several predictors of teacher job satisfaction: e.g., teacher self-efficacy (Admiraal et al.,
2019; Collie et al., 2012; Klassen et al., 2009; Klassen & Chiu, 2010; Lopes & Oliveira, 2020; Renbarger & Davis, 2019; Toropova et al.,
2021; Torres, 2019; Wang et al., 2020; Yoon & Kim, 2022), classroom discipline and school climate (Admiraal et al., 2019; Liu &
Bellibas, 2018; Lopes & Oliveira, 2020; Renzulli et al., 2011; Torres, 2019), student engagement and achievement (Admiraal et al.,
2019; Collie et al., 2012; Klassen et al., 2009; Klassen & Chiu, 2010; Liu & Bellibas, 2018; Renzulli et al., 2011; Sims, 2017; Skaalvik &
Skaalvik, 2011; Toropova et al., 2021; Torres, 2019; Wang et al., 2020), and various stressors (e.g., workload, time pressure, classroom
stress) play critical roles (Admiraal et al., 2019; Collie et al., 2012; Klassen & Chiu, 2010; Renbarger & Davis, 2019; Skaalvik &
Skaalvik, 2011; Torres, 2019; Wang et al., 2020). Other determinants include compensation and work conditions (Admiraal et al.,
2019; Liu & Ramsey, 2008; Renzulli et al., 2011; Toropova et al., 2021), teacher–student relationships (Admiraal et al., 2019; Collie
et al., 2012; Renzulli et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2020) and leadership quality, professional development, professional collaboration and
mentorship (Liu & Ramsey, 2008; Liu & Bellibas, 2018; Renbarger & Davis, 2019; Renzulli et al., 2011; Sims, 2017; Skaalvik &
Skaalvik, 2011; Smet, 2021; Song et al., 2018; Toropova et al., 2021; Torres, 2019; Wang et al., 2020; Yoon & Kim, 2022).
These studies frequently control for demographic and contextual variables such as age, gender, race, experience, contract type,
teaching level, qualification, school size, urbanicity, student socioeconomic status, and special needs prevalence (Admiraal et al.,
2019; Collie et al., 2012; Crossman & Harris, 2006; Klassen & Chiu, 2010; Liu & Ramsey, 2008; Liu & Bellibas, 2018; Renbarger &
Davis, 2019; Renzulli et al., 2011; Saiti & Papadopoulos, 2015; Sims, 2017; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2011; Smet, 2021; Toropova et al.,
2021; Torres, 2019; Wang et al., 2020; Yoon & Kim, 2022).
1.2. Mentorship
Within this extensive literature on teacher job satisfaction and turnover, mentorship has emerged as a promising strategy to
improve retention and overall well-being of novice teachers. Prior research suggests that mentoring leads to higher self-efficacy,
smoother induction, and the development of a robust professional identity, factors that are associated with greater job satisfaction
and lower attrition of mentees (Allen et al., 2004; Feiman-Nemser, 2001a, 2001b; Hobson et al., 2009; Ingersoll & Strong, 2011;
Rockoff, 2008; Ronfeldt & McQueen, 2017; Smith & Ingersoll, 2004). Despite these well-documented results of how mentees benefit
from mentoring, comparatively few studies have examined the advantages or disadvantages of being a mentor, a gap that is particularly
evident in quantitative, econometric studies within the context of (compulsory) education. Research in higher education and medical
contexts suggests that mentors may gain in terms of career satisfaction and recognition (Coates, 2012; Lunsford et al., 2018). A
meta-analysis across disciplines by Ghosh and Reio (2013) shows that employees who mentor others tend to report higher job
satisfaction. Some school-based studies hint at mentor growth and rejuvenated enthusiasm but often rely on qualitative methods such
as interviews with a limited number of cases (Hudson, 2013; Jaspers et al., 2014; Schwan et al., 2020), which make results difficult to
generalise.
Another strand of the literature investigates the characteristics typically associated with being a mentor. A recent systematic review
by Wang et al. (2025) develops a conceptual framework identifying three key dimensions of effective mentoring: cognitive (knowledge
and skills), social (relational skills, including communication and cooperation), and emotional (affective dispositions, motivation and
mental regulation). In general, mentors are more experienced teachers, who are motivated to grow professionally: social-utility values,
intrinsic motivation, intrinsic personal utility and professional training are associated with mentoring characteristics (Kuhn et al.,
2024; van Ginkel et al., 2016). Mentors often see themselves as lifelong learners with a strong personal learning orientation (Arnsby
et al., 2023; van Ginkel et al., 2016) and willing to take on or aspire to leadership roles (Gul et al., 2019; Sawalhi & Chaaban, 2021).
Sinclair et al. (2006) summarise that motivation of cooperating teachers is driven by a strong degree of “professional commitments to
self, students and the profession”.
Overall, while anecdotal and qualitative evidence points to potential benefits for those acting as mentors, there remains a need for
robust quantitative analyses, especially using large-scale educational datasets, to clearly establish whether and how serving as a
mentor influences teachers’ job satisfaction. Consequently, rigorous econometric research into mentor-focused outcomes in
compulsory education remains scarce, underscoring a clear gap in the literature.

TALIS covers a variety of themes related to teaching and learning environments, including teachers’ instructional practices, school
leadership, teachers’ professional practices, teacher education and initial preparation, teacher feedback and development, school
climate, job satisfaction, teacher human resource issues and stakeholder relations, and teacher self-efficacy (OECD, 2019a). In addition
to these themes, the dataset provides detailed information on teacher demographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, experience,
contract type) and school context characteristics (e.g., school size, urbanicity, student socioeconomic background, special needs
prevalence). These variables allow for a comprehensive analysis of the factors influencing teacher job satisfaction. Teacher job
satisfaction is measured in TALIS via an eight-item Likert scale that captures both overall satisfaction with the profession and with the
work environment (score range: 0–20) (OECD, 2019b). In TALIS, mentees and mentors are identified through two yes/no questions: “I
currently have an assigned mentor to support me” and “I am currently an assigned mentor for one or more teachers”. Mentoring is
defined as “a support structure in schools where more experienced teachers support less experienced teachers” and it should be “part of
a formal arrangement at this school” (OECD, 2019a). Teachers answering “Yes” to the former item are coded as “having a mentor,”
whereas those who answer “Yes” to the latter are coded as “being a mentor.”
To be able to contextualise the findings, we first present descriptive statistics for the entire dataset. The variables for which
descriptive statistics are reported in Table 1 are carefully selected based on existing literature on teacher job satisfaction determinants.
Descriptive statistics are presented for all variables used in the forthcoming analyses: for continuous variables, the mean and standard
deviation are reported, while for categorical variables, the number of observations and the share of each category is provided. The
inclusion of these variables is motivated by previous studies investigating determinants of job satisfaction: a more elaborate overview
of these determinants can be found in the literature review section of the Introduction.
The descriptive statistics presented in Table 1 provide an overview of the key variables included in the analysis, covering teacher
job satisfaction (as the dependent variable in the forthcoming models), mentorship status, demographic characteristics, employment
conditions, and various professional and workplace factors.
The dataset includes 123 299 teachers across 8 848 schools in 54 regions or language settings, implying that on average 14 teachers
per school were included in the regressions. Teacher job satisfaction (the dependent variable) shows a mean of 12.064 (SD = 2.004).
The theoretical minimum and maximum for job satisfaction are 0 and 20, respectively, meaning that the average of 12.064 suggests
that, on average, teachers report moderately high job satisfaction, though there is substantial variation across individuals. Regarding
mentorship, 13.3 % of teachers report having a mentor, while 14.2 % serve as a mentor. A very small percentage of the mentees (293
out of 16 426, i.e. 1.78 %) does not (yet) have a formal teacher qualification. The sample is predominantly female (68.8 %) and shows a
wide range of formal education levels, with the majority holding a tertiary education degree: ISCED 6 (52.9 %) or ISCED 7 (39.8 %).
Teacher age is broadly distributed, with nearly 60 % of respondents aged between 30 and 49. In addition, most teachers (70.3 %) chose
teaching as their first career option, and a large majority are employed on a permanent, full-time basis. Classroom composition varies,
with 76.4 % of teachers reporting that they teach some students with special needs, while 18.8 % report having no special needs
students in their classroom. Among the continuous explanatory variables, teachers report an average of 16.3 years of experience (SD =
10.6). They participated in an average of 4.46 professional development activities (SD = 2.36), with moderate self-reported needs for
professional development in teaching for diversity (mean = 9.92, SD = 2.00) and in subject matter/pedagogy (mean = 9.45, SD =
2.00). Measures of teacher self-efficacy (mean = 12.67, SD = 1.99), teacher-student relations (mean = 13.27, SD = 1.99) and various
stress factors (e.g., student behavior stress: 9.189, SD = 1.990; workload stress: 9.203, SD = 2.024) are also presented.
The regional descriptive statistics in Table 2 show that mean teacher job satisfaction score does not exhibit large variation across
countries or regions. However, there is substantial cross-regional variation in both mentorship indicators. On average, 13.3 % of
teachers report having a mentor, while 14.2 % serve as a mentor, yet the proportions differ markedly across countries. Some regions
report exceptionally high levels of mentoring participation, such as the United Arab Emirates (Arabic) (58.1 % having a mentor, 38.8 %
serving as a mentor), Kazakhstan (Kazakh) (41.7 % and 39.0 %), and South Africa (35.8 % and 34.3 %). In contrast, mentorship is
relatively rare in countries such as Slovenia (1.2 % having a mentor and 6.2 % serving as a mentor), Lithuania (2.2 % and 5.3 %), and
Italy (2.5 % and 5.3 %).
These differences suggest that the role of mentorship, both in terms of receiving and providing support, is highly context-specific,
and they provide a strong rationale for employing an econometric meta-analysis to examine how these distinct dimensions of
mentorship relate to teacher job satisfaction.

the one hand and teacher job satisfaction on the other hand, using the TALIS 2018 dataset. First, we estimate regional-level regression
models with school fixed effects to account for unobserved heterogeneity at the school level. Our primary regression model specifies
teacher job satisfaction as the dependent variable and mentorship status (‘having a mentor’ and ‘being a mentor’) as the key
explanatory variables. The main equation takes the following form:

However, since self-selection into the role of mentor may lead to endogeneity, we address this potential bias by applying a control
function approach (Wooldridge, 2015). This two-stage approach first estimates a probit model to predict mentor status and then includes the generalised residuals from this first-stage model in the second-stage outcome equation. These residuals are functions of the
inverse Mills ratio (Wooldridge, 2015), which corrects for the non-random selection into mentoring roles.
The first stage probit regression includes all control variables from the main equation as well as additional variables that the
literature (see Section 1.2) identifies as associated with being a mentor. These include scales measuring teacher motivation and
professional perceptions (i.e. personal-utility motivation to teach; social-utility motivation to teach; and perceived value and policy
influence of the teaching profession), as well as items reflecting the degree of participation in school management; participation in
teacher professional development networks; and the frequency of collaboration with colleagues to align student assessments.
To ensure reliable inference, we cluster standard errors at the school level to account for within-school correlations in teacher job
satisfaction. Additionally, we exclude 5 regions with fewer than 250 observations (see Table 2), as small sample sizes would lead to
insufficient degrees of freedom in the regressions, especially in the first stage of the control function where the probit model may
perfectly predict mentorship status.
In the second step, we adopt a meta-analytic framework by treating each of the remaining 49 regions as an independent study. We
first extract region-specific effect sizes and standard errors for the two key mentoring variables (having a mentor and being a mentor)
and then aggregate them using a random-effects meta-analysis model (Berkey et al., 1995; Raudenbush, 2009). Region-level estimates
are obtained from unweighted regressions.1 The estimated effect sizes of the regions are then weighted using inverse-variance methods
(i.e. the weight of every region is inversely related to the total variance), ensuring that estimates from regions with greater precision of
the estimated effect contribute more to the aggregated result.
This dual-step approach enables a comprehensive cross-regional analysis of how both having a mentor and serving as a mentor
influence teacher job satisfaction in diverse educational contexts.
To assess heterogeneity across regions, we report I² (the proportion of total variability in effect sizes that is due to genuine differences among regions rather than sampling error), tau² (the estimated variance of effect sizes across regions) and H² (the ratio of total
variability to sampling variability). Additionally, we conduct a z-test to evaluate the overall effect of mentorship and apply Cochran’s
Q test to determine whether observed heterogeneity exceeds what would be expected by chance.
4. Results
4.1. Forest plots
The first forest plot (see Fig. 1), which presents the association between having a mentor and teacher job satisfaction, yields an
overall random-effects estimate of 0.13 (95 % CI [0.09; 0.16]), indicating a positive, statistically significant relationship between
having a mentor and teacher job satisfaction. The vertical red line marks the null effect, while the vertical green line represents the
overall effect across all regional educational contexts. The heterogeneity tests show I² ≈ 0 % and a non-significant Q-test (Q(47) =
47.18; p = 0.47), suggesting that the effect is remarkably consistent across different educational contexts. While most individual
country estimates cluster around the overall (positive) effect size, some regions exhibit larger coefficients, such as the Czech Republic,
Norway (Bokmål) and Turkey, whereas others report near-zero or even slightly negative estimates (e.g., Chile, Lithuania and Slovenia).
However, none of the negative estimates is statistically significant. The absence of significant heterogeneity suggests that mentorship
programs generally enhance teacher well-being for mentees, regardless of country-specific differences in educational systems. This
pattern implies that having a mentor is positively associated with job satisfaction in a wide variety of settings, with few contextual
differences mitigating or enhancing that effect.
The second forest plot (see Fig. 2), which examines the association between serving as a mentor and job satisfaction, reveals a
stronger overall effect size of 0.82 (95 % CI [0.62; 1.02]), indicating a statistically significant and substantively meaningful positive
relationship. In contrast to the first plot, heterogeneity across regions is moderate (I² = 56.1 %; Q(47) = 110.29; p ≈ 0.00), indicating
that the association varies substantially across regions. While almost all regions demonstrate beneficial effects of mentoring for the
mentor, the range is broader than in the case of mentees, and few coefficients approach or fall below zero (though not significant).

5. Discussion
The findings of this study confirm mentorship as a potentially fruitful lever for increasing teacher job satisfaction in lower secondary education contexts. This study advances the ‘mentorship and job satisfaction’ literature in three concrete ways. First, whereas
10

International Journal of Educational Research 134 (2025) 102773

M. Smet

most large-scale quantitative work stops at the mentee side (Rockoff, 2008; Ronfeldt & McQueen, 2017; Smith & Ingersoll, 2004), we
provide parallel evidence for mentors. Second, by coupling within-school fixed-effects estimates with a random-effects meta-analysis
across 49 regions, we move beyond single-country results (Feiman-Nemser, 2001b; Rockoff, 2008; Ronfeldt & McQueen, 2017; Smith
& Ingersoll, 2004) and show which findings generalise cross-nationally. Third, our control-function strategy addresses the
self-selection into the role of mentor, yielding effect sizes that are robust to both observed and unobserved teacher heterogeneity.
Together, these contributions extend the conversation from descriptive associations to robust, generalisable evidence that mentorship
benefits not only novice teachers, but also more experienced teachers who engage in a mentor role.
Our meta-analysis shows that having a mentor is positively associated with job satisfaction in a manner that is remarkably consistent
across different countries and settings (I² ≈ 0 %). This aligns with existing research demonstrating that mentees benefit from improved
self-efficacy, smoother induction, and stronger professional identities (Allen et al., 2004; Feiman-Nemser, 2001a, 2001b; Hobson et al.,
2009; Ingersoll & Strong, 2011; Ronfeldt & McQueen, 2017; Smith & Ingersoll, 2004). In doing so, our results extend the established
notion that mentorship fosters teacher well-being and retention, providing robust, large-scale quantitative evidence from 49 international regions.
A key contribution of this study is the examination of being a mentor, a relatively underexplored aspect in the literature. We find that
serving as a mentor is also positively linked to job satisfaction (overall effect ≈ 0.82), although with considerably more heterogeneity
(I² = 56.09 %). This variation suggests that the effect on mentors themselves may be contingent on factors such as institutional support,
workload considerations, cultural norms, or the structure of mentoring programmes. While some contexts show strong positive returns
for mentors, others indicate negligible or non-significant effects, highlighting the importance of designing mentoring roles in ways that
are rewarding rather than burdensome.
5.1. Policy implications
From a policy perspective, these findings suggest that mentorship programmes can be a viable strategy for improving teacher wellbeing and reducing attrition, with benefits for both mentees and mentors. Nevertheless, the greater cross-regional variation in the being
a mentor dimension underscores the need for context-sensitive implementation. Policymakers and school leaders might consider
providing clearer role definitions, dedicated time for mentoring activities, and appropriate training or incentives to ensure that
mentorship duties do not merely add to mentors’ workload. Recognising and rewarding teachers who serve as mentors could lead to
greater engagement and job satisfaction, ultimately contributing to a more stable and high-quality teaching workforce.
5.2. Implications for teacher education
These results underscore the need to incorporate mentorship into teacher education and professional development strategies.
Schools and training programmes could strengthen induction by pairing novice teachers with trained, motivated mentors who have
sufficient time and institutional support. Clear role definitions, recognition for mentors (e.g. through reduced workload or financial
incentives), and targeted training for mentors can enhance the value they derive from this role while ensuring high-quality guidance
for mentees. By creating formal mentorship structures that account for local contextual factors, such as existing school cultures,
contractual obligations, and workload considerations, teacher education programmes may optimise both the mentee and mentor
experience, ultimately contributing to higher satisfaction and lower attrition overall.
5.3. Limitations
A few limitations should be noted. First, these results rely on cross-sectional, self-reported data from TALIS, limiting our ability to
infer strict causality. Although the control-function procedure reduces bias from teachers’ self-selecting into mentoring roles, it relies
on the exclusion restrictions and distributional assumptions of the first-stage probit; residual endogeneity cannot be ruled out. Second,
while school fixed effects help control for certain contextual factors, unobserved differences among individual teachers (e.g. personality traits, professional values) may still confound the results. Third, the measurement of ‘having a mentor’ and ‘being a mentor’ is
binary, and the quality, intensity, or duration of mentorship was not explicitly captured by the data. Finally, country-level policy
differences and cultural nuances likely shape how mentorship is practised; deeper qualitative or mixed-methods research could enrich
our understanding of these contextual elements.
5.4. Avenues for future research
Future studies might address these limitations by employing longitudinal or panel data to better establish causal effects. Researchers could also examine more fine-grained measures of mentoring quality or intensity, exploring, for instance, whether formal,
structured mentoring programmes yield different outcomes from informal peer support or whether the duration of mentorship programmes matters. Finally, linking changes in job satisfaction to actual teacher turnover rates would help clarify whether improvements
in satisfaction lead to measurable gains in retention.
6. Conclusion
This study contributes new evidence to the teacher job satisfaction literature by demonstrating that mentorship has a moderate but

robust positive association with teacher job satisfaction in lower secondary education. Whereas the benefits for mentees appear
consistent across a broad range of educational contexts, the effects for mentors vary more widely, suggesting that supportive environments and appropriate structural conditions can amplify or hinder the benefits of taking on a mentor role. These findings highlight
mentorship as a potentially high-impact strategy in ongoing efforts to foster teacher well-being and retention, while also underscoring
the need for nuanced, context-driven implementation.
