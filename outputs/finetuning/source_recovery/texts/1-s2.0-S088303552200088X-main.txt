A B S T R A C T  
This study identifies profiles for teacher collaboration related to five instructional practices 
(direct instruction, adaptive instruction, initiation of reading strategies, stimulation of reading 
engagement, and feedback provision) among middle school literacy teachers in South Korea. PISA 
2018 data were utilized for a latent profile analysis that revealed four profiles: High Collabora­
tion, Moderate Collaboration, Low Collaboration, and Assessment-centered Collaboration. A 
notable finding was the existence of a substantial Assessment-centered Collaboration group, 
comprising teachers whose primary collaboration focus was assessment standards. Moreover, it 
was found that the use of all five instructional practices was significantly higher among the 
higher-collaboration groups. Teacher collaboration profiles can aid in establishing clear goals and 
targeted support for sustained, impactful professional learning.   
1. Introduction 
Teacher collaboration can offer various benefits for teachers and students. Broadly defined as “two or more individuals interacting 
or working together to accomplish a specific goal” (OECD, 2020b, p. 148), teacher collaboration allows teachers to cope collectively 
with fast changing educational demands (Prenger et al., 2019) and engage in professional development activities (Dogan & Adams, 
2020), all the while increasing their self-efficacy (Goddard & Kim, 2018; Sehgal et al., 2017; Tichenor & Tichenor, 2018), improving 
collegiality (Little, 1990; OECD, 2020b), and helping them to manage uncertainty in their teaching (Cha & Ham, 2012). Importantly, 
teacher collaboration with a particular focus on instructional practices improvement has also been proven to positively influence 
students’ achievement (Ciampa & Gallagher, 2016; Hairon & Tan, 2017). For this reason, extensive research over the last two decades 
has delved into the causes and effects of teacher collaboration in diverse subjects, school levels, school climates, and leadership 
conditions (for reviews, see Goddard et al., 2015; Hargreaves, 2019; Vangrieken et al., 2015). 
Among the many content areas for which teacher collaboration has been emphasized, literacy education stands out as a worthwhile 
focus for collaborative practices for two key reasons. First, literacy education sets the basic requirements for education of other content 
areas in schools (Shelton et al., 2020). The prime goal of middle school literacy education may be to prepare students to engage 
critically with diverse written material, yet entwined within this goal is the broader need for students to “coordinate a complex set of

multiple sources of information” (Blanton et al., 2007, p. 79). With literacy at the core of most academic learning, a systematic 
approach to improvement teaching practices in that area is crucial. 
Second, specific teaching practices have been recommended to enhance literacy education (e.g., Afflerbach & Harrison, 2017; 
Blanton et al., 2007; Leu et al., 2015), and extensive research suggests that teacher collaboration on effective instructional practices 
among literacy teachers can improve their self-efficacy and their pedagogical and content knowledge in employing these practices (e.g. 
Cantrell & Hughes, 2008) and enhance rigor and consistency across literacy classrooms (Hairon & Tan, 2017), resulting in improved 
student outcomes and student engagement (Arya et al., 2015; Biancarosa et al., 2010; Culican, 2005; Goddard et al., 2015). However, 
there is dearth of large-scale empirical research that explores how literacy teachers collaborate and which instructional practices are 
the focus of their collaboration. 
This gap in the literacy teacher collaboration literature is even starker in the Korean context. At Korean middle schools, the 
importance placed on literacy means that the subject is sub-divided into separate literature, reading comprehension, and grammar 
courses. The result is that multiple literacy teachers must work together for curricular agreement and for a holistic look at student 
learning outcomes. Given that the subject of literacy itself involves a great deal of interpretation and reasoning, cross-checking in 
instruction between literacy teachers may be particularly relevant. Meanwhile, Korea has recently seen both top-down and bottom-up 
pushes toward teacher collaboration (Lee & Kim, 2016; Yoo & Jang, 2022). A core assumption at the heart of such programs is that 
increased collaboration enhances teachers’ self-efficacy and instructional practices (Fred et al., 2020; Kim & Lee, 2020). Yet little is 
known about the nature of these collaborations. As large-scale empirical studies on teacher collaboration are still lacking in 
non-western contexts (Chen et al., 2020), an exploration of profiles within the Korean context can provide directions for further 
research internationally. 
Against this backdrop, it is notable that changes were made the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) 
in its administering of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA). Conducted every three years, the PISA study 
focuses on comparing international trends in education to understand the associations among educational contextual variables and to 
assess the achievement of 15-year-old students. In recognition of the special role of literacy teaching, the PISA included a questionnaire 
specifically to literacy teachers for the first time in 2018. However, no study to date has emerged linking teacher collaboration to 
instructional practices using PISA’s findings. To address the gaps in the literature on Korean teacher collaboration, we have seized the 
opportunity afforded by the PISA’s robust dataset. 
The purpose of the present study is therefore to explore whether different teacher collaboration profiles exist, and if so, whether 
literacy teachers’ use of different types of instructional practices differs according to the profiles. Using Korean literacy teachers’ 
survey data from international panel data of the PISA 2018, this study employed latent profile analysis by distinguishing different 
groups and analyzing differences among each group’s use of five types of instructional practice (direct instruction, adaptive in­
struction, initiation of reading strategies, stimulation of reading engagement, and feedback provision) that featured on the PISA’s 
Teacher Questionnaire. 
2. Theoretical framework and review of the literature 
2.1. Theoretical framework 
The theoretical framework of this study draws from two key strands of research. The first strand relates to concepts about 
instructional practices in literacy education, and the second draws from theories about collaboration and its effects on instructional 
practices. 
For the first strand, we employ the conceptual framework of the PISA 2018 (OECD, 2019a), which links practices that enhance 
literacy skills and strategies to two models. One of these models is Klieme et al. (2009) description of teaching quality. In this model, 
teacher support, cognitive activation, and classroom management all predict growth in learners’ language skills, with teacher support 
primarily predicting learners’ motivation growth and cognitive activation predicting cognitive gains. The teacher support facet of 
Klieme’s model in then underpinned by self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985). With self-determination theory applied to 
classroom teaching, the teacher acts as a ‘significant other’ who supports students’ sense of ownership in the reading process, moti­
vating students toward more self-directed reading practices, and then to proficiency. 
For the second strand of the present study’s theoretical framework, we draw on Little’s (1990) conceptualization of teacher 
collaboration. Little positioned collaboration as collective work between teachers, including discussion and reflection on current 
practice and decision-making on future, individual planning. In this conceptualization, aid and assistance from the group could either 
lead to future joint work or could guide individual planning, but importantly, ultimately leads to gains in student achievement. 
2.2. Instructional practices in literacy education 
The OECD (2019a) notes the power of instructional practices to enhance students’ literacy proficiency, strategy awareness, and 
motivation. In this paper, we explore five evidence-based instructional practices. We explicate these practices and related research 
below and then highlight connections among the practices. 
2.2.1. Direct instruction 
Direct instruction (also known as teacher-directed instruction) is an explicit instructional practice that involves critical planning,

delivery and assessment based on clear learning objectives and success criteria (Humphrey & Feez, 2016). Not to be confused with 
“didactic teacher-led talking from the front” (Hattie, 2009, p. 204), direct instruction is based on teachers analyzing learners’ needs, 
monitoring understanding, and providing opportunities for practice (Humphrey & Feez, 2016). Explicit direct instruction in literacy 
teaching can promote mastery and aid in elucidating concepts and help struggling students (Vess et al., 2018). 
2.2.2. Adaptive instruction 
Adaptive instruction has long been identified as an effective instructional practice (Athanases et al., 2015). Teachers utilizing 
adaptive instruction engage in reflective instruction based on students’ learning and understanding, and make instructional decisions 
and actions based on their professional knowledge (Taylor, 2021; Vaughn et al., 2016). Whether teachers adapt their instruction 
mid-lesson (e.g., scaffolding) or in the planning stages (e.g., differentiated instruction; universal design), skillful adaptation requires a 
form of conceptual, “adaptive expertise” (Hatano and Inagaki, 1986 as cited in Athanases et al., 2015, p. 85) that transcends routine 
expertise. Among literacy teachers, this adaptive expertise can range from helping individuals with vocabulary and decoding skills 
(Nurmi et al., 2013) to changing lessons to follow students’ curiosity (Vaughn, 2019). 
2.2.3. Initiation of reading strategies 
The initiation of reading strategies involves teaching students to use before-, during-, and after- reading techniques that “support 
active engagement with text, facilitate comprehension monitoring, and enhance understanding” (Boardman et al., 2018, p. 175). 
Instructional practices within the initiation of reading strategies typically involve raising awareness of, modelling, and discussing such 
techniques as questioning, paraphrasing, inferencing-making, identifying main ideas, and using graphic organizers to reconceptualize 
texts and aid comprehension (Solis et al., 2012). Students may also be encouraged to use collaborative strategies to aid comprehension 
(Vaughn et al., 2011). Learners need awareness of how the parts of reading work together and practice in combining areas of reading; 
by dividing skilled reading into different pieces, teachers can aid with these processes (Afflerbach et al., 2008). 
2.2.4. Stimulation of reading engagement 
As an instructional practice, the stimulation of reading engagement refers to teachers supporting students’ motivation and op­
portunities to read (Afflerbach & Harrison, 2017; Merga, 2020). Reading engagement is vital to literacy achievement (Lee et al., 2021) 
and is one of the instructional practices most positively associated with students’ enjoyment of reading (Guthrie et al., 2013; OECD, 
2019b). Examples of methods used to stimulate engagement with reading at the middle school level include simply providing time for 
students to read (Pitcher et al., 2011), readers’ theater (Keehn et al., 2008), or engaging students in recreational reading (Whittingham 
& Huffman, 2009). 
2.2.5. Feedback provision 
Feedback provision relates to teachers’ pedagogical knowledge in finding problems and providing timely and appropriate solutions 
in students’ learning processes (Carless & Winstone, 2020). In literacy education, the positive relation of feedback and students’ 
learning has been studied extensively (e.g., Patthey-Chavez et al., 2004; and feedback provision is a key part of teacher support for 
student learning (Sukhram & Monda-Amaya, 2017; Van den Bergh et al., 2014). 
2.2.6. Connections between instructional practices 
Although the above instructional practices differ in some domains, they share a common focus as a socially mediated activity 
(Vygotsky, 1978/1995). All five practices relate positively to teacher support (OECD, 2019b) and are means by which students 
perceive that their teachers care about them and their learning. Feedback from teachers and adaptive instruction are closely associated 
(Echazarra & Mostafa, 2020), and prior PISA student reports on literacy teachers in Korea have found these areas to be particularly 
highly linked (OECD, 2019b). 
2.3. Teacher collaboration for literacy teachers 
Engaging in up-to-date, evidence-based pedagogies generally requires abandoning current instructional practices and taking on 
new ones. Collaboration in literacy education can aid with this process (Ciampa & Gallagher, 2016; Culican, 2005). For literacy 
teachers, in particular, collaboration has been linked to learners’ reading outcomes, self-efficacy, and metacognition, with benefits 
affecting content-area learning across the school curriculum (Afflerbach & Harrison, 2017; Culican, 2005; OECD 2019a; Ronfeldt et al., 
2015). Literacy teacher collaboration on effective instructional practices can improve literacy teachers’ pedagogical content knowl­
edge and self-efficacy (Cantrell & Hughes, 2008) and allows them to develop and practice instruction and assessment (Ciampa & 
Gallagher, 2016). It can also offer a context for curriculum and evaluation criteria development, help teachers to identify new practices 
collectively, reflect on their newly proposed practices, and enhance the effectiveness of implementation (Randall & Marangell, 2021; 
Van den Bergh et al., 2014). For example, increased teacher collaboration is connected to self-efficacy on the use of instructional 
practices that involve cognitive activation, such as critical thinking tasks and problem-based learning (OECD, 2020a). These collective 
efforts expand professional knowledge, encourage adherence to learning standards, and inform decision-making based on data 
collected and shared among teachers (Darling Hammond et al., 2017). 
Instructional practices that stress interactions between students and teachers in literacy education are enhanced by collegial 
professional development efforts between teachers (Ciampa & Gallagher, 2016; Culican, 2005). For example, feedback provision, a key 
instructional practice for literacy teachers, has been found to benefit from teacher collaboration, with effective, structural feedback a

2.4. Teacher collaboration and instructional practices: recent research in Asian contexts 
Recent studies in some Asian contexts have also explored connections between teacher collaboration and instructional practices 
using large-scale datasets. In a study of Shanghai middle schools, Fang (2021) found that once teacher-level variables were controlled 
for, professional development activities such as collaboration was a key predictor of the use of instructional practices. Meanwhile, 
Hairon and Tan’s (2017) study of schools in Singapore and Shanghai schools found that collaborative activities that specifically 
focused on instructional improvement and assessment standardization had a positive influence on students’ achievement. 
Among recent research in the Korean context, Shim and Lee (2021) found that the way teachers received feedback on their in­
struction, including from peers, connected positively to classroom preparation and engagement with students. Meanwhile, Woo and 
Kim (2020) found that team innovativeness, a part of teacher collaboration and cooperation, impacted on teachers’ student 
engagement practices. However, these studies in Asia, and specifically in Korea, did not focus specifically on literacy teachers, their 
collaboration, and their instructional practices. 
2.5. The research questions 
As noted in the introduction of this paper, despite the wealth of research on teacher collaboration in general, gaps remain. There is 
still a need to research specific domains of instructional practices and to identify subcategories of teacher collaboration in relation to 
instructional practices, as heterogeneity of the characteristics of teacher collaboration could be overlooked by a mere association of 
collaboration to instructional practices (OECD, 2019a; OECD 2020a; Ronfeldt et al., 2015). In response to the gaps in the literature, the 
following questions therefore guide this study: 
Research Question 1 (RQ1): Do different latent profiles of teacher collaboration exist, and if so, what characterizes them? 
Research question 2 (RQ2): If different latent profiles exist, to what extent does the use of instructional practices (direct instruction, 
adaptive instruction, initiation of reading strategies, stimulation of reading engagement, and feedback provision) differ among them? 
3. Method 
3.1. Data and sample 
We employed a secondary data analysis using PISA data collected by the OECD (2018) on Korean teachers. Data were collected 
from the Teacher Questionnaires of 1,266 full-time middle school Korean literacy teachers (60.0% female and 40.0% male). Table 1 
shows the demographic information of the participants. The literacy teacher qualification examination and pay are relatively uniform 
across the nation (Kim & Lee, 2020) and the overarching language arts curriculum in Korea is nationally administered, ensuring some 
sampling uniformity in terms of background and needs. 
3.2. Measures

engagement, direct instruction, adaptive instruction, and feedback provision, as included in the PISA 2018 Teacher Questionnaire 
(OECD, 2018) data set. The questionnaire involved a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 4 (e.g., never or almost never; in some 
lessons/ in most lessons/ in all lessons). Items included questions about teachers’ interactions with colleagues (e.g., To what extent do you 
agree with the following statements about regular cooperation among your fellow teachers of and yourself?) and with students (e.g., How often

3.3. Data analysis 
All analyses aimed at examining the existence of the latent profiles of collaboration level of middle school Korean literacy teachers 
(RQ1) and the differences in the use of instructional practices (initiation of reading strategies, stimulation of reading engagement, 
direct instruction, adaptive instruction, and feedback provision) among latent profiles (RQ2) employed the statistical package MPlus 
8.3 (Muth´en & Muth´en, 1998-2015). An auxiliary approach applying the Bolck-Croom Hagenaars (BCH; Bolck et al., 2004) method 
was used to test the significant differences in outcome variables (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014). 
Descriptive statistics and correlations were analyzed to check multivariate normality and correlations between variables. Latent 
profile analysis (LPA) was then utilized to identify the number of latent groups of middle school Korean language teachers based on the 
collaboration level. LPA is a method used to identify profiles that are not observable (probabilistic) through other methods such as 
cluster analyses (Morin et al., 2011). LPA is probabilistic in nature because the assignment of profiles is classified on the empirical 
estimation of most probable membership (Masyn, 2013), which increases the accuracy of the classification (Bechter et al., 2018). 
Moreover, as opposed to variable-centered approaches in other models, LPA focuses on the patterns represented by individual par­
ticipants across the variables selected for the study. In other words, LPA enables the identification of naturally occurring subgroups 
within the participants distinguished by key variables. Therefore, the LPA mitigates the loss of information or synergic effects that 
results from oversimplification (Litalien et al., 2017) and the multicollinearity that occurs with multiple subscales (e.g., Standage et al., 
2008) 
Thus, to estimate the LPA model that fit the data, the optimal number of latent profiles was determined by assessing several fit 
indices. To determine the optimal number of latent profiles, two to five latent profiles were tested to compare the fit indices including 
Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and the sample-size adjusted BIC (SABIC). The lowest 
information indices (AIC, BIC and SABIC) are generally considered most desirable (Schwarz, 1978; Sclove, 1987). The p-values of 
Vuong-Lo-Mendell-Rubin (VLMR) and Lo-Mendell-Rubin (LMR) likelihood ratio tests (LRT) were also used to compare the k profiles 
model to the k-1 profiles model. When the p -values are significant, the k profiles model is better than the k-1 model. Additionally, 
entropy values were considered. The classification is considered good when entropies are s≥.80 (Muth´en, 2004). 
After the optimal model was determined, the BCH method (Bolck et al., 2004) was used to compare the group differences of the 
distal outcome predictors—the instructional practices (direct instruction, adaptive instruction, and feedback provision)—across latent 
profiles. The BCH method is used to alleviate the potential biases created in estimating the correlations by using a weighted multigroup

4. Results 
4.1. Multivariate normality 
To satisfy normality, skewness values must be lower than 3 and the kurtosis lower than 10 (Kline, 2011). The skewness and kurtosis 
values of this study were found to be satisfactory, with the range from |.024| to |.804| and from |.155| to |.991|, respectively. The 
correlations among the variables were statistically significant (p < .05) (see Table 3). 
4.2. Latent profile analysis 
To determine the number of latent profiles of the level of teacher collaboration among middle school Korean literacy teachers, we 
added the number of profiles and comprehensively checked the information indices, model fit and quality of classification. The fit 
indices are reported in Table 4. 
The data revealed numerous findings. First, for AIC, BIC and SABIC, all of three indices decreased as more profiles were extracted. 
The number of profiles was decided at the point where the trends of AIC, BIC, and SABIC became gradual. A four-profile solution was 
found to be optimal. Second, the LMR-LRT and BLRT were considered; while LMR-LRT was not significant, BLRT was significant 
(p<.05). Third, the overall classification accuracy (entropy) was above .90 for the 2- to 5-profiles, and 4-profile was the highest (.961, 
excluding the 2-profile, which was too small for classification. Fourth, although the group for which the proportion was only 1.7% was 
too small, it was also included in the five-profile model, as it shed light on how the class collaboration of low collaboration group is 
performed. 
Overall, the evidence supported most clearly a four-profile solution when balancing fit, entropy, direct comparison, and model 
interpretability. Table 5 shows the form, mean and standard deviation of the decided latent profiles. Fig. 2 depicts the four profiles. 
Profiles were labelled based according to levels of collaboration. Profile 1 (34.8% of participants) showed high scores on all items 
and was therefore labelled the High Collaboration (HC) group. Profile 2 (25.6% of participants) showed scores above 3 on all items, 
with particularly high scores on items related to student achievement and standardizing marking but moderate scores on items related

5. Findings and discussion 
We aimed to identify the latent profiles of Korean middle school literacy teachers’ level of teacher collaboration. We also sought to 
examine the differences in the use of five instructional practices (direct instruction, adaptive instruction, initiation of reading stra­
tegies, stimulation of reading engagement, and feedback provision) in relation to profile membership. In brief, we found that teachers 
were classified into four different profiles according to the level of teacher collaboration, that is, a High Collaboration profile, a 
Moderate Collaboration profile, a Low Collaboration profile, and an Assessment-centered Collaboration profile. Trends for the five 
different instructional practices differed significantly among the four groups. As this study employed a nationally representative 
sample of teachers in Korea, generalization of the findings is possible within a Korean context. 
5.1. RQ1: Different latent teacher collaboration profiles exist 
In answer to RQ1, four latent profiles that describes the level of teacher collaboration were identified: namely, High Collaboration, 
Moderate Collaboration, Low Collaboration, and Assessment-centered Collaboration. The composition of the four profiles demon­
strated support for the distinction between levels of collaboration among Korean middle school literacy teachers. In detail, the level of 
collaboration shows mostly consistent and proportionate trends between the High Collaboration and Moderate Collaboration group, as 
shown in Fig. 2. 
However, a disparate trend for items 1 (We discuss the achievement requirements for Korean lessons when setting tests) and 2 (We discuss 
the criteria we use to grade written tests) reveals differences in teacher collaboration for the purpose of student assessment. This different 
trend indicates the existence of divergent factors which can be classified into a separate latent profile, the Assessment-centered 
Collaboration group, comprising 25.6% of the population. As items 1 and 2 are related to assessment standards, this trend supports 
the theoretical background for literacy education as the basis for students’ learning and understanding in other content and subject 
areas (Blanton et al., 2007; Freebody, 2007; OECD, 2019b; Shelton et al., 2020). This emphasis is also supported by research from 
Ronfeldt et al. (2015),and Vescio et al., and Adams (2008), which emphasized setting clear goals for teacher collaboration around 
students’ achievement and assessment to establish fair assessment norms. 
It is important to note that teachers in the Assessment-centered Collaboration profile already exhibited a relatively strong effort to 
collaborate. If the goal of a professional development program is increased collaboration, among the four profiles, it is possible that the 
most realistic movement may be from an Assessment-centered Collaboration profile to a High Collaboration profile by extending the 
range (but not necessarily the intensity) of collaboration. Since teachers’ collaboration in the Assessment-centered Collaboration 
profile is primarily focused on forms of feedback, teachers within this profile may benefit from increased collaboration for class 
preparation or student support. Diverse collaboration offerings may help such teachers find opportunities for comprehensive 
improvement of their praxis.

Moreover, it should also be noted that the teachers with a Low Collaboration profile, who comprised only 1.7% of the whole 
population and were seemingly the least collaborative group, performed lower on Items 1 and 2 (assessment-related items) than on 
other items. This study’s findings suggest that collaboration with a clear purpose may enhance collaboration. For Low Collaboration 
profile teachers, a purpose related to sharing information to inform balanced assessment practices could be a helpful starting point and 
cold contribute to balancing assessment practices, and subsequently achievement. 
5.2. RQ2: Profiles differ in the use of instructional practices 
Having distinguished four different latent profiles, we then supplemented the distinction with further information on the differ­
ences in using five important literacy instructional practices (direct instruction, adaptive instruction, initiation of reading strategies, 
stimulation of reading engagement, and feedback provision) across profiles. 
The findings suggest that teachers in the High Collaboration group used all five instructional practices more than teachers in the 
other three profiles. A comparison between the Assessment-centered Collaboration and Moderate Collaboration profiles also revealed 
significant differences. Kennedy (2016) noted the lack of research connecting collaborative professional development activities to 
enhanced classroom praxis. The findings of our indicate a positive association between a higher level of collaboration and the 
increased use of evidence-based instructional practices, suggesting that increased collaboration relates to improved classroom 
practice. 
Overall, although causal relations between teacher collaboration and the instructional practices within and between the latent 
groups cannot be drawn from this study’s methodological approach, the findings that a higher level of teacher collaboration is 
associated with increased use of five instructional practices imbued with characteristics of social interaction still carry weight. The 
High Collaboration group used all five of the instructional practices significantly more than the other groups in this study. The 
Assessment-centered Collaboration group, which had relatively high rates of collaboration for a clear purpose, differed significantly in 
use of the instructional practices from the Low Collaboration profile. 
In the comparison between the Assessment-centered Collaboration profile and the Moderate Collaboration profile, no significant 
differences were found in adaptive instruction and providing feedback. The reason could be explained by the existence of similar 
average means on items 5 and 7 in the two profiles, as shown on Fig. 2. In detail, both item 5 (We discuss ways to teach learning strategies 
and techniques to our students) and item 7 (We discuss ways to better identify students’ individual strengths and weaknesses) are practices that 
relate more to students’ learning strategies than to teaching strategies. Therefore, the lack of differences shown in both instructional 
practices provided in-depth description to distinguish the Assessment-centered Collaboration profile from the Moderate Collaboration 
profile. In other words, collaborating literacy teachers are more focused on assessment rather than on monitoring students’ learning or 
presenting learning strategies. The implication of this finding is that increased collaborative efforts may be required to address 
teachers’ praxis in areas other than assessment. 
Additionally, although the interpretation of differences between the Moderate and Low Collaboration profiles may be limited due 
to the size of the Low Collaboration group, it is noteworthy that there were clear differences in the profiles in terms of provision 
feedback and no significant differences shown on the other four instructional practices. This finding underscores how literacy teacher 
collaboration on feedback provision is underutilized in Korea, despite feedback being a crucial instructional practice for student 
learning (Hattie & Timperley, 2007; Van den Bergh et al., 2014). Feedback provision is also a practice that resists improvement at the 
individual level (Van den Bergh et al., 2014) and which benefits from collaborative reflection and professional learning (Darling-­
Hammond et al., 2017). Therefore, the finding from this research that providing feedback is a crucial indicator that differentiates the 
Moderate Collaboration and Low Collaboration profiles offers essential implications for professional learning programs. Professional 
development leaders must seek out authentic topics and aims that could be utilized in classroom settings for teachers otherwise 
reluctant to collaborate with peers. Feedback provision could be the hook that piques literacy teachers’ initial interest in collaboration. 
5.3. Conclusions and implications 
The close association of teacher collaboration to instructional practices is consistent with previous studies which emphasized the 
importance of literacy teacher collaboration in initiating reading strategies, stimulating reading engagement, direct instruction 
(Ciampa & Gallagher, 2016), adaptive instruction (Culican, 2005), and feedback provision (Echazarra & Mostafa, 2020). Considering 
the importance of literacy in fostering the communicative functioning of humans as social beings (Freebody, 2007) and of connecting 
to all other academic areas (Shelton et al., 2020), our findings are significant in highlighting the role of teacher collaboration in 
connection to these instructional practices. 
One advantage of LPA is the ability to discover granular constructs (Masyn, 2013), and the identification of the 
Assessment-Centered Collaboration profile is, indeed, a strength of the current study. The latent profiles here provide an in-depth 
perspective on teacher collaboration in relation to their sub-constructs. Concrete findings on teacher collaboration are scarce (Fred 
et al., 2020), yet it is known that clear and substantive goals are the foundation of effective and successful collaboration (Darling & 
Hammond, 2017). The differentiated perspective provided by the profiles discovered in this profile may aid in setting substantive goals 
for teacher collaboration programs. 
This study has clear implications for teachers, professional learning leaders, and researchers. Literacy teachers engaged in 
collaboration are encouraged to reflect on which profile might match their experience. If they find, for example, that their collabo­
rations focus solely on assessment and feedback provision, they may wish to revisit their goals working with other teachers. On the 
other hand, professional development leaders may find that assessment and feedback provision are key areas of interest for

collaborating literacy teachers, so might be prioritized within mandatory professional learning programs but then might be expanded 
and built upon by addressing other instructional practices for sustained, impactful professional learning. Finally, for researchers 
investigating teacher collaboration, it is hoped that the specific teacher collaboration profiles found in this study and their connection 
to instructional practices could be employed as frameworks to explore even more in-depth understandings of teacher collaboration and 
professional learning. 
6. Limitations and directions for future research 
Three key limitations of this study could be supplemented by future research. First, regression analysis is required to establish any 
causal relationship between teacher collaboration and use of chosen instructional practices. Although our results suggest differences in 
the use of instructional practices among the latent groups with different level of teacher collaboration, we have not explored whether 
teacher collaboration had directly or indirectly influenced the use of instructional practices. Such an analysis would draw clear 
directional guides on the effect of teacher collaboration on improving the use of instructional practices in detail. 
Moreover, as the PISA separates student and teacher surveys, matching teachers’ data with students’ data is difficult (Gil-Izquierdo 
& Cordero, 2018). Yet the relationships between data from teachers and data from students could yield direct correlations that may 
provide insights on the effectiveness of teacher collaboration on improving both teacher praxis and learner outcomes. Therefore, 
research that directly links the teachers’ reported level of teacher collaboration and their students’ achievement data and use of 
instructional practices should be collected at the classroom and school levels. 
Finally, there are inherent issues with all self-report questionnaires. It is possible, for example, that due to social desirability 
response (Paulhus, 1984), teachers may have responded positively on all of the survey items, creating higher collaboration and 
instructional practice scores. It is recommended that future research aim to bolster insights gained through the PISA data through 
qualitative methods such as direct observation of teachers’ collaboration and instructional practices.
