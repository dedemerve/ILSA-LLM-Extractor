Artiﬁcial Intelligence to Support Writing
Outcomes for Students With Disabilities
Samantha R. Goldman1, Sean J. Smith1, and Adam Carreon2
Abstract
Students with and without disabilities consistently fail to meet established writing benchmarks, highlighting the urgent need for
intervention and innovation in this critical area. According to the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP), key
criteria for assessing writing include the development of ideas, organization of ideas, and language facility and conventions. One
solution to improve writing outcomes is to leverage artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) tools to support struggling writers. The purpose of
this article is to deﬁne AI, examine its current integration into tools used by teachers to support struggling writers, and provide a
crosswalk between NAEP criteria and AI tools to enhance writing interventions.
Keywords
artiﬁcial intelligence, writing, national assessment of educational progress
Students with Disabilities Struggle
with Writing
Mr. Patel, an upper elementary special education teacher,
knows that writing is an essential skill for his students, as it is
how their knowledge is often assessed. However, he struggles
to effectively support their writing needs, leading to ongoing
student struggles. Recognizing these obstacles, Mr. Patel is
determined to ﬁnd new practices to improve his students’
writing outcomes.
Students with high-incidence disabilities (SWDs) often
struggle with academic writing tasks (Graham et al., 2017,
2018, 2020). According to the Simple View of Writing
(Berninger & Amtmann, 2003), writing involves three key
processes: transcription skills (encoding), self-regulation and
executive functioning, and drafting (idea generation and
translation). SWDs may encounter barriers to writing due to
any of these areas. For instance, traditional encoding difﬁ-
culties include visualizing and forming letters, coordinating
hand movements, and using writing utensils effectively (Seyll
& Content, 2020; SMET, 2020). Additionally, challenges in
self-regulation and executive functioning skills impact a
student’s goal setting, planning, and self-monitoring abilities.
These challenges are compounded by issues related to the
writing process, which encompasses planning, drafting, and
revising (Graham et al., 2017). SWDs may struggle with
working memory, organization, and idea generation during
this process (Dawson et al., 2019; Graham et al., 2017). The
combination of challenges with these skills impacts the
writing outcomes for SWDs.
Assessing Writing
When discussing writing outcomes for all students, but par-
ticularly SWDs, researchers look to the National Assessment
of Educational Progress (NAEP), part of the National Center
for Education and Statistics (NCES). The NAEP writing as-
sessment is the national writing assessment that measures
writing outcomes for students in grades 4, 8, and 12 (NAEP,
2023). It focuses on three domains of writing: (1) development
of ideas, (2) organization of ideas, and (3) facility and con-
ventions. When scores were last released in 2012, 95% of 8th
grader SWDs were not meeting the writing proﬁciency
benchmarks (NAEP, 2012). And, though scores have not been
released since, data continue to indicate consistent writing
struggles (Harris & McKeown, 2022).
To support SWDs and those who struggle with writing,
researchers recommend using evidence-based practices for
writing (Troia, 2014). These include: (1) providing time for
free writing, (2) focusing on products, (3) learning through
writing, (4) assessing and providing feedback, and (5) using
technology in instruction (Troia, 2014). Among these

surrounding AI in instruction, he is intrigued by its potential to
support his students and their particular needs.
Writing and Artiﬁcial Intelligence
Among the emerging technologies with the potential to cause
massive shifts in the ways in which we teach writing is ar-
tiﬁcial intelligence (AI; Langreo, 2023). With the launch of
ChatGPT, many teachers feared that students would no longer
need to learn to write, as they would be turning to the gen-
erative language model (e.g. Gemini, ChatGPT, Copilot) to
compose their essays for them (Klein, 2023). Students, ed-
ucators, and humans alike are in awe of the efﬁciency and
accuracy which generative language models (GLMs) can
output essay responses. They can write to a speciﬁed word
count, use a speciﬁc voice/ style, and even include citations
(Open.AI, 2023). With that said, these essays are far from
perfect. Findings from a recent study comparing essays written
by humans to those written by ChatGPT showed that the
average score of both groups was a “C” (Baˇsi´c et al., 2023).
The AI-generated essays lacked individuality, creativity and
require reﬁnement to meet the task. Additionally, GLMs
hallucinate, that is it makes up information, citations, etc., that
do not exist, passing them off as true or real (Diliberti et al.,
2024). While GLMs can output complete essays, we propose
that teachers teach students to use the GLMs as a “sidekick” to
their writing, teaching them how to leverage it as a support.
Therefore, the purpose of this article will be to explain what AI
is, describe the ways it is already integrated into tools edu-
cators are using to support struggling learners, particularly
those with disabilities, and new and emerging tools since the
launch of ChatGPT that have the potential to transform the
ways in which we support these students.
The Controversy Around AI
The launch of ChatGPT and the mainstreaming of AI that
ensued as a result has been cited as one of the most disruptive
educational technologies with the potential to upset the ways
in which teachers teach and students learn (Marino et al.,
2023). Whereas AI has been used in school settings previously
(e.g., Amazon’s Alexa, ALEKS, TurnItIn.com), the launch of
ChatGPT in November of 2022 and the subsequent surge of
AI-based tools (e.g., Gemini, Grammarly Go) led to many
preK-12 school districts to quickly consider and for many, to
declare a ban on AI-based tools during the 2022-2023 aca-
demic year (Klein, 2024). Initially, some educators feared with
the launch of ChatGPT that, students would use this tool to
complete classwork (e.g., essays, writing assignments),
passing the AI-generated product as their own learning out-
come (Langreo, 2023). In response, companies such as
TurnItIn, developed AI tools to attempt to detect the use of AI
in student products.
Conversely, the sudden explosion of AI should lead
teachers, researchers, and families to consider how AI can
facilitate instruction, aid learning, assist in product generation,
and a host of other supports involved in preK-12 instruction/
learning. While the initial reaction by school districts and
classroom educators might have been to ban its use (e.g.,
ChatGPT), the increase of AI tools is evidence that the genie
cannot be returned to the bottle and forgotten. The simple use
of the website “There’s an AI For That” (see https://
theresanaiforthat.com/) offers evidence that AI tools are and
will continue to be available across and for numerous learning
and instructional areas/needs. According to recent forecasts
(P&S Intelligence), educational investments in AI will balloon
from $2.13 billion in 2022 to $25.77 billion by 2030. Nearly
half or more of that expenditure is currently accounted for in
North America to offset teacher burden with respect to ad-
ministrative tasks, conduct data analysis and predictive ana-
lytics, offer virtual and augmentative learning opportunities,
and overall, to further personalized learning tools and options

Deﬁning Artiﬁcial Intelligence
Recognizing the need to explain how AI works and make it
relatable to his elementary students with disabilities, Mr. Patel
uses a story about a dragon named ChatGPT. This dragon eats
all the data on the internet, without caring if it’s true or not.
After eating, the dragon goes back to his cave and answers
people’s questions based on the data he ate. He can’t eat new
data whenever he wants, so sometimes his answers are based
on old information. Occasionally, the dragon is let out to eat
new data, then returns to his cave to answer more questions.
AI in its most basic sense is a computer or digital system
capable of performing tasks that previously were reserved for
human intelligence or capacity (Ruiz & Fusco, 2024). While AI
operates through various techniques, the most common meth-
odology, since the introduction of ChatGPT, is the GLM, spe-
ciﬁcally generative pre-trained transformers (GPTs). GPTs are
models that are trained on large datasets and use a predictive
model to create new text (Basham et al., 2023). GPTs may be the
current trend in education, however, other types of AI are
commonly used in classrooms to support teacher and student
outcomes. Table 1 offers a further breakdown of the various AI
systems, including a deﬁnition and examples of current products.
For example, programs such as ALEKS (see Aleks.com/),
Freckle (see Freckle.com), Achieve3000 (see achieve3000.-
com), and Khan Academy (see khanacademy.org), use a type
of AI known as intelligent tutoring systems. These programs
independently adjust the curriculum to match each student’s
current instructional level (Ruiz & Fusco, 2024). This allows
students to work at their own pace, often with audio and visual
instructions and feedback, without requiring the teacher to
create the intervention.
Natural language processing (NLP) models, that is, ma-
chine learning that understands, interprets, and can generate
human language (Marino et al., 2023) are also often used in
classrooms to support teacher and student output and out-
comes. In recent years, many of us think of Siri and Alexa, the
recommendation system within our streaming service (e.g.,
Netﬂix), growing features within our autonomous vehicles,
and many more applications impacting all facets of our life and
how we interact with society. In the classroom, teachers use
Alexa to set timers for centers, play calming music, and ﬁgure
out the weather during morning meetings (Hales et al., 2019).
Use in Learning
Various AI-based tools have been and are continuing to be
used by teachers and their students to further instruction and
learning, and improve overall teaching methods and in turn,
student learning outcomes. For over a decade, schools have
adopted various content and learning management systems
embedded with AI tools to facilitate personalized learning
(e.g., Schoology, IXL; Goldman et al., 2024). These tools
often feature customizable aids to adapt the content, supports,
and learning material based on student data. Likewise,
adaptive learning platforms (e.g., ALEKS, Freckle) use al-
gorithms to monitor student progress recommending in-
structional pathways for subsequent exercises and learning
resources (Goldman et al., 2024). With the introduction of AI
data analysis and predictive analytic tools (e.g., Powerschool,
Lightspeed Systems, and Instructure ), teachers and parents
have access to large amounts of student data. These data can
be used to identify patterns, understand student performance,
and provide suggestions for next-step interventions. Overall,
AI-based solutions act as teaching assistants, automating the
grading and assessment process, streamlining “paperwork”
and similar administrative tasks, and in turn, altering the way

Prior to the introduction of ChatGPTand similar AI tools in
late 2022, educators, students, and related constituents were
often not recognizing these tech-based tools as AI. Instead,
many of these digital solutions are seen as instructional or
educational technologies and for those with disabilities, as-
sistive technologies, digital tools that further individualize or
personalize the instructional process for the educator and the
learning experience for the student. Common supports for
students with learning disabilities including predictive text
(e.g., Co:Writer, Read&Write) and text-to-speech (e.g., Nat-
ural Reader) are based in AI. But these AI processes take place
“behind the scenes”. Today, however, with the ongoing in-
troduction of AI centric-solutions, educators, students, and the
accompanying family members or adults in the home, are
appreciating what AI is and the role it has had and will
continue to have in preK-12 education (Cardona et al., 2023).
The extensive capabilities of generative AI represent both
signiﬁcant strengths and notable challenges. ChatGPT, Co-
pilot, Gemini, and the like are capable of supporting students
with disabilities, and teachers, with various tasks, including
lesson plan development, math problem solving, and writing.
However, effective and efﬁcient use of these systems requires
time to learn and interact with the systems, as well as an
understanding of prompt engineering (Park & Choo, 2024).
Other tools, such as Magic School, Brisk, and DiffIt, leverage
the power of generative AI without the need for sophisticated
prompting. Thus, while educators could use these common
generative AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT, CoPilot) to support stu-
dent writing outcomes, the tools addressed in this paper are
more specialized and student-friendly, to ease the burden of
prompt engineering.
AI Literacy
After hearing the story of ChatGPT the Dragon, Mr. Patel’s class
discusses AI Literacy, highlighting the need to critically evaluate
the AI they use. One student shares that, like the dragon, AI can
eat all sorts of information. Another explains the need to be careful
with what information they feed the AI.
According to Digital Promise, AI Literacy is deﬁned as the
knowledge and skills necessary to evaluate, understand, and
use AI safely and effectively (Mills et al., 2024). As special
education teachers consider integrating AI into their writing
curriculum, it is imperative that they can critically evaluate
each tool with regard to areas such as data privacy, use and
accessibility, and purpose (Lee et al., 2024). To help guide
educators in assessing these tools, organizations have put forth
rubrics several resources. For instance, Common Sense Media
has developed AI principles, training, and guides for both
educators and families. These principles are putting people
ﬁrst, and prioritizing fairness, trustworthiness, and safety
(Common Sense Media, 2024). Additionally, they have cu-
rated and rated several AI tools using their rubric to support
educators with making informed decisions about the tools they
choose to, or not to, use. Other organizations, such as Mi-
crosoft and Experience AI (through The Raspberry Pi
Foundation) have developed teacher guides, lessons, and other
supports to help educators assess the quality of AI tools.
Using AI to Develop Writing Skills
While the NAEP may not currently be assessing writing,
indications point to the further integration of technology, and
considerations for the impact AI will continue to have on
writing with the next planned NAEP assessment in 2032
(NAEP, 2023). As we consider how to prepare special edu-
cation students to meet writing benchmarks, centering on the
existing NAEP rubric remains essential, as these criteria
promote behaviors of good writers (Fabrizio et al., n.d.). The
remainder of this article will explore various AI-based tools
that can support special educators in developing needed skills
in their struggling writers. Taken from the NAEP writing
criteria, these skills include the development of ideas, orga-
nization of ideas, and language facility and conventions.
While the NAEP criteria are designed to be evaluative, they
depend on the essential components of the writing process—
brainstorming and planning, drafting, editing, and revising —
to produce a cohesive composition. In the following sections,
each of these skills are deﬁned and recommendations for AI
tools to support the development of these skills are provided.
Finally, we provide a step-by-step guide for integrating AI
tools into the writing process through the lens of the NAEP
criteria (see Table 2).
Step One: Brainstorming, Planning, and the
Organization of Ideas
The organization of ideas domain is deﬁned as logical and
effective text structure, coherence between and within para-
graphs, and a maintained focus through the response (NAEP,
2023). This domain is broken into three categories. First is the
organization of ideas. This includes logically arranging ideas
into sentences and paragraphs that are coherent. This element
is essential because it establishes a clear presentation and
progression of ideas. Without organization, readers may be-
come confused or frustrated. The second element is logical
text structure. The ﬁnal element is coherence and focus. This
element ensures that included ideas are relevant to the pur-
pose, topic, and audience.
Organization of ideas is a challenge for SWDs, as they tend
to produce compositions that are disorganized and include
repetitive and/or irrelevant ideas (Murnan & Cornell, 2023).
Self-regulation of writing, another skill with which SWDs
struggle, is a key component of this domain. Self-regulation
includes skills such as managing attention and planning

Traditional outlines and graphic organizers are elevated

Step Two: Drafting and Development of Ideas
The development of ideas domain includes providing details
and examples within a composition relative to the purpose and
audience (NAEP, 2023). Skills involved in this domain in-
clude (1) providing depth and complexity of ideas, (2) ana-
lyzing and synthesizing ideas and thinking, and (3) developing
ideas through speciﬁc details and examples (NAEP, 2023).
Strong writers can develop ideas within text that illustrate their
points and help the reader to understand the topic (NAEP,
2023).
The development of Ideas relies heavily on a student’s
transcription skills. Students must be able to formulate an idea
and record it onto their document. This can be taxing on the
working memory (Dawson et al., 2019), as students must
remember their ideas, form them into coherent sentences, and
remember the words and letters that make them up. The ability
to accurately transcribe ideas is a signiﬁcant predictor of
writing achievement (Haug & Klein, 2018). Additionally,
SWDs struggle with quality, ideation, output, and knowledge
about writing when compared to their typically developing
peers (Graham et al., 2017).
Educational technologies that support the development
of ideas include word processing supports such as speech-
to-text and predictive text. These tools help struggling
learners and SWDs to generate and draft their ideas. Several
of these tools have been enhanced with AI, such as QuillBot
(see quillbot.com) and Co:Writer (see learningtools.don-
johnston.com). Quillbot helps with idea generation by
providing a platform for students to research and write in
the same window. Beyond idea generation support, it can
paraphrase, summarize, check citations, proofread, and
spell check. Co:Writer, a Don Johnston product, supports
struggling writers with idea generation through text pre-
diction. This tool uses a natural language processor to

Step Three: Editing, Revising, and Language Facility
and Conventions
The ﬁnal domain is language facility and conventions (NAEP,
2023). It is broken into three categories. First is sentence
structure and variety. This includes altering the length and
sentence type throughout the composition, which makes it
easier to read and more interesting. Next are word choice,
voice, and tone. This includes choosing words that meet the
overall purpose and adapting the tone to the audience. The
ﬁnal category is conventions which includes grammar, me-
chanics, and usage.
SWDs struggle with this domain because it requires them to
reread, revise, and make corrections to their work. In fact, it is
the least preferred part of the writing process (Evmenova &
Regan, 2019). These skills are taxing on working memory
(Graham & Harris, 2019), as SWDs are expected to evaluate
their work (Evmenova & Regan, 2019). This evaluation in-
cludes checking for wordiness, ﬁxing errors related to
punctuation and spelling, and ensuring their work is coherent,
clear, and concise. At an even more basic level, SWDs who
struggle with writing also struggle with reading (Graham,
2020). Thus, the emphasis on decoding and comprehension
skills makes this domain even more difﬁcult for SWDs.
Technology exists and has existed to support these needs
from embedded spell- and grammar checkers to more ad-
vanced and intuitive supports. Tools like Ginger (see Gin-
gerSoftware.com) and Grammarly (see Grammarly.com)
provide additional supports to SWDs, beyond the embedded
tools. Ginger provides context-based corrections, alternatives
for wordiness, synonyms, and spelling and grammar checkers.
Ginger also boasts AI technology that analyzes text at the
sentence level, rather than word level, which provides a more
holistic view of the composition and the errors that it might
contain. Additionally, the AI supports its ability to provide
suggestions for phonetic spellings, confused letter sequences,
incorrect use of homophones, run-on sentences, and text-to-
speech. Grammarly provides many of the same supports and
can detect tone, identify the audience and ﬁx the formality of
the language, enhance word choice, check for clarity and
conciseness, and provide real-time feedback.
Newer tools that have launched since ChatGPTwent public
to support this domain include WonderAI (see https://www.
getwonderai.com), and EditGPT (see editgpt.app). WonderAI
is a Chrome extension that edits for tone, length, spelling,
purpose, target audience, and style. This extension works on
top of the internet browser, meaning that students can use it
with Docs or emails. Students, ﬁrst, need to type their
composition. Next, they highlight and select their text. This
will trigger the extension’s icon. When students click on the
icon, the options to edit their composition will pop up.
WonderAI will make edit suggestions that they can choose to
accept, reject, or see other options.
EditGPT is another Chrome extension, which is also
available for Edge and Firefox, that assists in revising and
editing. What sets EditGPT apart is that it uses track
changes, which allows teachers and students to have dis-

enhance the composition. To use this extension, students

navigate to ChatGPT. They will need to type the prompt:
“proofread this” and paste their essay into the chat box.
Finally, turn the extension on in the menu bar. Alternative
prompts include “proofread this but only ﬁx grammar”,
“proofread this, improve clarity and ﬂow”, and “rewrite
this, improving prose” (see chrome.google.com/webstore/

As Mr. Patel and his students worked through these steps, he
noticed improvements in their writing quality. Moreover, his
students grew more conﬁdent in their abilities as writers. They

Conclusion
As our article has sought to illustrate, AI is transforming
the way teachers can instruct, students can learn, and how
both educators and their learners can produce products to
facilitate and personalize the educational experience.
While AI-based tools are not new, their capacity to produce
information and content are, dare we say, revolutionary.
The speed and ease of content development, coupled with
the varied application of AI offer users the capacity to
perform tasks including learning, problem-solving, com-
prehension, and decision-making at a level previously
unseen. AI is disrupting our typical or traditional preK-12
education model. In the area of writing, particularly for
students with identiﬁed writing challenges, this disruption
may very well be a positive outcome. For educators, AI
requires further consideration in how to align student
writing difﬁculties with what AI can do. If AI provides a
solution to a writing task, educators are charged with
considering ways to use this tool in a manner that supports
the learner’s engagement in the writing process and in the
end, empowering them further to be independent writers.
While AI’s capacity will continue to grow, change, and
further enhance what is possible in support of student
writing, educators hopefully will see this as a continuation
of the digital tools being developed and made available to
support struggling learners and their peers with identiﬁed
disabilities. These instructional and assistive technologies
(including AI) are often essential if our students are to be
successful, independent, empowered, and self-determined
learners.
