A B S T R A C T
This research aims to analyze how youth citizenship manifests diﬀerently according to their socioeconomic
background and identify the factors involved in the interaction between socioeconomic background and youth
citizenship. The sample was divided into three socioeconomic background groups of the upper, middle, and
lower according to household monthly income and parent academic attainment. Then, the diﬀerences in con-
sequences pertaining to youth citizenship were identiﬁed. Both cognitive and aﬀective-behavioral citizenship
were found to be lowest with youths belonging to the lower group, and increased with the socio-economic
background indicators of the family. On the other hand, an analysis of the factors that aﬀected youth citizenship
in relation to the socioeconomic background of the family showed that higher self-esteem, mature occupational
view, and good peer relations were correlated with higher levels of youth citizenship. In particular, youths in
lower groups showed greater levels of citizenship with the increase in the number of books read and the de-
mocratic pedagogical methods utilized within the classroom.
1. Introduction
Democracy is a form of a government that requires its participants
to possess a concept known as citizenship to guarantee its existence and
strength (Dietz, 1992). Thus, citizenship is an essential component in
the development of modern civilization and society. Diﬀerent ages and
societies demanded various characteristics of citizenship from their
participants, and the education thereof was traditionally divided into
three areas: knowledge, value, and function. Such education includes
the knowledge on the principles of political structure and process, basic
values of the political community, and political functions facilitating
eﬀective participation in a democratic society (Butts, 1980; Parker,
2003). Recently, active participation of students has become a heavily
emphasized component of citizenship training, reﬂecting the idea that
participating in solving the problems of the community, community
action, and deliberate and direct participation can help develop the
civic society (Parker, 2003; Walzer, 1992). That is, the particular
manifestation of citizenship demanded by the contemporary society
emphasizes the internalization of values, solution of social problems,
and active participation in community development over its knowl-
edge-based aspect. Students equipped with such a sense of citizenship
can serve as promoters of democratic ideals and decision-makers deli-
vering the values of social justice or human rights. They can also attain
critical thinking and eﬀective communication skills, and, thus, proac-
tively project their political voices within their community.
Family background is crucial in building the basic competencies
that allow the youth to eﬀectively engage with the society of the future.
Socialization within the family unit teaches youths how to actively
fulﬁll the roles demanded by the community, including that of a citizen,
and their personal responsibility within the political sphere (Ichilov,
1988; Schulz, Ainley, Fraillon, Kerr, & Losito, 2010). Youths tend to
show similarities with their beliefs on social values and political or-
ientation (Bengtson, 1975; Jennings, Stoker, & Bowers, 2009). This is
interpreted not as a result of direct transmission of values, but rather
the reﬂection of commonalities within their living sphere (Bengtson,
1975). Civic stimulation of their family background, which helps build
a sense of citizenship in the youths, can be found in diﬀerent forms such
as books and newspapers within the house, political discussions be-
tween parents and children, and conversations on social issues
(Jennings et al., 2009; Lipset, 1960; McDevitt & Chaﬀee, 2000; Verba,
Burns, & Schlozman, 2003). Familial contexts that aﬀect the develop-
ment of youth citizenship can often be traced to the socioeconomic
status of the family. Many previous studies have pointed to the eﬀect of
the family's socioeconomic status in the development of diﬀerent as-
pects of citizenship, such as political orientation, political tolerance,

Family socioeconomic status has a powerful inﬂuence on the de-
velopment of citizenship for youths. For instance, the social context of
Korea is characterized by the increasing polarization of society, income
inequalities, and education gap, which may lead the upcoming gen-
eration of adults to develop a gap in their political competence and
even their civic function. However, the political socialization of the
youth also takes place through a variety of other channels, such as peer
groups, youth groups, schools, and the media. Thus, diﬀerences in
youth citizenship from varying family socioeconomic backgrounds may
be mitigated with structural solutions within the society. Political,
pedagogical, and public policy interventions can limit the determining
power of family background on the development of citizenship for
students. School education is fundamentally a matter of public policy
(Peter, Edgerton, & Roverts, 2010). Public policy is compensatory in
that it redistributes resources among individuals and families, espe-
cially focusing on the needs of those who are at greater risk of failing in
a free-market system (Allmendinger, 1989). Thus, public education
should also serve to compensate for the diﬀerences in the amount of
support available to students according to their family backgrounds,
thereby limiting the degree to which the family background of the
youth determines their situation later in life.
This study follows the examples of other studies conducted in
Europe and North America to identify whether the socioeconomic
background of one's family has an impact on the citizenship of the
Korean youths, and identify the methods to limit its eﬀects. First, this
study sought to identify the present picture of family socioeconomic
background in Korea and their eﬀects by identifying the latent class
groups and their forms within the spectrum of family socioeconomic
background. Then, this study determined the actual diﬀerences in the
concept of citizenship among the students between the status groups to
provide an image of the youth citizenship in Korea and the impact of
family background on its development. Finally, this study analyzed the
factors aﬀecting youth citizenship according to the potential groups to
identify the potential methods of support toward the vulnerable groups
in citizenship development. Research questions for this study may be
deﬁned further as thus:
Research question 1. What is the number of latent class groups ac-
cording to the family socioeconomic background of the student, and
what are their forms?
Research question 2. How do the levels of student citizenship diﬀer
according to the family socioeconomic background and the sub-
sequent latent class groups?
Research question 3. What are some of the factors aﬀecting student
citizenship, according to their family socioeconomic background
and the subsequent latent class groups?
2. Review of literature
2.1. Concept and inﬂuence factors of citizenship
Citizenship is a competence whose demand is shaped by the social
relations formed in a speciﬁc temporal and societal context. Thus, ci-
tizenship reﬂects the values of the age and the society, and tend to
change rapidly according to the diﬀerent interests involved (Mo, Kim, &
Song, 2010). Citizenship is related to a number of various concepts like
civic concepts, social capital, civic knowledge, cultural diversity,
gender equality, and human rights. Schulz, Ainley, Fraillon, Losito, and
Agrusti (2016) conceptualized citizenship as a legal status allowing the
individual to become a part of a national or supranational legal com-
munity.
In the “IEA International Civic and Citizenship Education Study
2016 Assessment Framework”, Schulz et al. (2016) deﬁned “citizen-
ship” as “the legal status of being a citizen of a nation-state or
supranational legal community (for example the European Union)” and
“the fact of individuals' participation, or lack of participation, in their
communities”. The civic and citizenship framework identiﬁes these
aspects of cognitive and aﬀective-behavioral contents that should be
considered important learning performances of citizenship education
(Schulz et al., 2016).
In the “IEA International Civic and Citizenship Education Study
2016 (ICCS 2016), Korean youths scored very high on civic knowledge,
but have shown a low level of civic engagement. Korean students' civic
engagement has improved compared to 2009, but is still lower than
those in countries such as Norway and Sweden, and their experience
and willingness to participate outside of school have been very low
since 2009. Also, among Korean teenagers, the level of willingness to
contribute and participate in society as an adult was lower than the
ICCS average (Jang, Sung, Mo, Jin, Choi, Kim, & Hur, 2018). This study
focuses on the aspect of civic engagement, which has been identiﬁed to
be lacking with Korean youths.
A number of studies have associated the acquisition of civic
knowledge with the internalization of the civil society and civic rights
as aﬀective-behavioral concepts are inﬂuenced by a wide range of
factors that exceed the learning environment of the school, such as
families and society (Pancer & Pratt, 1999; Pancer, 2015; Schulz et al.,
2016). Pancer (2015) has proposed that family modeling serves as both
the starting point of citizenship and the basis for its sustainability. That
is, interactions and discussions between the parents and the children
create positive eﬀects toward the acquisition of youth citizenship as
their values and beliefs are relayed to their children and, thus,
strengthens the trust in civil society and its value system. Other factors
that were proposed to aﬀect student citizenship include the democratic
atmosphere of the school, positive interactions within the peer group,
positive eﬀects from neighbors, and the cultural norms of the commu-
nity. These results indicate that the acquisition of student citizenship
must take into account not only the eﬀectiveness of civil training in the
classroom but also the environmental and contextual frameworks sur-
rounding the student.
Some of the factors found to aﬀect the development of youth citi-
zenship are as follows: First, personal factors include gender (Yoon,
2013a, 2013b), self-esteem (Kim & Kim, 2017), voluntary service ex-
perience (Eun, 2002), academic achievement (Son, 2012), occupational
view (Kwak & Moon, 2010), educational goal (Isac, Maslowski,
Creemers, & van der Werf, 2014), the degree of attention to political
and social issues(Isac et al., 2014), and reading (Kim & Kim, 2017).
Girls were reported to generally have greater levels of citizenship than
boys, while having higher self-esteem was correlated with higher levels
of youth citizenship. Occupational view, or the attitude and thought
toward one's job and occupation, is often said to reﬂect the perspective
on life. Thus, having a more developed sense of occupational view
points to a somewhat self-evident trend of students with a robust sense
of values having a greater sense of citizenship as well.
Family factors also have a great inﬂuence on the development of
youth citizenship. Isac et al. (2014) proposed that family factors have
greater inﬂuence over those of school for student citizenship and,
thereby, emphasized the importance of family background on the de-
velopment of youth citizenship. A prominent discussion within the ef-
fect of family factors on the development of youth citizenship is the
inﬂuence of socioeconomic status (SES) of the parents. Two of the
studies conducted in Korea with regards to its eﬀect showed contra-
dictory results, one arguing for its eﬀect (Kim, 2012); and the other,
against its eﬀect (Chin, Kim, Lee, & Cho, 1997). The next factor is the
attitude of the parent, where those with supportive attitude were found
to facilitate the development of student citizenship (Heo, 2014), while
those who are more interested in political and social issues were also
found to improve the sense of citizenship of their children (Isac et al.,
2014).

2.2. Socioeconomic background and youth citizenship
Many studies have examined how the gap in citizenship capabilities
among the youth developed, and identiﬁed diversity in the family
background as the main explanation (Beck & Jennings, 1991; Connell,
1972; Davies, 1965). The diversity of the early experiences within the
family unit is linked to the civic knowledge and action of the youths.
The connection between the socioeconomic background of the family
and youth citizenship was extensively discussed in the ﬁeld of early
political socialization (Greenstein, 1965; Hess, 1968; Jennings & Niemi,
1974), but in the latter studies, family socioeconomic status was re-
legated to a mere variable that must be controlled. Recently, however,
the role of family socioeconomic status in the dissemination of civic
orientation, civic action, and citizenship between the generations has
begun to draw more attention (Kim, 2013). Such studies deﬁne the
socioeconomic status of the family as parental academic attainment and
family income (Jennings et al., 2009; Kim, 2013; Shah et al., 2009). An
important socioeconomic factor aﬀecting youth citizenship is the aca-
demic attainment of the parents (Verba et al., 2003). As academic at-
tainment is an enabling factor of the individual's occupation and in-
come, it is eﬀective in identifying the socioeconomic factor of the
family. However, the link among the academic attainment, occupation,
and income is often disrupted in the Korean society, making it diﬃcult
to conclude that parents with high levels of academic attainment
translate automatically to being a privileged family. Thus, familial in-
come is used to identify the economic status of the family, supple-
menting the measure of human capital provided by the academic at-
tainment of the parents.
Studies that have focused on the connection between the socio-
economic status of the family and youth citizenship suggest that the
former aﬀects the content and the quality of family education, hence
aﬀecting the latter (Connell, 1972; Ichilov, 1988; Niemi, Danforth Ross,
& Alexander, 1978). The political socialization of youths, where they
learn to live harmoniously within the community and voice the causes
for a public interest, was found to occur in similar forms within a given
family. This is interpreted as the result of commonalities existing within
the social status of the family rather than that of direct transmission
between generations (Beck & Jennings, 1982).
Thus, the socioeconomic background of a family has a positive ef-
fect on the promotion of youth citizenship, and previous studies con-
ﬁrm that youths with higher family socioeconomic status displayed
higher levels of citizenship as well (Kim, 2013; Shah et al., 2009; Zaﬀ,
Malanchuk, Michelsen, & Eccles, 2003). These results indicate that the
socioeconomic background of the family and the ﬁnancial and non-ﬁ-
nancial resources transmitted to the children can explain a considerable
portion of the youth citizenship gap. However, the family socio-
economic background is not a determinant of youth citizenship. The
socialization of youth and development of their citizenship take place
not only in the family but also across diﬀerent channels such as the
school,
media,
peer
groups,
youth
organizations,
and
schools
(Campbell, 2008; Ehman, 1970; McLeod, 2000). Shah et al. (2009)
likewise demonstrated that students who engage in citizenship-related
activities in the classroom and have a positive inﬂuence from their
peers are not aﬀected by the family's socioeconomic background. In
particular, schools can function as an equalizing factor in preparing
youths from vulnerable families toward civil society by supplementing
their citizenship development (Almond & Verba, 1963; Campbell, 2008;
Ehman, 1970).
Such public policy interventions can ensure that one's family of
origin is not the determining factor of one's sense of citizenship. This
study aims to identify the methods of support that can help mitigate the
determining power of familial support toward the development of
youth citizenship. Hence, this study must ﬁrst verify whether the fa-
mily's socioeconomic status has the determining power over youth ci-
tizenship. According to Kim (2013), the eﬀect of socioeconomic status
on youth citizenship is dependent on the political and social context of
the youth. In societies with greater polarization of wealth distribution,
the eﬀect of higher family socioeconomic status on youth citizenship
was found to be more prominent than the more egalitarian societies.
This is interpreted as the greater inequalities in family socioeconomic
status contributing to the massive diﬀerences in civic empowerment of
the parents, which subsequently results in great diﬀerences in the
amount of civic stimulation that their children are provided with. The
recently increasing polarization of wealth distribution in Korean society
gives credence to the need to verify the eﬀect of family socioeconomic
status on the development of youth citizenship. Furthermore, if there
are indeed great diﬀerences in the empowerment of youth citizenship
from the diﬀerences in family status, the problem of youth citizenship
no longer becomes an individual problem, but rather a collective one
that must be addressed with public policies.
3. Method
3.1. Participants
This study used the data of the Gyeonggi Education Panel Study
(GEPS). The Gyeonggi Education Panel Study (GEPS) is a longitudinal
study that collects and analyzes the educational activities of students
and economic education policies by annually tracking down the sample
panels composed of the initial respondents to the study in 2012. This
study mainly used citizenship items and others in the ﬁfth-year data of
GEPS, which was surveyed for eighth graders in 2016. This study used
the gender of the student items in the ﬁrst-year data of GEPS and the
parent information was analyzed by integrating the necessary parts of
the second, third- and fourth-year data. 6314 eighth graders were
randomly sampled; of them, 5740 (90.0%) were answered. Excluding
cases involving missing values from the analysis, our ﬁnal samples
consist of 4,785 students matched with parents of GEPS.
3.2. Measures and variables
Family socioeconomic background variables used to analyze the
latent classes within the sample were household income and academic
attainment of the guardian. The variable of household income was
given a score from 1 to 6, based on 2016 household income quintiles
(1 = below the 1st quintile, 2 = above 1st quintile, below 2nd quintile;
3 = above 2nd quintile, below 3rd quintile; 4 = above 3rd quintile,
below 4th quintile; 5 = above 4th quintile, below 5th quintile;

IEA's conceptualization of citizenship divides citizenship into the four
parts of civic societies and systems, civic principles, civic participation,
and citizen identity, which are further divided into cognitive and af-
fective-behavioral aspects. This study focused on the aspect of civic
participation to set the result variable as an actual level of participation
exhibited by the student, rather than their conceptual knowledge
thereof. Thus, citizenship in this study was distinguished into cognitive
participation and aﬀective-behavioral participation. Cognitive partici-
pation includes items such as “I believe that we can make a good society
through the power of citizens.”, “I think everyone should vote.”, “I
think it is right to ask for help from others when I am in need.”, “I think
it is right to give up one's seat to the weak on the subway or bus.”, “I
think it is right to show them around, if anyone asks for directions.”
Aﬀective-behavioral participation includes items such as “I have in the
past helped a neighbor or a friend in need.”, “I try to participate in
opportunities for volunteer service, if available.”, “I want to help, when
I see diﬃcult friends or neighbors.”, “People in the neighborhood
should help each other.”, “I help actively when I see a bully.”

personal and school variables. Personal variables include gender, self-

outside school. Self-esteem measures the amount to which the partici-
pants respect themselves, while occupational view measures the ma-
turity of the choice of the participants toward their job. Reading be-

3.3. Statistical analyses
This study is conducted to identify how the diﬀerences in the family
socioeconomic background aﬀect youth citizenship as well as the fac-
tors involved in the relationship thereof. To these ends, this study ﬁrst
sought to classify the latent classes using the family socioeconomic
background variables and examine whether there were diﬀerences be-
tween these latent classes. A mixture model combining distal outcomes
with Latent Proﬁle Analysis (LPA) was utilized for this purpose. Next, a
regression analysis was used to examine the factors aﬀecting citizenship
within each latent class.
The ﬁrst stage of the analysis, latent class classiﬁcation, evaluated
the statistical validity with statistical veriﬁcation, index comparison,
and the ratio of latent classes to verify the number of latent classes.
Statistical veriﬁcation utilized in this study followed the modiﬁed
veriﬁcation method developed by Lo, Mendell, and Rubin (2001),
which conﬁrms the validity of the model by increasing the number of
latent classes involved. Index comparison was conducted with BIC, AIC,
and ABIC coupled with the increasing number of latent classes. The next
step involved conﬁrming the ratio of latent classes to identify whether
there were cases of the smallest latent class being less than 5% in the
ratio (Berlin, Williams, & Parra, 2014).
After conﬁrming the number of latent classes, the relationship of
each latent class with distal outcomes was veriﬁed. When distal out-
comes are included in the model with latent classes during LPA, they
have the potential to aﬀect the classiﬁcation of latent classes as a whole.
To mitigate this eﬀect, the study adopted the 3-step approach of R-
3STEP (Bolck, Croon, & Hagenaars, 2004; Vermunt, 2010) to verify
whether the family socioeconomic background, deﬁned as latent
classes, had any relationship with the citizenship of the students.
Finally, this study analyzed the factors aﬀecting student citizenship
for each latent class. To this end, this study employed regression ana-
lysis classiﬁed according to latent classes deﬁned by family socio-
economic background, allowing the control of independent variables
outside the goal and identiﬁcation of the relative eﬀects of each vari-
able. SPSS and Mplus 8.0 programs were utilized for the analysis.

Korea has a huge socio-economic inﬂuence of families in educa-
tional opportunities and socioeconomic achievements. The recent ex-
pansion of education in Korea has not eased academic achievement and
class diﬀerences, and over the past 20 years, the percentage of upper
classes still remains constant. However, the proportion of the middle
class seems to be constantly changing (Lee, Kim, & Choi, 2016). In
many studies, Korean society was largely divided into three classes
(Chung & Noh, 2007; Ha, Oh, & Song, 2003; Hong, Song, Park, Lee, &
Lee, 2006; Park, Kim, & Jeon, 2016). Even if divided into several layers,
the structure of the middle and lower classes was analyzed without
changing (Song, Lee, & Choi, 2013). In addition, the results of a study
on Korea’s hierarchical nature as a welfare state focused on public
transfer expenditure and taxation are divided into three classes (Yoon,
2013a, 2013b). A study on the subjective sense of hierarchy among
Koreans also showed that the researchers showed a sense of attribution
to three groups of upper, middle and lower, and that Korea’s hierarchy
was also recognized as three groups (Kim, 2000). Thus, in this study, we
chose a three-class model for our subsequent analysis based on the
consistent previous ﬁndings of the upper, middle, and lower classes in
Korea.

(M = 4.344) and academic attainment of the guardian (M = 3.858),
and, thus, was named as the “middle group,” consisting of 2230 par-
ticipants and producing 46.6% of the total. The third latent class
showed high levels in both the monthly income (M = 4.767) and
academic attainment of the guardian (M = 5.184), and thus was named
as the “upper group, ” consisting of 571 participants and producing
12.0% of the total.
4.1.2. Youth citizenship in accordance with socioeconomic background
The analysis of distal outcomes revealed that each group had dif-
ferent levels of citizenship (see Table 3). First, cognitive citizen parti-
cipation was found to be the lowest in the lower group (M = 4.18),
followed by the middle (M = 4.27) and the upper (M = 4.28) groups.
Chi-square test conducted to verify the average diﬀerence in cognitive
citizen participation between the latent class groups validated their
statistical signiﬁcance (=23.64, p < .000).
Aﬀective-behavioral citizen participation was found to be the lowest
in the lower group (M = 3.55), followed by the middle (M = 3.66) and
the upper (M = 3.71) groups. The average diﬀerence of aﬀective-be-
havioral citizen participation between the latent class groups was also
found to be statistically signiﬁcant (=29.97, p < .000)
Thus, students in the lower group showed lower results in both
cognitive and aﬀective-behavioral citizen participation, and the results
were found to be higher with increasing levels of family socioeconomic
background. Average cognitive citizen participation was also found to
be higher than the aﬀective-behavioral in all other groups. This result
conﬁrms those of prior studies, which found that Korean youths tend to
have high levels of civic knowledge but are relatively lacking in the
behavioral aspect.
This study then sought to verify the precise aspects of comparison
that yielded this average diﬀerence. First, in cognitive citizen partici-
pation, the diﬀerences between the lower group and the middle group
(=19.642, p = 0.000), and the lower group and the upper group
(=12.169, p = 0.000) were found to be statistically signiﬁcant, while
that between the middle and the upper group (=0.074, p = 0.785) was
not found to be statistically signiﬁcant. Next, in aﬀective-behavioral
citizen participation, the diﬀerence between the lower group and the
middle group (=17.385, p = 0.000), and the lower group and the
upper group (=22.824, p = 0.000) were found to be statistically sig-
niﬁcant, while that between the middle and the upper group (=3.783,
p = 0.051) was not found to be statistically signiﬁcant.
4.1.3. Factors aﬀecting youth citizenship in accordance with socioeconomic
background
Diﬀerent latent class groups had various factors aﬀecting the citi-
zenship of the student (see Table 4). Five notable results were found in
terms of factors aﬀecting cognitive citizen participation: First, in all
groups, girls had higher levels of cognitive citizen participation than
boys in terms of gender. Second, in all groups, higher levels of self-
esteem, occupational view, and peer relations were associated with
higher levels of cognitive citizen participation. Third, students in the
lower group who showed higher levels of cognitive citizen participation
also had higher reading behavior, interest, and academic achievement.
Fourth, students in the lower group showed higher levels of cognitive
citizen participation with higher levels of democratic pedagogical
methods. Fifth, students in the middle group had higher levels of cog-
nitive citizen participation with higher levels of student-teacher re-
lationship.
Moreover, ﬁve notable results were found in terms of factors af-
fecting aﬀective-behavioral citizen participation (see Table 5): First, in
all groups, girls had higher levels of aﬀective-behavioral citizen parti-
cipation than boys in terms of gender. Second, in all groups, higher
levels of self-esteem, reading behavior, and participation in voluntary
service activities outside the school were associated with higher levels
of aﬀective-behavioral citizen participation. Third, in all groups, higher
levels of aﬀective-behavioral cognitive citizen participation were as-
sociated with higher levels of student-teacher and peer relationship.

5. Discussion
The level of youth citizenship serves as an indicator of the possi-
bility of the given society progressing toward a more mature one. In
other words, ensuring the citizenship competencies of the next gen-
eration is critical in steering the development of the given society into
one that shares a sense of social justice and democratic values. Korean
society is characterized by the historic tendency to focus excessively on
the accumulation of wealth in its pursuit of rapid economic growth, and
its lack of attention to the damages toward democratic ideals (Park &
Oh, 2013). Growing into a mature society requires the young genera-
tion of Koreans to be taught with both the values and norms, and their
active participation in the exercise of citizenship and the attention to-
ward issues of social justice.
A study conducted in 2016 by ICCS showed that Korean youths
scored excellently in democratic knowledge, but very low in partici-
pation. This is partly because of the relatively new transition of Korean
society toward the valuation of citizenship education and compe-
tencies. However, another important cause is the overemphasis of
knowledge-based education and the lack of praxis, despite the general
consensus on the importance of citizenship. Shin (2011) showed that
the public education curriculum of Korea focuses extensively in the area
of institutionalization. In particular, education in school focused ex-
cessively on knowledge transmission, rather than practice in daily life.
In other words, the citizenship training oﬀered by the Korean school
system is lacking in the crucial aspect of a democratic citizen, which is
active participation. This led the study to focus on the citizen partici-
pation component of citizen participation and divide it into cognitive
and aﬀective-behavioral aspects. Another problem with Korean citi-
zenship education is the lack of awareness of the need to accept the
diversity in student context and the subsequent needs, as well as the
application thereof (Shin, 2011). This indicates that the oﬃcial edu-
cation system does not oﬀer adequate levels of personalization, which
recognizes the independence of each studying agent, and the lack of
research and practical activities thereof.
Starting from the recognition that a highly inﬂuential component of
student citizenship is the context of the family, this study sought to
identify the methods through which a personalized system of education,
in which the uniqueness and diversity of the students can be re-
cognized, or may be facilitated with. This was the rationale behind the
classiﬁcation of student family socioeconomic background into three
groups using latent class analysis. The lower group was made up of
students with lower levels of family monthly income and the academic
attainment of the guardian, which were, in turn, mid-level and high in
terms of the middle and upper groups. The analysis of diﬀerences in
student citizenship according to these three groups showed that stu-
dents in lower groups had the lowest levels of cognitive and aﬀective-
behavioral citizen participation, with increasing levels to middle and
upper groups, respectively. This suggests that family socioeconomic
background did result in changes to student citizenship. Previous stu-
dies also showed similar results in parent socioeconomic status having a
positive eﬀect on student citizenship (H. Kim, 2014). This shows the
need for educational support that takes into consideration the vulner-
abilities of lower-group students.
Analyzing the changes to factors aﬀecting citizenship according to
the diﬀerences in family socioeconomic background factors revealed
that all groups had a lower average level of aﬀective-behavioral citizen
participation than cognitive citizen participation. This reaﬃrms the
ﬁndings of previous studies, namely that while the students agree upon
the importance of citizen participation for knowledge, their actual

This study divided the participants into three groups in terms of
their family socioeconomic background to take into account the unique
contexts posed by diﬀerent levels of family socioeconomic background
and their eﬀect on citizenship. Afterward, this study sought to analyze
the factors aﬀecting the citizenship of students to identify their im-
plications. The implications of this study are as follows:
First, students from families with lower levels of family monthly
income and the academic attainment of the guardian (hereafter lower
group) generally had lower levels of youth citizenship than other
groups. This shows that students from such vulnerable contexts must be
provided with personalized citizenship education. Most of the attention
regarding family background was conﬁned to its eﬀect on academic
achievement (Jang & Kim, 2015; Ryu & Kim, 2006) rather than youth
citizenship. This, in turn, symbolizes the excessive focus on economic
growth and the inadequate attention given to democratic values and
civil society in Korea. Although it is relatively late, eﬀorts must be
exerted in the public education system to provide adequate support to
students from vulnerable family backgrounds and allow them to enjoy
equal chances in building their citizenship and, thus, contribute to the
creation of a more mature society in Korea.
Second, students in lower groups tended to have greater levels of
cognitive citizen participation with higher levels of reading. Reading
oﬀers an indirect source of experience for students to train a holistic
perspective on their life and a capacity for deep thought. In particular,
students who did not have access to abundant sources of cultural capital
or a variety of experiences can compensate for these losses to an almost
equivalent level of eﬀect through reading. Thus, the school should
promote reading and provide material and human support to facilitate
the reading habits of students belonging to lower groups.
Third, students in lower groups derived greater beneﬁts to their
citizenship development with democratic pedagogical methods. A si-
milar result can be found in Kim (2014), where democratic pedagogical
methods utilizing deliberation were proven to be more eﬀective in
citizenship-building than lecture-based pedagogy. In particular, the
process in which the students discuss the issue actively with each other
and organize their thoughts through active interactions with students
and their peers was found to have a positive eﬀect on the citizenship-
building process. Isac et al. (2014) also found that classes, where
members engage with each other freely in the democratic atmosphere
using critical discussion were eﬀective in promoting youth citizenship.
Active discussions on controversial political and social issues were
particularly eﬀective in promoting student citizenship. The results of
this study reaﬃrmed those of the previous studies. In particular, this
study found that democratic pedagogical methods were especially
helpful in building the citizenship of students in lower groups. This
suggests the need to make the classroom environment of Korea a place
where students can exchange their opinions actively with their peers
and teachers under a democratic atmosphere. Furthermore, schools in
vulnerable districts with a higher ratio of students from low-income
families must be provided with special and active support from edu-
cational policy-makers in creating a democratic classroom environ-
ment.
Building one's citizenship competencies in youth through civic
education is of paramount importance to a democratic society. Without
a sound basis of education during this period, the acquisition of citi-
zenship competencies after adulthood becomes more diﬃcult to
achieve. Realizing a mature democracy requires the participation of
active citizens equipped with citizenship competencies. Hence, schools
must provide a suitable source of citizenship education to build the
citizenship capabilities of the students, while the older generations in
the society must possess a mature sense of citizenship to relay and share
the values of social justice and democratic values. Systematic support
toward citizenship-building for students in vulnerable conditions and
recognition of their diversity must, in turn, be increased as a part of this
eﬀort.
While this study has managed to generate meaningful results, its
exclusive utilization of data from Korea makes its applicability in other
contexts limited at best. Thus, there is a need for further studies to
utilize data from diﬀerent contexts to conﬁrm whether these results can
be replicated and generalized. Changes in youth citizenship through a
longitudinal lens toward adulthood should also be tracked.
