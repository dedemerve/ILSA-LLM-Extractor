Using machine learning to improve Q‑matrix validation
Haijiang Qin1 · Lei Guo1,2
Accepted: 7 April 2023 / Published online: 25 May 2023 
© The Psychonomic Society, Inc. 2023
Abstract
The Q-matrix, which specifies the relationship between items and attributes, is a crucial component of cognitive diagnostic 
models (CDMs). A precisely specified Q-matrix allows for valid cognitive diagnostic assessments. In practice, a Q-matrix 
is usually developed by domain experts, and noted as being subjective and potentially containing misspecifications which 
can decrease the classification accuracy of examinees. To overcome this, some promising validation methods have been 
proposed, such as the general discrimination index (GDI) method and the Hull method. In this article, we propose four new 
methods for Q-matrix validation based on random forest and feed-forward neural network techniques. Proportion of vari-
ance accounted for (PVAF) and coefficient of determination (i.e., the McFadden pseudo-R2) are used as input features for 
developing the machine learning models. Two simulation studies are carried out to examine the feasibility of the proposed 
methods. Finally, a sub-dataset of the PISA 2000 reading assessment is analyzed as illustration.
Keywords  Cognitive diagnosis · Q-matrix validation · Machine learning · G-DINA · Classification
Introduction
Cognitive diagnostic models (CDMs) are multidimensional 
psychometric models used to classify examinees into dif-
ferent attribute profiles according to their mastery or lack 
thereof in various skills through cognitive diagnostic assess-
ments. These skills are commonly latent variables, also 
known as attributes. Each CDM requires a Q-matrix (Tat-
suoka, 1983) that shows the relationships between items and 
attributes. In a cognitive diagnostic test with J items, each 
item has a q-vector (qj) of length K which corresponds to K 
attributes. Each q-entry (i.e., qjk) of qj will typically equal 1 
or 0, indicating whether attribute k is measured by item j. All 
of these qjs come together to form a Q-matrix.
The importance of the Q-matrix is self-evident, as it is 
the basis of CDM analyses. At present, the construction of 
a Q-matrix typically relies on the experience of the domain 
experts. The process of constructing the Q-matrix should 
have a theoretical foundation beforehand, which means that 
experts need to have professional experience while also thor-
oughly reviewing the literature to analyze examinees’ reports 
and rigorously discuss details when something is uncertain. 
However, these practices are often subjective in nature, and 
it is difficult to claim that a Q-matrix constructed using these 
approaches will be accurately specified. In fact, a Q-matrix 
is likely to contain a certain number of misspecifications in 
practical tests (Chiu, 2013; Li & Suen, 2013; Rupp & Tem-
plin, 2008), which can negatively affect the estimation of 
model parameters and classification accuracy of examinees 
(Gao et al., 2017; Im & Corter, 2011), potentially even caus-
ing model misfits (de la Torre, 2008). For this reason, a num-
ber of methods have been developed in recent years in hopes 
of being able to develop a more reliably accurate Q-matrix.
Some studies have explored methods to estimate a 
Q-matrix based on examinees’ direct responses without 
requiring an expert-constructed original Q-matrix. For 
instance, Chen et al. (2018) and Chung (2019) tried to esti-
mate a Q-matrix in a Bayesian framework. They found that 
the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm could 
efficiently estimate the Q-matrix, but that it would be signifi-
cantly negatively affected by a decrease in sample size and 
increase in number of attributes. Furthermore, the MCMC 
algorithm was found to be too time-consuming, generally

based on responses only, it has become more common to 
use empirical Q-matrix validation methods to improve 
Q-matrix validation, with a variety of methods proposed 
in recent years (de la Torre, 2008; Chiu, 2013; de la Torre 
& Chiu, 2016; Wang et al., 2018; Yu & Cheng., 2019; Ma 
& de la Torre, 2020; Nájera, Sorrel, et al., 2021b). De la 
Torre (2008) proposed an empirical method to validate the 
Q-matrix, called the δ method. The value of δj is defined as 
1 − sj − gj, in which sj and gj are both parameters of item j in 
the “deterministic inputs, noisy ‘and’ gate” (DINA; Haertel, 
1989) model. Wang et al. (2018) proposed a joint estimate 
method based on expectation–maximization (EM), using a 
maximum likelihood estimation or a marginal maximum like-
lihood estimation to estimate the best q-vector of each item. 
Yu and Cheng (2019) validated a Q-matrix by minimizing 
the residual-based statistic, which can also capture model 
misfit in cognitive diagnostic assessment. Chiu (2013) devel-
oped a nonparametric method by minimizing the residual 
sum of squares computed from the observed and expected 
responses of examinees. However, these methods are devel-
oped based on the reduced CDMs, particularly the DINA 
model. Because these reduced CDMs all vary in terms of the 
different assumptions involved and their complexity between 
attributes, prior to beginning any new analysis, the most 
appropriate model must be determined (Henson et al., 2009).
To incorporate the aspects of the most widely used reduced 
CDMs, three popular general or saturated CDMs have been 
proposed: the general diagnostic model (GDM; von Davier, 
2005), the log-linear CDM (LCDM; Henson et al., 2009), and 
the generalized DINA (G-DINA; de la Torre, 2011) model. 
Many choose to use these general models from the start, as 
they are considered the more efficient choice, particularly 
when it can be difficult to determine the most appropriate 
model (Ma et al., 2016). Several Q-matrix validation methods 
have been proposed for the saturated model, some of which 
have been demonstrated to work effectively, such as the gen-
eral discrimination index (GDI) method (de la Torre & Chiu, 
2016), the Wald method (Ma & de la Torre, 2020), and the 
Hull method (Nájera, Sorrel, et al., 2021b). The GDI method 
modifies q-entries in the Q-matrix according to whether the 
value of the proportion of variance accounted for (PVAF) 
reaches the predetermined cutoff points. The Wald method 
determines whether every q-entry in the Q-matrix is statisti-
cally necessary by conducting a series of Wald tests. The Hull 
method works by balancing fit and parsimony, and maximin-
ing the st index, which can be computed using the number of 
model parameters and the value of the PVAFs. These three 
methods have certain limitations compared to the general 
model. The GDI and Wald methods share a restriction in that 
they both require cutoff points that may be difficult to deter-
mine; thus, cutoff points for both are usually fixed at 0.95 
(de la Torre & Chiu, 2016; Ma & de la Torre, 2020; Nájera 
et al., 2019; Nájera, Sorrel, et al., 2021b), making it difficult to 
consider changes that the performance of the method suffers 
as a function of various data conditions. In contrast, the Hull 
method addresses these issues and can obtain a more accurate 
Q-matrix, although the full q-vector (represented as qj1∶K ) may 
be rarely selected. Item j with qj1∶K, with all q-entries equal 
to 1, means that all K attributes are measured by item j. This 
method removes all q-vectors that are not part of the convex 
Hull, and the full q-vector is only selected if the origin and 
the full q-vector remain (Nájera, Sorrel, et al., 2021b). This 
means, then, that the full q-vector is not selected as long as a 
convex Hull plot including three q-vectors exists.
In addition to the abovementioned methods that rely on a 
fit index (e.g., PVAF), one other method has recently been 
developed for general CDMs, based on multiple logistic 
regression (MLR-B; Tu et al., 2022). The MLR-B method 
establishes multiple logistic regression with the attributes 
likely to be measured by item j as independent variables, 
and examinees’ responses to the item measured as depend-
ent variables, which yields ­2K − 1 regression formulas. After 
removing all formulas that contain any nonsignificant regres-
sion coefficient, the one with the lowest Akaike information 
criterion (AIC) is selected. The q-vector corresponding to 
that formula is then the suggested q-vector.
Reviewing these methods of Q-matrix validation for the 
saturated model, we noticed that they all pointed to the same 
goal of searching for the most appropriate qj using all pos-
sible q-vectors, but every method followed different rules. 
For instance, the GDI method considers the cutoff point of 
the PVAF value as the most appropriate qj. The Wald method 
holds that either all measured q-entries are necessary, or a 
PVAF larger than 0.95 is the most appropriate qj. Meanwhile, 
the Hull method considers the qj with the best balance of fit 
and parsimony as the most appropriate. The goal of all these 
methods is similar to the recognition or classification task in 
machine learning (ML), which aims to find the most appropri-
ate solution out of all possible answers. As such, the validation 
of a Q-matrix could be considered an ML classification task.
In recent years, ML has become widely used across vari-
ous fields due to its outstanding problem-solving ability. ML 
algorithms learn the rules from data and provide solutions 
to low-efficiency problems, such as in educational testing 
(Gierl et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2009; Zhai et al., 2022). These 
algorithms have also been trialed in cognitive diagnostic 
assessment. For example, Chiu et al. (2009) used K-means 
cluster analysis and hierarchical agglomerative cluster anal-
ysis to cluster examinees with the same attribute profiles, 
which did not require specifying an item response model. 
Xue and Bradshaw (2020) trained three types of artificial 
neural networks to classify examinees, and some of these 
networks performed better than commonly used CDMs, 
including the DINA, reparameterized unified model (RUM),

Machine learning
ML is the study and development of approaches for con-
structing and improving problem-solving systems (or 
machine learning models—MLMs) that perform tasks 
associated with artificial intelligence (AI; Nilsson, 1998; 
Dietterich, 2003). Such tasks involved classification, pre-
diction, regression, and so on. In this paper we focused on 
classification, as Q-matrix validation could be considered a 
classification task. The goal of classification was to learn the 
form of a function C = f(x) by analyzing sample datasets con-
taining a number of (xj, Cj), and each of these satisfying the 
condition Cj = f(xj). In the ML field, the process of learning 
the function is also called “training.” xj is an N-dimensional 
vector (i.e., xj1, xj2, …, xjn, …, xjN) in that each dimension 
is real-valued according to the features of object j. Cj is the 
class which object j belongs to. In the context of Q-matrix 
validation, Cj can be considered one of all the possible 
q-vectors of item j.
Two effective ML methods that can be generalized to 
Q-matrix validation are introduced below.
Random forest
Random forest (RF) is an ensemble learning algorithm 
which combines ntree decision trees. Each of these trees, 
whose nodes are split using a random selection of features 
(Breiman, 2001; Goretzko & Bühner, 2020), is built by boot-
strap samples drawn from the sample dataset. The resulting 
trees are generated by averaging the results of ntree trees to 
avoid over-fitting, rather than depending on a single tree. 
These trees can differ from one another, as only mtry of all 
features are selected at random to build a tree. This makes 
the final outcome more robust with respect to noise. In gen-
eral, mtry takes the value of p/3 (generally used for regression

Feed‑forward neural networks
In fact, there are different topologies of artificial neural net-
works that can be categorized as feed-forward and recurrent

Creating machine learning models 
for Q‑matrix validation
Our next step was to train MLMs for Q-matrix validation. 
The training required empirical data (or sample datasets) to 
learn the function C = f(x). Because of that, both RFs and 
FNNs were supervised learning algorithms, which require 
a labeled dataset. However, this is unavailable in real data 
for cognitive diagnostic assessment. Therefore, we gener-
ated several simulated sample datasets to train the MLMs. 
This practice is consistent with that of Goretzko and Bühner 
(2020), who generated simulated datasets to train MLMs to 
predict the true number of factors in exploratory factor anal-
ysis. Features extracted from our generated sample dataset 
were then fed into the models, and evaluation metrics were 
developed to measure the quality of the MLMs.

Feature engineering and model training
Features were extracted such that they should contain the 
information as to which class the q-vectors of the items 
could be classified into. According to existing methods of 
Q-matrix validation (de la Torre & Chiu, 2016; Ma & de la 
Torre, 2020; Nájera, Abad, & Sorrel, 2021a; Nájera, Sorrel, 
et al., 2021b), both the PVAFs and McFadden pseudo-R2s 
were treated as components for constructing the methods. 
To avoid complex and superfluous input that could inflate 
the required dataset sample size (Ralston et al., 2003; Shai 
& Shai, 2014), only one of either the PVAFs or R2s were 
inputted when training the MLMs.

neural network, the data must be normalized (Duda & Hart, 
1973; Heaton, 2015; Nilsson, 1998; Ralston et al., 2003). 
The purpose of this is to avoid convergence difficulties

simulation studies as well as a real data analysis to further 
examine the performance of the MLMs.
Simulation studies
Two simulation studies comprising a variety of data condi-
tions were conducted. In Study I, we tested the performance 
of the MLMs that we had already trained in the previous 
section of this research using specific cases of training con-
ditions. Study II, meanwhile, used data conditions that were 
quite different in order to explore the robustness and feasibil-
ity of the MLMs when the conditions were different from 
their original training conditions. The factors considered in 
these two studies are summarized in Table 4.

Method
(1)	 Design
A mixed factorial design was employed, using the follow-
ing variables: number of attributes (K), item quality (IQ), 
rate of Q-matrix misspecification (QM), attribute distribu-
tion (AD), number of examinees (N), and ratio of number of 
items to attributes (JK). The GDI method, the Hull method, 
and the four validation methods based on MLMs were con-
ducted using the original Q-matrix.
The number of measured attributes in each Q-matrix was 
fixed at three, five, or seven. The Q-matrices simulated in 
this stage were comparable to those generated when devel-
oping the sample datasets. Attribute distribution was set as 
uniform, multivariate-normal, or higher-order (Chiu, 2013). 
A uniform distribution means that the occurrence probabili-
ties of all attribute profiles are equal. The multivariate-normal 
distribution was the same as the distribution used to gener-
ate the sample datasets in Section 5. Meanwhile, high-order 
distribution is another widely used distribution (Chiu, 2013; 
de la Torre & Chiu, 2016; de la Torre & Douglas, 2004; Ma 
& de la Torre, 2020; Nájera, Sorrel, et al., 2021b). In a high-
order distribution, the probability of mastering attribute k for

Simulation Study II
Design
Similar to Study I, Study II considered six factors: K, IQ, 
AD, QM, N, and JK. The first four factors—K, IQ, AD, and 
QM—were set the same as in Study I. The attribute distribu-
tion and the item quality in Study I had been set in a wide 
enough range. For a cognitive diagnostic assessment, the 
three types of attribute distributions (i.e., uniform, higher-
order, and multivariate-normal) are commonly used in most 
studies. Item quality represents the amount of error fluc-
tuation in the data, which can affect validation method per-
formance. However, the three types of item quality used in 
Study I covered the greatest range of variability (de la Torre 
& Chiu, 2016; Ma & de la Torre, 2020; Nájera et al., 2019; 
Nájera, Sorrel, et al., 2021b; Yu & Cheng, 2019). K and QM 
conditions as used in Study I were capable of indicating how 
validation method performance would change as the number 
of measured attributes or rate of Q-matrix misspecifications 
was increased.
The N and JK in cognitive diagnostic assessments are 
flexible, however, and can vary greatly. Therefore, we 
selected wider ranges for both N and JK for Study II. We 
set N as 200, 1000, and 1500 for small, moderate, and large 
examinee samples, respectively, and set JK as 3 and 10 to 
represent situations with short or long test lengths, respec-
tively. Meanwhile, the Q-matrices used in Study II were dif-
ferent from those used for training the MLMs, comparable 
to those employed by Nájera, Sorrel, et al. (2021b). Each 
Q-matrix contained at least two identical matrices, with each 
item measuring 1, 2, or 3 attributes. The rate of items meas-
uring 1, 2, and 3 attributes was 50%, 25%, and 25%, respec-
tively. The reason for this choice was that the Q-matrices in 
many empirical studies have been very simple (according to 
a review of empirical applications by Sessoms & Henson, 
2018), with most items (i.e., 91.8%) measuring only 1, 2, or 
3 attributes (Nájera, Abad, & Sorrel, 2021a).
Finally, the dependent variables used in Study II were the 
same as those used in Study I.

Discussion
When conducting cognitive diagnostic assessments, the 
Q-matrix is key to developing CDMs to achieve accurate 
attribute profile classifications. Due to subjective judgment, 
however, the Q-matrix as developed by domain experts can 
be mis-specified, which can lead to significant negative 
impacts on the classification of examinees, item parameter 
estimates, and model–data fit (Chiu, 2013; de la Torre, 2008; 
Gao et al., 2017; Im & Corter, 2011; Li & Suen, 2013; Rupp 
& Templin, 2008). Researchers have already proposed vari-
ous validation methods to improve Q-matrix accuracy; how-
ever, most of these methods are only applicable in reduced 
models, and existing generalized methods (such as the GDI 
and the Hull methods) have certain limitations. Taking a 
new perspective, then, this paper proposed using MLMs to 
validate the Q-matrix.
The GDI method requires setting a cutoff point to either 
a fixed value or by means of limited predictive formulas 
(Nájera, Abad, & Sorrel, 2021a; Nájera et al., 2019; Nájera, 
Sorrel, et al., 2021b), causing a lack of robustness. Further, 
existing research (as well as our current findings) show that 
the performance of the GDI method deteriorates substan-
tially with the variation of different data conditions (Ma & 
de la Torre, 2020;Nájera, Abad, & Sorrel, 2021a ; Nájera, 
Sorrel, et al., 2021b). This may be because the cutoff point 
becomes inappropriate when the data conditions change. 
Considering USR and OSR values, as presented in Figs. 6 
and 8, the USRs decreased as the OSRs increased rapidly 
when K and QM increased and item quality and N decreased. 
Our findings of abnormally large OSRs (e.g., OSRs for the 
GDI method were all over 0.4 when K = 7 in the simula-
tion Study I) indicate that the Q-matrix is over-specified. 
To improve the effectiveness of the GDI validation method, 
the cutoff point should be lower when the number of attrib-
utes and the QM increase and item quality and sample size 
decrease. However, the cutoff point is fixed at 0.95 in most 
existing studies, including in this paper. Additionally, the 
predictive formula as proposed by Nájera et al. (2019) may 
in fact be limited to the specific simulation conditions under 
which it was generated (Nájera, Abad, & Sorrel, 2021a; 
Nájera, Sorrel, et al., 2021b).
Although the Hull method addressed the problems caused 
by the cutoff point and did perform well in the two simula-
tion studies, it ultimately appeared to be too strict in its 
restriction of number of attributes, as it resulted in the largest 
USR results across all validation methods tested in both 
Study I and Study II. This could be due to the difficulty of 
adding an additional “1” into q-vectors when a certain num-
ber of attributes had already been specified. The core for-
mula of the Hull method, namely st = (fk−fk−1)∕(npk−npk−1)
(fk+1−fk)∕(npk+1−npk), 
results in the selection of the largest st, whose value is deter-
mined by both the variation in the number of model param-
eters and the variation in PVAF values. However, the num-
ber of model parameters (i.e., 2K∗ ) increases exponentially, 
while this is not the case of with PVAFs, which are always 
between 0 and 1. In other words, as the number of model 
parameters increases, the number of model parameters 
grows faster and faster, while the PVAF values grow more 
slowly as it approaches 1, making st values decrease and 
resulting in a convex Hull plot. This is why the Hull method 
usually under-specifies the Q-matrix. Note that the USRs of 
the Hull method were notably higher when K = 3 than when 
K = 5 or 7 in Study II (see Fig. 8.). This could be because 
the Hull method rarely suggests a fully specified q-vector. 
To verify this possibility in Study II, we recorded the num-
ber of fully specified q-vectors suggested by the Hull method 
when K = 3 (details in Supplemental Material: https://​osf.​
io/​4n3tj/), and found that less than 5% of the items were 
suggested as fully specified q-vectors, while roughly 25% of 
the items were fully specified in the simulated data in total. 
Theoretically, the Hull method was more likely to suggest a 
fully specified q-vector when K = 3, because only the q-vec-
tor containing two attributes needs to be removed from the 
Hull plot in this case. However, the ratio of the Hull method 
suggesting items as fully specified q-vectors remained low.
We did not take into account the MLR-B method in the 
studies. This is because the MLR-B method differs from the

1 3
six methods that were included in our studies, as it does not 
require any fit index, such as the PVAF, for example. The 
MLR-B can be seen as an application of ML in Q-matrix 
validation, due to its use of multiple logistic regression; 
however, it differs from the MLMs proposed in the cur-
rent article in that the MLR-B method performs regression 
tasks, while the MLMs in this article perform classification 
tasks. In addition, the MLM methods can directly classify 
the q-vectors of items into one of ­2K − 1 possible q-vectors, 
whereas the MLR-B method requires the comparison of the 
­2K − 1 regression formulas for each item.
Compared to the GDI and Hull methods, the MLMs pro-
posed in this paper all demonstrated superior performance in 
Q-matrix validation, according to the QRR, VRR, USR, and 
OSR values obtained in both simulated and real-data analy-
sis. The MLMs effectively avoided the issue of determining 
an appropriate cutoff point, and their QRRs and VRRs were 
higher than those of the GDI and Hull methods in most of 
the tested data conditions. Two ANOVAs with Bonferroni 
tests were conducted in each of the two simulation studies. 
Study I analysis results showed that the differences between 
each of the four MLMs were not statistically significant, 
while only the QRRs obtained using the RF-P method were 
significantly higher than those of the Hull method. Study 
II analysis results showed that there were no statistically 
significant differences between each of the four MLMs and 
the Hull method. Synthesizing the results of the two simu-
lation studies, the MLM-based methods showed clear evi-
dence of their robustness and feasibility. Overall, the results 
showed that the performance of the four proposed MLMs 
(i.e., RF-P, RF-R, FNN-P, and FNN-R) was similar in both 
of the simulation studies, and the average QRR values of the 
RF-P method were the highest in both studies. Furthermore, 
the real-data analysis results as presented in Section 7 prove 
that the Q-matrix suggested by the RF-P method had the 
best model–data fit of all the validation methods, suggest-
ing that this method should be recommended for primary 
use in practice.
It is worth noting, however, that despite the promis-
ing results of the proposed MLMs, there are nonetheless 
some limitations in the current study that deserve further 
research. First, as with other studies of this type (de la Torre 
& Chiu, 2016; Ma & de la Torre, 2020; Nájera, Abad, & 
Sorrel, 2021a; Nájera, Sorrel, et al., 2021b), the present 
study assumed that the number of attributes of the origi-
nal Q-matrix is fixed or known. However, there is advance 
study to explore the number of attributes in CDMs at present 
(Nájera, Abad, & Sorrel, 2021a). That is to say, we need to 
explore how to validate a Q-matrix when the true number 
of attributes measured by the Q-matrix may not be imme-
diately clear. Second, based on the features inputted during 
training, the MLMs trained in this paper can only validate a 
Q-matrix with the specified number of attributes. For exam-
ple, the MLMs trained with the features that are extracted 
from the Q-matrix with three attributes cannot validate the 
Q-matrix with other numbers of attributes. Although new 
specific MLMs which are capable of validating a Q-matrix 
measuring other numbers of attributes can be conveniently 
trained using the R code we uploaded in OSF (https://​osf.​
io/​4n3tj/), this restriction cannot be ignored in practice. It 
is also worth exploring new procedures to train MLMs that 
may be able to validate Q-matrices with different numbers 
of attributes, so that MLMs only need to be trained once. 
Third, while various machine learning algorithms exist, only 
the random forest and feed-forward neural networks were 
used in this paper. Applying other machine learning meth-
ods such as support vector machine or gradient boosting for 
Q-matrix validation should be addressed in future studies, as 
other algorithms may perform differently. Finally, the PVAF 
and the R2 were used as MLM input features in the current 
study. However, other indicators or statistics, such as Yu 
and Cheng's residual-based statistic (2019), should also be 
discussed in future research.
