The relationship between information literacy and digital self-efficacy in 
teaching information literacy for new teachers in Lithuania
Palmira Peˇciuliauskien˙e
Vytautas Magnus University, K. Donelaiˇcio Str. 58, LT-44248, Kaunas, Lithuania
A R T I C L E I N F O
Keywords:
Digital self-efficacy in teaching
Information literacy
Requalification programme teacher
Undergraduate education student
A B S T R A C T
This study aims to reveal the role of different components of information literacy (e.g. information search and 
evaluation, information processing and dissemination) in beginner teachers’ digital self-efficacy in teaching 
information literacy. Undergraduate students and requalification programme teachers from two Lithuanian 
teacher training universities took part in the study. Confirmatory factor analysis and latent multi-group structural 
equation modelling revealed the statistically significant effects of all information literacy components on digital 
self-efficacy in the teaching of information literacy both groups of teachers. The quantitative data analysis 
showed that two components of information literacy—perceived information evaluation and information pro­
cessing ability—had a strong direct impact on digital self-efficacy in both groups of respondents. This finding 
may help to support the improvement of teacher training and professional development programmes, with a 
focus on teaching information literacy.
1. Introduction
Today’s need for digital competences is driven by advances in 
computing technology and the ever-improving capabilities of digital 
systems and their autonomy based on artificial intelligence (OECD, 
2016b; Parker & Grote, 2020). Digital systems open new opportunities 
for individuals to perform increasingly complex tasks by managing in­
formation and interacting with highly personalised systems (Falck et al., 
2016; Hu et al., 2024; Larson & DeChurch, 2020; OECD, 2016c; Sub­
aveerapandiyan et al., 2024; Trixa & Kaspar, 2024; Vrontis et al., 2021). 
The continuous development of information and communication tech­
nologies requires users to continually update their digital competences. 
Acquiring and continuously improving digital competences is essential 
for successful participation in various fields of activity as part of the 21st 
century (Cosby et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024; Jha, 2023; Manjunatha, 
2023; OECD, 2016a; Ogegbo & Ramnarain, 2022).
The digital transformation of society has become permanent, forcing 
education policymakers to re-evaluate the validity of their educational 
models by updating the digital and information literacy of teachers so 
they can develop new digital skills (Arago et al., 2020; Fern´andez-Otoya 
et al., 2024). Global organisations have proposed several models of 
learners’ information literacy. The United Nations Educational, Scien­
tific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) has identified three key 
components of information literacy: accessing, evaluating, and creating 
information (UNESCO, 2013). The International Educational Achieve­
ment Assessment Association (IEA) has proposed the International 
Computer and Information Literacy Study Assessment Framework 
(ICILS), which identifies four components of learners’ information lit­
eracy: understanding computer use, gathering information, producing 
information, and digital communication (Fraillon et al., 2018). The 
European Digital Competence Framework for Citizens (DigComp) de­
fines the digital competence of citizens (Carretero et al., 2017; Ferrari 
et al., 2014). The main framework for conceptualising the digital 
competence of educators in teaching at the European level is the Dig­
CompEdu (Redecker & Punie, 2017). According to DigCompEdu, in­
formation literacy encompasses a set of abilities: identifying information 
needs, finding data, information and content in digital environments, 
accessing them and navigating between them and creating and updating 
personal search strategies (Ferrari, 2013; Vuorikari et al., 2022).
Today, educators are faced with an abundance of digital (educa­
tional) resources that they can use in their teaching (Fern´andez-Otoya 
et al., 2024; Reddy et al., 2020, 2022). On the one hand, educators need 
competence to manage the abundance of information and to effectively 
identify the resources that are most appropriate for learning objectives 
(Palacios-Rodríguez et al., 2023; Turpo-Gebera et al., 2023). On the 
other hand, educators need competences to develop students’ digital 
information skills and abilities, enabling them to use digital technologies 
creatively and responsibly to gather, analyse, and share information

(Gunathilaka et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022). Teachers 
play an important role in developing students’ digital literacy, preparing 
them to live in a technological society and teaching them how to manage 
information in technology-based workplaces (Ferrari et al., 2014; Hat­
levik, 2017; Shonfeld et al., 2021). DigComp suggests different profi­
ciency levels of information literacy (foundational, intermediate and 
advanced; Carretero et al., 2017). Teachers can acquire information 
literacy through education, training and experience.
Information literacy is a component of the digital competences set 
and comprises “basic ICT skills, which lead to digital competence” 
(Ferrari et al., 2014, p. 9). Teachers must be well equipped to help 
students develop their information literacy and confident that they can 
do so successfully (Christophersen et al., 2016, p. 241; Durodolu, 2018; 
Peiffer et al., 2020; Saban & Saban, 2022). Teachers’ belief in their 
ability to complete digital learning tasks and manage the challenges of 
digital education is referred as digital self-efficacy in teaching 
(Durodolu, 2018; Hatlevik & Hatlevik, 2018; Shonfeld et al., 2021).
Teachers’ information literacy and digital self-efficacy (DSE) in 
teaching are evolving constructs (Durodolu, 2018). The digitisation of 
learning environments encourages teachers to use online tools and da­
tabases and to use digitised environments to locate and evaluate infor­
mation (Shonfeld et al., 2021). The improvement of information literacy 
must take place gradually through the implementation of information 
literacy programmes, both at the undergraduate level and in teacher 
development programmes. However, researchers have noted that pro­
fessional training and development tends to focus more on pedagogical 
aspects rather than on information literacy itself (Shonfeld et al., 2021, 
p. 494). Furthermore, there is a lack of a holistic approach to the con­
struction of teachers’ information literacy. The most common tendency 
is to focus on the one component of information literacy—information 
search abilities—rather than on information literacy abilities themselves 
(Smith, 2013; Smith et al., 2013).
Teacher training programmes can be considered effective when 
teachers not only acquire new knowledge but are also able to success­
fully apply it in daily teaching practice (Tzafilkou et al., 2021). Ac­
cording to Desimone (2009), teachers’ professional development 
consists of three stages: 1) teacher knowledge, 2) teacher behaviour and 
3) student results. Strengthening of the transitions between stages can be 
achieved through well-prepared in-service training programmes that 
consider the different characteristics (e.g. age and professional experi­
ence) and training needs of the learners (Gadella Kamstra, 2024; Rap­
paport et el., 2020). Thus, identifying the role of information literacy in 
the digital self-efficacy of novice and advanced beginner teachers in 
teaching information literacy can foster a successful transition from new 
teachers’ 
knowledge to their behaviour and their students’ 
achievements.
This study aims to reveal the role of information literacy in the 
digital self-efficacy of undergraduate students and requalification pro­
gramme teachers in teaching information literacy.
The following research questions follow from the objective of the 
study. 
●How strong is undergraduate students’ and requalification pro­
gramme teachers perceived digital self-efficacy in teaching infor­
mation literacy?
● How does their perceived information literacy affect undergraduate 
students’ digital self-efficacy in teaching information literacy?
● How does their perceived information literacy affect requalification 
programme teachers’ digital self-efficacy in teaching information 
literacy?
2. Theoretical background
2.1. Digital self-efficacy in teaching
The educational environment in schools is being continually 
digitised, and these processes trigger the need to update digital com­
petences (Kane et al., 2020, pp. 1–58; Palan & Schober, 2021). Various 
terms are used to describe digital competences, including media 
competence, ICT literacy and ICT skills (Jin et al., 2020; Oberl¨ander 
et al., 2020). Some researchers have argued that digital competence is 
based on digital literacy (Ferrari, 2013; Ferrari et al., 2014). However, 
digital competence is not limited to digital literacy but is also related to 
the attitudes and mindsets of individuals (Jin et al., 2020). The mental 
representation of one’s own digital competences can have a significant 
impact on the development of digital competences and abilities (Peiffer 
et al., 2020; Ulfert-Blank et al., 2022). An individual’s beliefs about their 
ability to successfully achieve performance goals and be successful in a 
given task are referred to as self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997; 2001).
The concept of self-efficacy is derived from Bandura’s social cogni­
tive theory (SCT; Bandura, 1977). Self-efficacy relates to an individual’s 
future-oriented and context-specific cognitive beliefs about their own 
abilities (Bong & Skaalvik, 2003; Marsh et al., 2017). Teacher 
self-efficacy is understood as a teacher’s belief in their ability to achieve 
instructional goals in specific educational situations (Corry & Stella, 
2018; Gavora, 2010; Ma et al., 2021). Teachers’ self-efficacy has a 
positive impact on the innovativeness of the teaching process (Gavora, 
2010) through the promotion of a more innovative and supportive 
classroom activities (Alt, 2018). It is an essential concept in teaching, as 
teachers with high self-efficacy levels have stronger professional moti­
vation and make appropriate professional decisions that lead to higher 
student achievements (Baroudi & Shaya, 2022). Bandura (1994) noticed 
that self-efficacy can depend on the field of activity, and it is important 
to discuss self-efficacy as situated within a specific domain. An in­
dividual’s belief in their ability to meet performance goals in digital 
technologies is encompassed by the concept of DSE; Hatlevik, 2017; 
Hatlevik et al., 2018; Ulfert-Blank et al., 2022).
SCT posits that learning occurs in a social context with ongoing 
interaction of cognitive factors (knowledge, expectations and attitudes), 
behaviour (self-efficacy, skills and practice) and environmental factors 
(social norms and access in the community; Clark & Zimmerman, 2014). 
According to SCT, individuals with negative attitudes towards their 
digital competences (low DSE) are less likely to use digital technologies, 
even if their digital competence is high, while individuals with high DSE 
are more likely to use digital technologies (Hsia et al., 2014). SCT im­
plies a reciprocal interaction between the changing environment (digi­
talisation) and DSE: digitalisation leads to changes in teachers’ DSE, and 
changes in DSE lead to digitalisation processes in educational practice 
(Hatlevik, 2017; Hatlevik et al., 2018; Ulfert-Blank et al., 2022).
Empirical research has shown that teachers’ DSE is a key factor in the 
development of students’ digital skills and their use of digital technol­
ogies (Hatlevik et al., 2018). DSE impacts a learners motivation to use 
digital systems (Ertmer et al., 1994; Joo et al., 2000, 2018), leads to 
better results in online tests (Joo et al., 2000), impacts on learning 
motivation and learning outcomes (Chang et al., 2014) and predicts 
attitudes towards web-based instruction (Dogru, 2020).
2.2. Information literacy
Digital and informational literacy has become an important 
component of initial teacher education and in the continuous profes­
sional development of in-service teachers. Digital literacy includes 
cognitive, behavioural and social skills that enable effective learning 
and working in digital environments (Eshet, 2012, Hering, 2006).
The relationship between teachers’ information literacy and a tech­
nologically rich work environment supports the findings of SCT theory 
on the environment–behaviour relationship: a technologically rich work 
environment promotes information literacy and professional skills 
(Hegarty et al., 2010; Shonfeld et al., 2020). The digitalisation of in­
formation resources requires changes in the information literacy of 
teachers (Park et al., 2021; Shonfeld et al., 2021) as well as in the 
curricula of undergraduate and teacher training programmes. This study

International standards such as those of the Association of College 
and Research Libraries (ACRL), (ACRL, 2010), the American Library 
Association (ALA), (ALA, 2004), and the Society of College, National and 
University Libraries (SCONUL) (Bent & Stubbings, 2011), provide 
guidelines for the information literacy abilities of learners in higher 
education.
The Association of College and Research Libraries (ACRL) has 
described information literacy as a set of abilities that require students in 
higher education to identify the information they need, access the in­
formation they need effectively and efficiently, evaluate information 
critically and effectively, and understand the legitimate use of infor­
mation (ACRL, 2010). A comparison of the content and structure of 
information literacy in ACRL (ACRL, 2010) and DigComp 2.2 shows 
many similarities in content but some differences in the internal struc­
ture of information literacy. While the ACRL identifies five components 
of information literacy, DigComp 2.2 identifies three: searching, evalu­
ating and managing data.
The ALA (2004) defines information literacy as a set of three abili­
ties: (1) to recognise a need for information, (2) to locate information 
and (3) to evaluate information and use it as needed. SCONUL standards 
state that an information-literate person ethically collects knowledge 
and data, and uses, manages, summarises and creates information, 
thereby improving their information skills (Bent & Stubbings, 2011). 
According to the Information Literacy, Humanities and Social Sciences 
(IL-HUMASS) model, information literacy encompasses four categories: 
(1) information search, (2) information evaluation, (3) information 
processing and (4) information dissemination (Pinto, 2010; Pinto et al., 
2020). In this study, we used the IL-HUMASS model validated by Pinto 
(2010) to analyse the information search, evaluation, processing and 
dissemination of undergraduate students and requalification pro­
gramme teachers.
Information literacy in educational contexts is analysed as part of 
reading competence (ˇC´abyov´a et al., 2020), research competencies 
(Bulger & Davison, 2018), general learning abilities (Tang, 2018) and 
basic and professional competencies (Lebid & Shevchenko, 2020). In 
this study, we analysed information literacy as a part of digital compe­
tences (Carretero et al., 2017; Ferrari, 2013; Ferrari et al., 2014).
Previous research on the relationship between information literacy 
and self-efficacy has shown that those with higher self-efficacy have 
stronger abilities to solve information problems (Adalier & Serin, 2012; 
Kiliç-Çakmak, 2010), higher learning motivation (Pinto & Sales, 2010) 
and higher academic achievements (Bayram & Comek, 2009). Accord­
ing to Naveed and Mahmood (2022), business students’ age, study 
programme, study stage, proficiency in computers and English language 
are correlated with students’ perceived information literacy. Shonfeld 
et al. (2021) found statistically significant relationships between 
perceived information literacy self-efficacy and the information literacy 
of teachers and pre-service teachers.
Research on the associations between information literacy and 
teacher age has shown that older teachers are more able to find, evaluate 
and use information effectively and improve their information literacy 
and self-efficacy (Polin, 2023; Sloniowski, 2016; Tas¸kın et al., 2013). 
Participating in a digital programme impacts teachers’ perceptions of 
their information literacy (Shonfeld et al., 2020).
Some researchers have identified challenges to teachers’ information 
literacy and its development “to appraise, discover, utilise, fabricate and 
share information with the aid of modern technology” (Durodolu, 2018, 
p. 2). Some research has shown that teachers’ information literacy is 
inadequate and that there is insufficient emphasis on information lit­
eracy in teacher education programmes (Shannon et al., 2019). If 
teachers lack information literacy, this can have a negative impact on 
students’ information literacy development (Durodolu, 2018; Kohnen & 
Saul, 2018).
Today’s undergraduate students are digital technology natives, and 
they need information literacy to be confident consumers of digital 
content in their professional lives (Head et al., 2020; Perifanou et al., 
2021). Although the development of information literacy is included in 
teacher education and professional development curricula, research has 
shown that beginner teachers are not sufficiently prepared to apply 
digital technologies in their educational practice (Guðmundsd´ottir et al., 
2020; Hatlevik, 2017).
Research shows that teachers with lower information literacy skills 
are more likely to avoid solving information problems, while those who 
have confidence in their information literacy skills are more likely to 
engage more effectively in information problem solving activities 
(Kurbanoglu, 2010; Kurbanoglu et al., 2006). A review of research on 
the links between information literacy and information literacy 
self-efficacy shows that teachers’ perceptions of their information lit­
eracy self-efficacy are significantly correlated with their age, digital 
skills and participation in a digital programme (Shonfeld et al., 2021). 
Considering the theoretically grounded links between information lit­
eracy, digital literacy and digital self-efficacy, we explored the links 
between information literacy and digital self-efficacy in teaching in­
formation literacy in two groups of beginner teachers: undergraduate 
students and requalification programme teachers.
3. Methodology and methods
3.1. Establishment of null hypothesis
To answer the research questions, we formulated three research 
hypotheses. 
H1.
There will be a statistically significant difference in the perceived 
digital self-efficacy in teaching information literacy between under­
graduate students in education and requalification programme teachers. 
Requalification programme teachers will have higher digital self- 
efficacy than undergraduate students.
H2.
There will be a statistically significant effect of pre-service 
teachers’ (undergraduate students’) information literacy on their 
perceived digital self-efficacy in teaching information literacy.
H3.
There will be a statistically significant effect of requalification 
programme teachers’ information literacy on their perceived digital self- 
efficacy in teaching information literacy.
3.2. Research participants and data collection
The study was carried out in accordance with the methodological 
requirements for quantitative research. The main stages of the study 
were as follows: developing a theoretical research model, searching for 
valid and reliable research instruments, establishing a representative 
and reliable sample, organising a survey of respondents, analysing the 
data, and summarising the results.
This study on the role of different components of information literacy 
in the digital self-efficacy of pre-service teachers in teaching information 
literacy is a continuation of previous research conducted at Vytautas 
Magnus University of Lithuania (Peciuliauskiene et al., 2022). The re­
spondents in this study came from the largest teacher training centres in 
Lithuania: Vytautas Magnus University and Vilnius University. In 
Lithuania, a teacher’s qualification can be obtained in different ways: 
the concurrent way (a study module on pedagogy is integrated into a 
bachelor’s or professional bachelor’s pedagogical study programme), 
the adjacent way (a study module on pedagogy is given alongside a 
bachelor’s or professional bachelor’s non-pedagogical study pro­
gramme) and the consecutive way (the professional study programme is 
completed based on the study module on pedagogy after acquiring a 
tertiary qualification).
This study involved 191 undergraduate students pursuing teaching

3.3. Research instrument
In this study, we used a survey comprising two validated question­
naires: a simplified version of the IL-HUMASS and the DSE. The 
simplified version of the IL-HUMASS was validated by Maria Pinto 
(2010), and the DSE questionnaire was created and validated by Lina 
Markauskait˙e (2006).
The IL-HUMASS questionnaire was designed to measure the infor­
mation literacy of students studying the social sciences or humanities in 
higher education. The 25 questions of the IL-HUMASS questionnaire 
were used in this study, divided into four categories (information 
seeking, evaluation, processing and communication/dissemination). 
The IL-HUMASS questionnaire is a self-assessment by nature. For the 
eight information-seeking questions, we assigned codes from IS1 to IS8 
(Table 2). To determine respondents’ attitudes towards information 
retrieval skills, the IL-HUMASS questionnaire asked the following: Do 
you know and use automated catalogues, electronic sources of primary 
information (electronic journals, books, etc.), secondary sources of in­
formation (databases, encyclopaedias, dictionaries, etc.), electronic 
sources of informal information (blogs, discussion forums, mailing lists, 
etc.), accepted terminology in the field and online information searches 
(advanced search, logical operators, directories, specialised portals, 
etc.)? All IL-HUMASS questions had six possible responses: (1) Don’t 
have this ability, (2) Not at all confident, (3) Not very confident, (4), 
Moderately confident, (5) Quite confident and (6) Totally confident.
Five information assessment questions were coded from IE1 to IE5 
(Table 2). In answering these questions, students had to assess whether 
they recognised the validity and reliability of information resources, 
could distinguish the author’s purpose within the text, understood the 
properties of information, were aware of the importance of updating

The information processing category consisted of six questions, 
coded from IP1 to IP6 (Table 2). For this category, students were assessed 
on their ability to abstract/schematise information, recognise text

To assess the perception of digital self-efficacy in teaching informa­
tion literacy of the requalification programme teachers and under­
graduate students, we used a questionnaire developed by Markauskait˙e 
et al. (2006). This a questionnaire is designed to measure the digital 
self-efficacy of novice teachers, that is, their beliefs about their in­
tentions to use digital technologies in education in the future (“I believe 
that I will …”). In this study, we used only the part of this questionnaire 
that relates to digital self-efficacy in teaching information literacy. We 
coded the six digital self-efficacy questions from DSE1 to DSE6 (Table 2): 
developing information search strategies (DSE1), conducting informa­
tion searches on the internet (DSE2), managing digital information re­
sources (DSE3), integrating information and analysing problems (DSE4), 
evaluating online information sources (DSE5) and respecting netiquette, 
privacy and copyright (DSE6). All digital self-efficacy questions were 
rated on a five-point scale: (1) Very unlikely, (2) Unlikely, (3) Possible, 
(4) Probable and (5) Very probable.
Questionnaires about the effect of information literacy on digital self- 
efficacy in teaching information literacy were prepared and made 
available as online forms. The study was carried out in 2022.
3.4. Research model
The IL-HUMASS instrument and the DSE in teaching instrument 
allowed us to carry out an empirical analysis of the requalification 
programme teachers’ and undergraduate students’ perceptions of in­
formation literacy and their digital self-efficacy in teaching information 
literacy. We analysed the statistical relationships between five latent 
constructs: digital self-efficacy in information literacy (DSE), informa­
tion seeking (IS), information evaluation (IE), information processing 
(IP) and information dissemination (ID; Fig. 1).
We performed structural equation modelling (SEM) using AMOS 
28.0 software to test the relationships between the latent variables (DSE, 
IS, IE, IP and ID). SEM includes two components: a measurement model 
(confirmatory factor analysis [CFA]) and a structural model. Our 
structural model consists of four exogenous (IS, IE, IP and ID) and one 
endogenous (DSE) variable (Fig. 1). All variables (exogenous and 
endogenous) were unobserved.
We checked the internal consistency and convergent validity of the 
latent variables. The convergent validity of the latent variables was 
examined using average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reli­
ability (CR; Table 3). The internal consistency of the study instrument 
was checked using Cronbach’s α (Table 3). Cronbach’s α coefficients for 
all the latent variables were above .7, thus indicating that these variables 
had a high degree of reliability.

4. Results
4.1. Statistical analysis of digital self-efficacy in teaching information 
literacy
In this section, we describe the results of the first (H1) hypothesis 
testing. The first hypothesis (H1) was formulated as follows: There will 
be a statistically significant difference in the perceived digital self- 
efficacy in teaching information literacy between undergraduate stu­
dents and requalification programme teachers.
Initially, we checked whether the data set of latent variables (DSE, IS, 
IE, IP and ID) exhibited normal distribution. The absolute values of 
skewness and kurtosis indicate the normality of the latent variables data 
set (absolute values are less than 2; Table 5).
Descriptive statistics for undergraduate students’ and the same data 
for requalification programme teachers DSEi in teaching information 
literacy are presented in Table 5. These data show that the mean ranks of 
perceived DSEi in teaching information literacy were similar for both 
groups. Both undergraduate students (x = 4.26 ± . 096) and requalifi­
cation teachers (x = 4.47 ±.797) were found to be the most satisfied 
with their ability to teach school students how to conduct searches on 
the internet (Table 5).
In addition, undergraduate students rated their ability to successfully 
teach ‘netiquette’, privacy and copyright very favourably (x = 4.26 ± .
936). Both requalification programme teachers (x = 3.36 ± .911) and 
undergraduate students (x = 3.21 ± . 904) were the least confident 
about developing their students’ information search strategies. How­
ever, the mean ranks of the perceived DSEi in teaching information lit­
eracy of re-qualification programs teachers are slightly higher than the 
means ranks of undergraduate students (Table 5).
To test the first hypothesis, we used Mann–Whitney criterion, 
considering that the question (DSE1—DSE6) scales were ranked and the 
target groups were independent. We compared the means of the 
DSE1—DSE6 question scores between the target groups. We found sta­
tistically significant differences between undergraduate students’ and 
requalification programmes teachers’ beliefs about their future success 
in teaching online information search abilities (U = 6944.00, p = .019) 
and netiquette, privacy and copyright (U = 7044.50, p = .034). The 
mean scores of responses to these questions were higher for requalifi­
cation programme teachers. The first hypothesis was supported, 
particularly in teaching information search abilities (DSE1) and neti­
quette, privacy and copyright (DSE2) (Table 5).
4.2. Structural equation model analyses. The second and the third 
hypotheses testing
In this section, we describe the results of testing the second (H2) and 
the third (H3) hypotheses, which were formulated as follows. 
H2.
There will be a statistically significant effect of pre-service 
teachers’ (undergraduate students’) information literacy on their 
perceived digital self-efficacy in teaching information literacy.
H3.
There will be a statistically significant effect of requalification 
programme teachers’ information literacy on their perceived digital self- 
efficacy in teaching information literacy.
We performed SEM to test our second hypothesis based on under­
graduate students’ data and revealed the direct effects of perceived in­
formation search literacy (βIS = .425, p < .001), information evaluation 
literacy (βIE = .834, p < .001), information processing literacy (βIP =
.849, p < .001) and information dissemination literacy (βID = .384, p <
.001) on DSE in teaching information literacy (Table 6). The two most 
powerful determinants of undergraduate students’ DSE in teaching in­
formation literacy were perceived information evaluation literacy and 
information processing literacy.
We performed SEM to test our third hypothesis based on the data of 
requalification programme teachers. The direct path coefficients of the 
initial structural model are presented in Table 7. The results indicate 
that perceptions of information search literacy (βIS = .376, p = .002), 
information evaluation literacy (βIE = .821, p < .001), information 
processing literacy (βIP = .774, p < .001) and information dissemination 
literacy (βID = .335, p = .003) positively predicted digital self-efficacy in 
teaching information literacy for requalification programme teachers.
The values of the standardised beta (β) confirmed the significant 
effect of information evaluation literacy and information processing 
literacy on the DSE in teaching information literacy of requalification 
programme teachers. The results showed that perceived information 
search literacy and information dissemination literacy had a smaller 
effect on the DSE in teaching information literacy (Table 7). The coef­
ficient of determination (R2) showed a strong linear relationship be­
tween the variables (IE–DSE and IP–DSE; Table 7).

depends not only on digitally enriched environments but also on 
teachers’ cognitive (knowledge, expectation, attitudes) and behavioural 
(self-efficacy, skills, practice) abilities (Clark & Zimmerman, 2014).
This study explores the role of the information literacy of beginner 
teachers—undergraduate students and requalification programme 
teachers—in DSE in teaching information literacy at school. The study 
was carried out with undergraduate students pursuing a teaching 
qualification in concurrent and adjacent ways and with in-service 
teachers acquiring a teaching qualification in consecutive ways. The 
demographic data revealed that the requalification programme teachers 
were older and had more years of teaching experience (Table 1).
The first hypothesis aimed to test whether there was a difference in 
the perceived DSE in teaching information literacy between the two 
groups. Insights from theoretical literature sources (Polin, 2023; Slo­
niowski, 2016; Tas¸kın et al., 2013) led to the hypothesis that requalifi­
cation programme teachers would have higher DSE in teaching than the 
undergraduate students, and this hypothesis was supported, particularly 
in teaching information search abilities and the topics of privacy and 
copyright in information management. Our findings are consistent with 
previous research on digital self-efficacy across age groups. Research 
shows that older learners have higher levels of digital self-efficacy in 
technology adoption and continued use compared to younger learners 
(Buils et al., 2024; Fern´andez-Morante et al., 2023; Fern´andez-Morante 
et al., 2024; Reddy et al., 2020; Sharma et al., 2019; Shroff et al., 2011). 
Moreover, undergraduate students are not necessarily beginners in 
digital technologies. However, it is very likely that their digital 
self-efficacy in teaching information literacy is weaker, not because of 
their experience with digital technologies, but rather because they have 
less pedagogical experience (Table 1).
The second hypothesis aimed to test the effect of undergraduate 
students’ information literacy on their DSE in teaching information lit­
eracy. This hypothesis was supported for all components of information 
literacy (information seeking, evaluation, processing and communica­
tion/dissemination), with information evaluation abilities and infor­
mation processing abilities having the strongest effect. This is in line 
with the findings of Karaseva (2016) that information search literacy 
positively affects teachers’ self-efficacy. Hatlevik and Hatlevik (2018)
noted that teachers’ DSE included general DSE and DSE for educational 
purposes. We investigated DSE for educational purposes and found 
positive effects for all information literacy components in the DSE in 
teaching information literacy.
The result of the third hypothesis testing indicated that requalifica­
tion programme teachers’ perceptions of their information search, in­
formation evaluation, information processing and information 
dissemination abilities positively predicted their DSE in teaching infor­
mation literacy. The strongest effects were for information evaluation 
abilities and information processing abilities, similar to the effects 
shown for undergraduate students. Previous research has shown that 
older teachers accumulate experience over time and use their informa­
tion literacy skills to help them find, evaluate and use information 
effectively (Sloniowski, 2016; Tas¸kın et al., 2013).
The results of the second and third hypothesis testing highlight the 
fact that having the literacy to search for information allows teachers to 
feel more confident (Kurbanoglu et al., 2006). In addition, the experi­
ence of anticipating information retrieval strategies is directly and 
positively related to perceived self-efficacy (Karaseva, 2016). Higher 
digital self-efficacy is related to higher confidence in using digital 
technologies in education (Hatlevik & Hatlevik, 2018; Reddy et al., 
2020). To advance the development of teachers’ digital competences 
and to achieve a higher level of digital self-efficacy in initial teacher 
training and professional development, we should consider the role of 
information literacy in this process.

There are some limitations to our study. The first relates to the timing 
of the investigation. We conducted the study in 2022 after the COVID-19 
pandemic. During the COVID-19 pandemic, teachers and undergraduate 
students acquired more digital competences. Research has shown that 
digital self-efficacy and digital competence increase significantly with 
the increasing use of digital technology (Ma et al., 2021).
The second limitation is linked to the relatively small pre-service 
teacher population in Lithuania. Despite the small population size, the

6. Conclusions and implications
The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organiza­
tion (UNESCO) and the European Union (EU) emphasise the importance 
of information management and the practical and ethical use of tech­
nology to address information management problems in work, social, 
and daily life (Kayhan & Korkmaz, 2024). Our study on the relationship 
between information literacy and digital self-efficacy in teaching in­
formation literacy for new teachers in Lithuania is related to the Euro­
pean Digital Competence Framework for Educators—DigCompEdu. We 
analysed the competences of undergraduate students and requalification 
programme teachers in the second area (Digital Resources) and the sixth 
area (Facilitating Learners’ Digital Competences) of DigCompEdu. In the 
Digital Resources area, we explored new teachers’ information literacy 
competences, such as information retrieval, analysis, and sharing. 
Another focus of our research was on the Facilitating Learners’ Digital 
Competences area of the DigCompEdu model: we analysed new teachers’ 
digital self-efficacy in teaching information literacy. The quantitative 
data analysis revealed that two components of information liter­
acy—perceived information evaluation and information processing 
ability—had a strong direct impact on the digital self-efficacy of un­
dergraduate students and requalification programme teachers.
Overall, these results revealed that the perceived information eval­
uation literacy and information processing literacy of both undergrad­
uate students and requalification programme teachers were powerful 
predictors of their DSE in teaching information literacy. Comparing the 
numerical values of the standardised and non-standardised coefficients 
based on undergraduate students’ (Table 6) and requalification pro­
gramme teachers’ (Table 7) data, we observe that the numerical values 
of these coefficients are higher based on undergraduate students’ data.
The theoretical and empirical insights generated in this research 
might be used as a guide to improve information literacy in initial 
teacher training programmes, especially for those engaged not only in 
the promotion of information literacy but also in the preparation of 
teachers to teach information literacy to school students. The positive 
correlations found between all information literacy components (infor­
mation seeking, evaluation, processing and communication/dissemina­
tion) and digital self-efficacy in teaching information literacy suggest 
that all information literacy components are strong predictors of 
teachers’ digital self-efficacy in teaching information literacy to school 
students.
This study was carried out as part of the restructuring process of the 
higher education network in Lithuania, which included the creation of 
two major teacher training centres. One of them was established in 
Kaunas, Lithuania, following the merger of the Lithuanian University of 
Educational Sciences with Vytautas Magnus University (VMU). Another 
teacher training centre was set up at Vilnius University in Vilnius. Both 
undergraduate students and requalification programme teachers from 
the two centres participated in this study. One of the objectives of the 
ongoing reform of higher education in Lithuania is to strengthen teacher 
training. The reform of the teacher education system in Lithuania aims 
to ensure that teachers enter school with a high quality of education and 
to create conditions for their continuous professional development. Our 
study addresses this challenge by providing guidelines for improving 
initial teacher training and requalification programmes.
Initial teacher training programme curriculum designers can benefit 
from the results of this study by considering the different roles of in­
formation literacy (information seeking, evaluation, processing and 
communication/dissemination) in digital self-efficacy in teaching in­
formation literacy. Information evaluation and information processing 
abilities are powerful predictors in digital self-efficacy in teaching in­
formation literacy. We recommend including information literacy sub­
ject in initial teacher training and requalification programmes. More 
time should be devoted to the development of teachers’ information 
processing and information evaluation abilities in initial teacher 
training and requalification programmes.
We did not investigate in what form teachers’ information literacy 
should be developed or whether information literacy programmes 
should be stand-alone or integrated with other programmes. Other 
studies have suggested that integrating information literacy pro­
grammes into digital programmes is effective (Shonfeld et al., 2020). 
Integrating information literacy into digital programmes improves the 
information literacy self-efficacy of teachers, especially older teachers 
(Shonfeld et al., 2021).
