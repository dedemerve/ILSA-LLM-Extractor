In the contemporary educational landscape, identifying the key factors that influence students’
creative thinking has become a critical issue. While much of the existing research has focused on
single-variable analyses, this study adopts a broader perspective by simultaneously examining a
wide range of potential factors. Drawing on data from the PISA 2022 creative thinking assessment
in East Asia and framed by Socio-Ecological Theory, this study employs two machine learning
methods—LASSO regression and Random Forest—to predict and analyze the determinants of
students’ creative thinking. The analysis considers 26 factors across three dimensions: individual,
microsystem, and mesosystem. The results indicate that the LASSO regression retained between
10 and 18 key predictors in parsimonious models across different economies (17 for East Asia as a
whole, 17 for Macao (China), 18 for Singapore, 15 for Hong Kong (China), 11 for Chinese Taipei,
and 10 for South Korea). Furthermore, the Random Forest results demonstrate that the overall
East Asian model achieved an R² of 0.3414, with three most important predictors being the Index
of Economic, Social and Cultural Status (ESCS), self-efficacy in digital competencies, and curiosity. Meanwhile, the results also show that the explanatory power of the models varied among
economies (with R² values ranging from 0.2241 to 0.3656), as did the rankings of predictor
importance: for Macao (China) (R² = 0.3656) and Singapore (R² = 0.3649), the top three predictors consistently included ESCS, self-efficacy in digital competencies, and school size; for Hong
Kong (China) (R² = 0.2887), the top three were school size, ESCS, and participation in creative
activities outside school; for Chinese Taipei (R² = 0.3194), they were self-efficacy in digital
competencies, ESCS, and class size; and for South Korea (R² = 0.2241), the top predictors were
ESCS, curiosity, and imagination. The findings provide empirical evidence for policymakers,
educators, and families, helping them target educational resources more effectively to the areas
most likely to enhance students’ creative thinking.

1. Introduction
With the iterative advancement of digitalization and artificial intelligence, societies are facing increasing complexity and uncertainty. In response, the focus of education has shifted from traditional knowledge transmission to the cultivation of core competencies
to address future challenges (Van Laar et al., 2020). Among these, creative thinking stands out as a key 21st-century skill, defined as the
ability to generate novel and effective solutions and widely regarded as a core driver of individual and societal progress
(Thornhill-Miller et al., 2023). Acknowledging this global educational trend, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and
Development (OECD) introduced the first large-scale international assessment of creative thinking in PISA 2022. Its framework defines
creative thinking as the product of interactions between cognitive processes, such as generating, evaluating, and improving ideas, and
external contextual factors, emphasizing that creativity is not an isolated individual trait but is profoundly shaped by the environment
(OECD, 2023, 2024b, 2024a).
This dual emphasis on cognition and context in the PISA framework aligns closely with the principles of Socio-Ecological Theory,
which posits that individual development is shaped within a set of nested environmental systems (Bronfenbrenner, 1979). Consequently, this study adopts Socio-Ecological Theory as its core analytical framework. It provides an ideal theoretical lens to systematically investigate how factors within the individual system (e.g., student characteristics), microsystem (e.g., immediate
environments such as family and peers), and mesosystem (e.g., the school environment) influence the development of students’
creative thinking. This integration not only enhances the theoretical depth of the research but also enables a more comprehensive
understanding of the complex ecology of creative thinking development.
Against this backdrop, East Asian educational systems offer a unique and critical context for research. Specifically, data from PISA
2022 reveal a profound paradox. On the one hand, Confucian-heritage economies such as Singapore, Macao (China), Chinese Taipei,
Hong Kong (China), and South Korea consistently lead the world in mathematical literacy (OECD, 2023). On the other hand, their
performance in creative thinking shows significant divergence. Singapore and South Korea perform well above the OECD average,
whereas Hong Kong (China), Macao (China), and Chinese Taipei hover near the average (OECD, 2024b). This phenomenon of “high
academic achievement-creativity divergence” challenges the conventional stereotype of the East Asian educational model as one that
“sacrifices creativity for high scores” (Boman, 2022). It also provides a “natural experiment” for investigating the factors influencing
creative thinking. Within a relatively consistent macro-level Confucian cultural context, what factors lead to these different outcomes
in creative thinking development?
To address this research gap, this study leverages PISA 2022 East Asian data, guided by Socio-Ecological Theory, and employs two
machine learning methods (Christensen et al., 2025; Grimmer et al., 2021; Zhai et al., 2025), LASSO analysis and Random Forest, to
overcome the limitations of traditional regression models in handling high-dimensional and highly multicollinear data (Senaviratna &
Cooray, 2019), thereby identifying and investigating the key predictors of creative thinking. This study makes two main contributions.
First, theoretically, it pioneers the application of Socio-Ecological Theory and machine learning methods to the newly available PISA
2022 creative thinking dataset for East Asian economies. Second, practically, its findings will provide evidence-based insights for
educational policymakers in East Asia and globally, aiding in the construction of educational ecosystems that more effectively nurture
students’ creative thinking.
2. Literature review
2.1. The definition of creative thinking and the assessment in PISA 2022
A foundational theory in the field of creative thinking is Guilford’s (1950) Structure of Intellect (SOI) model. Within this framework, the cognitive operation most closely associated with creative thinking is “divergent production,” which refers to the ability to
generate diverse solutions from given information. The output of divergent thinking is typically measured by three core indicators:
fluency (the number of ideas), flexibility (the diversity of idea categories), and originality (the uniqueness of ideas) (Guilford, 1950).
Building on Guilford’s work, Torrance (1968) further operationalized these concepts and added a fourth dimension, elaboration (the
ability to develop and refine ideas). This work culminated in the development of the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking (TTCT)
(Torrance, 1968), which have been widely used in educational contexts internationally. Centered on divergent thinking, this theory
established one of the most influential and enduring operational definitions and assessment approaches to creative thinking in
educational research. In recent years, advancements in neuroscience have propelled research from behavioral observation to the
exploration of brain mechanisms. By integrating brain signal analysis techniques, researchers have conducted spatial reasoning-based
assessments, Eureka effect-based analyses, and analogical reasoning-based assessments to evaluate creative thinking (Ghosh et al.,
2020, 2021, 2024a, 2024b, 2025; Ghosh & Konar, 2024). These approaches supplement the aforementioned classic behavioral
measurements with novel assessment strategies and neurological evidence.
As research on creative thinking continues to deepen, subsequent studies have further expanded and refined its definition. A
broadly accepted contemporary definition describes creativity as “the interaction among aptitude, process, and environment by which
an individual or group produces a perceptible product that is both novel and useful as defined within a social context” (Pang & Plucker,
2012; Plucker et al., 2004). Drawing upon previous research, the OECD defines creative thinking in the PISA 2022 assessment as “the
competence to engage productively in the generation, evaluation and improvement of ideas that can result in original and effective
solutions, advances in knowledge and impactful expressions of imagination” (OECD, 2023).
The OECD’s PISA model of creative thinking emphasizes the processual nature of creativity, focuses on “small creativity”, and
includes two components: cognitive processes and contexts of use.: cognitive processes and contexts of use. The assessment of creative

which students engage in everyday creative activities, including four types of task situations: written expression, visual expression,
social problem solving, and scientific problem solving (OECD, 2023).
2.2. Factors influencing creative thinking
Researchers have studied the influencing factors of creative thinking from different perspectives, mainly through two stages: (1) In
the first stage, researchers represented by Guilford and Torrance focused on the ontology of creative thinking (Guilford, 1950; Torrance, 1968), mainly from three perspectives of psychometrics, cognition and personality, emphasizing creative products, processes,
and individuals, with particular attention to intra-individual variables influencing creative thinking. (2) Later, with the deepening of
research, researchers represented by Robert Sternberg and Teresa M. Amabile turned their attention to factors such as environment and
culture, focusing on the creative environment, emphasizing the interaction between the individual and the environment, and focusing
on the external variables that influence the individual’s creative thinking (Amabile, 1988; Sternberg, 1998, 2003). Building on this,
other researchers have actively explored the internal and external variables that influence creative thinking.
2.2.1. Internal Factors
Research concerning internal factors has primarily focused on the influence of demographic backgrounds, motivations and beliefs,
cognitive characteristics, and socio-emotional skills on creative thinking. Within the demographic domain, studies have examined the
effects of students’ gender and age (Shah & Gustafsson, 2021; Shaha et al., 2020). Regarding motivations and beliefs, researchers have
investigated the impact of creative thinking self-efficacy (Muti’ah et al., 2022), self-directed learning self-efficacy (Nurdin et al., 2024),
and self-efficacy in digital competencies (Susanti & Laily, 2024; Xiong et al., 2022). From a cognitive perspective, key factors found to
influence creative thinking include include openness to creativity (Kaufman et al., 2016), imagination (Kind, 2022; Woolley et al.,
2020), and curiosity (Hagtvedt et al., 2016). Concurrently, research in the field of neuroscience has further advanced this area from the
perspective of brain signal analysis, employing spatial reasoning-based assessments (Ghosh et al., 2021, 2024b), Eureka effect-based
analyses (Ghosh et al., 2020; Ghosh & Konar, 2024), and analogical reasoning-based assessments (Ghosh et al., 2024a, 2025) as
cognitive tasks to evaluate individuals’ creative thinking. In the socio-emotional realm, the role of cooperation has also been identified
as a significant area of inquiry (Baloche, 1994; K. Lu et al., 2019; Segundo Marcos et al., 2020). Previous research has established that
these various internal factors influence the cultivation of creative thinking.
2.2.2. External Factors
Regarding external factors, researchers have primarily approached the topic from the perspectives of the family, the classroom, and
the school. In terms of family, researchers have focused on indicators of economic, social and cultural status (Heinla, 2006; Parsasirat
et al., 2013), family support (Tang, Duan, et al., 2022), creative home environment (H. J. Kim et al., 2015) and other factors influencing students’ creative thinking. In terms of classroom, researchers focus on the effects of teachers and students’ peers on students’
creative thinking, for example, researchers focus on the effects of teachers’ digital teaching competence on students’ creative thinking
(Pikhart et al., 2024; Suciati, 2018). They focused on class size (Morgan & Forster, 1999; Otte, 1966) and the effect of creative peer
environments (Budge et al., 2013) on students’ creative thinking. In the context of schools, researchers have taken a broader view and

Structural Model of Creativity (Amabile, 1988), to present a framework for understanding the factors influencing creative thinking in
classroom contexts (Fig. 2).
2.3. Factors influencing creative thinking from the perspective of socio-ecological theory
The PISA framework for factors influencing creative thinking has limitations, as it primarily considers individual internal resources
and motivation alongside the external social environment at the classroom level, a categorization that lacks sufficient granularity and
comprehensiveness. To incorporate a more extensive set of variables and systematically organize these predictors, this study adopts
Socio-Ecological Theory (as illustrated in Fig. 3) as an interpretative framework (Bronfenbrenner, 1979). It should be noted that, given
the use of machine learning methods to exploratorily identify key predictors, this study does not aim to empirically test interactions
across levels (the individual, microsystem, and mesosystem). Instead, Socio-Ecological Theory functions primarily as an interpretive
framework in this study, utilized to systematically categorize and interpret the key predictors selected by machine learning, rather than
to validate the interaction mechanisms among the multi-layered systems.
The theory suggests that the learning process is influenced by the environment at several levels. Specifically, the socio-ecological
theory includes the following levels: (1) the socio-ecological system focuses individual system focuses on the individual and includes
the biological characteristics and personality traits of the student; (2) the microsystem consists of the immediate environment in which
the student lives and includes the family, peers and teachers; (3) the mesosystem represents the school climate, including school
resources and policies; and (4) the exosystem and macrosystem refer to the larger social system, which includes the political, economic
and cultural characteristics of the society/country. This study focuses on the role of individual, microsystem and mesosystem factors
because these are the levels closest to student learning and have the most direct impact on learning outcomes. This study does not
include exosystemic and macrosystemic factors because East Asian countries share a similar Confucian cultural background, so only
the individual, microsystemic and mesosystemic levels of influence are considered (Wang et al., 2023).
Students’ creative thinking is influenced by a number of factors, and this paper draws on the literature and Socio-Ecological Theory
to select individual, microsystemic and mesosystemic factors to explain students’ creative thinking. Among them, individual factors
include 10 variables of basic demographic variables (students’ age, students’ gender), curiosity, imagination, creativity and openness
to intelligence, creative thinking self-efficacy, self-directed learning self-efficacy, cooperation, self-efficacy in digital competencies,
and student absenteeism; and at the microsystem level, the main factors are related to family and peer environment; at the microsystem
factors, the main factors are the index of economic, social and cultural status, family support, creative home environment, ICT resources, creative peer environment, class size, distance learning resources and teachers’ level of digital pedagogical competence; at the
mesosystem level, it is concerned with the school environment and social institutions (e.g. museums, science museums), with eight
variables: school size, school type, participation in creative activities outside of school, participation in creative activities at school,
creative school environment, sense of belonging, school safety risks and being bullied. The final model of factors influencing students’
creative thinking is shown in Fig. 4, and a table describing the variables can be found in Appendix Table 1.

2.4. Educational background in East Asia
East Asian students have consistently performed well in multiple rounds of PISA assessments. Researchers attribute this success to
the influence of Confucian culture, which emphasizes education, diligent learning, and high standards (Ho, 2013; King & McInerney,
2014; K. Lau, 2014). Both teachers and students in East Asia are influenced by Confucian values and share an exam-oriented school
culture (So & Hu, 2019). Students often participate in two or more extracurricular activities from as early as kindergarten (E. Y. H. Lau
& Cheng, 2016), which may consume their cognitive resources. While this contributes to strong academic performance, it may hinder
the development of creative thinking skills.
However, the results of PISA 2022’s assessment of creative thinking reveal significant variation across East Asian economies.
Singapore ranked first, followed by South Korea, while Chinese Taipei, Macao (China), and Hong Kong (China) achieved only average
performance (OECD, 2024b). These disparities may result from differences in individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors within
East Asian economies.
For example, Singapore’s Gifted Education Programme (GEP), implemented since 1984, provides enriched curricular content
designed to maximize students’ learning potential and foster “productive creativity” (Hairon, 2021; Loo, 2016). In contrast, Confucian
values prevalent in South Korea have been negatively correlated with creativity, particularly adaptive creativity and creative
advantage (K. H. Kim, 2009; K. H. Kim et al., 2011). Specific Confucian elements, such as suppression of expression and gender
inequality, may further inhibit creative expression (K. H. Kim et al., 2011). In China, regional developmental disparities indicate that
critical and creative thinking are predominantly nurtured in elite schools, where high-achieving students tend to demonstrate greater
creativity (Z. K. Liu et al., 2015). To address the lack of creativity among Chinese students, the government has introduced educational
policies aimed at fostering creativity to enhance the nation’s education system and societal innovation capacity (Pang & Plucker,

Given this context, the factors and variables influencing students’ creative thinking are likely to differ across East Asian economies.
While previous studies have analyzed the factors influencing creative thinking across the broader East Asian region, they have largely
overlooked the inherent heterogeneity among individual economies (H. Chen et al., 2026). This study conducts a comprehensive
analysis by incorporating all potential variables into a unified model and performing supplementary analyses for each economy. This
approach allows us to uncover commonalities and differences in the patterns of predictors.
2.5. The present study
Building on the discussion above, this study adopts socio-ecological theory and employs machine learning analytical methods to
investigate how individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors predict East Asian students’ creative thinking. Specifically, we focus
on two research questions:
(1) Relative Importance: What is the relative importance of individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors in fostering creative
thinking among East Asian students? This question is examined by incorporating multiple variables from the PISA 2022 dataset.
(2) Key Predictors: What are the key variables that best predict creative thinking? The findings aim to provide actionable insights
for guiding educational practices.
3. Data Sources and Analytical Methods
3.1. Data Source
The data for this study were sourced from the 2022 cycle of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA). PISA is a
long-term, large-scale international educational assessment initiated and led by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and
Development (OECD). It is designed to systematically evaluate the extent to which 15-year-old students, nearing the end of their
compulsory education, can apply their knowledge and skills to real-life situations and challenges. The assessment is conducted every
three years, and PISA 2022 was the first cycle to include creative thinking as a core innovative domain.
To ensure the rigor of the assessment process and the cross-national comparability of the data, PISA 2022 was implemented according to a set of standardized procedures. The assessment were primarily conducted in 2022 within participating economies, with
specific dates determined by each jurisdiction’s academic calendar. The entire assessment was computer-based, and each participating
student completed a cognitive test and a background questionnaire. The creative thinking tasks were developed based on the

aforementioned competency model for the PISA test of creative thinking, comprehensively measuring students’ abilities to generate,
evaluate, and improve ideas. The overall project was supervised by the OECD Secretariat, while each participating economy designated a National Project Center to implement the assessment in strict accordance with international technical standards. This process
covered all stages, from school and student sampling, translation and adaptation of test materials, to field administration and data
collection, thereby providing a high-quality, comparable empirical basis for this research.
The analysis in this paper focuses on five East Asian economies: Singapore, South Korea, Hong Kong (China), Macao (China), and
Chinese Taipei, comprising a total of 29,208 cases. The sample distribution is as follows: Singapore (N = 6,606; mean age = 15.78;
49.2% female), South Korea (N = 6,454; mean age = 15.77; 48.5% female), Chinese Taipei (N = 5,857; mean age = 15.81; 51.3%
female), Hong Kong (China) (N = 5,907; mean age = 15.82; 47.9% female), and Macao (China) (N = 4,384; mean age = 15.81; 48.8%
female). The mean age of the entire sample was 15.80 years (SD = 0.28), with females accounting for 48.6% and males 51.4% (see
Table 2 in the Appendix for details). By identifying the key factors that influence creative thinking within these educational systems,
this study aims to offer valuable insights into how to cultivate innovative talent and promote high-quality, equitable education.
3.2. Variable Selection
The dependent variable in this study is students’ creative thinking performance, while the independent variables are shown in
Fig. 4. PISA measures students’ creative thinking beyond specific subjects or contexts, aiming to assess their creative engagement from
an interdisciplinary learning perspective. The total score for students’ creative thinking in PISA 2022 ranges from 0 to 60. According to
the PISA framework, each student is assigned 10 plausible values based on their responses and performance on test items, reflecting a
reasonable range of their abilities (OECD, 2023, 2024b). In this study, the average of the 10 plausible values is used as the dependent
variable, with a mean of 34.971 and a standard deviation of 10.312 (see Table 1).
The independent variables in this study were derived from the PISA 2022 student and school questionnaires and were categorized
into three levels: 10 individual variables, 8 microsystem variables, and 8 mesosystem variables. Following the methodology outlined in
the PISA 2022 Technical Report (OECD, 2024c), these variables are classified based on their construction into simple indices, scale
indices, and complex composite indices. Simple indices are derived from arithmetic transformations of one or more questionnaire
items (e.g., students’ age, students’ gender, student absenteeism, class size, school size, school type). Scale indices are weighted

research, please refer to Table 1 in the Appendix. This table provides detailed information on their corresponding PISA item names,
response scales, aggregation procedures, and a representative sample item for each variable.
3.3. Analytical Methods
This study employed a two-stage machine learning approach to identify and rank the key predictors of creative thinking. It should
be explicitly clarified that, compared to more traditional regression-based approaches commonly applied in PISA data analyses, the
aim of introducing machine learning methods in this study is not to estimate causal effects. Instead, when dealing with variables
exhibiting high multicollinearity in the PISA dataset, traditional regression models often face the risks of unstable parameter estimation and model overfitting (Senaviratna & Cooray, 2019). Therefore, this study aims to exploratorily identify key predictors under
conditions of high multicollinearity (Christensen et al., 2025; Grimmer et al., 2021; Zhai et al., 2025).
The analytical strategy combined LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression with a Random Forest
model. LASSO regression was chosen for its ability to perform parsimonious variable selection by applying a penalty term that shrinks
the coefficients of less relevant predictors to zero, thereby reducing model complexity and addressing multicollinearity (Hastie et al.,
2017; Tibshirani, 1996). Subsequently, the Random Forest model, a powerful ensemble algorithm, was used to rank the importance of
the variables selected by LASSO. It provides a robust measure of variable importance (%IncMSE), which quantifies the decrease in
model accuracy when the values of a variable are permuted (Breiman, 2001; Liaw & Wiener, 2002). This combined approach was
intended to leverage the parsimony of LASSO and the predictive robustness of Random Forest.
The analysis was conducted primarily in R (version 4.2.1) using the glmnet, ranger, and ggplot2 packages, with preliminary analyses performed in SPSS 27.0. Given the complex sampling design of PISA, two types of sampling weights were incorporated into the
analyses. Specifically, the SENWT weight was applied when analyzing the overall East Asian data, while the W_FSTUWT weight was
utilized when independently analyzing the data of the five individual East Asian economies. The specific analytical pipeline was as
follows:
Step 1: Preliminary Correlation Analysis and Visualization. For the overall dataset only, we first conducted correlation analyses to
examine the bivariate relationships between potential predictors and creative thinking. To accommodate the different variable types,
point-biserial correlation was used for binary categorical variables. For continuous and ordinal variables, Spearman’s rank correlation
was employed as a robust method suitable for non-normally distributed data (Bishara & Hittner, 2012). The resulting correlation
matrix was visualized as a heatmap using the ggplot2 package in R (Wickham, 2016).
Step 2: Variable Selection via LASSO Regression. We applied a LASSO regression model (using the glmnet package) to select the
most significant predictors. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined via 10-fold cross-validation, a robust technique
for assessing model generalizability (Stone, 1974). The λ value that minimized the root mean squared error (RMSE) was selected. At
this λ, variables with weaker predictive power had their coefficients shrunk to zero, effectively removing them from the model.
Step 3: Predictor Importance Ranking via Random Forest. The subset of variables selected by the LASSO model was then entered
into a Random Forest model (using the randomForest package). We assessed the relative importance of each predictor by its percentage
increase in mean squared error (%IncMSE). A higher %IncMSE value indicates greater importance for that variable in predicting the
outcome (Breiman, 2001).
Step 4: Subgroup Analyses. To explore regional variations, Steps 2 and 3 of the analytical procedure were repeated for the individual datasets of the five East Asian economies (Singapore, South Korea, Hong Kong (China), Macao (China), and Chinese Taipei).

5. Discussion
Although an increasing body of research has examined the factors influencing students’ creative thinking, the combined effects of
various factors across different economies within the East Asian cultural context remain underexplored. Existing studies using PISA
2022 data have predominantly focused on the impact of individual factors. For instance, researchers have investigated gender differences in creative thinking (Taylor, 2025), examined whether school activities can cultivate students’ creativity (Hernández-Ramos
& Araya, 2025; Kagan & Dumas, 2025), explored the influence of personal beliefs and environmental support on the creative thinking
of Chinese students (Zeng, 2025), and analyzed the role of extracurricular activities and supportive environments for students in East
Asia (J. Li et al., 2025). Therefore, this study utilizes PISA 2022 data, employing Socio-Ecological Theory as an interpretative
framework, to systematically identify and analyze the predictors influencing East Asian students’ creative thinking across three levels:
the individual (10 variables), the microsystem (8 variables), and the mesosystem (8 variables). Our findings reveal that different
factors show varying degrees of association with students’ creative thinking, with some playing a significant positive role while others
are negatively correlated. These results warrant deeper investigation to uncover the underlying reasons, offering valuable insights for
educational policies aimed at fostering students’ creative thinking and innovative capacities to better prepare them for future
challenges.
5.1. The Impact of Different Factors on Creative Thinking
PISA employs rigorous analytical methods, with representative samples and high-quality variables worldwide, which supports the
robustness of the results in this study and providing empirical evidence for identifying factors influencing students’ creative thinking.
The following discussion highlights the most important factors and presents new findings that differ from existing research.
Among individual factors, self-efficacy in digital competencies emerges as the most critical one. Existing research has shown that in
digital environments, self-efficacy in digital competencies enhances their creative thinking (Behnamnia et al., 2025; Tang, Mao, et al.,
2022). Additionally, students’ curiosity, imagination, and self-directed learning self-efficacy also promote creative thinking (Hagtvedt
et al., 2016; B. Liu et al., 2023; Schutte & Malouff, 2020; Tang, Mao, et al., 2022; Woolley et al., 2020). Furthermore, the results
indicate that, on average, 15-year-old girls demonstrate superior creative thinking compared to boys, revealing a significant gender
differences. This could be attributed to the fact that girls, in general, tend to be more imaginative than boys and are more likely to
approach problems from multiple perspectives (OECD, 2024a; Shah & Gustafsson, 2021; Shaha et al., 2020). In terms of age, different
stages of cognitive development and learning experiences may also impact the expression of creative thinking (Shaha et al., 2020).
ESCS is a crucial factor in the microsystem influencing students’ creative thinking. Research has shown that students from higher
socioeconomic backgrounds generally perform better in creative thinking compared to their peers, and this advantage may widen the
disparity among students (Z. Lu et al., 2024). Furthermore, as socioeconomic levels rise, creative thinking appears to improve
(Castillo-Vergara et al., 2018). This could be attributed to the fact that families with more economic and cultural resources tend to offer
more enabling learning environments and support, thereby fostering students’ creative thinking (Jankowska et al., 2024). Additionally, information and communication technology (ICT) is another key factor. When integrated into curriculum activities, ICT can
stimulate divergent thinking and support increasingly complex creative tasks (Allegra et al., 2001). This was particularly evident
during the COVID-19 pandemic, when students who participated in the PISA 2022 assessment experienced school closures and remote
learning, with schools providing ICT resources to support them. However, the study found that the preparedness of schools for remote
teaching was negatively correlated with students’ creative thinking. One possible reason for this is that inadequate preparation for
remote teaching can adversely affect students’ creative thinking (Pelikan et al., 2021; Petkeviciute & Balciunaitiene, 2021). Additionally, the shift from traditional face-to-face teaching to online learning limits direct interaction between teachers and students,
making it challenging for students to adapt to the new mode of learning (Espino-Díaz et al., 2020; Petkeviciute & Balciunaitiene,
2021).
School type is a key mesosystem factor affecting students’’creative thinking. School type reflects the quality of education to some
extent, which in turn impacts students’ creative thinking. The results indicate that participation in both in-school and extracurricular
creative activities negatively affects students’ creative thinking, which contrasts with existing research findings (Conner et al., 2018).
Creative activities are generally presumed to be positively correlated with creative thinking, making the negative association found in
some contexts an unexpected result. This outcome may be attributable to several factors. In-school creative activities require dynamic
student-teacher interaction (MacDowell & Michael, 2014), but East Asian educational environments tend to be teacher-centered, with
limited classroom interaction (Langen & Stamov Roßnagel, 2023), which may result in lower-quality creative activities that are less
conducive to the development of students’ creative thinking. Additionally, extracurricular creative activities may increase students’
academic burden due to the excessive time required (Hong et al., 2011; C. Li et al., 2023; Ryan et al., 2017).
Compared to mesosystem factors, individual and microsystem factors have a more significant impact on creative thinking. This
study not only identifies factors that promote students’ creative thinking but also recognizes factors that hinder the development of
creative thinking, including gender, school preparedness for remote teaching, and participation in creative activities both inside and
outside of school.

The economies of East Asia that participated in PISA 2022 (Singapore, South Korea, Chinese Taipei, Hong Kong (China), and Macao
(China)) exhibit significant differences in creative thinking. Singaporean students ranked first, with an average score of 41; South

Kong (China) and Macao (China) had an average score of 32, which is below the OECD average of 33 (OECD, 2024b).
An analysis of the key predictors of creative thinking across East Asia as a whole and within the five individual economies reveals
that microsystem and mesosystem factors have a more pronounced impact on students from Singapore, Hong Kong (China), and Macao
(China). In contrast, individual and microsystem factors play a larger role in Chinese Taipei and South Korea. Beyond the common
factors influencing creative thinking across the region, this study also highlights significant differences in microsystem and mesosystem variables, which can be attributed to distinct cultural and educational contexts.
Among individual factors, self-efficacy in digital competencies significantly influences the creative thinking of students in Chinese
Taipei. This refers to the goal-referenced belief in one’s ability to use and engage with digital technologies confidently, critically, and
responsibly (Ulfert-Blank & Schmidt, 2022). Digital competence has become a core competency dimension supporting creative
problem-solving and creative expression in contemporary digital learning contexts, and individuals’ efficacy beliefs regarding their
own digital competencies serve as a crucial cognitive mediator for translating this competence into creative practice (Chu, 2025).
Chinese Taipei not only values creative thinking, with the local government contributing approximately three to five million US dollars
annually to its cultivation in primary and secondary schools (Kuo et al., 2017), but is also renowned for its information education and
infrastructure. Since the promulgation of the “Master Plan for ICT in Elementary and Secondary Education” in 2001, educational
policies have continuously promoted digital applications and mobile services in schools at all levels, thereby developing students’
digital competencies and fostering their creative thinking (C.-H. Chen et al., 2020). Additionally, curiosity has a major impact on the
creative thinking of students in South Korea. Curiosity, the desire for new knowledge and experiences, is an innate need for exploration
and a fundamental component of intrinsic motivation (Karwowski et al., 2019; Oudeyer et al., 2016). Students in South Korea are
situated in an educational environment that places a high value on both creative thinking and academic success. Their motivation to
explore new knowledge and their willingness to handle novelty and uncertainty are integrated and synergized during the socialization
process (Ye et al., 2015). Furthermore, curiosity is positively correlated with academic achievement (Wavo, 2004). Because university
admissions quotas are limited, parents and schools invest substantial resources to help students achieve high levels of academic
performance to compete for entry into top secondary schools, thereby increasing their probability of university admission. During this
process, students' curiosity might also be continuously stimulated and reinforced; in turn, such an elevated level of curiosity may
potentially serve as a positive predictor of creative thinking (Kashdan & Yuen, 2007).
Among the microsystem factors, the ESCS ranks as the most or second most important predictor across both the overall sample and
the five individual economies (ranking first in Singapore and South Korea, second in Chinese Taipei and Hong Kong (China), and third
in Macao (China)). This aligns with the patterns revealed in the PISA 2022 analytical report, indicating that family socio-economic
background is a crucial factor explaining variations in student performance. East Asian parents have traditionally placed a high
value on their children’s education, generally viewing it as a vital pathway for altering family destiny and achieving social class
mobility. They are actively involved in home, school, and tutoring activities. Consequently, the academic and creative thinking success
of students is largely attributed to their strong economic background, combined with sustained and deep parental support and
involvement (E. Kim, 2002; Y. C. Kim & Jung, 2020; Lee, 2012). Furthermore, in the context of the widespread prevalence of “shadow
education” in East Asian societies, greater family economic capital often directly determines whether students can afford high-quality
extracurricular tutoring, participate in various interest-expanding activities, and even access non-standardized, more creative learning
resources (Y. C. Kim et al., 2020). In other words, families with more favorable economic conditions are not only able to provide their
children with more diverse channels of knowledge input but are also better equipped to create spaces for free exploration and
innovative practice. Taken together, these two aspects indicate that the influence of the ESCS on the cultivation and development of
students’ creative thinking cannot be overlooked.
Among the mesosystem factors, school size has a significant impact on the creative thinking of students in Hong Kong (China) and
Macao (China). In highly dense urban environments like Hong Kong (China) and Macao (China), larger schools may generally possess
more abundant funding to develop diversified, internationally aligned innovative curriculum systems; such increased exposure to
innovative curricula can facilitate the development of students’ creative thinking (Koussihouèdé, 2020; Patston et al., 2021).
Furthermore, among East Asian economies, Singaporean students notably outperform their peers in creative thinking. Why do Singaporean students exhibit such remarkable creativity? This can be traced to the role of the mesosystem. Based on the data regarding
mesosystem factors, this study finds that in Singapore, in addition to the influence of school size, a creative school environment and
participation in creative activities at school also exert a certain impact on students’ creative thinking. This achievement is partially
attributed to Singapore’s Gifted Education Programme (GEP), implemented since 1984, which selects students from the fourth grade
and offers an abundant curriculum designed to fully realize their learning potential and foster “productive creativity” (Hairon, 2021;
Loo, 2016). Additionally, Singaporean classrooms ensure purposeful curricular activities that encourage students to enhance their
learning abilities through self-exploration and active learning, which, in turn, develops their independent thinking and creativity
(Chiam et al., 2014). Due to the creative school environment and creative activities at school, the mesosystem influences the individual
system, enhancing Singaporean students’ creativity and openness to intelligence, making creativity and openness to intelligence a key
predictor in Singapore. Thus, schools can foster a creative school environment and use school-based creative activities as an entry
point, avoiding additional academic burdens while developing curriculum content that fosters student creativity.

structural support for creative thinking within each educational system.
The results of this cross-national comparison reveal that the cultivation of creative thinking is influenced by both universal factors,
such as the importance of socioeconomic status and curiosity, and factors that are deeply shaped by culture and policy. The case of
Singapore demonstrates that when creative curricula and activities are systematically embedded at the institutional level, both the
level and predictability of students’ creative thinking improve. The experience of South Korea shows that family support plays a key
compensatory role within the educational system. Chinese Taipei offers a new pathway, enhancing self-efficacy in digital competencies
within digital environments to foster creativity. In contrast, the comparison between Hong Kong (China) and Macao (China) serves as a
reminder that without a creativity-oriented educational system, individual traits may support the creativity of some students, but the
overall explanatory power of the model significantly decreases.
6. Conclusions
Grounded in Socio-Ecological Theory, this study employed machine learning methods (LASSO and Random Forest) to rank the
relative importance of 26 individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors influencing East Asian students’ creative thinking. The
findings identify key factors that positively or negatively associated with the development of creative thinking: in the overall East
Asian model, the ESCS, self-efficacy in digital competencies, and students’ curiosity are the top three predictors. Specifically, certain
variations exist across individual economies: in Hong Kong (China) and Macao (China), the most important feature variable is school
size; in South Korea and Singapore, it is the ESCS; and in Chinese Taipei, it is self-efficacy in digital competencies. These results
underscore the necessity of providing multidimensional support targeting individual, microsystem, and mesosystem factors to enhance
students’ creative thinking. Future education policies should prioritize addressing students’ personalized needs, optimizing the
allocation of educational resources, and fostering a more inclusive and innovative educational environment. Such efforts aim to equip
students with the capabilities to tackle future challenges effectively. Methodologically, this study leveraged machine learning techniques to analyze large-scale international assessment data, offering an advanced approach to exploring complex relationships among
highly correlated variables. Compared to traditional statistical methods, machine learning effectively helps address issues such as
multicollinearity and overfitting while evaluating the relative importance of numerous predictors.
7. Limitations
This study also presents the following limitations. First, regarding theoretical application and the interpretation of results, this
study primarily utilizes Socio-Ecological Theory as an interpretative framework. Due to data structure constraints, interactions between levels (the individual, microsystem, and mesosystem) are not empirically tested. Therefore, future research could further
investigate the impact of cross-level interactions on students’ creative thinking. Second, from a methodological standpoint, based on
the cross-sectional data from PISA 2022, the aim of the machine learning methods employed in this study is not to estimate causal
effects, but rather to exploratorily identify key predictors under conditions of high multicollinearity; thus, the results reflect predictive
correlations rather than causal relationships. Simultaneously, some important predictors may aggregate influences from multiple
system levels (e.g., the ESCS is a complex composite index). Such feature variables intertwine effects from multiple levels, ultimately
affecting the prediction results, which may explain why the ESCS tends to dominate the predictor importance rankings. Finally,
regarding sample representativeness and data sources, the analysis is based exclusively on data from five East Asian economies. Given
the specific educational and cultural contexts of this region, the generalizability of the study may be limited; therefore, it is necessary
for future research to conduct cross-cultural examinations across broader global samples. In terms of data sources, this study relies on
PISA questionnaires and test data, which focus on the socio-educational ecological level, making it difficult to capture physiological
brain signals when students’ creative thinking is stimulated. In recent years, the field of neuroscience has made beneficial progress in
micro-cognitive assessments. Future interdisciplinary research could consider combining macro-level educational assessments with
micro-neurocognitive measurement methods, such as electroencephalogram (EEG), functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), or
graph neural networks (Ghosh et al., 2020, 2021, 2024a, 2024b, 2025; Ghosh & Konar, 2024), thereby complementing the understanding of the cultivation and assessment of creative thinking with a more nuanced and microscopic perspective.
