Keywords:

Global competence is the key determinant of student success in an interconnected, diverse, and rapidly changing
world. This study examined the simultaneous influence of student- and school-level factors on students’ global
competence across diverse cultural contexts in China, Germany, Turkey, and Mexico. The sample comprised
25,677 students (grades 7–12) and 771 schools from the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA
2018). Hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) was utilized for data analysis. Findings indicated that family cultural
capital, students’ ICT competence, and cooperation consistently affect students’ global competence across varied
countries. In contrast, the effects of school location, participation in international exchange programs, and
foreign language learning in the curriculum on students’ global competence significantly differ among four
countries. Distinguishing the commonalities and differences in influences on students’ global competence across
cultures guides the creation of educational strategies that foster cross-cultural understanding and global cooperation, and become responsible global citizens.

1. Introduction
Over the last two decades, the rise of globalization has underscored
the significance of global competence as a key determinant of student’s
success in an increasingly interconnected world. Numerous nations have
thus prioritized the development of students’ global competence within
their national education frameworks (OECD, 2018; Tsiligiris and Lawton, 2018; UNESCO, 2015). The Council of Chief State School Officers
(CCSSO) proposed that as the capacity and disposition to understand
and act on issues of global significance, global competence is crucial for
ensuring they understand and navigate the complexities of global market operations, the challenges and opportunities of transnational production, and the role of social entrepreneurship in human development.
Improving these competencies will help students adapt to future job
markets and engage with issues related to economic and cultural
inequality (Tsiligiris and Lawton, 2018).
UNESCO promotes global citizenship education by developing
cognitive, social-emotional, and behavioral skills, highlighting their
connection to global competence. This comprehensive approach enables
learners to grasp global governance, appreciate diversity, and acquire
essential civic skills. Ultimately, it aims to nurture informed and

responsible global citizens who actively address contemporary global
challenges (UNESCO, 2015). In alignment with these directives, a
plethora of initiatives have been launched to enhance students’ global
competence, including the promotion of extensive language learning,
the establishment of exchange programs for students and teachers, and
the integration of international experiences into the educational syllabus (Trede et al., 2013).
Since the OECD (2018) proposed a framework of global competence
for 21st-century students, global competence has become a hot topic in
international education again. Reviewing the relevant literature, lots of
them just pay great attention to comparing the differences and similarities among different global competence frameworks (Simpson and
Dervin, 2019; Lee and Stankov, 2023; Kim et al., 2023), while fewer
studies explore students’ global competence development in actual with
empirical study, especially figure out the influencing factors of secondary school students in a comprehensive way (Hu and Hu, 2021).
According to Bronfenbrenner (1979)’s theory, students’ global competence can be influenced by both personal, such as gender and global
self-efficacy, and school contextual factors, such as school location, international exchange programs, foreign language learning (Jang et al.,
2023; Meng, Zhu, and Cao, 2018; Wu and Zhang, 2024). Moreover,

Based on the framework of knowledge, attitudes, skills, and values
provided by the Council of Europe (2016) through its auditing of 101
existing conceptual schemes for global, intercultural, and civic competence, the OECD proposed a framework for global competence for the
21st century. This framework encompasses “knowledge about the world
and other cultures; skills to understand the world and take action; attitudes of openness, respect for individuals from diverse cultural backgrounds, global mindedness, and a commitment to valuing human
dignity and diversity” (OECD, 2018, pp. 12–18). To better measure
students’ global competence, OECD gave a more specific operational
concept and defined global competence as a multidimensional capacity,
which contains four main dimensions: “Examining issues of local, global
and cultural significance; understanding and appreciating the perspectives and world views of others; engaging in open, appropriate and
effective interactions across cultures and taking action for collective
well-being and sustainable development” (OECD, 2018, p. 9–11). The
first dimension indicates when students form views on global issues,
they have abilities to combine and reason global knowledge in critical
and effective ways. For the second dimension, it is necessary for students
to perceive others’ different perspectives and behaviors, especially those
who come from multiple cultural backgrounds. The ability to find
common ground while respecting differences is essential in an interconnected world. The third dimension highlights the importance of
students approaching interactions with people from diverse backgrounds with an open mind and respect for cultural norms. Key elements
include engaging sensitively and curiously, behaving appropriately to
honor these norms, and communicating effectively so that everyone can
express themselves and understand each other. The final dimension
stresses the role of young people as active, responsible members of society who engage with local and global issues. It recognizes their influence in various areas and the need for informed actions that amplify
their voices. Ultimately, globally competent individuals aim to improve
their communities and promote a just, peaceful, and sustainable world.
A thorough review of the literature shows that global competence is a
multidimensional concept that shares common underlying values with
related constructs, including intercultural competence, intercultural
communicative competence, global consciousness, and global citizenship. These constructs exhibit convergences in their foundational elements. The content and dimensions of global competence can be
summarized as follows: the core aspects of global competence encompass an understanding of local, global, and intercultural issues, along
with the knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values necessary to navigate
and engage effectively within a globally interconnected context.

2. Literature review
2.1. The conception of global competence
Global competence is not a new but a controversial concept, which
conveys meanings that are similar to or partially overlap with “intercultural competence” (Fantini, 2009), “intercultural communicative
competence”(Byram and Golubeva, 2020), “global consciousness”
(Gacel-Avila, 2005), and “global citizens” (Shiel, 2006). When defining
global competence, researchers often draw on perspectives from these
concepts (Council of Europe, 2016; Hunter et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2020).
After reviewing the academic literature on definitions, frameworks, and
assessment tools for global and intercultural competencies, as well as
relevant theoretical and empirical studies, Liu et al. (2020) identified
three primary components of global competence: knowledge and understanding, which encompasses world knowledge, an understanding of
globalization, and international academic knowledge; skills, including
the use of tools, cross-cultural communication, and international academic discourse; and attitudes and values, characterized by the intent to
interact, an openness to understand, respect, and appreciate people
outside one’s own culture, and an appreciation for one’s own culture
while recognizing that one’s worldview is not universal. Through
investigating 133 international educators and 42 human resources directors of multinational groups, Hunter(2004) put forward that global

2.2. Factors influencing students’ global competence
Global competence is increasingly recognized as a vital skill for
success in the 21st century, prompting many countries to prioritize
global education. Understanding the factors that contribute to the
development of global competence has become an essential focus of
research in this field (Jang et al., 2023; Sakamoto, 2022; Yang and Li,
2024).
Foreign language learning: Foreign language learning has been
viewed as a crucial accelerator to students’ global competence (Guo
et al., 2024; Meng et al., 2018; Sakamoto and Roger, 2023). The European Council (2002) recommends that students learn at least two foreign
languages, ideally starting at a young age, to enhance their global skills.
Meng et al. (2018) conducted the research with 206 Chinese international students in Belgium through a web-based survey, and the results
indicated that proficiency in English and the local language significantly
predicted students’ global competence, accounting for 32 % of the
variance in their global competence scores.

including study abroad opportunities, is a prevalent strategy for internationalization adopted by many countries (British Council, 2016;
Valls-Figuera et al., 2023; Gaitán-Aguilar et al., 2024). In the United
States, exchange programs have been a key part of educational practices
since 2001, supported by various state governments. The British Council
notes that countries like the US, UK, China, Russia, and Colombia have
adopted policies to promote equitable access and sustainable development in education internationalization (British Council, 2016. Valls-Figuera et al. (2023) conducted case study on students from Latin America
and the Caribbean studying in Barcelona and found that international
student mobility greatly enhances students’ multicultural competence.
Participants reported a better understanding of cultural differences,
improved self-acceptance, and increased adaptability, along with a
broader knowledge and deeper appreciation of multiculturalism.
Intercultural communication: Research shows that communicating
with people from different cultures greatly enhances students’ global
competence (Kunschak and Strotmann, 2023; Huang, 2023). This need
applies not only to mobile students but also to those who stay in their
home countries. To foster global competence in all students, schools
should create opportunities for intercultural interaction (Hofmeyr,
2023; Sabaté-Dalmau et al., 2024. Wang and Kulich (2015) found that
domestic students in China improved their intercultural competence
significantly through diverse cultural interactions. Their study also
indicated that online exchanges effectively develop students’ intercultural skills. Similarly, in the UK, engaging in intercultural activities
helps students form new friendships and boosts their cross-cultural
competencies, such as affective openness, embodied openness, ethical
orientation, and generosity (Huang, 2023).
Teacher’s intercultural beliefs: Teachers’ intercultural beliefs are
increasingly seen as essential for developing students’ global competence (Tichnor-Wagner et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2024). To better prepare students for global challenges, the idea that “All Teachers Are
Global Educators” has emerged. Tichnor-Wagner et al. (2019) developed
a theoretical framework highlighting the knowledge, skills, and attitudes teachers need. This includes an understanding of global issues and
current events, facilitating intercultural conversations that promote
active listening, critical thinking, and recognition of different perspectives, as well as encouraging empathy and appreciation for diverse
viewpoints (Tichnor-Wagner et al., 2019, pp. 8–10).
ICT competence: In the 21st century, communication technologies
like social media and mobile devices create new avenues for crosscultural interactions, enhancing students’ global competence (Butum
et al., 2022). To take advantage of these opportunities, students must
develop advanced ICT skills. Butum et al. (2022) found a close association between university activities that involve ICT skills and the growth
of students’ global competence. Zhang and Zhou (2023) studied 17,055
15-year-olds in in great China region using Multilevel Structural Equation Modelling (MSEM) based on self-determination theory. Their
findings showed that students’ perceived autonomy, competence, and
relatedness in ICT use positively affected their intercultural knowledge,
skills, and attitudes.
Cooperation: Research shows that cooperation among students and
schools greatly enhances students’ global competence (Bohman and
Miklikowska, 2021; Sakai et al., 2021; Zhang and Zhu, 2020). When
students participate in cooperative activities with teacher support, they
are more likely to build friendships with a diverse range of peers,
including those who are different from themselves (Bohman and
Miklikowska, 2021). Additionally, collaborative programs like the
Global Lesson Study help teachers improve their intercultural communication skills and diversify their teaching approaches (Sakai et al.,
2021). These initiatives not only benefit teachers but also foster more
cross-cultural interactions in schools, enhancing the overall quality of
global competence education (Zhang and Zhu, 2020).
Cultural capital and contexts: Students’ global competence is
closely related to cultural factors, such as family cultural capital and
societal contexts. Family cultural capital, particularly parents’

educational backgrounds, plays a significant role. Educated parents tend
to value cultural diversity and instill tolerant attitudes in their children,
passing these values down to the next generation (Tsang et al., 2020;
Margaryan et al., 2021). Beyond family influences, a country’s cultural
context also shapes students’ global competence. Different nations
develop unique values based on local economic, social, cultural, and
educational factors (Lee and Stankov, 2023; Kerkhoff and Cloud, 2020).
For example, in traditional societies, social norms may limit students’
freedom to express their opinions. In contrast, regions that prioritize
self-expression promote openness and freedom, allowing students to
develop critical and independent thinking skills, which are vital for
global competence (Sakamoto, 2022).
In addition, some other factors also influence students’ global
competence (Wu and Zhang, 2024; Solhaug and Kristensen, 2020; Jang
et al., 2023). One key demographic factor is gender; studies show that
girls often develop better global competence than boys, demonstrating
higher intercultural awareness, knowledge of diversity, empathy,
emotional intelligence, perspective-taking, and sensitivity (Solhaug and
Kristensen, 2020). Another important factor is the school’s location.
Students in more economically developed areas tend to have higher
global competence levels (Jang et al., 2023), indicating that the resources and opportunities available in these regions significantly
contribute to developing global awareness and skills.
In summary, students’ global competence is influenced by several
factors, such as foreign language learning, student and teacher mobility,
interactions with international individuals, and teachers’ intercultural
beliefs. These influences can be divided into individual-level and schoollevel factors. By exploring both levels, we can avoid the statistical errors
found in earlier studies that incorrectly generalized individual findings
to whole groups. Additionally, analyzing these factors within different
cultural contexts offers valuable insights into the universal influences on
global competence and how cultural backgrounds uniquely shape this
competency.
Therefore, three research questions guided the present study:
(1) How do students’ individual factors (e.g., gender, cultural possessions, perception of co-operation at school, and perceived ICT
competence) influence students’ global competence across cultural contexts?
(2) How do school factors (e.g., school location, school principal’s
view on teachers’ multicultural and egalitarian beliefs, exchange
programme, and foreign languages in curriculum) influence students’ global competence across cultural contexts?
(3) What are the similarities and differences in the factors influencing students’ global competence across cultural contexts?
3. Method

test mainly assessed students’ knowledge and cognitive skills of global

knowledge, cognitive skills, social skills, and attitudes toward global
competence. Though there are 79 countries or economies participated in
PISA 2018, only 27 countries and economies participated in both the
global competence test and the global competence module within the
student questionnaire. Additionally, students from 39 countries and
economies completed only the global competence module in the questionnaire (OECD, 2020a). OECD employs the systematic probability
proportional method to size sampling: In the first stage, schools are

4. Variables
4.1. Dependent variables
According to the framework of global competence in PISA 2018 and
specific situations of selected four countries, four scales were chosen
from student questionnaires to measure students’ global competence in
the present study. The first scale ‘self-efficacy regarding global issues’ is
the indicator of sub-competence ‘Examining issues of local, global and
cultural significance’. This scale consists of six items requesting students
answer from “1 = I couldn’t do this” to “4 = I could do this easily”. For
example, “Explain how carbon dioxide emissions affect global climate
change”. Higher scores on ‘self-efficacy regarding global issues’ indicate
students perceive stronger self-efficacy in their ability to deal with
global competence tasks. Cronbach’s α of ‘self-efficacy regarding global
issues’ were, respectively, 0.91, 0.84, 0.88, and 0.88 for China, Germany, Turkey, and Mexico.

unified and standard instrument to test students’ global competence,
serving as the foundation for this research database. A four-dimensional
world values framework guided the selection of participant cultural
contexts, encompassing students from China, Germany, Turkey, and
Mexico. The analysis of the factors influencing students’ global
competence in these diverse settings not only sheds light on the universal and unique determinants across different cultural contexts but
also underscores the critical need for educational systems worldwide to

Regarding homogeneous influences, family cultural capital, students’ ICT competence, and cooperation skills all have impacts on students’ global competence development across four countries. Among
these factors, family cultural capital consistently shows a positive
impact on global competence. This finding aligns with Schulz et al.
(2018), who analyzed ICCS 2016 data and found a strong positive correlation between students’ civic literacy and their family’s cultural
capital. Families with substantial cultural capital often have more
educated parents who can provide better educational resources and
opportunities for their children. For example, children of parents proficient in foreign languages often have greater access to language
learning resources (OECD, 2020a). Additionally, these parents tend to
have more liberal and open-minded attitudes, which reduce geographic
prejudice in their children. This openness facilitates children’s integration into diverse cultural settings and enhances their adaptability in a
globalized educational landscape (Margaryan et al., 2021).
Secondly, students’ ICT competence significantly influences all four
dimensions of their global competence: Higher ICT competence levels
correlate with advanced global competence development. This finding
further enhances the understanding of the relationship between digital
literacy and global competence. Enhanced ICT skills enable students to
use communication technologies, such as social media and mobile devices, more effectively, allowing them to better understand global socioeconomic changes and engage in more efficient global learning.
Consequently, students improve their ability to comprehend and
respond to information from around the world (Butum et al., 2022).
Furthermore, in the era of digital transformation, improved ICT
competence empowers students to explore and perceive different cultures actively. It cultivates cross-cultural communication skills and enhances the ability to cooperate with others in a globalized context
(Bingzhuan, 2018).
Thirdly, cooperation significantly contributes to the development of
all four dimensions of global competence among students in the countries studied. This aligns with Sakamoto’s (2022) analysis of foreign
language teachers, university students, professionals with international
experience, and global competence researchers in Japan. Sakamoto
found that promoting cooperation and cross-cultural communication
skills helps enhance students’ openness to learning, critical thinking,
self-awareness, and communication abilities. Moreover, students with a
strong sense of cooperation usually have an open mindset and a keen
interest in learning. They possess a higher awareness of intercultural
communication, which allows them to broaden their international perspectives and enhance their global thinking, international cooperation,
and communication skills, thereby improving their level of global
competitiveness (Butum et al., 2022).
Lastly, gender plays a role in the development of students’ global
competence, with girls generally outperforming boys across the four
countries—except in Germany, where boys excel in self-efficacy
regarding global issues, enriching previous research (Jang et al.,
2023). Girls significantly excel over boys in global knowledge and attitudes, which may be attributed to girls typically having a stronger
interest and abilities in language learning and cross-cultural communication compared to boys (Jang et al., 2023). Furthermore, girls often
exhibit greater emotional nuance and higher levels of openness, tolerance, and respect for different cultures. This results in enhanced intercultural awareness, empathy, and sensitivity (Holm et al., 2009; Solhaug

and Kristensen, 2020), enabling them to interact effectively with diverse
groups and adapt to various cultural environments while maintaining a
broader multicultural perspective and flexibility.
In addition to homogeneous factors, due to differences in culture,
systems, and educational traditions among the four countries, the study
reveals heterogeneous factors affecting students’ global competence in
each country. Firstly, geographical location has a significant positive
impact on the global competence of students in Germany, Turkey, and
Mexico, excluding China, with students in more advantageous locations
showing higher levels of development in global competence. These
findings are consistent with the previous research (Zhang and Wen,
2018). Zhang and Wen (2018) found that students living in more advantageous geographical locations not only have access to higher quality
school educational resources but also to more libraries, museums, cultural and art venues, and other cultural resources, enriching their
campus life outside of school. The situation in China may be related to its
unique geographical characteristics, which is constrained by the narrow
size of the Hong Kong SAR, with nearly 80 % of the schools surveyed in
this study being in cities or metropolitan areas, whereas for other
countries the proportion is much smaller: Germany (26.6 %), Turkey
(64 %) and Mexico (51.4 %).
Exchange programs significantly impact global competence for students in Germany, Mexico, and China, but not for those in Turkey.
Generally, these programs enhance students’ global competence. For
example, in AFS (American Field Service) programs, students spend
months living with host families in another country, allowing them to
see how culture shapes values and decisions. They gain new perspectives, question their assumptions, and expand their understanding of
cultural differences and global challenges, which helps them appreciate
diverse viewpoints (OECD, 2020a). However, various studies have
suggested that studying abroad does not necessarily lead to improved
attitudes and dispositions; in some instances, it can even be a stressful
experience for students (Berg, 2009). Turkey, with its distinct historical
and cultural orientation, presents a different scenario. As the country
hosting the largest number of refugees in the world (UNHCR, 2020),
Turkey exhibits a relatively high level of ethnocentrism. This nationalist
tendency can significantly affect students’ intercultural communication
apprehension (Alici and Kaya, 2022), potentially hindering the development of global competence among Turkish students participating in
exchange programs. Moreover, countries that emphasize values of
self-expression and secular rationality are more likely to foster openness
towards other cultures. This encourages students to seek broader perspectives and diverse understandings through exchanges (Sakamoto,
2022). This trend is evident in Germany, China, and Mexico. In contrast,
Turkey, where traditional and survival values intersect, faces greater
challenges related to cultural identity and adaptation for students
involved in international exchange programs (Yeganeh, 2024).
Lastly, the foreign languages in the curriculum positively influences
the global competence of German students and Turkish students, while
adversely affecting that of Mexican. However, an increase in the number
of foreign languages learned by Mexican students correlates with a
higher development of their global competence. This contrasting phenomenon between Mexico highlights existing issues in foreign language
education. It is undeniable that Mexico prioritizes foreign language
learning and teaching for their students. According to the OECD, only
14.8 % of students in Mexico do not learn foreign languages in school.
Furthermore, the number of foreign languages studied in Mexico contributes to students’ awareness of global issues, attitudes toward immigrants, and cognitive adaptability (OECD, 2020a). However,
compared to Germany, foreign language teaching in Mexico is more
teacher-centered and linguistically oriented. Borjian (2015) gathered
perspectives from 76 English teachers regarding English education and
revealed that 21 % of the teachers noted that the focus remains on
teaching grammar skills rather than emphasizing communicative
competence within the Mexican educational system. Furthermore, the
narrow scope of the English curriculum can be partially attributed to a

7. Conclusion and implications

This article presents the research findings of China’s National Social
Science Fund General Education Project, "Cultivating Adolescents’
Conception of Justice Through the Lens of Public Reason" (Grant No.
BEA240072).

This study addresses the gap of previous research in exploring the
factors influencing students’ global competence, particularly by integrating both individual and school-level factors. Additionally, it situates
the exploration of these influences within a broader cultural context.
Through a comparative analysis of countries represented on global
cultural maps, the study aims to draw meaningful conclusions about the
effectiveness of various educational strategies. These findings contribute
to the understanding of how to equip young people from diverse cultures
with the necessary skills and knowledge to navigate an increasingly
interconnected world as informed and responsible global citizens. The
findings showed that family cultural capital, students’ perceived ICT
competence, and perception of co-operation at school were positive and
significant factors influencing students’ global competence across varied
countries. While the effects of school location, participation in international exchange programs, and foreign language learning in the formal
curriculum on students’ global competence significantly differ among
diverse countries backgrounds. The common and differing influencing
factors suggest that, when considering how to enhance students’ global
competence on a global scale with various cultural contexts, we can
focus on the following key points: Firstly, it highlights the need for
schools and educational authorities to address disparities in international activity participation and resource access due to differences in
family cultural capital and school location. Efforts should include
providing equitable learning opportunities and compensating for
developmental gaps in global competence. Secondly, the importance of
digital literacy is emphasized, suggesting schools integrate information
technology into curricula and encourage students to engage in digital
exploration and social media use. This approach aims to improve students’ information discernment and facilitate cross-cultural communication and cooperation through online platforms. Lastly, the study
underscores the significance of foreign language learning and cultural
exchange programs, advising that teaching strategies should encourage
students’ cultural perceived and exchanges rather than being overly
teacher-centered and grammar-oriented, and be culturally sensitive to
maximize benefits and minimize potential cultural barriers.
The present study has certain limitations. Firstly, while the framework for global competence is based on the one provided by the OECD,
which was developed through the synthesis of existing concepts of
global, intercultural, and civic competence, some research indicates that
this framework may reflect a Western-centric perspective (Sakamoto,
2022). This limitation could hinder a comprehensive understanding of
global competence within diverse cultural contexts. Therefore, future
research should adopt a more critical approach to examining the concept
of global competence, giving consideration to its diversity and
complexity across different cultural environments. Secondly, the PISA
2018, the first circle to assess students’ global competence, involved
participation from fewer than half of the countries, with only 39 countries and economies represented. This limitation resulted in significant
data gaps, particularly from major countries such as the United States.
Future studies should explore a broader sample to enhance the generalizability of findings. Thirdly, the present study focused exclusively on
student- and school-level predictors of global competence, neglecting
the influence of teachers, which is critical for students’ development in
this area. Lastly, the reliance on cross-sectional data restricts the ability
