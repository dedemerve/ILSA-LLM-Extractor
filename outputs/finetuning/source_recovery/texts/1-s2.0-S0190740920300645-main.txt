This study aims to understand how bullying victimization impacts on adolescents' academic achievement
through the serial mediation of school belonging and academic engagement in rural China. Altogether 813 rural
adolescents in southwestern China (359 boys, 454 girls, M age = 15 years, SD = 0.00) completed the
Multidimensional Peer Victimization Scale-Revised (MPVS-R), Psychological Sense of School Membership
(PSSM) scale, Utrecht Work Engagement Scale-Student, and the Program for International Student Assessment
(PISA) tests. The results indicated that: (1) boys reported signiﬁcantly higher bullying victimization and lower
school belonging and academic engagement than girls; (2) school belonging and academic engagement were
identiﬁed the serial mediators between bullying victimization and academic achievement in the models for the
whole sample and boys group; (3) the mediation model for girls group diﬀered from that for boys, as academic
engagement did not predict academic achievement in girls. The ﬁndings highlight the importance of fostering a
positive school climate to ﬁght against negative consequences associated with bullying victimization.

1. Introduction
School bullying has become a global concern for the public as well
as a ﬁerce battleﬁeld for educational psychologists (Ladd, Ettekal, &
Kochenderfer-Ladd, 2017). Globally, about 246 million children were
suﬀering from school violence and bullying (including cyberbullying)
(UNESCO, 2017), and about 32% students in 144 countries have ever
been bullied by their peers at school (UNESCO, 2019). Bullying victimization has been associated with physical (stomachaches and headaches) and psychological (depression and anxiety) problems, and poor
academic achievement (Cook, Williams, Guerra, Kim, & Sadek, 2010;
Glew, Fan, Katon, Rivara, & Kernic, 2005; Luk, Wang, & SimonsMorton, 2010; Pabian & Vandebosch, 2016). Therefore, understanding
how bullying victimization shapes students' physical and psychological
development is decisive in bullying prevention and intervention.
However, most existing studies have extensively explored bullying
victimization in urban schools in European-American contexts (Doumas
& Midgett, 2019), leaving rural schools in developing countries understudied. In China, for instance, 50.6% of students in rural middle
schools reported having ever been bullied by peers (Li, Zhang, & Wang,
2018). This is remarkably higher than the global level (32%) reported
by UNESCO (2019), indicating that rural Chinese adolescents are facing
a more diﬃcult situation than the urban ones. Recent studies found that

individual or a group of youths, which involves an observed or perceived power imbalance that is repeated or highly likely to be repeated
(Olweus, 1993). In the U.S., about 20.8% of students reported being a
target of bullying (U.S. Department of Education, 2017), and 50% of
students reported being afraid of being bullied (Luxenberg, Limber, &
Olweus, 2014). In Western China, about 50.6% of students reported
that peers had ever bullied them in middle schools (Li et al., 2018).
Further, a recent study in rural China found that a total of 31.6% of the
left-behind children reported bullying victimization and were associated with being drunk frequently, poorer peer and family

Bullying prevention and intervention programs are needed urgently to

Recently, more and more bullying studies have focused on the
victim of bullying, especially the results of victimization (Garandeau &
Salmivalli, 2019; Kelly et al., 2015; Moore et al., 2017; Salmivalli &
Peets, 2018). Bullying victimization refers to the phenomenon that a
child is considered to be the target of the bullying and exposed to the
kind of types harms from one or more peers (Olweus, 1993; Sophie
et al., 2017). Bullying victimization is associated with physical, psychological and academical problems such as being depressed, anxious,
and avoidant (Cook et al., 2010) and having stomachaches and headaches, behavior problems (Kim et al., 2015) and poor academic
achievement (Glew et al., 2005). Longitudinal evidence indicates that
the students who are bullied in primary schools continue to experience
victimization into middle school and are at higher risk for school disengagement (Buhs, Gary, & Herald, 2006). In addition, bullying victimization is also related to increased suicide attempts and completions
in adulthood (Klomek et al., 2009). Recently, Cao et al. (2020) found
that loneliness partially mediated the association between bullying
victimization and suicidal ideation while gender moderated the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation in rural
Chinese adolescents. In addition, Li et al. (2019) found that boys at
rural schools and with low academic performance were most likely to
bully. Therefore, we hypothesize that the working mechanism of bullying victimization might be gendered in Chinese rural adolescents.

1.3. Academic engagement and achievement
Academic engagement is a psychological state of concentration or
involvement, which is a positive, fulﬁlling state of mind related to
learning (Fredricks & Paris, 2004; Ouweneel, Blanc, & Schaufeli, 2014;
Salanova, Schaufeli, Martínez, & Bresó, 2010). Brieﬂy, academic engagement has three major constructs: (1) vitality is outstanding energy
and resilience in learning, and a will to work hard for academics; (2)
dedication means that an individual has a strong sense of meaning,
devotes themselves wholeheartedly to learning, and dares to accept
challenges; and (3) centration is a kind of wholehearted pleasure state,
where one focuses on learning and experiencing pleasure (Fang, Shi, &
Zhang, 2008).
A recent study has found that academic engagement is inﬂuenced by
bullying victimization (Ladd et al., 2017). The experiences of being
bullied could cause students to develop negative school-related attitudes and motivations. In addition, bullying victimization would discourage student's propensity to initiate and actively participate in
classroom activities. Accordingly, less independent participation will
cause compromised classroom learning and achievement (see Finn,
1989, 1993; Fredricks & Paris, 2004; Ladd et al., 2000). These ﬁndings
jointly indicated that academic engagement might be highly correlated
with academic achievement (Furrer & Skinner, 2003; Salanova et al.,
2010). Accordingly, we hypothesize that academic engagement might
play a mediating role between bullying victimization and academic
achievement and will test this hypothesis in the study.

1.2. School belonging
Belonging is widely deﬁned as an individual’s identiﬁcation or
emotional bonding of being a member of one group (Demanet & Houtte,
2012; Hagerty, Lynchsauer, Patusky, Bouwsema, & Collier, 1992;
Hagerty & Patusky, 1995). School belonging refers to a student's sense
of being accepted, respected, and included by peers and adults in a
school community (Goodenow, 1993). This concept is similar or at least
related to other concepts such as school membership, perceptions of
school climate, school connectedness (Reaves, Mcmahon, Duﬀy, & Ruiz,
2018; Wang & Degol, 2016). School belonging is particularly crucial for
students, as it is associated with both academic successes (Wang &
Eccles, 2012; Wang & Holcombe, 2010) and psychological adjustment
(Lester, Waters, & Cross, 2013; Loukas, Cance, & Batanova, 2016). This
is because school belonging plays the role of mediator thus can buﬀer
students against bullying and the negative associated consequences
(Duggins, Kuperminc, Henrich, Smalls-Glover, & Perilla, 2016;
Goldweber, Waasdorp, & Bradshaw, 2013; O’Brennan & Furlong, 2010;
Raskauskas, Gregory, Harvey, Rifshana, & Evans, 2010). In particular, a
strong sense of school belonging is associated with lower levels of
bullying victimization (Duggins et al., 2016; O’Brennan & Furlong,
2010), bullying perpetration (Goldweber et al., 2013; Raskauskas et al.,
2010), and internalizing problems associated with being a target of
bullying (Goldweber et al., 2013).
Besides, school belonging is highly correlated with student academic development: the stronger the school belonging, the better the
academic performance (Goodenow & Grady, 1993; Goodenow, 1993;
Prince & Hadwin, 2013). On the one hand, when experiencing the lack
of respect, love and acceptance from peers or teachers at school, students are more likely to refuse to invest in their studies and accordingly,
encounter some learning diﬃculties and school failure. On the other
hand, when experiencing being nicknamed, squeezed, or even beaten,
students will ﬁnd it diﬃcult to go to school because of the unhappy
campus life (Biggart, O'Hare, & Connolly, 2013). Therefore, school
belonging is directly aﬀected by school bullying (Biggart et al., 2013)
and will indirectly aﬀect academic performance (Goodenow & Grady,
1993; Goodenow, 1993; Prince & Hadwin, 2013). Therefore, based on

1.4. The context of this study
China has two diﬀerent worlds: rural versus urban areas. And the
self-reported prevalence of victimization ranged from 2% to 66% in the
two diﬀerent areas (Chan & Wong, 2015). In particular, more than half
of rural students have ever been bullied by peers in middle schools (Li
et al., 2018). About 94.96 million students were living in rural China
(Statistical Report, 2016), and accordingly, there might be more than
48 million students suﬀering from school bullying. Thus, empirical
studies on Chinese adolescents in rural schools are needed urgently. In
addition, studies on school bullying in Chinese adolescents are critical
in theoretical development and practical improvement, as school bullying might have both universal patterns and culturally sensitive
characteristics (Li, 2008). In particular, China is a collectivism society,
whereas European-American countries are individualism society. Thus,
examining school bullying among Chinese adolescents could help to
verify the universal patterns and to identify the society-speciﬁc features
therefore to better prepare prevention and intervention programs in
developing countries (Han, Zhang, & Zhang, 2017).
Previous bullying studies have revealed signiﬁcant gender diﬀerences in school bullying and bullying victimization in both non-Chinese
and Chinee societies. The studies in European-American societies found
that boys typically reported higher levels of both perpetration and
victimization than girls (Card, Sawalani, & Little, 2008; Menesini &
Salmivalli, 2017), the classroom context had more signiﬁcant eﬀects on
girls' than on boys' bullying perpetration (Salmivalli & Voeten, 2004),
genders moderated the relationship between bullying and suicidal
ideation (Klomek, Sourander, & Gould, 2010). And the studies in rural
China found that loneliness partially mediated the association between
bullying victimization and suicidal ideation while gender moderated in
the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation
(Cao et al., 2020; Li et al., 2019). It is thus very sensitive to model the
eﬀects of bullying victimization on boys and girls, separately, in this
study.

The Chinese version of MPVS has been employed to explore the characteristics of peer victimization and the bidirectional relationship between peer victimization and internalizing problems among rural-tourban migrant children in China (Guo, Chen, Ye, Pan, & Lin, 2017).
MPVS-R contains 20 items that could be classiﬁed into ﬁve constructs:
physical, social, verbal, attack on property, and electronic. The students
were asked how often the following things happened to them in the past
30 days: “Other students called me names,” “Other students made fun
of me,” “Other students picked on me,” and “I got hit and pushed by
other students.” They were invited to rank their responses on a ﬁve
point-Likert scale ranging from “Never” (0) to “Almost Always” (4), and
higher scores indicated more self-reported victimization. In a validation
study (Betts et al., 2015), MPVS-R was found a useful tool with satisfactory reliability and validity for assessing bullying victimization in
adolescents. In this study, Cronbach alpha coeﬃcients ranged from 0.81
to 0.86 for the testing items and the Cronbach alpha for MPVS-R was
0.83.

Accordingly, this study is dedicated to understanding the underlying
mechanisms between bullying victimization and academic achievement
in Chinese students with two gendered models. In particular, we assume
that the two mediators – school belonging and academic engagement –
might co-play a serial mediating role in the relationship of bullying
victimization and academic achievement. This means that bullying
victimization impacts on academic achievement through school belonging and academic engagement, in a sequential manner. This study
will test this serial mediation model with an extensive scale survey of
Chinese adolescents.
In particular, we proposed the following four hypotheses (Fig. 1) for
this study:

2.2.2. School belonging
Perceived school belonging was assessed using the validated
Chinese version of the 18-item Psychological Sense of School
Membership (PSSM) scale (Goodenow, 1993). The original English
version of PSSM was found a valid and reliable tool to access teenagers'
school belonging and had a satisfactory Cronbach's alpha in both urban
and suburban students. Cheung and Hui (2003) translated and validated its Chinese version in an independent study. The Chinese PSSM
has two major constructs: school belonging (13 items, i.e., “I feel like
part of this school”, “the people in this school noticed that I was good at
doing something”) and feeling of rejection (5 items, i.e., “this school has
diﬃculty accepting students like me”, “sometimes, I feel like I don't
belong to this school”). The construct of school belonging had an alpha
of 0.88 and an Eigenvalue of 6.75, and that of the feeling of rejection
had an alpha of 0.71 and an Eigenvalue of 1.34. In this study, Cronbach
alpha coeﬃcients ranged from 0.73 to 0.91 for the testing items and the
Cronbach alpha for PSSM was 0.82.

H1: school belonging plays a mediating role between bullying victimization and academic achievement;
H2: academic engagement also plays a mediating role between
bullying victimization and academic achievement;
H3: school belonging and academic engagement co-play a serial
mediating role in the relationship of bullying victimization and
academic achievement.
H4: the model for boys is diﬀerent from that for girls.
2. Method
2.1. Participants

2.2.3. Academic engagement
The academic engagement was assessed using the validated Chinese
version of the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale—Student (Fang et al.,
2008; Schaufeli, Salanova, González-romá, & Bakker, 2002). The Chinese version has 17 items and three constructs: vitality, dedication, and
focus. The students were asked to address the following questions to
reﬂect their academic engagement: “when studying, I am full of energy”, “I ﬁnd learning very valuable and meaningful,” and “when
studying, I feel that time passes quickly”. In a validation study, it was
found a valid and reliable instrument for measuring Chinese teenagers'
engagement in learning (Yan, Wang, Li, Zheng, & Xu, 2018). In this
study, Cronbach alpha coeﬃcients ranged from 0.82 to 0.85 for the
testing items, and the Cronbach alpha for academic engagement was
0.84.

This study was part of a contract research project on junior secondary students' quality initiated and funded by the educational authorities of a rural area in G Province. This rural area is located in
southwestern China and consists of four districts and two counties. It
has 106 junior secondary schools altogether, and 102 of them are located in rural areas. The ﬁrst author got the contract through an oﬃcial
bidding system and was authorized by the Department of Education to
conduct this project in the 106 schools during 2018. One Grade 9 class
was randomly sampled from each participating school, resulting in 106
participating classes. All the students from the participating Grade 9
classes were invited to participate in this contract research project. As
the participants ranged in age between 14 and 17 years, only the 15years who were resident in rural areas and had completed all the surveys and tests were included in this study. All the students from urban
areas and of other ages were excluded from this study. This is because
the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA) exam was
designed to evaluate the academic performance of 15-year-olds in the
subject of reading, math, and science by the Organization for Economic
Co-operation and Development (OECD). In total, altogether 813 students (M age = 15 years, 359 boys, 454 girls) from 102 rural classes
were recruited for this study. By average, no more than eight students
were recruited from each participating class, which is an appropriate
ratio to control for the class and school eﬀects.

2.2.4. Academic achievement
Academic achievement was assessed using the publicly released
Chinese version of the Program for International Student Assessment
(PISA) exam. An internationally standardized academic achievement
test organized by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and
Development (OECD), PISA aims to evaluate the academic performance
of 15-year-olds in the subject of reading, math, and science. Its Chinese
version was ﬁrst applied to the 15 year-olds in Shanghai in 2009 (Sellar
& Lingard, 2013), and later on, released online for public use (http://
pisa.nutn.edu.tw/sample_tw.htm). In this study, the oﬃcial Chinese
versions of Reading (2015), Math (2012), and Science (2008) tests were
employed to evaluate the students' academic achievement. The raw
score for each student was converted to a standardized score (z score) in
the statistical analysis in this study.

2.2. Measures
2.2.1. Bullying victimization
Bully victimization was assessed using the Multidimensional Peer
Victimization Scale-Revised (MPVS-R) (Betts, Houston, & Steer, 2015).

4. Discussion
The primary objective of this study was to understand how bully
victimization impacted on academic achievement via the serial mediation of school belonging and academic engagement in 15-year-olds in
rural China. In particular, we were fascinated in understanding how
school bullying changed the perceived school belonging and mitigated
academic engagement and accordingly, rendered academic achievement in diﬀerent paths for the girls and boys. The results indicated a
serial mediation of school belonging and academic engagement between bullying victimization and academic achievement for the whole
sample and the boys group. This section will discuss the major ﬁndings
and their implications for program development and educational
policy.

as school belonging will have to work on academic achievement

programs that target antibullying behaviors in boys are essential for
improving adolescents’ sense of belonging to the school, and in turn,

coping could be promoted in boarding students, especially in boys, to
encourage their school belonging and academic engagement, and
eventually enhance their academic achievement. This approach may be
especially appropriate for the Chinese cultural context, which emphasizes collective values and restoration of interpersonal harmony. In
addition, the ﬁndings of this study can provide educational implications

have gendered path models and need gender-based prevention and
intervention programs to stop school bullying in rural schools.

4.2. The gendered path of serial mediation eﬀects
This study did not ﬁnd any empirical evidence to support the serial
mediation eﬀects of school belonging and academic engagement for
girls. Instead, it found support for the serial mediation model in the
whole sample and boys group, respectively. In particular, this study
found that boys were more bullied than girls, which is consistent with
the previous studies (Garandeau & Salmivalli, 2019; Kelly et al., 2015;
Moore et al., 2017). Boys usually are the leading group with the use of
violence, such as bullying (Olweus, 1993). And boys were more frequently targeted by victimization than girls (Sweeting, Young, West, &
Der, 2006). Similarly, Cao et al. (2020) found that a moderating eﬀect
of gender existed in the relationship between bullying victimization and
suicidal ideation. All these ﬁndings jointly suggest that gender diﬀerence should be taken into account when addressing the issue of bullying
victimization and academic achievement as well as developing bullying
prevention eﬀorts.
Importantly, in the model for girls group, we did not ﬁnd evidence
of a direct eﬀect of academic engagement on academic achievement. In
contrast, in the boys group model, academic engagement acted as a
buﬀer between school belonging and academic achievement. This
means that for male adolescents in rural China, the eﬀect of school
belonging on academic achievement was mediated through academic
engagement. One potential explanation for the contrasting ﬁndings
between boys’ and girls’ models is how we deﬁne academic engagement. The gendered path found in this study is partially supported by
literature on the tendency for girls to report valuing interpersonal relationships more than boys (Broidy & Agnew, 1997; Ma & Huebner,
2008). Girls also tend to rely on interpersonal relationships to support
much more than boys (Feingold, 1994). From this perspective, it makes
sense that having secure, supportive, interpersonal networks, a core
component of school belonging is more protective for girls than boys
(Kochenderfer-Ladd & Skinner, 2002).
Coincidently, existing studies have conﬁrmed the diﬀerential eﬀects
when assessing exposure to peer victimization and the consequences
stratiﬁed by gender (Krygsman & Vaillancourt, 2017; Lester, Cross, &
Shaw, 2012). Gender is a critical component of any discussion including
school belonging variable (Krygsman & Vaillancourt, 2019; Mirowsky &
Ross, 1995; Oliﬀe, Kelly, Bottorﬀ, Johnson, & Wong, 2017), and has
been pinned as a strong correlate of bully victimization with boys at
higher risk of both perpetrating and being victimized (Espelage & Holt,
2013; Gruber & Fineran, 2016). Therefore, it would be essential to
examine how the eﬃcacy of those prevention programs may diﬀer for
boys and girls. For boys, it would be necessary to think of diﬀerent
protective factors, such as the use of coaches and mentors to promote
their school belonging and academic engagement. For girls, schoolbased programs should address their academic and social functioning
and focus on direct instruction social-emotional competencies and social-cognitive interventions (Espelage & Hong, 2019).
