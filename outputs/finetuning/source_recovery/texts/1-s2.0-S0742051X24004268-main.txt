A B S T R A C T
This study explored emotion profiles and their relationships with students’ perceived teaching quality in 
mathematics classrooms (i.e., general and content-focused teaching). The data were collected from 1900 students 
(50.9% male; Mage = 13.88, SD = 1.16) in 76 mathematics classrooms across 11 secondary schools in Jiangsu, 
China. Three profiles of achievement emotions were obtained, namely positive (49.5%), medium (37.9%) and 
negative emotion profiles (12.6%). Facets of general teaching were more closely related to emotion profiles 
compared with content-focused teaching. The results indicated that adopting effective teaching to improve 
students’ learning experience is promising.
1. Introduction
Teachers and students have been exchanging knowledge in educa­
tion with an emphasis on cognitive capacities. While intelligence could 
explain nearly a quarter of the variance in academic success (Strenze, 
2007), a large portion remains unexplained. Since the beginning of the 
21st century, researchers have realized the pervasiveness of emotions 
among students and teachers in school education. Evidence showed that 
emotions in the classroom were closely linked to aspects of students’ 
learning, including self-regulation strategies, self-concept, engagement, 
persistence, and motivation (Beswick et al., 2023; Kitsantas et al., 2021; 
Liu et al., 2018; Middleton et al., 2023). It is therefore essential to 
thoroughly comprehend the antecedents of emotions within academic 
settings.
In addition to transmitting knowledge, high-quality teaching helps 
students develop cognitive and non-cognitive abilities through an 
emotionally supportive environment (Blazar & Kraft, 2017). Although 
correlations between teaching quality and learning-related emotions 
have been confirmed in many prior studies (e.g., Chen & Lu, 2022; Buri´c, 
2015; Liu et al., 2018; Maulana et al., 2016), the aspects of teaching 
have been examined separately, making it difficult to determine which 
facet of teaching contribute to specific emotions. Additionally, previous 
studies have often highlighted general pedagogical skills like clarity and 
classroom management, yet content-specific pedagogy remains under­
explored. Effective teaching hinges on Common Content Knowledge 
(CCK)—mathematical concepts known to the general public—and 
Specialized Content Knowledge (SCK)—in-depth knowledge for teach­
ing mathematics effectively. A feedback system for teachers should 
evaluate both CCK and SCK to enhance content delivery. Building on the 
foundational work of Philipp et al. (2007) and Lannin et al. (2013), our 
understanding of teacher knowledge has deepened, particularly in 
differentiating

correlation between diverse teaching styles and academic emotions (e.

between specific teaching characteristics and their impact on students’ 
academic emotions. By meticulously examining the distinct roles that 
each teaching characteristic plays, we aim to uncover the intricate dy­
namics that influence students’ emotional engagement with their 
studies, potentially leading to a more comprehensive understanding of 
how teaching approaches can be tailored to foster a positive and 
conducive learning environment.

and control-value theory (CVT), our study acknowledges the significant 
role these emotions play in the learning process, shaped by social and 
cultural contexts. The diversity in teaching practices, as examined by

1.1. Teaching quality: general versus content-focused teaching
At present, the definition of teaching quality mainly revolves around 
dimensions of general teaching, which include general pedagogical 
skills such as classroom management, teacher support, and cognitive 
activation. Many studies commonly employed a three-dimensional 
framework to assess teaching quality, as exemplified by the Classroom 
Assessment Scoring System (CLASS) developed by Pianta and Hamre 
(2009), which identifies classroom organization, emotional support, and 
instructional support as key aspects of teaching quality. Similarly, Laz­
arides and Buchholz (2019), and Klieme et al. (2009), have discussed a 
three-component model encompassing classroom management, teacher 
support, and cognitive activation. However, it is important to distin­
guish between general teaching practices and content-focused peda­
gogy. General teaching refers to skills and methods that are applicable 
across various subjects and grade levels, such as classroom management, 
emotional support, and cognitive activation. These skills are funda­
mental for creating a conducive learning environment and supporting 
students’ overall educational experience. For instance, effective class­
room management ensures that lessons proceed smoothly, minimizing 
disruptions and maximizing instructional time. Teacher support in­
volves providing emotional and academic assistance to students, 
fostering a positive teacher-student relationship. Cognitive activation 
includes strategies that engage students in higher-order thinking and 
problem-solving, applicable in any subject.
In contrast, content-focused teaching, or pedagogical content 
knowledge (PCK), involves the specialized knowledge and instructional 
techniques required to effectively teach specific subjects, such as 
mathematics or science. PCK encompasses an understanding of the 
content itself, the common misconceptions students might have, and the 
most effective ways to present the content to enhance understanding. 
For example, in mathematics, this might involve knowing multiple 
methods to solve a problem and understanding which method might be 
most accessible to students at different learning levels. It also includes 
the ability to explain complex concepts in simpler terms and to design 
subject-specific instructional materials and activities that promote deep 
learning. Blazar and Archer (2020) highlight a critical perspective in the 
ongoing debate regarding the effectiveness of conceptual versus direct 
teaching methods, particularly in the context of mathematics instruction 
for English Language Learners (ELLs). Their study underscores the 
importance of content-specific instruction, utilizing the Mathematical 
Quality of Instruction (MQI) instrument to examine its impact on stu­
dent achievement across the domains of mathematics and English. This 
critique aligns with the notion that while general instruction is funda­
mental, it may not sufficiently address the intricacies of subject-specific 
knowledge, potentially leading to gaps in mathematical understanding 
and explanations. Therefore, successful teaching not only caters to the 
social, emotional, and behavioral needs of students but also demands 
proficiency in delivering content-specific information, as supported by 
Pianta and Hamre (2009). Consequently, evaluations of teaching quality 
should encompass both general pedagogical skills and specialized con­
tent knowledge.
The ’Mathematical Quality of Instruction’ (MQI) conceptual 
framework examines teaching quality using five dimensions by 
surveying the interaction between students, teachers and content. Each 
element is evaluated in one of three ways: teacher-content, teacher- 
student, or student-content. They are mathematical richness, linking 
classroom practice to mathematics (or working with students and 
mathematics), errors and imprecision, student engagement in meaning- 
making and reasoning, and links between classroom work and mathe­
matics. While the initial focus was on students’ perceptions of general 
teaching practices, it is essential to delineate Common Content Knowl­
edge (CCK)—the mathematical understanding shared by the broader 
public—and Specialized Content Knowledge (SCK)—the in-depth, 
pedagogical knowledge necessary for effective mathematics instruc­
tion. The study’s primary emphasis on the first three dimen­
sions—mathematical richness, classroom-mathematics linkage, and 
addressing errors—aligns with both CCK and SCK. However, the latter 
two dimensions, which involve student engagement in meaning-making 
and the mathematical relevance of classroom activities, delve into the 
realm of SCK. These aspects, often not assessable by students due to their 
specialized nature, are critical for educators to foster a deeper under­
standing of mathematics.
Scholars have examined various teaching characteristics to deter­
mine whether general and content-specific instruments capture the 
quality of mathematics teaching. Hamre et al. (2013) provide a frame­
work for teacher effectiveness that, while focusing on emotional sup­
ports, classroom organization, and instructional supports, also subtly 
acknowledges the significance of content-specific teaching. This may 
enhance our understanding of how content-specific teaching can effec­
tively interact with general teaching strategies. Blazar et al. (2017)
found that the items in MQI produced factors that were distinct from 
those evaluated by CLASS, through the analysis of video-recorded 
classes. Likewise, Chen, Leung, and She (2023) also uncovered that 
general teaching was distinct from mathematics-focused teaching, sug­
gesting that general and math-focused instruction capture different as­
pects of teaching quality. As Hill and Grossman (2013) emphasized, 
while statements about general and content-specific characteristics on 
assessment tools may appear similar, their purposes may differ 
depending on the context. However, previous research has primarily 
concentrated on either general or content-focused teaching, without 
making an effort to establish connections between the two and explore 
broader aspects of teaching. Therefore, it is crucial to assess the structure 
of teaching quality before conducting a detailed analysis.
1.2. Teaching quality and achievement emotions
Achievement emotions refer to the emotions that students experi­
ence related to their academic activities and outcomes. These emotions 
are significantly influenced by how students perceive their teachers’ 
support. Teacher support occurs when students perceive the care and 
trust of their teachers, as well as their teachers’ willingness to help them 
when needed (Trickett & Moos, 1973). This support can be categorized 
into various forms, including emotional, academic, instrumental, 
appraisal competence and autonomy support (Lei et al., 2018; Liu et al., 
2021; Maulana et al., 2016). In this study, following previous research 
conducted in Chinese classrooms (e.g., Lu, Chen, & Qi, 2023; Liu et al., 
2018), teacher support encompasses academic, emotional, and appraisal 
support. Earlier research has consistently demonstrated that teacher 
support fosters students’ positive emotions and reduces negative emo­
tions (Kunter et al., 2013; Lazarides & Buchholz, 2019; Lei et al., 2018). 
For instance, Lazarides and Buchholz (2019) found that teacher support 
promoted enjoyment and reduced anxiety, but not boredom. Similarly, 
Liu et al. (2021) reported that high levels of teacher support were 
associated with greater enjoyment, relaxation, and less anxiety reported 
by students; however, there was no association between teacher support 
and boredom. These mixed findings suggest that while teacher support 
generally enhances positive emotions like enjoyment and reduces 
negative emotions like anxiety, its impact on boredom may depend on

other contextual factors. In contrast, Goetz et al. (2013) found a negative 
relationship between supportive teaching and boredom. As such, more 
studies are required to examine the relationship between teacher sup­

Cognitive activation refers to teachers’ practices that involve 
providing challenging tasks, activating prior knowledge (Hiebert & 
Grouws, 2007), connecting new material to previous knowledge or daily 
life (Baumert et al., 2010), encouraging student participation (Klieme 
et al., 2009) and prompting students to reflect and evaluate solutions 
(Stefanou et al., 2004). Typically, cognitive activation is reflected in 
three essential components of mathematics instruction: challenging ac­
tivities, activation of past knowledge, and content-related discourse 
practice (Lipowsky et al., 2009). These practices inspire students to use 
higher-order thinking skills to build extensive knowledge bases. 
Empirical research on cognitive activation and achievement emotions is 
limited and complex. Some studies have identified that when students 
perceive more cognitive activation, they exhibit more enjoyment and 
interest (Lazarides & Buchholz, 2019; Lazarides & Raufelder, 2021) and 
less boredom (Lazarides & Buchholz, 2019), as well as reduced anxiety 
(Lu et al., 2023). However, other research has been unable to establish a 
link between cognitive activation and enjoyment (Kunter et al., 2013) or 
between cognitive activation and anxiety (Lazarides & Buchholz, 2019; 
Lazarides & Raufelder, 2021).
Classroom management involves the actions instructors take to 
establish a disciplined setting for students’ academic and social- 
emotional learning (Evertson & Weinstein, 2006). Effective classroom 
management includes clearly communicating classroom rules and rou­
tines (Kunter et al., 2007), managing disruptive behavior or preventing 
troublesome behavior (Nash et al., 2016), organizing teaching effec­
tively and promoting student engagement (Lazarides & Raufelder, 
2021). In this study, classroom management is operationalized as 
effective behavior management in class, allowing students to focus on 
learning tasks and activities. Previous studies have shown that class­
room management is positively related to students’ enjoyment in 
mathematics (Kunter et al., 2013) and negatively related to boredom 
(Lazarides & Buchholz, 2019). Westphal et al. (2018) also found that 
classroom management negatively correlates with boredom but not with 
enjoyment. Additionally, Chen & Lu, 2022 clarified the effectiveness of 
classroom management in relieving students’ boredom in mathematics, 
while its effect on enjoyment varies across different educational contexts 
(e.g., Hong Kong versus England). Direct control or rules from teachers 
in Hong Kong classrooms might undermine students’ enjoyment by 
decreasing their mathematics self-concept. Similarly, Tsai et al. (2008)
demonstrated that teachers’ direct behavioral control—manifested 
through actions such as oral warnings, hints, and the implementation of 
rewards or punishments—negatively impacted student interest in 
mathematics.
In contrast, studies on the connection between content-focused 
teaching and achievement emotions or affect-related factors are highly 
inadequate. Scattered evidence showed that math-focused instruction 
was significantly associated with students’ competence beliefs and 
enjoyment in mathematics (Chen et al., 2023; Blazar & Kraft, 2017). To 
elaborate, mathematical richness was positively linked to students’ 
competence beliefs and enjoyment in mathematics, while teachers’ er­
rors and imprecision showed opposite correlations. The term "richness" 
in this context refers to the depth of mathematical concepts and skills 
being taught to students, which is reflected in the content, 
problem-solving techniques, and pedagogical methods used, such as the 
development of mathematical generalizations. On the other hand, errors 
and imprecision in mathematics instruction were primarily related to 
significant mathematical errors or oversights, unclear or imprecise 
expression or notation, and lack of clarity in the teaching approach 
(Blazar et al., 2017).
Working with students in mathematics captures teachers’ responses 
to students’ ideas, correcting students’ errors and difficulties, respond­
ing to students’ mathematical output and creativity, detecting 
mathematical understanding of students’ performance, and continuing 
instruction on their ideas (Blazar et al., 2017). Few studies have directly 
linked working with students to achievement emotions. However, prior 
work has shown that when teachers give students more constructive 
feedback, their perceived autonomy, confidence, and interest in learning 
increase significantly compared to their peers (Kiemer et al., 2015). A 
recent study by Chen et al. (2023) may provide some supportive evi­
dence, and they identified that working with students is positively 
correlated with enjoyment of mathematics, while it is negatively 
correlated with boredom.
1.3. Emotion profiles
Mixed emotions can occur in academic contexts when various ele­
ments of a situation evoke different emotional responses, or when 
emotions shift quickly and are perceived as happening simultaneously 
(Hoemann et al., 2017; Russell, 2017). Exploring emotion profiles holds 
great potential in identifying emotional distributions and their re­
lationships with possible antecedent factors, such as classroom teaching. 
This information can be used to develop more effective teaching stra­
tegies, create more emotionally supportive classroom environments, and 
improve students’ academic performance and well-being. In addition, it 
can also be used to examine the consistency of the results obtained by 
two different methods (variable-centered vs. person-centered methods). 
Emotion profiles are distinct combinations of emotions that students 
experience in relation to their academic activities. For example, Rob­
inson et al. (2017) identified four affective profiles among adult stu­
dents: positive, deactivated, negative, and moderate-low. Similarly, 
Robinson et al. (2020)
found four emotion profiles—positive, 
moderate-low deactivated, negative, and moderate high all emo­
tions—based on happiness, excitement, boredom, and frustration. Tze 
et al. (2020) identified three emotion profiles—positive, negative, and 
mixed—considering the fluctuations of emotions. Such knowledge is 
instrumental in developing teaching strategies that not only support 
cognitive activation and reduce negative emotions like boredom and 
anxiety but also foster environments conducive to positive emotional 
experiences, thereby enhancing students’ academic performance and 
well-being. This dual approach provided a comprehensive analysis of 
the consistency of results, revealing that while classroom management 
strategies may not increase enjoyment, they effectively mitigate 
boredom. These insights suggest that a deeper exploration of emotion 
profiles could yield valuable strategies for improving the emotional 
climate of classrooms and, by extension, student learning experiences.
In addition to identifying emotion profiles, some studies have 
explored their antecedents and effects. Robinson et al. (2020) examined 
the relationship between academic control and value and emotion 
profiles, finding that students with high academic control were more 
likely to be in the positive emotion profile, whereas academic value 
showed little relationship with emotion profiles. They also found stu­
dents with positive affective profiles performed better in science and had 
higher science career intentions compared to those with negative and 
deactivated profiles. Similarly, Wu et al. (2023) found that students with 
low learning anxiety had better academic scores compared to those with 
moderate and high learning anxiety.
Although there has been no investigation into the connection be­
tween emotion profiles and students perceived teaching quality, we 
hypothesize that a correlation exists, drawing on previous associations 
observed between students perceived teaching quality and academic 
emotions (e.g., Kitsantas et al., 2021; Lazarides & Buchholz, 2019).
1.4. The present study
This study aims to delineate distinct emotional profiles derived from 
a range of achievement-related emotions. Additionally, it will explore 
the connection between various teaching dimensions rated by students 
and these emotional profiles in Chinese mathematics classrooms. This

2. Method
2.1. Participants
Before the investigation, the Human Research Ethics Committee in 
the author’s academic institution granted ethical approval for this 
research. A total of 1900 questionnaires were collected, and participants 
were from 76 mathematics classrooms in 11 secondary schools in 
Jiangsu, China (50.9% girls; Mage = 13.88, SD = 1.16). Even with a large 
sample size, small group sizes (n < 5; McNeish et al., 2016) make 
group-level effects difficult to detect. Thus, this study excludes group 
sizes under five. After deleting outliers and invalid data, 1762 data were 
finally included in the analyses (49.3% girls), with participating class 
sizes ranging from 5 to 49 among 68 classrooms. Besides, there are 396 
students (22.5%) from Grade 7, 846 students (48.0%) from Grade 8, 520 
students (29.5%) from Grade 9; 425 students (24.1%) from rural and 
suburban areas and 1336 students (75.9%) from urban areas and one 
student (.1%) failed to report his/her family location. As the study 
focused on students’ perceptions of teaching, no direct observation or 
evaluation of teachers’ practices by experts was included.
2.2. Measures
All studied variables were assessed with a 5-point Likert scale 
focusing on the mathematics classroom, ranging from 1 (strongly 
disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).
Achievement emotions were assessed with 23 items using the 
Achievement Emotions Questionnaire (AEQ; Pekrun et al., 2002). 
Sample items of several emotions are "I am looking forward to learning a 
lot in math class (enjoyment)," "I find mathematics boring (boredom)," and "I 
feel embarrassed when I cannot answer the teacher’s questions correctly in 
class (shame).” Cronbach alpha (α) of each emotion ranged from .747 to 
.947.
General teaching rated by students was assessed with 16 items. In 
detail, teacher support was assessed with six items, combining with the 
PISA 2012 Student Questionnaire and Patrick et al. (2007). The sample 
item is "My mathematics teacher gives extra academic guidance when I need 
it.” Cognitive activation was examined with five items based on the PISA 
2012 Student Questionnaire. The sample item is “The teacher presents 
problems that require students to apply what they have learned in new con­
texts.” Classroom management was assessed with five items selected 
from the Disorderly Behaviour scale in the TIMSS 2019 Student Ques­
tionnaire, using reverse coding. The sample item is “Students do not listen 
to what the teacher says.” The Cronbach alpha (α) is .921, .931 and .862 
for teacher support, cognitive activation and classroom management, 
respectively.
Content-focused teaching rated by students was assessed with 11 
items modified from the MQI scale (Learning Mathematics for Teaching 
Project, 2011). Richness, working with students and errors were 
measured with 4, 4 and 3 items, respectively. The sample item of the 
three teaching dimension is “Developing generalizations based on multiple 
examples (richness),” “My mathematics teacher builds instruction on student 
ideas or methods (working with students and mathematics)” and “Lack of 
clarity in teachers’ launching of tasks or presentation of the content (errors 
and imprecision).” The Cronbach alpha (α) of the three teaching di­
mensions is .959, .954 and .837, respectively.
2.3. Data analyses
Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to examine the struc­
ture of students’ perceived classroom teaching. Three hypothesized 
measurement models were fitted to empirical data in this study. To be 
more precise, this study investigated three distinct models: the single 
model, the two-dimensional model, and the six-dimensional model. The 
single model entails using all the measurement items to represent a sole 
variable, namely, teaching quality. The two-dimensional model in­
corporates a general teaching dimension and a content-focused teaching 
dimension. On the other hand, the six-dimensional model encompasses 
separate dimensions for teacher support, cognitive activation, classroom 
management, richness of mathematics, working with students and 
mathematics, and errors and imprecision. Besides, a two-level CFA of 
Model 4 was further examined. The well-established indices of TLI and 
CFI (≥.90) and RMSEA and SRMR (≤.08) show a good model fit (Hu & 
Bentler, 1999).
In addition, latent profiles of achievement emotions were examined 
with Mplus 8.3 (Muth´en & Muth´en, 1998–2019). To find out how many 
profiles were reflected in students’ achievement emotions, the current 
study used LPA with one to five profiles. With 200 random starts and 50 
first iterations, all LPA models were freely estimated. Building on pre­
vious LPA research (e.g., Chen & Leung, 2024; Jiang & Zhang, 2023), a 
variety of fitness metrics were utilized. The Akaike Information Crite­
rion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), adjusted BIC for 
sample size (aBIC), entropy measure, Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted like­
lihood ratio test (LMR), and the LMR’s p-value were among these met­
rics. A lower value for the AIC, BIC, and aBIC suggests a better model fit. 
The entropy measure determines the clarity of the divisions between 
categories, with a value approaching 1 signifying more precise catego­
rization. A significant LMR result implies that a model with k classes 
outperforms a model with k-1 classes. Furthermore, a category is 
deemed insignificant if it comprises less than 5% of the total sample. The 
optimal number of profiles was determined by considering statistical 
results, theoretical justification, and the clarity of the profiles.
This study used the R3STEP method to analyze the potential con­
nections between perceived teaching dimensions and emotional pro­
files. By calculating Odds Ratios (ORs), this study will quantify and 
compare the impact of different teaching dimensions on the probability 
of emotional profile classification, thereby assessing the likelihood of 
individuals belonging to specific emotional profiles. The R3STEP anal­
ysis will provide a statistically significant evaluation of the relationship 
between students perceived teaching dimensions and emotional expe­
riences, thereby revealing the key teaching factors that affect the clas­
sification of students’ emotional profiles. Lastly, considering the nested 
data structure, the complex option was used with “Estimator = MLR”.

3.2. Descriptive analyses
Descriptive analyses of the involved variables are displayed in 
Table 2. The correlations among the main variables are statistically 
significant. Perceived teaching were positively connected with positive 
emotions while negatively connected with negative emotions, with a 
range from −.53 to .57. Stronger correlations were observed between 
perceived teaching dimensions, such as teacher support and richness, 
reflecting their shared focus on instructional quality. In contrast, slightly 
weaker correlations with emotions like pride may reflect influences 
beyond classroom teaching, such as individual achievement or peer 
recognition. Similarly, while perceived teacher support and cognitive 
activation were negatively correlated with negative emotions, these 
correlations were moderate, suggesting that perceived teaching quality 
can mitigate, though not fully eliminate, negative emotions. ICC1 values 
were calculated, and multilevel analysis was employed to decrease 
standard errors.
3.3. Profiles of achievement emotions
Table 3 presents the fit indices for the LPA models with one to five 
latent profiles for seven achievement emotions. With each additional 
profile included, AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased, indicating 
improved model fit. Additionally, the entropy value for each model 
exceeded .88. The two-profile solution produced a much better fit than 
the one-profile model, as indicated by the pLMR and the noticeable 
improvement in BIC scores. Although the four-profile solution provided 
slightly better fit indices than the three-profile solution, the pLMR 
indicated no significant difference between them, and the reclassified 
students with medium emotions were minimal. Given the factor mean 
scores of the three profiles and an entropy value of .90, the three-profile 
solution provided a clearer and more interpretable classification. Thus, 
based on statistical fit and interpretability, the three-profile solution was 
used in this study.
Names were given based on the mean value of seven achievement 
emotions in each distinct profile. As shown in Fig. 1, students in the 
positive profile reported a high level of positive emotions accompanied 
by the lowest negative emotions (N = 873, 49.5%). Students in the 
medium profile showed moderately high positive and negative emotions 
(N = 667, 37.9%). Lastly, students in the negative profile reported the 
lowest positive emotions but the highest negative emotions (N = 222, 
12.6%).
3.4. Students’ perceptions of teaching as predictors of profile membership
Table 4 demonstrates the results on the prediction of students’ 
perceived teaching quality on their emotional profile membership. The 
results showed that students who perceived more teacher support, 
activating teaching, well-structured classroom and mathematical rich­
ness tend to be categorized into the positive profile than in the medium 
profile (OR = 4.52/2.06/2.45/3.75) and negative profile (OR = 6.11/ 
3.49/8.08/4.01). Similarly, students perceived more activating teach­
ing, well-structured classroom and mathematical richness fit better into 
the medium profile than the negative profile (OR = 1.70/3.29). Besides, 
students perceived more interaction with teachers (i.e., working with 
students) are more likely to fit into the positive (OR = 3.60) and medium 
profiles (OR = 1.84) than the negative profile. Lastly, students perceived 
more errors and imprecision of teachers in mathematics class are less 
likely to be included in the positive (OR = .39) and medium profiles (OR 
= .56) compared with the negative profile; meanwhile, those students 
are less likely to be included in the positive compared with the medium 
profile (OR = .70).
Notably, the probability of distribution from the medium emotion 
profile (P2) to the positive emotion profile (P1) and from the negative 
emotion profile (P3) to the medium (P2) profile is characterized by 
distinct changes in students’ perceptions of teaching quality. In detail, 
the effect of teaching on students’ emotional profile from P2 to P1 is 
more obvious than that of students’ emotional profile from P3 to P2, 
except for classroom management. The findings indicate that the 
perceived quality of teaching has a notably greater positive effect on 
enhancing the likelihood of P2 students’ emotions to P1, while the effect 
on enhancing the likelihood of P3 students’ emotions to P2 is limited.

4. Discussion
This study surveyed the relationship between students perceived 
teaching and achievement emotions in mathematics using the latent 
profile analysis. Firstly, the study’s findings build on previous research 
(Blazar et al., 2017; Harme et al., 2013) by demonstrating that a 
comprehensive understanding of classroom teaching, as perceived by 
students, requires considering both general and content-specific per­
spectives. The empirical data from this study further suggest that a 
nuanced understanding of teaching efficacy involves integrating both 
general and domain-specific frameworks. Specifically, our findings 
highlight the importance of supportive teaching practices and structured 
classroom environments, aligning with the concept of general teaching 
quality. While not addressing all aspects of content-specific teaching, the 
results suggest that general teaching practices (e.g., teacher support, 
classroom management) play a significant role in shaping students’ 
emotional responses, which are crucial for effective classroom dy­
namics. Utilizing a person-centered approach, this investigation has 
identified three emotion profiles predicated on a spectrum of seven 
distinct emotions. They are positive, medium and negative emotion 
profiles, which is similar to the previous classification of emotion pro­
files (e.g., Robinson et al., 2017, 2020; Tze et al., 2020). Moreover, 
consistent with earlier research (Sachisthal et al., 2021), a significant 
proportion of students fall into the positive emotion profile, with the 
medium emotion profile coming next; the negative emotion profile has a 
smaller representation.
The significant relationship between students perceived teaching 
and emotion profiles indicated the effectiveness of teaching on 
enhancing students’ positive learning experiences. As this study 
revealed, when students perceived high teaching quality, they showed 
high levels of positive emotions and low levels of negative ones. 
Considering the positive emotion profile is the ideal type, this study will 
focus on the discussion on the student group with medium and negative 
emotion profiles compared with students in the positive emotion profile, 
so as to understand how to promote their positive learning experiences 
from the perspective of teaching.
Concerning the relations between different teaching dimensions and 
emotion profiles, the results showed that when students perceived more 
teacher support, they are more likely to be included in the positive 
emotion profile compared with the medium profile, followed by math­
ematical richness. Teacher support in this study denotes teachers’ care, 
guidance and feedback for individual students, encompassing 
emotional, academic and appraisal support. Teacher support, as teach­
ers’ positive attitude and behaviour, often provides students with 
guidance, encouragement and prompts in learning according to their 
needs. This respectful and supportive approach to learning helps to 
actively inspire and develop students’ interest in learning by listening, 
understanding, and responding positively to their needs and allowing 
them to express their thoughts and opinions. In addition, teachers and 
students are brought closer together by emotions, and their belonging in 
the classroom is nurtured (Hattie, 2008). These actions by the teacher 
impact the student’s experience in a positive way. Furthermore, math­
ematical richness emphasizes the content, including multiple methods, 
generalizations and multiple mathematical explanations. Fan et al. 
(2015) identified teaching with variation and whole-class teaching as 
two primary characteristics of how mathematics is taught by Chinese 
educators within the Chinese context. Compared to using a single 
problem-solving method, a rich teaching situation can stimulate stu­
dents’ learning enthusiasm and satisfy their curiosity and desire for 
exploration during problem-solving contexts. This process can enhance 
students’ learning values and beliefs in their abilities, which in turn 
promotes positive academic emotions in accordance with CVT (Pekrun, 
2006).

are interesting and require more discussion. This study found that stu­
dents were more likely to be included in the medium profile than the 
negative profile and to be included in the positive profile than the me­
dium profile. Moreover, the role of classroom management is more 
pronounced in the former case, which is different from other dimensions 
of teaching. Effective classroom management is essential for reaching 
teaching objectives as it reduces time wastage and strives to provide 
students with more effective learning time (Oliver & Reschly, 2007). 
Students are required to listen carefully to the teacher and obey the class 
rules in the listening-oriented learning style in the Chinese classroom 
context (Ding et al., 2008; Leung, 2001). As such, when students 
perceived a well-structured classroom environment, they tend to show a 
high level of positive learning experiences. Regarding the robust effect 
of classroom management on promoting the emotions of students in the 
negative emotion profile, one potential explanation is that given the 
close relationship between academic emotions and performance 
(Pekrun et al., 2017; Robinson et al., 2020), the negative profile group 
may underperform in mathematics. Therefore, they are more likely to 
become the target of teachers’ attention in class. Teachers’ special 
attention to these students may be seen as a form of support, helping to 
reduce their negative emotions and boost their self-confidence. When 
teachers strive to create a positive classroom atmosphere, students with 
a negative emotional profile may respond more sensitively to that at­
mosphere because they may be more eager for recognition and support. 
This atmosphere can promote their engagement and motivation to learn. 
Conversely, students with more positive emotions may already have 
stronger intrinsic motivation, so they may be less sensitive to the 
teacher’s attention. This suggests that students’ emotional experiences 
may influence how they respond to teacher behavior. This dynamic 
relationship needs to be examined further.
Notably, the relationships observed between cognitive activation, 
working with students and mathematics, and emotion profiles are sta­
tistically significant; however, they are modest in strength. This in­
dicates that while there is a discernible association, it is not as 
pronounced as one might observe in a ’strong relationship’ scenario. In 
detail, when students perceived a high level of activating and interactive 
teaching, they were more likely to be included in the positive and me­
dium profiles than the negative profile. According to Klieme et al. 
(2009), cognitive activation refers to methods used by teachers to 
encourage students’ participation in reflective and constructive 
higher-level thinking. These methods include challenging their beliefs 
by drawing on their prior knowledge, presenting cognitively challenging 
problems, and allowing them to explore and create unique ways of 
processing information. During the process, students’ competence be­
liefs might be enhanced, which would increase their positive learning 
experiences. Notwithstanding, learning tasks in the activating context 
are not easy or even highly challenging and often require students to 
engage in autonomous, active thinking and active construction. As such, 
their contribution to students’ positive learning experiences is limited. 
Besides, the correlation between cognitive activation and emotion pro­
files may be moderated by students’ ability levels. That requires further 
study.
Lastly, errors and imprecision were also significantly linked to 
emotion profiles. More specifically, when students perceive the inac­
curacy of teaching, they are more likely to be assigned into the negative 
emotion profile compared to positive and medium profiles. Errors and 
imprecision denote errors made by teachers in the teaching process, 
inaccuracies in the expression of mathematical language and unclear 
teaching activities. The vagueness of the teacher’s wording often leads 
to difficulties for students to understand and increases their cognitive 
load, which might intensify students’ negative learning experiences.
4.1. Limitation and future directions
Several limitations should be noted when interpreting findings. First, 
we simply focused on student perceptions of teaching quality since the 
validity of student views is widely regarded as adequate, despite some 
findings suggesting that the degree to which teacher and student view­
points agree varies on the specific instructional facet under consider­
ation (Herbert et al., 2022; Wagner et al., 2016). For future research, 
demonstrating actual connections of student emotions with actual 
teacher practice would require a variety of evaluative measures to be 
validated. This could encompass, but is not limited to, direct teacher 
assessments or third-party observations, which may provide a reliability 
and dose analysis to accurately assess teaching quality and its impact on 
student 
emotions. 
For 
example, 
some 
studies 
showed 
that 
teacher-reported classroom management is more closely related to 
affect-related constructs compared with learning support and teacher 
clarity because classroom management is more accessible to monitor for 
teachers (Aldrup et al., 2018; Chen, 2023). According to Worthen et al. 
(1997), classroom observations are more objective than student and 
teacher ratings when evaluating teaching practices since they will be 
less affected by the social desirability effect. In conclusion, such diverse 
perspectives could significantly enhance the robustness and validity of 
the findings, offering a more comprehensive picture of the educational 
landscape. Second, this research only assessed six aspects of teaching 
effectiveness without examining other specific teaching methods. It is 
recommended that future studies include the evaluation of specific 
teaching activities in order to implement effective teaching in­
terventions. Third, it is important to note that this study has a 
cross-sectional design, which means that it cannot establish a 
cause-and-effect relationship between teaching quality and achievement 
emotions. In order to investigate whether high-quality teaching can 
actually enhance students’ positive learning experiences, future studies 
should consider utilizing a longitudinal design. Fourth, the samples in 
this study are not fully representative of Chinese students. These samples 
were solely drawn from one developed eastern province of China (i.e., 
Jiangsu), and it is uncertain whether the findings of this study can be 
generalised to students in some of the less developed areas of the central 
and western regions. Also, this study is exploratory. Future research 
needs to consider the diversity of the sample students to gain a more 
detailed and comprehensive understanding of students’ emotional pro­
files and their relationships with dimensions of teaching characteristics. 
Lastly, past studies indicated that malleability beliefs of emotions may 
shape math-related achievement emotions (e.g., Raccanello et al., 
2022). As such, a critical area for future investigation is to explore the 
potential mediating effects of emotion regulation on the relation be­
tween teaching characteristics and the development of achievement 
emotions in mathematical contexts. This additional layer of analysis 
could offer deeper insights into how educational practices can be 
tailored to support emotional well-being and academic success in 
mathematics.
4.2. Conclusion and implications
Using latent profile analysis, this study explored the profiles of 
achievement emotions and their relationships with perceived teaching 
quality in Chinese mathematics classrooms. The findings clarified the 
structure of quality of teaching, with general and content-focused in­
struction being independent of each other. Besides, three emotion pro­
files were uncovered based on seven distinct emotions. Finally, the 
results confirmed significant relationships between students perceived 
teaching quality and their achievement emotions.
To sum up, knowing how different dimensions of teaching relate to 
students’ emotions can help teachers better comprehend achievement 
emotions, especially which characteristics successfully guide a positive 
trajectory or alter negative trajectories. Inspired by the findings, several 
implications were proposed. First, the results suggest that the simulta­
neous analysis of teaching quality, including general and content- 
focused instruction, is worthwhile, contributing to our understanding 
of the role of different teaching on achievement emotions. Second, when 
students perceived more supportive, well-structured and rich teaching

in class, they tend to be categorized into positive and medium emotion 
profiles than the negative emotion profile. This emphasizes the signifi­
cance of teachers creating a supportive and well-organized classroom 
environment, while also delivering content that enriches students’ 
overall learning experience. Third, the inverse correlation between er­
rors and emotion profiles underscores the necessity to minimize math- 
related misrepresentation within the classroom setting.
In conclusion, while our study has shed light on the intricate inter­
play between teaching dimensions and student emotions, it is important 
to approach these findings with a degree of tentativeness. The 
complexity of the relationship between task difficulty, cognitive 
engagement, and students’ emotional experiences cannot be overstated. 
Our research suggests that cognitively demanding tasks, though essen­
tial for deep learning, may not always robustly elicit positive emotions. 
This nuanced understanding should inform future educational strate­
gies, ensuring they are tailored to foster both effective learning and 
positive emotional experiences in students. Furthermore, we also 
emphasize that our findings are grounded in the current empirical evi­
dence available to us. It is essential to recognize that this field of study is 
dynamic and ever-evolving. As new research is conducted and fresh 
insights are gained, our understanding of these complex educational 
phenomena may be refined or even redefined. Therefore, the conclu­
sions drawn today should be viewed as part of an ongoing dialogue 
within the academic community, one that will undoubtedly continue to 
develop with future scholarly contributions.
