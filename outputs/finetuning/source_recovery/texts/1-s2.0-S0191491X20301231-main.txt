With the main goal of identifying the process factors associated with school eﬀectiveness in secondary education,
this work presents an innovative methodological proposal. Based on secondary data from the Spanish sample of
PISA 2015, high- and low-eﬀectiveness schools were selected by analysing the residuals of the school level (level
2) in multilevel models. Subsequently, decision trees were used to identify the process variables with a greater
predictive power for the identiﬁcation of high- and low-eﬀectiveness schools. While the hierarchical linear
models obtained show inter-school variance scores greater than 10%, decision trees achieve a precision greater
than 90%. We conclude by analysing the suitability of using decision trees in data originating from large-scale
assessments, and examining the obtained factors associated with school eﬀectiveness.

1. Introduction
The pervasiveness of large-scale assessments, together with the
public availability of their complete databases, has been an important
breakthrough for the study of school eﬀectiveness in compulsory education (Verger, Parcerisa, & Fontdevila, 2019). Thanks to this data, it is
possible to implement studies of school factors associated with eﬀectiveness with high levels of complexity and depth in the analysis, which
can lead to improvements in educational processes and policies in the
diﬀerent regions assessed (Chapman, Muijs, Raynolds, Sammons, &
Teddlie, 2016; Murillo, 2007). In addition, thanks to the prevalence of
computer equipment with high processing capabilities, it is now possible to apply statistical techniques with a diﬀerent approach than
classical inferential and multivariate analysis, which optimise the obtaining of valuable information from mass data (Zhou & Chen, 2018). In
this regard, this work’s main goal is the application of educational data
mining techniques to study factors associated with school eﬀectiveness.
Thus, this study has the dual purpose of showing an innovative procedure in an area in which the use of traditional statistics is still
widespread and providing valuable information about factors associated with school eﬀectiveness in secondary education to support the
decisions of national educational institutions.
1.1. School Eﬀectiveness Research
The publication of the Coleman et al. (1966) report started an

⁎

important debate about the impact that educational policies and the
school system itself have on the academic performance of students,
beyond the input eﬀects of their environment and socio-economic level.
Its main conclusion was that the diﬀerences between student scores
could be explained almost entirely through socio-demographic factors
and that school factors such as resources or staﬀ training did not have a
noticeable eﬀect on student performance (Hill, 2017). These ﬁndings
generated a strong response within the educational research community, eﬀectively starting a line of research that delved into student
achievement and its associated factors, focusing both on socioeconomic
factors and on school-level characteristics (Chapman et al., 2016).
Since then, educational research has shown a growing interest in
ﬁnding evidence of the eﬀect of genuine school factors on academic
development (Gamoran & Long, 2007). While the ﬁrst research works
proposed in the ﬁeld were primarily focused on outlier schools and case
studies with non-standardised student performance data (Purkey &
Smith, 1983), the discipline evolved over time through the generation
of new, more robust statistical techniques such as multilevel models
(Goldstein, 1995), the elaboration of integrated models to explain the
relevance of school environments and school-level factors (Kyriakides &
Creemers, 2008; Scheerens, 1991) or the availability of standardised
student performance tests at an international level.
This set of studies eventually became integrated into the so-called
Eﬀective Schools Movement (ESM) (Lezotte, 1989), which mainly focuses on identifying and characterising schools and regions that are
particularly eﬀective or ineﬀective. These studies aim to generate

1.2.1. Multilevel models
As stated in the above section, the use of multilevel models in
educational research in recent years with large-scale assessments is
growing. Although some works analyse the PIRLS and TIMSS tests using
performance as a criterion variable (Costa & Araújo, 2017; Gilleece,
2015; Winnaar, Frempong, & Blignaut, 2015), it is the study of the PISA
tests that has been disseminated most widely (Blanco-Blanco et al.,
2014; Cordero-Ferrera et al., 2015; Dronkers & Robert, 2008; Gamazo
et al., 2018; Gil-Flores & García-Gómez, 2017; Guo, Li, & Zhang, 2018;
Sebastian et al., 2017; Tan & Hew, 2017; Zhang & Liu, 2016). These
works include, depending on their objective, performance in science,
mathematics or reading as criterion variables. There are, however, few
cases in which two or three of these competences are included as criterion variables to carry out a more complete analysis of reality
(Blanco-Blanco et al., 2014; Gamazo et al., 2018; Meroni, VeraToscano, & Costa, 2015; Skryabin, Zhang, Liu, & Zhang, 2015; Zhang &
Liu, 2016).
There are some systematic reviews and meta-analyses that reveal
common patterns in the results of these studies with secondary data,
like the relevance of socioeconomic and contextual factors (Banerjee,
2016; Hopfenbeck et al., 2018), or the importance of process factors,
both at student level – positive attitudes towards school and learning
(Banerjee, 2016) and at school level – teacher expectations and implication, adaptation of instruction or educational leadership (Drent,
Meelissen, & van del Kleij, 2013). However, both these reviews and the
main national and international studies published in recent years indicate divergent results in some fundamental questions related to
school processes such as degrees of school autonomy (Cheng, Ko, & Lee,
2016; Han, 2018; Hanushek, Link, & Woessmann, 2012), parental
participation and academic support (Gilleece, 2015; Sebastian et al.,
2017), use of textbooks, type of student grouping (Choi & Calero,
2013), methodologies used by the teacher in the classroom (Choi, Choi,
& McAninch, 2012; Gil-Flores & García-Gómez, 2017) and instructional
time (Huebener, Kuger, & Marcus, 2017). More consistent results are
found in other questions such as classroom coexistence and climate
(Choi & Calero, 2013; Guo et al., 2018; Mikk, Krips, Säälik, & Kalk,
2016), use of ICT (Petko, Cantieni, & Prasse, 2017; Skryabin et al.,
2015; Tan & Hew, 2017; Zhang & Liu, 2016), educational leadeship
(Gil-Flores & García-Gómez, 2017; Guo et al., 2018) and staﬀ teaching
and job satisfaction (Marcenaro-Gutierrez, Luque, & Lopez-Agudo,
2016).
The aforementioned studies widely apply multilevel models including both input variables and process variables, obtaining models
that are not parsimonious. Furthermore, it can be observed that decision making on the statistical analyses is based exclusively on signiﬁcance indices, disregarding the analysis of indices of size of the effect. In this regard, it is worth keeping in mind the biases associated
with the non-compliance with previous assumptions and the increase of
statistical signiﬁcance when large samples are available (Kaplan,
Chambers, & Glasgow, 2014). This last issue generally involves obtaining signiﬁcant results when the eﬀect sizes are minimal.

Scheerens, 1991) or the generation of appropriate school environments

Despite the eﬀorts made in the ﬁrst years of the ESM, the approach
experienced considerable criticism from diﬀerent research ﬁelds. On
the one hand, the movement engendered some backlash from the ﬁeld
of sociology on account of the lack of a theoretical perspective and the
disconnection between schools and their social and political contexts
(Angus, 1993). On the other hand, some educational researchers expressed concerns about the movement being essentially focused on
school input and output factors (input-output paradigm) without taking
into account intermediate processes. Thus, in the 1980s and 90 s,
concern over educational context and processes increased, with the
context-input-process-output (CIPO) based approach becoming widespread (Scheerens, 1991). Under the CIPO approach, educational research mainly focused on the micro level of schools, with particular
concern for both increasing the number of variables used and for applying more complex and holistic methodologies to identify eﬀective
schools. Linear hierarchical models (or multilevel models) became a
benchmark statistical technique to respond to this issue (Goldstein,
1995). While this work is based on the CIPO model, in order to simplify
the explanation of the variables involved in the models, Context and
Input variables will be heretofore referred to as Input, Process variables
will remain the same, and Output variables will be referred to as Performance.
During the early years of the 21st century, the ESM gained new
momentum thanks to the emergence of large-scale assessments both
nationally and internationally, popularising in educational research the
use of linear hierarchical models to analyse school eﬀectiveness interand intra-regionally (Chapman et al., 2016). These models are developed with two fundamental objectives: ﬁrst, to calculate the schools’
own residual in models with input predictor variables in order to
identify schools of high and low eﬀectiveness (Gamazo, Martínez-Abad,
Olmos-Migueláñez, & Rodríguez-Conde, 2018); and second, to identify
factors associated with school eﬀectiveness through the aggregation of
process variables with previous models (Blanco-Blanco, López-Martín,
& de Miguel, 2014; Cordero-Ferrera, Pedraja-Chaparro, & SimancasRodríguez, 2015; Costa & Araújo, 2017; Sebastian, Moon, &
Cunningham, 2017; Tan & Hew, 2017).
At this point, it is important to note that international large-scale
assessments, like PISA, do not provide longitudinal data of the student’s
achievement. This is a critical issue in the school eﬀectiveness ﬁeld,
because longitudinal data allow us to obtain value-added models,
which are more suitable to study this topic (Chapman et al., 2016).
However, the application of multilevel models with this kind of data
has a broad interest in scientiﬁc literature (Burušić, Babarović, & Velić,
2016).
From this historical perspective, we can deﬁne school eﬀectiveness
in a simpliﬁed way as ‘the relationship between observed outcomes and
expected outcomes given the socio-economic context of education
systems’ (Lenkeit & Caro, 2014, p. 147).

1.2.2. Educational Data Mining
Large-scale assessments such as the Programme for International
Student Assessment (PISA) contain huge amounts of data: cognitive and
non-cognitive student factors; teacher training, engagement, beliefs,
attitudes and job stability; socio-economic and cultural student and
school characteristics, etc. This fact joins the issues of statistics based on
statistical inference and most of the techniques based on variancescovariances matrices: existence of restrictive prior assumptions, problems of ﬁt with a large number of variables, and overrepresentation of
signiﬁcant results with very large samples.
From this perspective, it seems pertinent to consider data mining
techniques as a valuable alternative for the analysis of data from largescale assessments (Martínez-Abad & Chaparro-Caso-López, 2017). Data

necessary to perform a subsequent model validation. The most widely
used validation method to check the classiﬁcation rules obtained from
the training sample is the k-folds cross-validation. This procedure is
implemented in the following steps (Martínez-Abad, 2019; Witten et al.,
2016):

2. Methodology
1 Division of the initial sample in k approximately equal partitions.
2 From these partitions, it is possible to obtain k groups of independent sub-samples (of size n/k) and their respective k remaining sub-samples of size n-(n/k).
3 In any of those groups, the remaining sub-sample is used as training
set (to compute the model) and the sized n/k sub-sample is used as
test set (to test the accuracy of the computed model).
4 Thereby, the same learning procedure is executed k times on k
diﬀerent training sets, and the k obtained models are tested respectively in the k diﬀerent testing sets.
5 Finally, the k error estimates are averaged to obtain an overall error
estimate and the ﬁt indices.

To address the issues presented, this research follows a two-phase
approach. First, as noted above, we intend to avoid the use of gross
academic performance. Therefore, the ﬁrst phase consists of the application of multilevel models with a twofold objective: to study the

selection of outlier schools according to the diﬀerence between their

approach, it proposes the innovation of using a purely quantitative
perspective in the second phase through the use of decision trees for the
exploration of process factors and their association with school eﬀectiveness.

2.2. Design
This study has a non-experimental research approach based on
transversal data. In this way, secondary panel data from the PISA 2015
sample was analysed (OECD, 2017). Therefore, the variables included
in the study are not manipulated, they are analysed in their natural
context.

The applied models were developed using the gross performance of
students in PISA as a criterion variable. Therefore, the models obtained
are not value-added models.
School selection was based on the following criteria (Gamazo et al.,
2018): a school was considered for the high-eﬀectiveness group when
its residual in the 3 estimated models remained in the ﬁrst quartile, and
low-eﬀectiveness if its residual was in the last quartile in all 3 models.
Although in Spain each autonomous community has broad educational
competences, the selection of high- and low-eﬀectiveness schools could
not be carried out in each region independently since not all regions
had the suﬃcient variance in the school level to allow the application of
the multilevel models. Instead, the region of origin was taken into account after the selection to see whether there were any diﬀerences in
the proportion of high- and low-eﬀectiveness schools among the Autonomous Communities.

2.3. Participants
The target population of this work was Spanish secondary school
students who, at the time of assessment, were 15 years old. The initial
sample obtained by PISA 2015 in Spain was 32,330 students and 976
schools. The sample used, however, was reduced to 896 schools and
31,236 students by eliminating all schools that participated with less
than 20 participants in the assessment. After application of the ﬁrst
phase, in which high- and low-eﬀectiveness schools were selected
through the application of multilevel models, a sub-sample of 8,347
students and 239 schools was obtained. Out of the total number of
students in this sub-sample, 3,801 belonged to high-eﬀectiveness
schools and 4,546 belonged to low-eﬀectiveness schools. Of the total
number of schools included in the sample in this second phase, 109
were high-eﬀectiveness and 130 low-eﬀectiveness.

2.5. Procedure and analysis of the data
Firstly, the multilevel models were applied using the specialist statistical software HLM 7. This software enabled the use of both the 10
plausible values provided for the 3 competences, and the sample
weights of students and schools, which are necessary to ensure a correct
representation of a country’s PISA-eligible population (Kaplan & Kuger,
2016). Although it was not possible to use replicas of the students’
sample weights, robust estimators (Huber-White adjustment) were
used, which seem to compensate for the related bias (Lavy, 2015;
Lopez-Agudo, Jerrim, Marcenaro-Gutierrez, & Shure, 2017). Regarding
missing value treatment in multilevel models, HLM7 allows for them at
student level variables, all of which had relatively low percentages
(between 0% and 7.8%), and the missing values from the school-level
variables were replaced through regression imputation, although none
of the variables included in the ﬁnal models had missing values.

were selected (α = .05). To get more parsimonious models, we applied
models with random eﬀects only on School intercepts. This means that

that is, the slopes are ﬁxed. The level 2 residuals (schools) were calculated through the technique of empirical Bayes estimation

Just like in the ﬁrst phase of the study, according to the recommendations of the OECD (2017), the data was weighted based on
the sample weights available for the schools. Since the school was used
as the unit of analysis in this phase, the student-level variables were
aggregated with the average values for each school.
Algorithm J48 (the extension available in Weka 3 to implement
C4.5) enables the treatment of missing data using an innovative
weighting approach. When a predictor variable with missing data is
selected to generate a rule, the instance with missing data “is assigned
to each of the branches with a weight proportional to the number of
training instances going down that branch, normalized by the total
number of training instances” (Witten et al., 2016, p. 230). The same
procedure will be performed until reaching the terminal nodes if there
are in the tree other rules with missing values. Finaly, “pieces of the
instance contribute to decisions at lower nodes in the usual way
through the information gain or gain ratio calculation, except that they
are weighted accordingly” (Witten et al., 2016, p. 213).
After obtaining a model with acceptable ﬁt levels from the training
set, with the intention of ruling out problems of overﬁtting, a stratiﬁed
cross-validation was applied from 10 sub-samples. To facilitate this
comparison and to enable the analysis of the sampling ﬁt of the ﬁnal
decision tree shown in the diagram within the results section, we include the ﬁt indices obtained both in the training set and with the 10folds cross-validation procedure. The ﬁt indices analysed for both the
overall model and each of the 2 subgroups (high- and low-eﬀectiveness
schools) were the true positive (TP) rate, the model’s precision and the
area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve.
Similarly, as indicators of overall ﬁt, the kappa index and the root relative squared error (RRSE) were analysed. In the event of obtaining
acceptable and similar ﬁt indices in the training set and validation, the
model is considered appropriate.

In the second phase of the study, the free software Weka 3.8.1 was
used for the application of classiﬁcation algorithms, speciﬁcally decision trees (Witten et al., 2016). Based on previous studies, which show
that C4.5 builds trees with good predictive power in the analysis of
these kinds of data (Martínez-Abad, 2019), this algorithm was used for
the construction of the models. C4.5 is an extension of the ID3 algorithm (Quinlan, 1986), whose use is extremely widespread in EDM
studies (Martínez-Abad & Chaparro-Caso-López, 2017; Rodrigues et al.,
2018). This algorithm allows the use of predictor variables of a qualitative, quantitative and ordinal nature using the information gain ratio
in the construction of the trees, which generates simple and more easily
interpretable models (Quinlan, 1986). A decision tree consists of a
group or nested rules, which generate an inverted tree. A usual single
tree has an initial node (ﬁrst rule), intermediate nodes, and terminal
nodes (leaves), all of them joined by branches. Each node is associated
with a predictor variable (attribute) and shows a rule. The attribute
associated with one node is selected using the information gain (index
of the importance of the attributes, or predictor variables, in the subset
included in one node) of this variable in the node. A rule divides the
range of scores or categories of the attribute into several sub-ranges.
Each of these sub-ranges is represented in the tree with a single branch,
which leads to a lower intermediate node or to a terminal node. Thus,
each instance (unity of analysis) travels through the tree from the initial
node to a terminal node only through one path, so that in each intermediate and terminal node only the subset of instances that comply
with the previous rules are included. Therefore, while all instances of
the sample are included in the initial node, only the subset of instances
that ﬁt the previous rules are included in each intermediate and

3.2. Decision tree
Based on the deﬁnition provided by Turney (1995), a model can de
considered as stable when the applied algorithm reaches constant results in diﬀerent samples of the same population. To address this issue,
we tested the model in 10 diﬀerent subsamples of the training sample
(obtaining subsamples from the k-folds cross-validation procedure
available in Weka), analysing the statistical dispersion of the accuracy.
In this sense, we obtained the standard deviation and the coeﬃcient of
variation (CV) of the accuracy levels reached on 10 sub-samples
(Martínez-Abad, 2019).

A tree with a total of 25 branches and 13 terminal nodes (leaves)
was obtained. The model generated includes a total of 11 variables, of
which only the READ_MINS variable is included more than 1 time (2
times). According to previous studies (Martínez-Abad, 2019), in which
CV scores reached levels close to 30%, stability score in this model were
acceptable in sub-samples: average accuracy = .862; Sx = .184; CV =
21.136%.
As can be observed in Table 4, the ﬁt indices are highly satisfactory,
showing percentages of correctly classiﬁed schools close to 90% with
both the training set and cross-validation procedure. The results obtained from the training set show true positives and precision rates
higher than 90% for the classiﬁcation of both high- and low-eﬀectiveness schools. While the area under the ROC curve is almost 95%, the
kappa index is close to 85. Lastly, the RRSE is less than 52%, which
means that this model practically doubles the percentage of well-classiﬁed schools compared to the simpler constructible model (a model in
which only one predictor variable is included). In these kinds of models,
to decide the inclusion of the higher predictive power variable in any
iteration, a statistic called information gain is estimated. Regarding the
cross-validation results, it can be observed that the ﬁt in both the
prediction of high- and low-eﬀectiveness schools and the overall prediction is still satisfactory. In terms of the percentage of correct predictions (87.5%), the area under the ROC curve (91.6%) and the kappa
index are maintained at high levels. Regarding the RRSE, it rises to
64.7%, which is not considered to be a problematic value. The model
obtained with the training set is therefore conﬁrmed, and a bias within
it due to overﬁtting in the predictions is ruled out.
The confusion matrices, which summarise the performance of the
predictive models obtained in both the training set and validation (including also percentages of True and False Positives, Precision and
Errors), show high ﬁt ratios with low rates of error and erroneous
prediction (Table 5).

is usually used. Thus, when this method is applied, there is a loss of
variability of the individual scores in these variables, and the prediction
can ﬁt better.

Despite the strengths mentioned above, some notable limitations
can be identiﬁed in this study. On the one hand, it should not be forgotten that the work is based on cross-sectional and sample data,
meaning that school eﬀectiveness is considered from a static temporal
perspective. In the same way, this question leads to the impossibility of
establishing causal relationships based on the data collected
(Fernandez-Cano, 2016). Also worth noting, in relation to the replicability of this study, is the lack of systematicity in the perspective of the
PISA assessments, which has led to certain indicators and scales being
changed, added or removed over the course of its editions (GonzálezSuch, Sancho-Álvarez, & Sánchez-Delgado, 2016). We should also bear
in mind that this study has been carried out with the sample obtained in
Spain, which in its turn entails a limitation in the number of observations, particularly from a data mining approach. In spite of the already
justiﬁed optimum characteristics of the Spanish sample for the application of the analyses, cultural factors that can bias the generalisation
of the results to other environments should be taken into account
(Fernandez-Cano, 2016). Finally, although the selection of outliers is
common practice in school eﬀectiveness literature (Chapman et al.,
2016), the decision to select only schools with very high or low levels of
eﬀectiveness might be controversial, since it leaves out approximately
70% of the schools from the initial Spanish PISA sample. To this eﬀect,
a question arises regarding the use of aggregated student-level variables
in the decision trees applied, in which the unit of analysis was the
school.
Therefore, for future studies, the need arises to delve deeper into the
above questions, establishing similar studies in other regions and works
that address the biases associated with using aggregated data from
statistical data mining techniques. Similarly, in the interest of conﬁrming the evidence mentioned here with greater degrees of experimentation, longitudinal studies that provide greater control of the
methodological process and the variables that may be causing spurious
eﬀects between the target and predictors should be carried out. Lastly,
it would also be of interest to explore the implementation of these
analyses with other data mining techniques, such as random forest.

This study allows for the obtaining of a model with highly satisfactory ﬁt indices, higher than those achieved in other studies that
integrate EDM into the analysis of factors associated with performance
in large-scale assessments (Kılıç et al., 2017; Liu & Ruiz, 2008; Liu &
Whitford, 2011). In the cross-validations of their models, these works
obtain overall levels of precision of around 70%, not exceeding in any
case an overall precision of 82%, and achieving kappa indices close to
or below .5. In addition, unlike other recent studies (Kılıç et al., 2017;
Şara et al., 2015), the reduced size of the decision tree obtained has
facilitated the analysis and interpretation of the eﬀects of the variables
included in the model on school eﬀectiveness.
With regard to the factors identiﬁed in the model, the main variables found are in line with those of previous studies, with the main
advantage being that the classiﬁcation algorithms used enable simpler
and more detailed analysis of existing relationships between these
factors. First, we observe the enormous weight of a high average attendance at academic extracurricular classes by the students of a
school. This evidence is found in both works carried out under a solely
multivariate perspective (Teodorović, 2012), and in works that develop
EDM (Liu & Whitford, 2011). Secondly, the evidence related to the
availability and frequency of use of ICT, especially in the academic
ﬁeld, stands out. This evidence is also usually found in eminently
multivariate works (Alderete et al., 2017; Rozgonjuk & Täht, 2017;
Skryabin et al., 2015). It is conﬁrmed that academic environments with
excessive technology not only are not associated with high eﬀectiveness, but seem to be clearly associated with low eﬀectiveness. However,
school environments in which students perceive themselves as competent and autonomous in the use of ICT are associated with high eﬀectiveness (Gamazo et al., 2018; Zhang & Liu, 2016). In addition, the
amount of time the students actively participate in the class is also a
factor associated with high eﬀectiveness commonly found under a
classical data analysis perspective, based on inferential and multivariate analysis (Drent et al., 2013; Huebener et al., 2017). However,
the models applied in this study have made some progress in this respect, establishing the maximum and minimum recommended times of
actual student work within each reading class, whose recommended
time is between 40 and 50 minutes (note that in compulsory secondary
education in Spain, 4 weekly lessons in the core subject associated with
reading in the reference language are taught). Both the existence of
uninterrupted lessons with excessive durations that exceed an hour, and
little active work on the part of students in each reading lesson are
factors clearly associated with low eﬀectiveness in schools.
The school autonomy factor is more contentious. While some previous studies show a direct association between autonomy and performance (Cordero-Ferrera et al., 2015; Hanushek et al., 2012), other
works ﬁnd an inverse relationship (Han, 2018; Meroni et al., 2015). The
evidence of this work supports the latter, showing that excessive autonomy on the part of the school head in the labour management of the
teaching staﬀ of the school can be associated with low eﬀectiveness.
In addition to the variables commonly found in previous studies,
which provide evidence on the reliability and robustness of the models
applied, other less frequently encountered factors are found signiﬁcant
in this study. In this group, evidence is found regarding student perceptions of cooperation, and the lack of critical thinking with respect to
the environment.
Therefore, the fundamental contribution of this research lies in
proposing a work methodology that, under a purely quantitative perspective, combines two groups of data analysis techniques widely used
and recognised in the study of factors associated with performance. This
methodology may represent progress in the study and proposal of
