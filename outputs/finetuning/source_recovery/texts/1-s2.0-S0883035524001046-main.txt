This study explored the relationship between tablet/computer use for at-home schoolwork and
reading achievement using 2016 U.S. data from the Progress in International Reading Literacy
Study. Multilevel analysis revealed that weekly and daily users had lower reading achievement
than non-users, with gender moderating these associations. Subgroup analyses demonstrated that
monthly users in the advanced-performing group had higher reading achievement than non-users,
while daily users in the high-performing group had lower reading achievement. We suggest
personalized support for primary school students, tailored to their reading proficiency, to improve
their device use for at-home schoolwork and enhance their reading abilities. This research advances our understanding of the intricate relationship between digital device use and reading
achievement, providing insights for educational stakeholders.

1. Introduction
An increasing number of young readers, aged 9 to 11, in the United States (U.S.) utilize digital devices such as tablets, laptops, and
desktops as everyday tools for schoolwork at home. This trend is facilitated by the widespread availability of mobile technology and
high-speed Internet connections (Edwards et al., 2017; Pew Research Center, 2020; Zhang et al., 2015).
Proficiency in reading is a critical factor for academic achievement and social engagement (Alivernini, 2013; McElvany & Schwabe,
2019). Young readers’ learning experiences and opportunities within their home environment are significantly associated with their
reading achievement (Mullis & Martin, 2019). With the increasing prevalence of digital reading in households and the growing
importance of reading comprehension in academic success, young readers are incorporating tablet/computer reading into their daily
learning routines (Delgado et al., 2018; McElvany & Schwabe, 2019).
Despite the growing use of digital devices for home-based learning, research has primarily focused on the relationship between
tablet/computer use in schools and reading performance, yielding mixed results. Some studies report a positive association (OECD,
2015; Ponzo, 2011), while others find the opposite trend (Barshay, 2019; Hu et al., 2018; OECD, 2015; Ponzo, 2011), and some show
no significant differences (Fuchs & Woessmann, 2004).
Furthermore, non-linear findings can emerge depending on the frequency of device use within a single study using the same
dataset. For instance, the OECD (2015) examined the relationships between ICT use at school and digital reading performance using
data from PISA 2012 results. The findings revealed that in 16 countries/economies, students who used ICT for Internet browsing once

3.2.3. Student-level covariates
The student questionnaire collected data on students’ age, gender, and household tablet/computer ownership. As student-level
covariates for statistical control, we included age, gender, language spoken at home, and home tablet/computer possession. To
enhance the clarity of our models, we centered the age variable before introducing interactions with tablet/computer use for
schoolwork at home. Regarding gender, we created a dummy variable to represent female students. Furthermore, we generated a
dummy variable to represent students’ use of the test language at home, with those not using the test language as the reference group.
Similarly, we constructed a dummy variable to represent home tablet/computer possession, with those not having these devices at
home as the reference group.
3.2.4. School-level covariates
In the principal questionnaire, school principals provided information on school-level variables, including socioeconomic status
(SES), location, the percentage of students entering with literacy skills, and shortages of technology-related school resources. These
variables were used as school-level covariates in the study.
We generated two dummy variables to distinguish schools with mid- and high-level school SES from those with low-level SES as the
reference group. We created two dummy variables for school location to represent schools in urban areas and mid-to-small-sized
towns, with suburban schools as the reference group. To assess school-level literacy skills, we used a continuous variable derived
from PIRLS data, where higher values corresponded to schools with students demonstrating higher literacy proficiency.
Additionally, we assessed the insufficiency of technology-related school resources using two items reported by principals or school
administrators: (1) the shortage of audio-visual resources and (2) the shortage of computer technology at the school level. Response
options for these items included the following categories: 1 (Not at all), 2 (A little), 3 (Some), and 4 (A lot). The Cronbach’s alpha for
these two items was 0.78, indicating good reliability. From there, we combined these two items to generate a composite variable,
where a higher value signifies a more significant shortage in technology-related school resources.

4. Results
We presented descriptive statistics for student and school characteristics in Table 1. Of the participants, 50.1 % were females. The
average age of the participants was 10.17 years (SD = 0.44), and their average reading score was 549.34 (SD = 78.81). Almost all the
participants (94.1 %) reported having tablets/computers at home. Regarding the frequency of tablet/computer use for schoolwork at
home, most participants reported using them at least weekly, with 47.8 % using them every day or almost every day and 21.0 % using
them once or twice a week. In terms of school SES, 63.1 % of the students (N = 2629) belonged to low SES schools (95 schools), while
19.5 % (N = 804) were enrolled in mid-SES schools (29 schools), and 17.4 % (N = 701) were in high SES schools (21 schools). As for
school location, 78 schools were situated in mid-to-small-sized towns and rural areas, 34 in urban areas, and 37 in suburban areas.
No multicollinearity issues were identified in the correlations and variance inflation factors among the independent focal variables
and covariates (details available upon request). We conducted a null model to assess design effects (Kish, 1965), which involved
intra-class correlations (ICCs) and the number of students per cluster. The design effect (DEFF) was calculated using the formula:
DEFF = 1 + (Nc − 1)* ρ,
where ρ represents the ICC, and Nc is the average cluster size. The U.S. demonstrated a DEFF of 7.5, calculated as DEFF = 1 + (28–1)*
0.239. According to established criteria (Maas & Hox, 2005; Peugh, 2010), when DEFF exceeds 2, school effects should be taken into
account. Therefore, conducting multilevel analyses of the PIRLS 2016 U.S. data was justified, as the U.S. DEFF exceeded the threshold
of 2.

4.1. Findings from multilevel models
We used multilevel models to explore the relationships between tablet/computer use frequency for schoolwork at home and
reading achievement (Table 2).
In examining the associations between tablet/computer use for schoolwork at home and reading achievement (RQ1), the results, as
displayed in Model 2 of Table 2, revealed significant findings. Using a tablet/computer for schoolwork at home once or twice a week
and every day or almost every day, compared to not using it at all, demonstrated an inverse relationship with reading achievement (B =
− 8.62, SE = 3.19, p < .01 for once or twice a week; B = − 24.74, SE = 2.77, p < .001 for every day or almost every day). In particular,
when holding student- and school-level characteristics constant, tablet/computer use was associated with a decrease of 8.62 and 24.74
points in reading achievement for weekly and daily users, respectively, compared to non-users.
We further examined interactions to investigate whether the associations between tablet/computer use frequency for schoolwork at
home and reading achievement differed between male and female students (RQ2). The coefficients for the weekly and daily uses of a
tablet/computer for schoolwork at home still had inverse relationships with reading achievement in the model with gender interactions (Model 3 of Table 2), albeit with slight changes in magnitude (B = − 16.74, SE = 4.52, p < .001 for once or twice a week; B =
− 33.12, SE = 3.82, p < .001 for every day or almost every day). As for the interactions, the results revealed significant interactions. The
results indicated significant interactions with weekly (B = 17.10, SE = 6.39, p < .01) and daily users (B = 17.68, SE = 5.76, p < .01).
Specifically, it was observed that the inverse relationships between tablet/computer use frequency for schoolwork at home and reading
achievement discernibly differed between male and female students. Importantly, these negative relationships were less pronounced
for female students than male peers, meaning that such negative relationships were more of an issue for male students than females
regarding weekly and daily tablet use. However, the significant gender interactions with the weekly or daily tablet/computer use for
schoolwork at home varied across schools, meaning there were significant gender interactions in some schools but not in others.
We also examined random effects to allow the associations between tablet/computer use frequency and reading achievement to
vary randomly across schools. However, the results indicated that these random effects did not significantly contribute to the models.
The parameter estimates were close to zero; subsequently, their standard errors were not computed.

For the intermediate-performing group (achieving at or above 475 but below 550, represented by Group 3), no significant associations were observed between tablet/computer use for schoolwork at home and reading achievement. Within the advanced-performing group (achieving at or above 625, Group 5), significant associations were observed between
tablet/computer use for schoolwork at home once or twice a month (compared to not using it at all) and reading achievement (B =
5.46, SE = 2.69, p < .05). This indicated that a one-unit increase in tablet/computer use for schoolwork at home once or twice a month
was associated with a 5.46 point increase in reading achievement, after controlling for all student- and school-level factors in the
model. Notably, no significant gender interactions were observed in any of the reading performance groups.
Subsequently, we conducted a further analysis of tablet/computer use for schoolwork at home, focusing on its frequency and
percentage within distinct reading performance groups categorized by benchmark scores (Table 4).
In terms of students using tablets/computers for schoolwork at home every day or almost every day, the low-performing group had
the highest proportion (59.7 %), followed by the intermediate-performing group (51.5 %), the high-performing group (45.0 %), and
the advanced-performing group (32.6 %). On the other hand, students who never or almost never used tablets/computers for
schoolwork at home were most prevalent in the advanced-performing group (23.3 %), followed by the high-performing group (20.5
%), the low-performing group (18.6 %), and the intermediate-performing group (18.4 %). Daily tablet/computer users were more
concentrated in the low-performing group, while non-users were more prevalent in the advanced-performing group.
In the subsequent section, we discuss the primary findings of our study and their significant implications for educators, researchers,
and policymakers dedicated to enhancing reading achievement through digital devices for educational outcomes at home.
5. Discussion
This study investigated the relationships between tablet/computer use for schoolwork at home and reading achievement (RQ1).
Our exploration extended to whether these relationships differed between male and female students (RQ2) and across four distinct
reading performance groups (RQ3). The multilevel analyses of PIRLS 2016 U.S. data revealed that students who used tablets/computers for schoolwork at home weekly or daily tended to have lower reading achievement than non-users. These relationships were
more pronounced among male students than their female counterparts.
The story becomes more nuanced when examining different reading performance groups. Within the advanced-performing group,
monthly tablet/computer users for schoolwork at home displayed higher reading achievement than non-users. In contrast, daily
tablet/computer users in the high-performing group exhibited lower reading achievement than non-users. These findings suggest that
the relationship between at-home digital device usage and reading achievement is not uniform but varies depending on the student’s
reading performance level.
5.1. Frequent tablet use for schoolwork at home and decreased reading achievement
Contrary to our initial hypothesis, U.S. fourth-grade students who used tablets/computers for schoolwork at home, whether once or
twice a week or every day or almost every day, exhibited lower reading achievement compared to their peers who never or almost
never used such devices. These findings corroborate previous research consistently illustrating a negative relationship between
frequent tablet/computer use at home and reading performance (Hadlington et al., 2019; OECD, 2015; Rosén & Gustafsson, 2016;
Vigdor et al., 2014).

The results underscore that frequent tablet/computer use for schoolwork at home is associated with decreased reading achievement. Despite the ostensibly productive nature of activities like homework, learning, or studying facilitated by these devices, improved
academic outcomes may not be consistently realized. One possible explanation for these findings is that students employing tablets/
computers for schoolwork may perceive their activities as productive, yet their level of focus or engagement might be suboptimal. This
lack of engagement becomes particularly noticeable when these devices are used for schoolwork at home more frequently than once or
twice a week, posing a challenge for students to sustain their attention on academic tasks.
The findings further suggest that young readers who use tablets/computers may be more susceptible to unintentionally engaging in
multitasking, a behavior associated with their ability to sustain focus and stay engaged in their academic tasks (Lee et al., 2012; May &
Elder, 2018). This inclination toward multitasking is particularly evident when students use these devices informally and without
regulation in a home setting, as indicated in prior research (Edwards et al., 2017; Stephen et al., 2013; Vigdor et al., 2014). In essence,
the prevalence of technology-mediated multitasking may persist as a disruptive factor, undermining young readers’ sustained
attention to their schoolwork and overall engagement in the learning process (Pempek et al., 2010; Richards, 2010).
This heightened susceptibility to distractions, stemming from frequent technology-mediated multitasking, can lead to compromised sustained attention and engagement, ultimately resulting in an inefficient learning experience that negatively shapes reading
achievement. Thus, the connection between the observed decrease in reading achievement and the potential influence of multitasking
behaviors on sustained attention and engagement becomes a significant aspect to consider in understanding the broader implications
of technology use in a home learning environment.
Providing essential guidance and support to primary school students is crucial for fostering responsible use of tablets and computers
at home. This involves imparting an understanding of the importance of sustained attention for productive, self-directed learning and
equipping students with strategies to enhance focus and engagement when utilizing these devices for schoolwork at home. To
effectively deliver this guidance and support, parents, guardians, or caregivers can play an active role in monitoring and assisting their
children to maintain concentration on their schoolwork while using tablets/computers at home. By providing attentive supervision
and mitigating distractions, they can ensure their child stays focused on the task at hand, facilitating the successful completion of
assignments.
5.2. Significant gender interactions in the associations between tablet/computer use for schoolwork at home and reading achievement
Contrary to our initial expectations, our findings substantiate significant gender differences in the associations between tablet/
computer use for schoolwork at home and reading achievement. These distinctions are particularly conspicuous among those who
engage with these devices on a weekly and daily basis. Specifically, frequent tablet/computer use for schoolwork at home is negatively
associated with reading achievement more prominently for male students than their female counterparts.
These findings align with previous research, emphasizing gender interactions that lead to more pronounced negative associations
between tablet/computer use and reading achievement among boys than girls (Rosén & Gustafsson, 2016). Our research suggests that
gender-specific preferences in digital reading may be at play despite girls exhibiting equal frequency and confidence in using home
computers as boys in the digital age (Pruet et al., 2016; Subrahmanyam et al., 2000). Male students may prioritize digital device use
over schoolwork, while female students may emphasize schoolwork over digital device use. This observed gender interaction may be
attributed to variations in how students engage with these devices, utilize them for schoolwork, or sustain their attention to tasks.
These factors can significantly shape how male students approach these devices for school-related work and could subsequently be
related to their reading achievement, particularly when their device usage exceeds weekly thresholds.
Understanding why frequent tablet/computer use is more inversely related to reading achievement among male students than
among female students is crucial for tailoring educational strategies and interventions to address the needs of both genders more
effectively. To deepen our understanding of this gender interaction effect, further research should focus on how male and female
students utilize tablets/computers, their study habits, and their perceptions of how digital technology contributes to their learning and
reading achievement.
In light of these findings, educators, researchers, and policymakers should consider providing gender-specific guidance or education on the strategic use of tablets and computers for educational purposes at home. This guidance should inspire both boys and girls
to use these devices for schoolwork judiciously, ultimately contributing to enhanced reading performance. Practical guidance methods
may involve allocating dedicated time for schoolwork with these devices at home, establishing clear standards or guidelines for usage,
and regular monitoring to ensure practical and effective usage for schoolwork at home.
5.3. Variations in the associations between tablet/computer use for schoolwork at home and reading achievement across reading
performance groups
To contextualize our discussion, we restate our primary findings. Our study has revealed a negative association between both
weekly and daily use of tablets/computers for schoolwork at home and reading achievement. However, subgroup analyses uncovered
discernible variations in these associations within distinct reading performance groups. In the high-performing group, our findings
indicate that U.S. fourth-grade students who used tablets/computers for schoolwork at home every day or almost every day experienced a decrease in their reading achievement compared to those who never or almost never used these devices. In contrast, within the
advanced-performing group, U.S. fourth-grade students who used tablets/computers for schoolwork at home once or twice a month
exhibited increased reading achievement compared to their peers who never or almost never used such devices.
These results challenge our initial evidence, suggesting that the potential benefits of utilizing tablets/computers for school-related

tasks at home are most evident when the frequency aligns with individual reading levels. Such tailored usage may foster homework
completion, enhance learning, and bolster studying efforts, ultimately contributing to improved reading performance. Our findings
imply the existence of a critical frequency threshold: When young readers use tablets/computers at home for schoolwork at or near this
critical frequency, it appears to be associated with increased reading performance. On the other hand, exceeding this critical frequency
may result in a decline in reading achievement.
Our primary evidence shows that U.S. fourth-grade students who used tablets/computers for schoolwork at home once or twice a
week, as well as every day or almost every day, experienced a decline in their reading achievement. Intriguingly, advanced-performing
students displayed increased reading achievement when using these devices for schoolwork at home once or twice a month. Based on
these findings, we posit that the critical frequency for advanced-performing students may be the monthly use of tablets/computers for
schoolwork at home. Beyond this frequency, a decline in reading achievement may ensue, aligning with the primary findings.
This presumption is grounded in the observation that U.S. fourth-grade students who used these devices for schoolwork at home
weekly or daily showed decreased reading achievement. At the same time, advanced achievers benefit from monthly usage, showing
increased reading achievement. To solidify this assumption, further research is needed to determine the critical frequency for different
reading-level groups, ultimately ensuring optimal student performance with tablets/computers at home for schoolwork and narrowing
performance gaps among various reading-level groups.
In contrast to the observed negative associations among high-performing students who used tablets/computers weekly, it is
noteworthy that these associations were not evident among low- and intermediate-preforming students, whether they used these
devices daily, weekly, or monthly. This suggests that the frequency of tablet/computer use is not significantly related to the reading
achievement of lower-achieving students, including those with intermediate performance. Put simply, we cannot establish a clear
connection between the frequency of tablet/computer use for schoolwork at home and the reading performance of these students.
Previous research has consistently shown the benefits of digital device use for lower-achieving students, indicating that these
students tend to derive more significant advantages from using digital devices for educational purposes (Ferrer et al., 2011; Pruet et al.,
2016). Specifically, special needs students have demonstrated improved outcomes using assistive technology devices (Maor et al.,
2011). It is plausible that struggling students are more motivated to complete their schoolwork when utilizing digital devices like
tablets/computers at home. These devices have the potential to enhance motivation and enrich the learning experience, aspects that
might be missing without such devices (Groff, 2013).
An argument also exists that lower-achieving students may have greater access to such devices. Disparities in access are evident
among students with Special Educational Needs (SEN) in Ireland, where specialized education practices lead to more frequent ICT
access (Department of Education & Science, 2008; Gilleece & Eivers, 2018). In the U.S., students with individualized education
programs (IEPs) may benefit similarly from using these devices at home to cater to their unique learning needs and goals.
However, it is essential to note that educational practices related to IEPs vary significantly across different school districts and
states in the U.S., making it challenging to generalize opportunities for all low-achieving students. This variation in at-home device
access in the U.S. cautions against drawing a definitive conclusion regarding whether lower achievers use these devices more
frequently or experience more significant benefits in terms of schoolwork. Consequently, it is not surprising to find no significant
association between tablet/computer use frequency and reading performance among lower-achieving students.
Taken together, existing evidence suggests that digital device use can benefit low-achieving students in terms of reading
achievement. However, our study does not establish a significant relationship between tablet/computer usage frequency and reading
achievement among these students. While it is plausible that lower-achieving students may experience increased motivation and
enriched learning experiences through digital device use, our findings do not indicate a clear connection between the frequency of
device use and changes in reading performance. In simpler terms, our study suggests that, despite having greater motivation and access
to digital devices, lower-achieving students may not necessarily see a direct connection between how often they use these devices and
their reading performance.
In conclusion, our study underscores the importance of considering subgroups with varying reading performance levels in
educational research. These findings illuminate the need to tailor curricula and policies that promote a synergetic partnership between
home and school. Through effective home-school collaboration, young readers can develop responsible use of digital devices for
school-related tasks at home and receive support tailored to their needs based on their reading levels, ultimately leading to improved
reading outcomes.
5.4. Implications and limitations
This study has profound implications for educators, researchers, and policymakers dedicated to addressing reading achievement
gaps through integrating digital devices in at-home education, fostering a robust home-school partnership for digitally mediated
reading education. The comprehensive findings can inspire educators to cultivate collaborative curricula, engaging both schools and
households in guiding primary school students toward self-directed learning through home tablets and computers. The success of
collaborative efforts among educators, parents, guardians, caregivers, and students in promoting at-home digital reading can ensure a
seamless learning experience at home (Looi et al., 2012).
Researchers should discern the critical significance of accounting for nuanced group differences and varying levels of reading
performance when investigating the relationships between digital device use at home and reading achievement. It is imperative for
researchers to adopt a performance level-specific approach in further investigations and educational interventions for primary school
students. Tailoring strategies to effectively address the distinct needs revealed by varying reading performance levels can contribute to
the development of more targeted and impactful digital reading education initiatives for primary students.

We expect this study to encourage policymakers to view digital reading at home as a complementary practice with the potential to
enhance young readers’ overall reading performance. Policymakers might consider developing or adapting policies to systematically
support young readers in effectively leveraging digital devices within their homes. Recognizing the collaborative nature of successful
interventions, policymakers may consider the possibility of fostering joint efforts among teachers, parents, guardians, and caregivers.
Through such collaborative initiatives, a synergistic support system can be established to encourage and facilitate young readers in
their digital reading practices at home.
An alternative approach, such as quantile regression (Koenker, 2005), can be considered for future research. This approach allows
for modeling the entire conditional distribution of the outcome (reading performance in this study). It offers visualization based on the
ranking of student performance concerning the frequencies of tablet/computer use. In our study, tablet/computer possession was
examined as one of the student-level characteristics, with our primary focus directed towards investigating school-level SES rather
than student-level SES. Given that a significant proportion of students reported having a device at home (94.1 %), we acknowledge that
this variable alone does not fully encompass all dimensions of student SES. To address this limitation, we also included school SES as a
separate covariate to capture broader socioeconomic factors at the school level. Nonetheless, we recognize the potential benefits of
incorporating additional indicators of individual student SES, such as the number of books in the home and the availability of home
study supports. Including these variables as controlling variables in future studies would provide a more comprehensive understanding
of the socioeconomic context of students and further enhance the validity of research findings.
It is essential to acknowledge that our research findings, while predictive of associations between variables, should not be interpreted as evidence for a causal relationship. This limitation arises from the constraint of cross-sectional data examining temporal and
causal relationships. Future research should explore the factors that underlie the connection between the frequent use of tablets/
computers for educational purposes at home and reading achievement across various performance levels in reading.
6. Conclusions
Our study aimed to deepen our understanding of the relationships between tablet/computer use for educational purposes at home
and academic achievement in reading education. We advocate for personalized guidance to primary school students, empowering
them to strategically utilize digital devices for their practical academic pursuits within the home environment, thereby enhancing their
reading capabilities. The impact of such support is most prominent when precisely tailored to individual students’ proficiency levels,
aligning with their unique learning needs. Furthermore, we underscore the importance of collaborative efforts between families and
educational institutions, equipping students with a customized reading education that integrates digital devices.
In conclusion, our research highlights the indispensable role of personalized support and collaborative engagement, laying the
groundwork for a more enlightened future in reading education. Within this framework, we endorse the discerning use of technology
as a crucial catalyst, seamlessly unlocking the full potential of every young reader and enriching their academic journey.
