ABSTRACT/RESUMÉ 
Adult skills and productivity: New evidence from PIAAC 2023 
The 2023 Survey of Adult Skills (PIAAC) reveals large cross-country differences in adult skill levels that 
carry important implications for productivity. Cross-country analysis shows a positive relationship between 
labour productivity and the average level of adult skills at the industry level –partly reflecting a positive link 
between adult skills and R&D intensity – which is estimated to account for one-quarter of cross-country 
differences in industry productivity. But the ways in which skilled workers are allocated to different job roles 
and firms also matters. Higher productivity is observed in industries with lower labour market mismatches 
and where high-skilled workers are employed in larger and more dynamic firms. Differences in these 
patterns of allocation can account for at least 12% of the productivity gaps between countries. These 
findings highlight the importance of policies aimed at enhancing adult skills and structural reforms to 
improve labour market adaptability and reallocation.

1. Introduction
Human capital is widely recognised as a key determinant of cross-country differences in living standards 
– primarily through its impact on productivity (Hall and Jones, 1999; Jones, 2016; Hanushek 2017) – and
is thus a structural policy priority for many countries (OECD, 2018). Recent OECD research  found that
nearly one-sixth of the productivity slowdown in advanced OECD countries could be accounted for by the
slowdown in the pace of human capital accumulation (Andrews et al., 2024). It also suggested that OECD
countries differed in their capacity to allocate human capital efficiently, although the aggregate nature of
the exercise prevented deeper analysis of this phenomenon. Thus, the release in December 2024 of the
latest Programme for the International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) is particularly timely,
as it provides a fresh opportunity to examine the channels linking the level and allocation of adult skills to
productivity.
A large body of literature, including OECD research, has investigated the skills-productivity nexus via the 
role of skills shortages and mismatch, managerial quality and organisational structure, and skill 
composition on firm-level productivity performance. For example, Adalet McGowan and Andrews (2017) 
used PIAAC to illustrate the relevance of skill mismatch for labour productivity via the allocative efficiency 
channel but focused less on how the level of skills shaped industry productivity. Other papers find that skill 
shortages and mismatch reduce firm productivity by increasing hiring costs, compelling firms to employ 
less productive workers, or limiting their ability to invest in innovation (Fox and Smeets, 2011; Ilmakunnas 
et al., 2004; Marcolin and Quintini, 2023; OECD, 2024c). Across OECD economies, skill mismatch remains 
a significant issue, particularly in fast-changing high-tech and science-driven industries, where firms 
struggle to align skill supply with market demand (Andretta et al., 2021; Bijnens and Dhyne, 2021; Criscuolo

Against this background, the contribution of this paper is to study how the level and allocation of adult skills 
relate to labour productivity performance, including an assessment of the relative magnitude of these two 
channels. In doing so, this paper leverages a novel cross-country, sector-level dataset derived from the 
newly published PIAAC data and examines how adult skills are intertwined with productivity through two 
key channels: i) the direct level of skills (PIAAC) effect on labour productivity in non-farm business sectors; 
and ii) the allocation of skills to different job roles and types of firms. This latter channel reflects the capacity 
of economies to more efficiently allocate their existing stock of skills at any point in time – by minimising 
labour market mismatches – as well as to redeploy scarce high skilled labour over time to underwrite the 
expansion of dynamic firms. A further layer of analysis explores how skills shape labour productivity 
performance via the R&D channel. Regression analysis combined with closing-the-gap (between country-
level skills and global best practice) simulations are employed to illustrate the economic significance of 
these channels. 
The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. The next section highlights key insights from the 2023 
PIAAC data. Section 3 introduces an analytical framework to examine the transmission channels using 
sector-level datasets. Section 4 then empirically evaluates the significance of these channels using cross-
country sector-level regressions and conducts simulation exercises to demonstrate the economic 
relevance of the results. Section 5 provides a brief policy discussion in light of the empirical findings. 
2. The 2023 Survey of Adult Skills helps shed new light on the human capital –
productivity nexus
The Programme for the International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC), an OECD initiative, 
aims to evaluate and analyse adult skills across various OECD countries. This comprehensive survey 
assesses key information-processing skills such as literacy, numeracy, and problem-solving in technology-
rich environments for adults aged between 16 and 65. These skills are considered essential for individuals 
to effectively participate in society and for fostering economic growth. The first cycle of PIAAC was 
conducted over three rounds between 2011 and 2018 and involved 31 OECD and 6 non-OECD countries. 
The second cycle began in 2022, with participation from 29 OECD and 2 non-OECD countries. The results 
from the first round of this second cycle, published in December 2024, provide updated insights into the 
evolving skills landscape of the adult population (see Box 1 for more details). 
In 2023, PIAAC scores – whether measured by literacy, numeracy or problem-solving – varied significantly 
across countries, reflecting stark disparities in adult skill levels across countries (Figure 1). Based on a 
simple average of the three PIAAC components, the top performers include Finland, Japan and Sweden, 
whereas Chile, Poland and Portugal have the lowest PIAAC scores (Figure 1, Panel A). And the scale of 
these differences is material: average PIAAC scores in the top three performing countries are around 10 
percent higher than the OECD average and 25 percent higher than in the bottom three performing 
countries.

An analysis of literacy, numeracy, and problem-solving rankings across countries reveal a degree of

Box 1. Measuring adult skills in the PIAAC survey 
The scores calculated from the PIAAC survey are based on questionnaires administered to 
households representing the population aged 16 to 65 (and aged 16-75 in the United States) in 29 
OECD and 2 non-OECD countries (Croatia and Singapore). The data were collected in 2023 and 
published in December 2024. 
PIAAC aims to evaluate adult competencies across a spectrum of skills, ranging from fundamental 
reading and arithmetic to advanced problem-solving skills. To accomplish this, PIAAC measures 
proficiency in literacy, numeracy, and problem-solving. The tasks for each domain are designed to be 
realistic, culturally relevant, and based on real-world scenarios that are significant or applicable in 
various contexts. The content and questions within these tasks are crafted to mirror the everyday 
activities of adults across different cultures, as well as the evolving nature of complex, digital 
environments, even if they may not be familiar to all adults in every country. 
PIAAC has been conducted in the language(s) of each country, using tablets exclusively. The user-
friendly nature of the tablets ensured that even those with minimal digital experience could complete 
the assessment. PIAAC is also the first adult skills assessment to be adaptive, presenting participants 
with test questions tailored to their performance level based on their responses to earlier questions. 
PIAAC consists of two main components: the Background Questionnaire (BQ) and Direct Assessment. 
The BQ gathers information on the respondent’s demographics, education, employment, and skills 
used at home and work. The Direct Assessment measures literacy, numeracy, and problem-solving 
skills. The 2023 PIAAC survey introduced a new domain called adaptive problem solving (APS), which 
assessed dynamic and adaptive problem-solving strategies, replacing the digital problem-solving in 
the 2011-17 PIAAC surveys. Therefore, while literacy and numeracy results from the two PIAAC cycles 
are comparable, the digital problem-solving and adaptive problem-solving domains cannot be 
compared.

3. Adult skills and productivity: the analytical framework 
As discussed in this Section, a nuanced understanding of the latest PIAAC results requires an analysis of 
granular – sector-level – data to pin down the key mechanisms linking adult skills and productivity, including 
via the innovation and reallocation channels. 
Skills and productivity: Towards a better understanding of the channels 
A key observation is that within any given sector, a higher average level of adult skills will directly support 
aggregate productivity performance (Figure 2). A greater proportion of highly skilled workers – relative to 
lower-skilled workers – will enable both the broader diffusion of new and existing ideas and the generation 
of new ideas, via investments in R&D.  
But adult skills will also impact productivity via indirect channels through the allocation of skills. Since the 
stock of adult skills is relatively fixed in the short to medium term, the extent to which skilled workers are 
allocated efficiently across firms will also matter for sector-level productivity (Figure 2). Earlier OECD 
research found that higher rates of skill mismatch within industries go hand in hand with lower labour 
productivity due to inefficient resource allocation (Adalet McGowan and Andrews, 2017). From the 
perspective of a single firm, hiring an over-skilled worker may be beneficial for productivity.3 But over-
skilling in any given firm could be harmful for aggregate productivity if there exist more productive firms 
that could better utilise these skills but find it difficult to expand due to a lack of suitable labour.4 Indeed, 
frictions to the matching process are likely to be particularly costly to aggregate growth in R&D intensive 
sectors, to the extent that it impedes innovation and the scope for productivity spillovers to other sectors 
(Acemoglu et al. 2018; Adalet McGowan and Andrews, 2017; Lehr, 2024; Moretti, 2021).5

In an economy where firms are relatively homogenous, the potential gains to aggregate productivity from 
a better allocation of mismatched workers would be relatively small. In practice, however, the degree of 
firm heterogeneity is striking:  
• 
First, highly productive firms co-exist with low productivity firms: even within narrowly defined 
industries in the United States, firms at the 90th percentile of the total factor productivity (TFP) 
distribution are twice as productive as firms at the 10th percentile (Syverson, 2004).  
• 
Second, the same is true with respect to the firm size distribution, with many small firms co-existing 
with a smaller number of very large firms, which are typically more productive (Bartelsman et al., 
2013). 
• 
Finally, firms vary greatly in their growth potential: many firms do not grow at all, a small cadre of 
young firms tend to disproportionately drive net job creation, while small and old firms tend to 
destroy jobs on net (Haltiwanger et al 2013; Criscuolo et al 2014).  
This widespread firm heterogeneity implies that aggregate productivity will also depend upon how skilled 
workers – which are currently in short supply (OECD, 2024a) – are allocated across firms and matched to 
various job roles. At any point in time, aggregate productivity is an increasing function of static allocative 
efficiency, which measures the extent to which scarce resources are allocated to their highest valued use 
in the form of higher quality (i.e. more productive) firms (Haltiwanger, 2011; Andrews and Hansell, 2021). 
Dynamic allocative efficiency captures the extent to which resources are moving towards higher quality 
firms over time. Achieving static allocative efficiency in one period requires sufficient dynamic allocative 
efficiency in preceding periods, and differences in this process is now a leading explanation for why some 
countries are more productive than others (Bartelsman et al., 2013; Hsieh and Klenow, 2009).  
Skills and productivity: the estimation framework 
To analyse and identify the effects of the level and the allocation of skills on productivity, this paper exploits 
a novel cross-country sector-level database created by merging sector-level labour productivity data from 
the OECD National Accounts database with a range of skills indicators drawn from the 2024 PIAAC 
dataset. This dataset encompasses twelve one-digit industries across 25 OECD countries (listed in Table 
1). The analysis focusses exclusively on non-farm business sectors, excluding non-market-based sectors 
and sectors such as agriculture, public administration, arts, health, education and mining, mainly because 
of problems associated with the measurement of labour productivity in these sectors. The dataset is purely 
cross sectional and focuses on 2023 (and nearby years). Appendix A provides data definitions, sources 
and selected descriptive statistics. 
Figure 3 provides a snapshot in the cross-county variation in the level of adult skills across key industries 
in the non-farm business sector. On average, adult skills are higher in intangible-intensive sectors such as 
finance & insurance and professions, scientists & technicians than elsewhere. For example, the average 
level of adult skills in finance & insurance is 15 per cent higher than in administrative and support services. 
Nevertheless, there remains significant cross-country variation in the level of adult skills within any given 
industry, which we exploit for identification purposes in this paper.

Box 2. Measuring labour market mismatch 
Three main measures of labour market mismatch can be calculated using the 2023 PIAAC data. 
• 
Qualification (vertical) mismatch compares workers’ highest qualification (level of education) 
to the qualification required for their job. Qualification levels are based on the International 
Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) 2011 and grouped into 4 categories: lower 
secondary or less, upper secondary, post-secondary non-tertiary and tertiary.  Workers whose 
highest qualification falls below or exceed the required qualification are considered as under- 
or over-qualified, respectively. 
• 
Field of study (horizontal) mismatch compares workers’ field of study (area of education) 
with the area of their current job. This measure is derived from a list of occupations (at the 3-
digit level International Standard Classification of Occupations) that are normatively deemed 
suitable for each field of study. Nine fields of study are mapped into the main occupational 
categories (see Box 4.3.in OECD, 2024b, for further details). Workers are classified as 
mismatched if their field of study does not correspond to the area of their current job. 
• 
Mismatch based on self-assessment (also referred to as skills mismatch) identifies workers 
as over-skilled if they perceive themselves as under-employed in their current job and under-
skilled if they report being challenged by their current work. The definition of skill mismatch has 
changed between the 2011 and 2023 PIAAC datasets, preventing any comparison over time. A 
significant issue with mismatch based on self-assessment is the presence of country-specific 
biases. For instance, in countries like Italy, Spain, and the United States, a disproportionately 
large share of workers describes themselves as over-qualified, while virtually none consider 
themselves under-qualified. In contrast, in Northern European countries such as Estonia and 
Finland, a notable proportion of workers perceive themselves as under-qualified.

Labour market mismatch 
Labour market mismatch arises when workers are employed in jobs that are either too demanding or not 
challenging enough. This can occur either because a worker’s qualification does not match the required 
qualification or because the worker’s field of study is misaligned with the type of job he/she is employed 
in. Qualification and field-of-study mismatches are prevalent in OECD countries. On average, nearly 35 
percent of workers in the OECD are employed in jobs that require a lower or higher qualification than their 
highest level of qualification (Figure 7, Panel A). Similarly, over 35 percent of workers holds jobs that do 
not align with their field of study (Figure 7, Panel B). These mismatches differ across countries. For 
instance, Korea has the highest level of field of study mismatch but performs much better in terms of 
qualification mismatch. Conversely, Switzerland excels in minimising qualification mismatch but faces 
challenges in terms of field of study mismatch. 
Labour market mismatch is potentially most acute when measured by the combination of qualification and 
field of study mismatch – that is, when workers are mismatched both in terms of qualification and field of 
study. According to this metric, more than 10 percent of workers on average across the OECD experience 
mismatch (Figure 7, Panel C). The incidence of mismatch tends to be higher in industries such as transport, 
hospitality and administrative services and lower than average in ICT, finance and professional services 
(Figure 7, Panel D).

skills are higher in larger firms on average across the OECD (Figure 8, Panel A), but the strength of this 
connection varies significantly across countries (Figure 8, Panel B).

Economic magnitudes 
How large are the aggregate labour productivity gains from closing the sector-level labour market mismatch 
gap to the best-performing sectors/countries? This exercise is similar in spirit to the one conducted for the 
gaps in adult skill levels.26 These simulations suggest that closing the mismatch gap would be associated 
with a productivity boost of almost 5 percent on average across OECD countries (Figure 11). This is in the 
same ballpark as estimated by Adalet McGowan and Andrews (2017). 
One interesting exercise is to compare the potential productivity gains from closing adult skill gaps (Figure 
5) and closing mismatch gaps (Figure 11). There is a group of countries, including Finland and Norway, 
that have high adult skills and low mismatch, resulting in low potential productivity gains from both channels 
in the policy experiments. Conversely, Korea lags in terms of adult skills and has significant mismatches, 
indicating large potential productivity gains from closing both the level of skills and allocation of skills gaps. 
These patterns are very much consistent with the negative correlation between the level of adult skills and 
labour market mismatch across industries in OECD countries illustrated in Figure A3 in Appendix A. But 
this relationship is far from perfect. Some of the countries that would gain the most from closing mismatch 
gaps are not necessarily the ones that would benefit the most from closing adult skill gaps and vice-versa. 
For instance, labour market mismatch is low in many sectors in Poland and Portugal. As a result, potential 
productivity gains amount to one percent or lower. By contrast, Japan experiences substantial mismatches 
and could see productivity gains of up to 9 percent, which contrasts with the almost zero potential 
productivity gains from improving adult skills (Figure 5).

What proportion of the cross-country labour productivity gaps can be explained by the allocation of skills? 
The implied productivity gains reported in Figure 11 are compared to the observed overall productivity gap. 
For an average OECD country, differences in labour market mismatch accounts for about 12% of cross-
country labour productivity gaps with best practice (Figure 12). This is a conservative estimate because 
dynamic allocative efficiency effects will eventually feed into static allocative efficiency. In some countries 
(e.g. New Zealand, Italy and Canada), the tendency for skilled workers to be disproportionately allocated 
to growing firms – at the expense of declining firms – is quite weak. But if such countries could redeploy 
skilled workers towards growing firms like what is observed in the average OECD country, then labour 
productivity could be 3% higher. Similarly, the coefficient estimates imply that moving from the bottom 
quartile of the distribution to the OECD average (see Figure 9, Panel B) would be associated with average 
productivity gains of almost 1% percent. While this suggests that the aggregate gains from redeploying 
high skilled workers to dynamic firms are likely to be material, a more systematic accounting exercise – of 
the contribution of dynamic allocative to cross-country productive performance – is left to future research.

5. Policy discussion 
The results, which illustrate the importance of adult skills for productivity performance, raise at least two 
questions for policy, which should be the focus of future research. First, given the strong positive 
relationship between sectoral labour productivity and the average skill level of workers, what policy can do 
to increase the skillset of workers in the economy? Second, how can countries more effectively allocate 
the existing stock of adult skills, considering that sector-level labour productivity tends to be higher when 
labour market mismatch is lower and skilled workers are more likely to be allocated to better performing 
firms? 
Before addressing these two policies questions, Figure 14 summarises how countries perform on these 
two dimensions – adult skills and labour market mismatch – with a view to sharpen the focus on 
productivity-enhancing policies. The top right quadrant includes countries with above-average adult skills 
and below-average labour market mismatch. In this regard, Finland, Norway and the Netherlands serve 
as role models as they effectively leverage a high-skill workforce with an appropriate allocation. Countries 
in the bottom right quadrant – particularly Japan and to a lesser extent the United Kingdom – perform 
relatively well on PIAAC but also experience high mismatch. From a productivity perspective, these 
countries may not be making the most of their human talent, suggesting a need to also focus on policies 
that improve labour market matching. Meanwhile, human-capital and skill augmenting policies are a policy 
priority for those countries in the two left-hand side quadrants, noting that countries in the bottom left 
quadrant – including Korea and Lithuania – face the dual productivity challenges arising from headwinds 
from below-average adult skills and above-average labour market mismatch.

The first policy question is crucial for countries that need to boost their adult skills. Work-related training is 
crucial for improving adult skills as it significantly enhances adaptability and productivity in the workforce. 
Participation in training programmes is strongly correlated with higher PIAAC scores. But in many OECD 
countries, the incidence of training is very low (OECD, 2020; Figure 15, Panel A), especially amongst 
workers with low education who potentially have the most to gain from participating in work-related training 
programmes (Figure 15, Panel B). This underscores the importance of education policies at younger ages 
to build foundational skills. Recent OECD research emphasises that participation in good-quality early-
childhood education, exposure to high-quality teachers, school assistance to homework and a regulated 
use of personal digital devices in school all help improve student test performance (Andrews et al. 2024). 
These fundamental skills will make children and workers more adaptable to changing job requirements 
and technological advancements. 
OECD research also emphases flexibility in adult learning that allows individuals to choose when, where, 
how, and what to learn. This is essential for increasing participation and inclusiveness in adult education. 
Flexibility is also key in addressing labour market disruptions caused by digitalisation and the green 
transition (OECD, 2023). Many OECD countries have policies to support green and AI-related training, but 
the availability of such courses varies widely. Low-skilled workers are less likely to access these training 
opportunities, highlighting again the importance of policies and incentives for broader participation (OECD, 
2024c).

On the second policy question - of utmost importance for countries with large labour market mismatch - 
the scope for policy to leverage this channel may well be higher in the near term, though the economic 
magnitude of the allocation of skills is more modest than that of enhancing of average skills, Preliminary 
econometric evidence (Box 4) suggests that financial frictions and insolvency regimes that inhibit corporate 
restructuring and excessively penalise failed entrepreneurs disproportionately hinder productivity 
performance in more dynamic sectors. But future research could more directly explore the role of policies 
in supporting the efficient allocation of skills.  
On this front, it is significant that the share of workers employed in growing businesses is positively 
associated with the extent to which policy supports reallocation and adaptability in labour markets, as 
proxied by a composite indicator that combines product market regulations, employment protection 
legislation, insolvency regimes and ALMP spending (see Andrews et al. 2024; Figure 16). One could also 
imagine that the spread of occupational licensing, non-compete clauses and mobility-sapping housing 
policies could also be relevant. All of this highlights the continued relevance of structural policies that 
promote more fluid labour markets and firm dynamism to support efficient matching, at a time when the 
headwinds to human capital accumulation have never been stronger (Andrews et al. 2024).

6. Conclusion 
This paper exploited the 2023 Survey of Adult Skills (PIAAC), which reveals large cross-country differences 
in adult skill levels. Average PIAAC scores in the top three performing countries are around 10 percent 
higher than the OECD average and 25 percent higher than in the bottom three performing countries.  
Cross-country sector-level analysis suggests that the latest PIAAC outcomes have important implications 
for aggregate productivity in at least two ways. First, there is a robust positive correlation between the level 
of labour productivity and the average level of adult skills in the non-farm business sectors. Assuming a 
causal relationship, cross-country sector-level analysis implies that i.) closing the sector-level gap in PIAAC 
outcomes of the average OECD country to the top three performing countries could lift the average OECD 
labour productivity level by 17 per cent; ii.) This level of skills effect can potentially account for on average 
one-quarter (and up to one-third) of cross-country sector-level labour productivity gaps; and iii.) There is a 
positive relationship between R&D intensity and adult skills and over one-third of the impact of adult skills 
on labour productivity can be accounted for by the R&D channel (which supplies the generation of new 
ideas). This leaves plenty of scope for adult skills to impact productivity via other channels, such as the 
adoption of existing technologies. 
Second, the mismatch of workers in terms of qualification and field of study and the effective allocation of 
skilled workers to different job roles and firm types varies across countries, with important implications for 
productivity. Labour productivity is higher in sectors where labour market mismatch is lower and where 
high-skilled workers are more likely to be allocated to larger – as opposed to smaller – firms. Productivity 
is also higher when high skilled workers are deployed to growing firms, while it tends to be lower when 
they are trapped in declining firms. Finally, assuming a causal relationship, closing the labour market 
mismatch gap of the average OECD country to the best performing countries can potentially accounting 
for more than one-tenth of the cross-country sector-level productivity gaps to best-performing countries. 
These findings suggest that high priority should be assigned to understanding the scope for policies – 
including adult training programmes – to raise the average level of adult skills. However, significantly 
improving adult skills through raising the foundational skills of younger generations is a process that spans 
across generations. While the aggregate productivity impact of the allocative channel is more modest, it 
may be more leverageable by policy in the near term, highlighting the role of structural reforms to support 
labour market reallocation and adaptability.

Annex C. Adult skills and macroeconomic 
productivity – evidence from QASR 
To illustrate the aggregate productivity implications of the 2023 PIAAC data, we update the OECD 
Quantitative Assessment of Structural Reforms (QASR) model that links human capital (measured as 
‘inferred’ PIAAC scores) to country-level productivity through cross-country time series regression analysis 
for OECD countries. Details of the calculation of ‘inferred’ PIAAC data and the macroeconomic productivity 
regressions are provided in Box B1. The country-level human capital stock measure exhibits a robust 
positive correlation with productivity: the estimated elasticity implies that a one percent increase in human 
capital stock goes in tandem with a nearly 2 percent improvement in long-term productivity.27 
The coefficient estimates from the multi-factor productivity regressions are applied to the percentage 
difference of a country’s average PIAAC score and the average of the top three performing countries to 
calculate the associated productivity gains from full convergence. Figure B1 illustrates these potential 
gains: closing the gap between the OECD average and the top 3 best PIAAC performing countries would 
be associated with an 18 percent increase in average OECD multi-factor productivity For a typical laggard 
country, convergence (in adult skills) with the top-performing OECD countries could go in tandem with 
productivity levels higher by between 20 to 30 percent, while for Chile, the potential productivity gains are 
over 50 percent.  
Figure B1 also highlights the contributions of literacy, numeracy and problem-solving gaps to these 
productivity gains. On average, the individual domains contribute relatively evenly, accounting for 
approximately one-third each. However, some countries demonstrate domain-specific variations. For 
example, in Chile, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and the United States, closing numeracy gaps would 
yield the greatest productivity improvements. Conversely, in the Nordic countries and selected European 
nations, the largest relative benefits would arise from closing literacy gaps. Nevertheless, these literacy-
related gains are modest in absolute terms, as these countries are already near the PIAAC frontier and 
perform exceptionally well in numeracy.

Cohort-level PIAAC regressions 
PIAAC is a good measure of the quality of a country’s human capital stock as it measures the literacy, 
numeracy and problem-solving skills of the entire working age population. Nevertheless, it has two 
shortcomings for empirical analysis. First, for each country, data is available for only two years, with the 
release of the second PIAAC cycle in December 2024. This limitation rules out its use in cross-country 
time-series panel regression analysis, one of the workhorses of the OECD Quantitative Assessment of 
Structural Reforms (QASR) framework. Second, the number of countries (around 40) involved in PIAAC 
is insufficient for a purely cross-sectional regression analysis at the country level. 
To increase country and time coverage, regression analysis is used to estimate adult test scores for 
earlier periods and additional countries (see Égert, de La Maisonneuve and Turner, 2024). This is 
achieved by regressing PIAAC scores on student test scores (quality of education) and mean years of 
schooling (quantity of education), using data that pools all cohorts together. In this method, adult test 
scores of any given cohort are paired with mean years of schooling of the specific cohort and PISA 
student test scores that the very same cohort took at the age of 15. Since data on mean years of 
schooling and student test scores are available over time and for a larger set of countries, the regression 
is used to extrapolate adult test scores for countries and years for which PIAAC data are not readily 
available. For every OECD country, the stock of human capital, represented by the country-level PIAAC 
scores, is calculated as the cohort-weighted average of the estimated cohort-level adult test scores. 
This approach is appealing not only because it extends the country and time coverage but also because 
the relative weights of the quantity and quality of education are estimated rather than imposed. 
Existing OECD work (Égert, de La Maisonneuve and Turner (2024) used PIAAC data from 2011 to 
2018 (the first PIAAC cycle). This paper combines the first cycle with data from the 2024 second cycle 
and re-estimates the cohort-level PIAAC regressions on the dataset that pools the first and second 
PIAAC cycles together. The cohort-level regression results on the new pooled PIAAC dataset (Table

Human capital stock in country-level productivity regressions 
The country-level measure human capital stock shows a robust positive correlation with productivity in 
cross-country time-series panel regressions (the QASR productivity regressions), suggesting that 
improvements in human capital are accompanied by productivity gains at the country level (Table B2). 
The estimated elasticity is close to 2, implying that a one percent increase in human capital stock goes 
in tandem with a more than 2 percent improvement in productivity in the long run.  
When compared to earlier estimates reported in Égert, de La Maisonneuve and Turner (2022) and in 
OECD internal updates, the elasticity declines from above 2 to below 2. This change relates to two 
factors: i.) the updating of the human capital measure using more recent PISA (2022) and PIAAC (2024) 
data, and ii.) the substantial update for more recent years (from 2018 to 2022).
