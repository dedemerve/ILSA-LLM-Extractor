With the rapid growth of information and communication technology (ICT), educational resilience in the digital world has gained increasing scholarly attention. Nevertheless, few studies
regarded ICT engagement as an adjustment outcome in the process of resilience. Based on the selfdetermination theory, ICT engagement involves ICT interest, perceived ICT competence,
perceived autonomy related to ICT use, and perceived social interaction in ICT use. Using data
from 53 countries/economies in the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
2018 dataset, our study aims to unfold key factors of resilience in ICT engagement from a global
perspective and compare the classification performance of four machine learning models to
discriminate resilient students from non-resilient students. By implementing four machine
learning techniques [i.e., Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Tree (DT), Random Forests (RF),
Support Vector Machine (SVM)], 15 key factors were selected from 102 contextual features to
discriminate resilient students from non-resilient students in ICT engagement. Surprisingly, the
results indicated that ICT usage for entertainment was the most predominant factor. Rather than
the amount of ICT resources, intrinsic motivation in ICT usage was identified as the fundamental
factor of resilience in ICT engagement. In addition, SVM and RF showed better stability and
classification abilities than LR and DT. Our research findings not only broaden the operational
definition of educational resilience but also develop a methodological paradigm for implementing
machine learning techniques in resilience research. This study provides significant implications
for educators to foster students’ intrinsic motivation with ICT-supported teaching strategies,
which further enhances their resilience in ICT engagement.

1. Introduction
In today’s knowledge explosion era, information and communication technology (ICT) literacy plays a crucial role in people’s
studying and working over the lifespan. ICT literacy involves a comprehensive cluster of skills, which is defined as “using digital
technology, communications tools, and/or networks to access, manage, integrate, evaluate, and create information in order to function
in a knowledge society” [Educational Testing Service (ETS), 2002, p.2]. Transcending beyond the cognitive components, Zylka et al.
(2015) first introduced the metacognitive and motivational elements of ICT literacy, also called ICT engagement. ICT engagement is a

With the popularity of household ICT devices, the “digital divide” has emerged as a form of social inequality (Korupp & Szydlik,
2005). That is, students from low socioeconomic (SES) families might have fewer opportunities to foster ICT skills and less positive ICT

could be independently responsible and highly engaged in ICT use, who are recognized as resilient students (e.g., Zheng et al., 2023). In
the field of education, numerous studies mainly focused on academically resilient students (e.g., Clavel et al., 2022; Özcan & Bulus,
2022) rather than their emotional, motivational, or social aspects. Commonly, educational resilience research tended to implement
conventional regression analysis, which could only focus on limited factors (e.g., Agasisti et al., 2021; Aydın & Erdem, 2022). To

engagement through machine learning techniques. Therefore, the fundamental objective of this study is to unfold key factors of
resilience in ICT engagement from a global perspective, as well as compare the model performance of four machine learning techniques
in the classification of resilient students and non-resilient students. In this sense, the current study provides fresh insights into two main
aspects of educational resilience research. On one side, it broadens the conceptual framework of educational resilience from the
perspective of non-cognitive adaptation in the digital world. On the other side, it develops an innovative methodological paradigm for
employing machine learning techniques in educational resilience research.
2. Literature review
2.1. ICT engagement
The concept of engagement has gained great attention in various disciplines, such as nursing (Cooper et al., 2023), management
(Mone et al., 2018), sociology (Kim et al., 2023), and education (Mao & Lee, 2023). In the field of education, learning engagement is
defined as “students’ psychological state of activity that affords them to feel activated, exert effort, and be absorbed during learning
activities” (Wong & Liem, 2022, p.120). Moreover, engagement is a multi-faceted construct theoretically based on the concept of
intrinsic motivation (Lin et al., 2023). Focusing specifically on e-Learning activities, ICT engagement is regarded as a vital indicator to
foster ICT skills in a self-determined way (Goldhammer et al., 2017).
Initially conceptualized by Zylka et al. (2015), ICT engagement is composed of three aspects based on a questionnaire with 48
items, including ICT interest, self-concept of ICT usage, and social exposure to ICT. To revise the ICT engagement questionnaire,
Goldhammer et al. (2017) further extended ICT engagement by “autonomy related to ICT use” and introduced this four-dimensional
construct to the PISA ICT familiarity questionnaire. As an innovative construct, ICT engagement comprised four significant dimensions
based on SDT (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Kunina-Habenicht & Goldhammer, 2020). To be specific, ICT interest refers to individuals’ persistent
enjoyment of ICT activities, perceived ICT competence represents individuals’ self-concept of ICT skills and knowledge, perceived autonomy related to ICT use refers to individuals’ use of ICT in a self-regulated way, and perceived social interaction in ICT use reflects
individuals adopt ICT to communicate experience with peers (Goldhammer et al., 2017).
2.2. Resilience in ICT engagement
Resilience is defined as “the process of, capacity for, or outcome of successful adaptation despite challenging or threatening circumstances” (Masten et al., 1990, p.426). That is, adversity and positive adaptation are two major components of resilience (Windle,
2011). Compared with advantaged students, disadvantaged students are widely considered to have lower levels of learning engagement than their advantaged counterparts (e.g., Ackert, 2018; Fang et al., 2022; Tomaszewski et al., 2020). However, some
SES-disadvantaged students persistently engage in learning despite difficulties and challenges, which is recognized as a process of
motivational resilience (Skinner et al., 2014). Of note, the concept of engagement is closely intertwined with motivation. On one hand,
engagement is a crucial outcome of motivational resilience (Flanagan & Symonds, 2021). On the other hand, the process of motivation
involves a “meta-construct” called engagement (Skinner & Raine, 2022).
Borrowing from the concept of motivational resilience, our study defines resilience in ICT engagement as individuals persistently
engage with ICT activities in a self-regulated way (e.g., show a keen interest in ICT, have a great sense of ICT self-efficacy, use ICT
independently, and communicate through ICT devices consistently) despite their disadvantaged family background. Following the
guidelines of the OECD report, our study further operationalizes the concept of resilient students as individuals whose economic, social,
and cultural status (ESCS) is located at the bottom quarter within one’s economy, but ICT engagement located at the top quarter across
economies (OECD, 2011, 2019a).
From a social-ecological perspective, it is suggested to examine key factors of resilience in multiple systems (Ungar, 2011; Ungar &
Theron, 2020). To be specific, Garmezy (1985) categorized protective factors of resilience into individual characteristics, relationships,
and external systems. Thus, concerning the protective factors of resilience in engagement, three crucial domains are taken into
consideration in previous studies. Regarding personal traits, intrinsic motivation, such as formulating autonomous mastery goals was
one of the positive predictors for SES-disadvantaged students’ engagement (Benita et al., 2022). With respect to relationships, social
support (i.e., peer, family, and teacher support) could foster students’ self-efficacy, which further improves their e-learning engagement (Wang et al., 2023). Particularly, a positive student-teacher relationship contributed to cognitive and emotional engagement for
low SES students (Cunninghame et al., 2020). Concerning external resources, high-quality instructional practices, such as teacher

2.3. The present study
Following the PISA 2018 assessment framework, the optimal subset of factors was derived from student, home, and school contexts
(OCED, 2019b). By comparing the classification performance of four machine learning approaches, the current research aims to
comprehensively unfold key factors of resilience in ICT engagement. Our study offers innovative contributions to resilience research in
several ways. First, high ICT engagement was considered as the positive adaptation in this study, while the majority of educational
resilience research regarded academic performance as an indicator of adjustment (e.g., Cheung, 2017; Cordero & Mateos-Romero,
2021). It thus highlights the importance of non-cognitive factors as positive adaptation in the resilience framework. Second, a
comparative study on four machine learning methods was conducted to identify a subset of eligible factors, which further niched the
methodological gap in resilience research. Finally, our study is dedicated to drawing a generalized conclusion by utilizing a
cross-cultural sample (i.e., 53 countries/economies) in PISA 2018. To clarify our research aims, two research questions are addressed
as follows:
Research question 1: What are the classification performance of four machine learning approaches [i.e., Logistic Regression (LR),
Decision Tree (DT), Random Forests (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM)] in the classification of resilient and non-resilient students in
ICT engagement?
Research question 2: What are the key common factors of resilience in ICT engagement through machine learning approaches [i.e.,
Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Tree (DT), Random Forests (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM)]?
3. Method
3.1. Data
The PISA 2018 database was utilized in the current study, which can be downloaded from the OECD’s official website (https://
www.oecd.org/pisa/data/2018database/). In PISA 2018, 53 countries/economies were administered an optional ICT familiarity
questionnaire, which aimed to investigate students’ usage of digital devices (OECD, 2019a). Thus, this study involves 53 countries/economies that have participated in PISA 2018.
Following the operational definition of academic resilience in the OECD’s official report, academically resilient students (ARS) are
defined as those who are in the bottom quarter of ESCS in their country/economy but achieved in the top quarter in that country/
economy (OECD, 2019a). To broaden its definition in the digital age, positive adaption alters from academic success to high ICT
engagement, while adversity maintains the same construct of disadvantaged ESCS. Therefore, resilience in ICT engagement is
composed of two dimensions: (1) the bottom quarter of ESCS; and (2) the top quarter of ICT engagement. First, a total of 68,959
students were identified as ESCS-disadvantaged students, which pertained to our research sample. In other words, the major concern of
our study is students whose ESCS are in the bottom quarter within their countries/economies. Second, aiming at this target population,
we further screened students whose ICT engagement is in the top quarter across 53 countries/economies. In a nutshell, 68,959
ESCS-disadvantaged students were classified into 13,418 ICT engagement resilient students (IRS) and 55,541 non-ICT engagement
resilient students (NRS) in our study. The supplementary materials describe the percentage of IRS and NRS at the regional level (see
Appendix A).
3.2. Measures
As the independent variables, 102 contextual factors were derived from student, teacher, and school background questionnaires in
PISA 2018. According to the questionnaire taxonomy, variables are divided into four major categories: (1) student background (e.g.,
ST004D01: Gender); (2) non-cognitive/metacognitive factors (e.g., METASUM: Meta-cognition: summarizing); (3) teaching and
learning (e.g., TCSTRATREAD: Teacher’s initiation of reading strategies); and (4) governance (e.g., PASCHPOL: School policies for
parental involvement). In addition to the PISA 2018 questionnaire taxonomy, the rationale for selecting 102 contextual factors is
rooted in the social-ecological definition of resilience (Ungar & Theron, 2020). It examines key factors of resilience in multiple systems,
including self-system, microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, and macrosystem proposed by Bronfenbrenner’s (1979) ecological systems theory. Scaling by the item response theory, it is verified that the chosen factors are reliable and valid following the PISA technical
report (OECD, 2020). Specific descriptions of each independent variable are represented in the supplementary materials (see
Appendix B).
The dependent variable is a binary indicator (i.e., 1 and 0), which is composed of two dimensions. The first dimension refers to
disadvantaged ESCS (i.e., ESCS in the bottom 25 % within each region), while the second dimension indicates high ICT engagement (i.
e., ICT engagement in the top 25 % across 53 countries/economies). Since disadvantaged ESCS was clearly defined by OECD (2019a),
the second construct, ICT engagement should be further selected and determined in the ICT familiarity questionnaire. It is an

3.3. Analytical strategy
The analytical plan involved four crucial stages, including data preparation, data preprocessing, primary analysis in four machine
learning approaches, and model evaluation. All data analysis procedures were conducted through the Python 3.9.13 program. The
coding documentation libraries are publicly free accessed from imbalanced-learn (https://imbalanced-learn.org/dev/) and scikit-learn
(https://scikit-learn.org/stable/).
3.3.1. Data preparation
At the initial stage, PISA 2018 student, teacher, and school databases were merged as an integrated set. Moreover, a confirmatory
factor analysis (CFA) was utilized to verify the ICT engagement construct. In line with the ICT engagement model (Goldhammer et al.,
2017), four indicators were extracted from the ICT familiarity questionnaire (i.e., INTICT, COMPICT, AUTICT, and SOIAICT). Their
factor loadings were further computed in this step.
3.3.2. Data preprocessing
Beforehand, the raw dataset was split into the training set (80 %) and the test set (20 %). To begin with the data preprocessing, the
first step refers to data cleaning. Second, we adopted the k-nearest neighbor (kNN) imputation to impute the noise. Third, the data was
further normalized (i.e., ranging from 0 to 1). In the final step, we implemented a combined resampling method (i.e., SMOTETomek) to
solve the problems caused by an extremely imbalanced database in the current research (i.e., IRS and NRS classifications).
3.3.3. Primary analysis
Four supervised machine learning approaches were implemented in the primary analysis, including Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Tree (DT), Random Forests (RF), and Support Vector Machine (SVM). Although these algorithms were widely implemented in ILSA
research (e.g., Chen et al., 2021; Hernandez-Ramos & Martinez-Abad, 2023; Hu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022), a comparative study
on these supervised classifiers is needed to unfold the common factors of resilient students in ICT engagement.
Logistic Regression (LR). The goal of LR is to yield the predicted probability of an occurring event. It is common to use maximum
likelihood estimation to calculate the beta coefficient in LR. The optimal coefficient was chosen via multiple iterations, which further
made the optimization of log odds. Although LR is easily applied in large-scale research, it has a restricted capacity to avoid illusory
features (Gorostiaga & Rojo-Álvarez, 2016). To deal with the high-dimension data, the “liblinear” solver was applied with a Coordinate
Descent algorithm in our study.

Decision Tree (DT). DT produces a top-down growing tree, which involves a group of nested rules (Martínez-Abad et al., 2020). A
single tree begins with an initial node and ends with multiple terminal nodes. Since DT is able to generate a recursive tree-like pattern,
this model is advantageous to simply and clearly interpret the importance of features. By contrast, one prominent weakness of DT is
easily suffered from over-fitting problems (Chen & Guestrin, 2016). In other words, it brings challenges to adjust the model parameters. By applying the Gini criterion, we employed a Classification and Regression Trees (CART) algorithm to yield the largest information gain.
Random Forests (RF). As a combination of multiple DTs, RF optimizes the predicted outcomes through bagging and random feature
selection (Breiman, 2001). Unlike the algorithm of DT, RF splits the node by a subset of features randomly (Liaw & Wiener, 2002).
Moreover, RF intuitively describes the relative importance of each feature (Pink, 2016). Although RF shows more advantages in
avoiding over-fitting issues than DT, there are pitfalls to the problem caused by the entirely randomized trees (Tang et al., 2018). To
estimate the relative importance of features, the impurity-based feature importance model was developed.
Support Vector Machine (SVM). First proposed by Vapnik, SVM aims to investigate the optimal margin in a multidimensional hyperplane (Cortes & Vapnik, 1995). SVM is superior to dispose high dimensional patterns (Chen et al., 2022) and non-linear feature
classifications (Chen & Zhai, 2023). However, SVM seems to be a ‘Black Box’ (Li et al., 2008) and hard to train the large-scale dataset
(Zhang et al., 2019) compared with other supervised classifiers. To select the optimal features, the algorithm of recursive feature
elimination (RFE) was implemented in our study.

ten-fold cross-validation in the process of model evaluation. Five crucial model evaluation metrics [i.e., Accuracy, the area under the
receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), Recall, Precision, and F-measure] were utilized and their mathematical formulae were

To define five evaluation metrics (i.e., Accuracy, AUC, Recall, Precision, and F-measure), the confusion matrix should be interpreted. It is composed of four indicators: True Positive (TP), True Negative (TN), False Positive (FP), and False Negative (FN). The
confusion matrix represents the numbers of predicted and actual values: (1) TP reflects IRS is correctly predicted as IRS; (2) TN means
NRS is correctly predicted as NRS; (3) FP reflects NRS is wrongly predicted as IRS; and (4) FN means IRS is wrongly predicted as NRS.
Accuracy is used to measure the overall correctness of TP and TN. However, it is not an efficient metric for the imbalanced dataset since
it might ignore the impact of the minority class (Kulkarni et al., 2020). Therefore, other evaluation metrics should be used to measure
the classification ability of an imbalanced model. A high AUC indicates that machine learning models can effectively discriminate IRS
from NRS. According to Juba and Le (2019), Recall and Precision are more suitable to evaluate the imbalanced model. On one side,
Recall is highly correlated to FN. A smaller FN means IRS is less likely to be wrongly predicted as NRS, which further decreases the
underestimation of IRS. On the other side, Precision is associated with FP. A smaller FP means NRS is less likely to be wrongly predicted
as IRS, which further reduces the overestimation of IRS. As a harmonic average of Recall and Precision, the F-measure is appropriately
used to identify the optimal features from the holistic perspective (Bekkar et al., 2013).
4. Results
4.1. Classification performance of the machine learning techniques
Accuracy, AUC, Recall, Precision, and F-measure were computed ten times by adopting ten-fold cross validation. The classification
performance is categorized into five levels, which refers to fail (50 %~60 %), poor (60 %~70 %), fair (70 %~80 %), good (80 %~90
%), and excellent (90 %~100 %) (Xia et al., 2013).
First, with a poor to fair classification performance, the evaluation metrics of LR ranged from 67 % to 75 % on average (i.e., AUC =
74.05 %, Accuracy = 68.13 %, Recall = 72.29 %, Precision = 67.14 %, F-measure = 69.51 %). Second, taking account of five

To sum up, LR was the poorest classifier among four machine learning approaches. Although RF and SVM had similar AUC and
Accuracy scores on average, the Recall scores of SVM were higher than RF, and the Precision scores of RF were higher than SVM. Taken
together, F-measure is a harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, which could be regarded as a compromise (Zhang & Zhang, 2009).
According to the F-measure scores, SVM showed a better classification ability than RF.
4.2. Classification performance of the top features
Ten-fold cross validation was implemented to compute the Accuracy, AUC, Recall, Precision, and F-measure of the top 50 features
(see Figs. 2-6). In the top 50 features, RF had the highest AUC and Precision scores compared with other machine learning approaches.
Concerning the Recall and F-measure indicators, SVM depicted the best classification performance among four classifiers. On the
contrary, LR showed the worst classification performance. Interestingly, no matter in which evaluation metrics, the classification
performance of DT fluctuated and even dropped when it reached a certain feature.
Taking account into five evaluation metrics, the classification performance of LR, DT, and RF tended to level off before the top 15
features. Surprisingly, the classification performance of SVM stabilized after the 40th feature. One significant finding was that the first
few features caused a sharp upgrade of the RF classification performance, which reached a steady state quickly. Although SVM had a
slower rate of improvement in its model classification performance, it gradually outperformed RF in terms of the F-measure. Similar to
RF, LR and DT maintained relatively convergent in the preliminary stage, while their classification performance was much lower than
RF in each evaluation indicator.
4.3. Descriptions of the common key features
To determine the number of optimal features in four machine learning approaches, five evaluation metrics were considered
comprehensively in our study. After around the 15th feature, the classification performance of LR tended to be stable. Furthermore, the
number of optimal features was around 10 for RF. DT was the fastest converging classifier, which flattened at the 5th feature according
to most metrics. In stark contrast to the above machine learning approaches, SVM showed a slight rise after the 30th feature, which was
the slowest converging classifier. For each machine learning approach, the optimal features are described in the descending order of
classification performance in Appendix C.

5. Discussion
5.1. Comparison of machine learning techniques
Regarding the classification performance of four machine learning techniques, there are two main findings in the current research.
First, LR depicted the lowest classification ability, and the most unstable classifier was DT. In contrast, RF and SVM were rather stable
9

International Journal of Educational Research 131 (2025) 102607

J.-q. Zheng et al.

and showed much higher classification performance than LR and DT. Although numerous resilience research adopted LR to identify the
protective factors of resilient students (e.g., Cheung, 2017; Cheung et al., 2014; Ozcan & Bulus, 2022), those studies mainly focused on
a particular category of indicators. It thus reveals a major limitation of LR, which requires researchers to explicitly identify the associations between independent and dependent variables in advance (Tu, 1996). However, 102 contextual factors were utilized in our
study, which could lead to multiple complex non-linear relationships. In this sense, it might explain why LR was the weakest classifier
in this research. Although LR and DT had a prominent advantage in their running speed, both depicted relatively lower classification
performance than RF and SVM in our study, which is consistent with previous research (e.g., Chen et al., 2021; Liu & Wu, 2005; Shao &
Lunetta, 2012; Wang et al., 2022). In addition to the primary weakness of LR as mentioned before, another possible reason is the
overfitting problem rooted in DT, which could not lead to an optimal classification solution (Bishop, 2006; Chen & Guestrin, 2016). It
might unravel the reason behind the unstable classification tendency of DT.
Second, SVM yielded the maximum number of optimal features compared with other classifiers. Prior studies adopted RF to identify
the key factors of resilient students, and the number of optimal features was around 10 (e.g., Wang et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2023),
which is remarkably similar to the current research. However, the number of top features selected by SVM was much larger than RF,
DT, and LR in our study, which is in line with previous studies (e.g., Gorostiaga & Rojo-Álvarez; Chen et al., 2021). Interestingly, most
studies evidenced that the top 20 features were recognized as optimal combinations in SVM (e.g., Chen et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2022),
whereas the top 40 features were confirmed as an eligible set in our study. One possible explanation is that previous research adopted a
onefold classification outcome (e.g., reading performance), while the classification outcome in our study is a multifaceted construct (i.
e., ICT engagement: ICT interest, perceived ICT competence, perceived autonomy related to ICT use, and perceived social interaction in
ICT use). In this regard, it might increase the number of top feature selection to some extent. In other words, SVM is able to unfold more
potential factors that provide sufficient insights into the intervention program in students’ resilience development. By contrast, RF
tends to identify the primary factors of resilience rather than adopting an overarching and systematic view. Compared with RF, SVM is
much more time-consuming in the classification of IRS and NRS. Therefore, researchers are suggested to use RF if they tend to identify
the fundamental factors of resilience in an instant way. Adopting a more comprehensive scope, it is recommended for researchers to
unfold protective factors of resilience through SVM.
5.2. Key findings on factors contributing to resilience in ICT engagement
With respect to the optimal features of resilience in ICT engagement, two key findings should be proposed for discussion. First, the
most significant common feature was ENTUSE, which discriminates IRS from NRS in four machine learning classifiers. In addition, IRS
were more likely to use ICT out of school in a recreational way (e.g., play computer games) than NRS. At first glance, it is widely
evidenced that digital games have negative impacts on students (e.g., Ferguson & Olson, 2013; Tortolero et al., 2014), which contradicts our research findings. However, the digital gaming environment could facilitate students’ engagement and motivation, which
is able to foster their resilience in the face of adversity (Granic et al., 2014). Interestingly, Scholes et al. (2022) investigated that
disadvantaged SES students had more positive attitudes towards video games than their advantaged peers in the digital context. It
might further imply that ESCS-disadvantaged individuals tended to show more interest in using ICT for the purpose of entertainment,
and resilient students in ICT engagement are commonly recognized as ‘digital game enthusiasts’. Thus, it is suggested that designing
and implementing digital game-based learning in educational contexts to foster students’ academic performance and learning motivation (Hwang et al., 2021; Prensky, 2001). To be specific, three effective teaching strategies could be used in digital game-based
learning, including 1) providing feedback for students to enhance their performance; 2) emphasizing the significance of authentic
experience and linking real-world situations to students’ textbook knowledge; 3) scaffolding and structuring students’ learning processes (Erhel & Jamet, 2013; Tay et al., 2022).
A second finding was that IRS might gain more ICT resources than NRS (e.g., a large amount of ICT devices). Nevertheless, prior
research revealed that there was no significant relationship between the number of computers and resilience (Ferrera & Rodríguez,
2015; Marcenaro, 2014). According to Chiao and Chiu (2018), high-frequency ICT usage for social interaction and information search
even amplified the SES-achievement gap. Therefore, it could be inferred that the primary reason behind resilience in ICT engagement
might not be the amount of ICT resources, but students’ intrinsic motivation in ICT usage (Zheng et al., 2023). Of note, one of our key
findings showed that IRS were more likely to have positive attitudes towards learning, future work, and their lives (e.g., high
self-efficacy). Our research finding is consistent with the previous studies, which evidenced that a great sense of learning enjoyment,
self-efficacy, and interests contributed to a resilient student (e.g., Clavel et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). To be specific, Drossel et al.
(2023) found that there was a significant relationship between ICT self-efficacy and student resilience in their computer and information literacy, which corroborated our research findings. To enhance students’ intrinsic motivation in ICT, educators are suggested to
guide students to comprehend and reflect on their ICT attitudes, as well as categorize ICT activities into different difficulty levels
(Aesaert & Braak, 2014). In this sense, it might contribute to students’ self-efficacy, which further fosters their positive attitudes
towards ICT.
5.3. Feature selection insights
Theoretical contributions of the current study are divided into two aspects. On one side, this study develops a methodological
paradigm in educational resilience research by comparing the classification performance of four machine learning techniques. To be
specific, SVM and RF are superior in dealing with the imbalanced dataset (i.e., 19.46 % IRS and 80.54 % NRS in the current study).
During the process of feature selection, it is suitable to utilize RF to unfold the fundamental factors, while SVM is good at
10

comprehensively identifying key factors. Since the nature of resilience research pertains to an imbalanced issue, the current study
enlightens researchers to reconsider the research aims of resilience research by employing feature selection techniques in four machine
learning approaches. On the other side, key factors selected by four machine learning techniques pertain to intra-individual factors,
interpersonal factors, and socio-ecological factors (Liu et al., 2017). It thus aligns with a social-ecological definition of resilience, which
refers to “the capacity of both individuals and their environments to interact in ways that optimize developmental processes” (Ungar,
2013, p.256). Therefore, it not only validates the social-ecological definition of resilience but also enriches the resilience framework
and broadens the operational definition of educational resilience in the digital age.
With respect to the practical implications, the current study enlightens educational practitioners and stakeholders in two ways.
First, the Support Vector Machine plays a crucial role in selecting optimal features that foster improved students’ resilience in ICT
engagement. In this sense, machine learning models serve as useful tools to classify optimal features into different dimensions. Second,
it is significant to combine explainable artificial intelligence techniques with machine learning models, which provide a clear and
comprehensive understanding of resilience in ICT engagement for educators and policymakers.
6. Limitations and directions for future research
Several limitations should be proposed to enlighten further studies. First, our study merely considered ICT engagement as one of the
adjustments in the process of resilience. However, adjustment is a multidimensional indicator (OECD, 2018). That is, the percentage of
resilient students might vary across different dimensions of adjustment. In this sense, researchers are suggested to investigate and
compare academic, social, emotional, and motivational adjustment (e.g., reading performance, sense of belonging, life satisfaction,
and self-efficacy), which further extends the research findings in the current resilience study. Second, PISA conducts a cross-sectional
research design, which could not generalize to a cause-effect result. In other words, it is difficult for this study to indicate a causal
relationship between key factors and resilience in ICT engagement. Therefore, future studies could utilize a longitudinal large-scale
dataset to infer a causal relationship in a dynamic resilience process. Notably, longitudinal studies are expensive and
time-consuming, which might also suffer from the risk of selection bias among participants. In this sense, future work should seek a
research balance between the cross-sectional and longitudinal design. Third, the current study is restricted to one single cycle (i.e.,
PISA 2018), which could merely indicate the phenomenon of a certain period. Since key factors of resilience might vary across time,
different PISA cycles should be utilized to compare key factors contributing to resilience in the digital age. Finally, four machine
learning techniques were implemented to discriminate IRS from NRS in this study, which is limited to a specific ranking list of key
factors of resilience in ICT engagement. It is recommended that scholars could adopt other machine learning or deep learning models to
comprehensively identify key factors in resilience research, such as Naive Bayes, XGBoost, and Multi-layer Perceptron. By employing
multiple machine learning approaches, future work should consider the structure of database, data privacy, as well as other practical
and ethical issues.
7. Conclusion
The importance of the research lies particularly in identifying 15 common factors of resilience in ICT engagement from a global
perspective. By comparing four machine learning techniques, using ICT outside of school for entertainment played the most significant
role in discriminating IRS from NRS. Of note, the classification abilities of RF and SVM were superior to LR and DT. To enhance
students’ resilience in ICT engagement, educators are suggested to implement digital game-based learning and guide students to reflect
on their ICT self-efficacy, which can facilitate ESCS-disadvantaged students’ ICT engagement and motivation. These findings offer
fresh insights into the educational resilience framework from the perspective of non-cognitive adjustment as well as innovative
techniques in resilience research in the digital world.
