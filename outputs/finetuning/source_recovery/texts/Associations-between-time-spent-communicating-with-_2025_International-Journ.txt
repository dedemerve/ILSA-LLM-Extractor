Background: While educational policy emphasizes the importance of strong school–family relationships in
enhancing student outcomes, their impact on teachers has been less thoroughly examined. Understanding how
time spent on parent communication influences teacher self-efficacy and stress can provide valuable insights into
teacher well-being.
Purpose/Focus of Study: This study investigates the relationship between the time teachers spend communicating
with parents and its effects on teacher self-efficacy and stress. Additionally, it examines whether participation in
professional development programs focused on teacher-parent collaboration moderates these effects.
Research Design: Using data from 122,584 teachers in the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS)
2018, this study employs quantitative analysis to explore associations between communication time, teacher selfefficacy, and parent-related stress. It also evaluates the role of professional development in mitigating stress
linked to parent communication.
Conclusions: Findings indicate that one hour of communication with parents is associated with increased teacher
self-efficacy but also heightened parent-related stress. Participation in professional development programs targeting teacher-parent collaboration alleviates the negative stress effects of parent communication. These results
underscore the multifaceted nature of teacher-parent relationships and highlight the importance of targeted
professional development in supporting teacher well-being.

In the ecology of educational systems, relationships between proximal stakeholders, such as teachers and parents, significantly influence
students’ educational experiences (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006).
Teacher-parent communication plays an important role in this
ecosystem, as both parties collaboratively nurture children’s development inside and outside school. Empirical evidence underscores that
effective teacher-parent partnerships enhance individualized instruction
(Epstein, 2018), improve academic achievement (Jeynes, 2007), and
reduce behavioral challenges, particularly among disadvantaged students (Kim et al., 2013). Supportive communication also amplifies
parental engagement in home- and school-based learning (Grace &
Gerdes, 2019), positioning it as the cornerstone of students’ success and
school reform.
Despite global recognition of teacher-parent communication as
central to educational reform (Karaagac et al., 2022; Sahlberg, 2016), its
impact on teacher well-being remains underexplored. Parents can be

either “personal adversaries or public allies” to teachers, serving as both
stressors and supporters in their work (Hargreaves, 2000, p. 201). Based
on the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model (Bakker & Demerouti,
2017), communication with parents can serve as a resource for promoting quality teaching, while also presenting a pressure-filled demand
that teachers as professionals must manage. Previous research implies
this paradox, indicating that while positive interactions bolster teachers’
emotional resilience and self-efficacy (McGrath, 2007; Skaalvik &
Skaalvik, 2010), frequent communication concurrently exacerbates
stress, potentially escalating into parental hostility (Moon et al., 2020).
This ambivalence complicates efforts to promote school-family partnerships, leaving policymakers and school leaders grappling with a
dilemma. On one hand, mandating increased communication risks
amplifying stress and burnout, on the other hand, reducing engagement
may undermine student outcomes and teacher efficacy.

The dual impacts of teacher-parent communication
Teacher-parent communication can embody a paradoxical relationship, simultaneously fostering self-efficacy and exacerbating stress.
Within the Job Demand-Resources (JD_R) framework, job demands are
the physical, psychological, social, or organisational aspects of work that
require sustained effort and can therefore drain employees’ energy,
whereas job resources are the features of work that help them achieve
goals, offset demands, or stimulate growth (Bakker & Demerouti, 2018).
Grounded in the JD-R model, this duality positions communication as
both a resource and a demand. Constructive exchanges give teachers
social and informational resources—such as supportive feedback, timely
student information, and shared problem-solving—whereas frequent or
conflict-laden interactions impose emotional and time pressures that
heighten strain (Aldrup et al., 2018; Hakanen et al., 2006). Thus,
teacher-parent communication may serve as a supportive resource when
it provides emotional validation and collaborative problem-solving, yet
it also becomes a demanding source of stress especially when interactions turn contentious or excessively frequent.
Specifically, teacher self-efficacy—the belief in one’s ability to
positively influence student learning (Tschannen-Moran & Hoy,
2007)—is fortified through reciprocal, collective, and trust-based interactions (Choi, 2023). When parents acknowledge teachers’ expertise,
such as valuing instruction strategies, educators internalize such validation as intrinsic motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000). The literature reveals that 67% of teachers receiving weekly parental praise report
heightened efficacy, which correlates with reduced burnout and sustained instructional commitment (Mahan et al., 2010; Skaalvik &
Skaalvik, 2020). These dynamics also align with relational trust theory,
where mutual respect fosters collaborative problem-solving and reinforces teachers’ professional identity (Bryk & Schneider, 2002;
Erichsen & Reynolds, 2020). Cross-national analyses further confirm
that efficacy thrives in contexts valuing teacher autonomy, such as
systems prioritizing pedagogical freedom over standardized metrics
(Jeon, 2017).
Conversely, stress arises when communication is framed by marketdriven accountability paradigms that position parents as clients and
teachers as service providers (Biberman-Shalev et al., 2024; Blackmore
& Hutchison, 2010). In systems where parental satisfaction measures
directly influence teacher evaluations, educators face role conflict,
which is balancing pedagogical expertise with consumer-style expectations (Guo et al., 2018). For instance, 34% of teachers globally identify
parent interactions as a primary stressor, rising to 52% in low-trust
environments (OECD, 2020). Hostile encounters, such as verbal
aggression, reported by 37% of U.S. teachers (McMahon et al., 2023),
elevate burnout risk by 42% (Madigan & Kim, 2021).
The coexistence of efficacy and stress arising from teacher-parent
communication is shaped by the frequency and quality of interactions.
For instance, frequent communication in communities with high
parental expectations and regular check-ins simultaneously enhances
teachers’ sense of efficacy through collaborative goal-setting but also
amplifies stress due to relentless performance pressures. This dual
pattern underscores the inadequacy of viewing communication as universally beneficial or detrimental; instead, it highlights the need to
examine how interaction frequency produces divergent consequences.
Despite theoretical recognition of this duality, empirical studies have
rarely tested its simultaneous effects in a comparable way. Leveraging
large-scale data, the current study evaluates whether and the extent to
which time spent communicating is related to teacher well-being, both

Communication between teachers and parents
Teacher-parent partnership is a cornerstone of effective education
systems and student success, bridging the home and school environments (Hargreaves, 2000). Extensive research demonstrates that
collaborative relationships between teachers and parents significantly
enhance academic achievement, with studies showing 15–20% improvements in learning outcomes (Jeynes, 2017; OECD, 2020). These
partnerships extend beyond transactional interactions, embodying a
professional responsibility for teachers to engage families as
co-constructors of student success (Epstein, 2018). Central to this
collaboration is mutual respect and cultural responsiveness, where
teachers honor familial expertise while guiding pedagogical practices
(Cheatham & Ostrosky, 2011). Effective and respectful communication,
characterized by transparency, active listening, and shared
decision-making is key in building trustful relationships with parents as
part of school social capital, and contributes to the development of
students (Yang, 2017).
Teacher-parent communication manifests in three forms such as
formal, spontaneous, and informal. Formal interactions include scheduled conferences, progress reports, and individualized education plan
meetings, which provide structured opportunities to align goals
(Seitsinger et al., 2008). Spontaneous exchanges occur through
impromptu conversations before or after school or via messaging platforms, allowing timely responses to emerging concerns. Informal
communication fosters relational bonds through casual discussions
during school events. However, poorly managed communications, such
as overly critical exchanges, can erode trust, particularly during sensitive discussions like disciplinary actions. Maintaining clear boundaries,
such as designated times for parent inquiries, could balance accessibility
with the professionalism of teachers (Graham-Clay, 2024).
In brief, the content of teacher-parent communication typically revolves around three domains, including academic progress, behavioral
guidance, and resource coordination (Garbacz et al., 2015). Teachers
share academic updates, offer strategies for skill development, and

The original TALIS included 50 countries. However, we excluded
Australia and the Netherlands due to low response rates, as noted by the
OECD (2019). We also excluded data from France, Russia, and Spain
because key variables were missing. As a result, the analytic sample
includes teachers from 45 countries. Most countries had high response
rates for both schools and individual teachers. For individual country
analyses, we used the respective teacher sample weights. For the pooled
analysis, we rescaled the teacher sample weight to create senate
weights, ensuring that each country contributed equally regardless of
differences in sample size.
We identified 135,588 teachers who worked full-time and taught at
least one academic subject. After removing outliers whose total working
hours were extremely high (over 80 hours a week) or low (less than 20
hours), the remaining sample consisted of 133,358 teachers. Because the
variables selected for subsequent analyses had low rates of missing data,
ranging from 0.1% to 2%, we performed listwise deletion of missing
cases for all of the variables used. Additionally, we excluded 441 samples from schools in which the number of teachers was less than 2
(0.36%). We excluded work hours, more than 80 hours per week and less
than 20 hours, accounting for the top 1% of the distribution in the
pooled sample. The final sample included 122,584 teachers in 12,034
primary and secondary schools in 45 countries, consisting of 23,484
primary and 99,100 secondary teachers, with 31% male and 69% female
teachers. Descriptive statistics for the analytical sample from the pooled
dataset are presented in Table 1, and descriptive statistics for selected
variables and sample sizes by country are presented in Online Appendix

Professional development and teacher–parent communication
PD has the potential to ensure that communication is not a doubleedged sword for teachers, addressing the duality of its relationship
with self-efficacy and stress, considering its role in influencing teacher
psychology (Yang, 2020). Communication-specific PD equips educators
with targeted strategies to navigate complex interactions with parents
and address systemic barriers (Visković & Višnjić Jevtić, 2017). A
meta-analysis of 39 teacher-training interventions found that programs
emphasizing communication strategies (e.g., active listening, conflict
resolution) and cultural responsiveness increased teachers’ confidence
in engaging families by 63% while reducing stress associated with
adversarial interactions by 28% (Smith et al., 2021). These programs
shift communication from a transactional obligation to a relational skill,
enabling teachers to reframe parental involvement as a shared endeavor
rather than a performance metric. For instance, teachers trained in
solution-focused scripting—a technique emphasizing collaborative
problem-solving—reported 40% fewer contentious interactions, even in
high-stakes discussions about academic struggles (Kraft & Dougherty,
2013).
The urgency for PD in this domain arises from systemic gaps in
teacher preparation. While 89% of teachers globally participate in PD
annually, only 32% receive training on family communication (OECD,
2020), leaving many unprepared for realities like managing aggressive
parental behavior or addressing cultural mismatches. This gap exacerbates stress, particularly for early-career teachers: 72% of novice educators report feeling overwhelmed by parent interactions, compared to
34% of veterans (Hannon & O’Donnell, 2022). Teachers trained in
bidirectional dialogue techniques—such as eliciting parental insights
during meetings—report stronger self-efficacy (Bryk & Schneider,
2002). Building on these studies, the current study assesses whether
participation in PD in communication with parents moderates the
stress-efficacy paradox.

The current study
Building on evidence that teacher-parent communication can influence teachers in multifaceted ways, the current study investigated the
relationship between time spent communicating with parents and these
dual outcomes. Teacher self-efficacy serves as a positive indicator of
communication’s benefits, reflecting educators’ confidence in their
ability to impact student learning, whereas stress acts as a negative
marker of its toll. The study further explored whether participation PD
targeting parental engagement moderates the associations between
communication time and teacher well-being. To identify universal patterns rather than context-specific variations, the analysis included all
available countries in the dataset. Two research questions guided this
inquiry: (1) How is time spent communicating with parents associated
with teacher self-efficacy and parent-related stress? and (2) To what
extent does PD focusing on teacher-parent communication moderate
these associations?

Analytical strategies
This study aimed to provide a generalizable understanding of how
time spent communicating with parents impacts teacher well-being,
transcending national and institutional contexts. While the TALIS
2018 dataset’s inclusion of 45 countries enhances external validity, internal validity concerns persist due to unobserved confounders inherent
in cross-sectional data. To address these limitations, we employed
school fixed-effect regression models, absorbing environment effects
and adjusting for individual-level covariates.
The school fixed-effects model leverages the random selection of
approximately 20 teachers per school, comparing educators within the
same institutional environment. This approach can mitigate bias from
unobserved school-level factors (e.g., leadership styles, parent demographics) and cross-country disparities by eliminating time-invariant
school characteristics. The analytical sample was restricted to schools
with at least two teachers to ensure that school fixed-effect could be
estimated reliably. As a robustness check, we confirmed that the main
findings remained consistent when the minimum teacher count per
school was increased to ten.

Teacher self-efficacy and stress
First, we used a composite variable developed by TALIS to measure
teacher self-efficacy as our first dependent variable. This composite
measure was constructed using confirmatory factor loadings with three
subscales: classroom management self-efficacy, instructional selfefficacy, and student engagement self-efficacy. Each subscale consisted
of four items addressing the respondent’s confidence and attitude in the
classroom. The composite self-efficacy scale had high reliability, with
Cronbach’s alpha reaching 0.9 across countries (OECD, 2019). We
standardized the composite teacher self-efficacy variable across the
pooled data, setting a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 to facilitate interpretation of the results in terms of effect size.
Second, the respondents indicated their sources of stress from 11
sources on a 4-point scale, ranging from not at all to a lot. Sources of
stress included excessive lesson preparation, maintaining classroom
discipline, and modifying lessons for students with special needs. Among
the 11 sources of stress, we used one item addressing stress related to
responding to parents’ or guardians’ concerns as our second dependent
variable. We also standardized this variable using pooled data, with a
mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1.

Table 2 shows that an additional hour of communication with parents was associated with a 0.03 standard deviation increase in teacher
self-efficacy at a 0.05 statistically significant level, even after controlling
for a series of control variables and adjusting for school fixed-effects.
Under the same conditions, one hour of communication with parents
was also significantly associated with a 0.06 standard deviation increase
in teachers’ parent-related stress. The magnitude of the stress coefficient

was almost twice as high as the coefficient for self-efficacy, suggesting
that more time spent communicating with parents was associated with
increases in both teacher self-efficacy and parent-related stress, but this
association was stronger for stress than for self-efficacy. Additionally,
we conducted subgroup analyses by school level, which showed
consistent patterns across groups. The effect of parent communication
on self-efficacy was smaller for primary (b = 0.013, SE = 0.006) than
secondary teachers (b = 0.035, SE = 0.004) (Online Appendix C).
Table 2 also shows that perceived stress due to student behavioral
problems was a strong predictor of lower teacher self-efficacy. Because
the different perceived sources of stress were interrelated, as shown in
the second column of Table 1, other sources of stress were positively
related to teacher self-efficacy due to collinearity. As expected, teacher
cooperation, job satisfaction, and belief in teaching as a social utility and
a proxy for motivation were significantly positively related to teacher
self-efficacy. We also found that years of experience, being a female
teacher, and having a college degree predicted higher teacher selfefficacy. Conversely, job satisfaction was negatively associated with
parent-related stress, while a graduate degree was not associated with
parent-related stress.
In addition to the pooled sample results, within-country analyses
were conducted using the same models for each country to examine
variations in the relationship between teacher-parent communication
and psychological outcomes. Fig. 1 illustrates that the frequency of
communication with parents was positively associated with teacher selfefficacy in approximately two-thirds of the countries studied. Notably,
the effect size exceeded 0.05 standard deviations in Singapore, Israel,
and Australia, indicating a strong positive association. However, the
relationship was not statistically significant in countries such as
Argentina, Estonia, and Japan. Importantly, no country demonstrated a
negative association between communication and teacher self-efficacy,
reinforcing that communication generally supports teachers’ professional competence.
The findings for parent-related stress reveal a more pervasive
pattern. Communication was significantly associated with increased
stress levels in 39 out of 45 countries, with exceptions including Brazil,
Georgia, and Saudi Arabia. Finland exhibited the most pronounced
stress impact, with an effect size of nearly 0.15 standard deviations,
followed by countries such as Estonia, Croatia, and Bulgaria, where the
effect sizes ranged around 0.1 standard deviations. These results highlight that while communication fosters self-efficacy in many contexts, its
stress-inducing effects are more widespread and often stronger, which
supports the main results from the pooled data. Interestingly, two-thirds
of the countries showed significant associations for both self-efficacy
and stress, supporting the dual role hypothesis of teacher-parent
communication. For example, in Singapore, teachers who spent more
time communicating with parents reported higher levels of both selfefficacy and parent-related stress with effect sizes of approximately
0.1 standard deviations each. A similar pattern was observed in many
other countries, including the United States, Slovak Republic, and New
Zealand, where both outcomes were positively associated with effect
sizes of nearly 0.07 standard deviations. However, some countries
exhibited significant associations only for parent-related stress but not
for self-efficacy, for instance, Japan, South Korea, and Mexico, suggesting that the dual dynamic may not universally apply across all
contexts.

Discussion
The dual role of teacher-parent communication, enhancing selfefficacy while exacerbating stress, reveals a paradox central to modern
education systems, where educators navigate the complex interplay of
accountability as professionals and well-being. Leveraging crossnational data from 45 countries, this study demonstrates that frequent
parent interactions empower teachers through trust-based partnerships
and burden them with heightened emotional labor. Overall, teachers
dedicating one additional hour per week to parent communication reported a 0.03 SD increase in self-efficacy alongside a 0.06 SD rise in
stress. These findings align with the JD-R model, positioning communication as both a job resource fostering professional growth and a job
demand amplifying occupational strain. In addition, the moderating role
of PD was particularly noteworthy, implying that increased knowledge
and skills in teacher-parent collaboration may alleviate teachers’ stress
caused by communicating with parents, highlighting the potential
vulnerability of teachers without adequate preparation, given the
inevitable involvement of parents throughout their teaching careers.
The subsequent sections will discuss several implications of these
findings.
First, these findings highlight the positive aspects of teacher-parent
interactions by showing that higher levels of communication with parents were associated with greater teacher self-efficacy. From a traditional perspective, teaching is linked to intrinsic values such as pride,
confidence, and deep affection for students, rather than external rewards
(Bullough & Hall-Kenyon, 2012). Research consistently indicates that
close teacher-student relationships enhance job satisfaction and motivation, creating a passionate and dynamic learning environment
(Bullough & Hall-Kenyon, 2012; Wang et al., 2020). This commitment
extends to supporting students’ families. Likewise, establishing meaningful connections with parents may also bolster teachers’ self-efficacy.
Notably, subgroup analyses show that this positive correlate was
observed in two-thirds of the countries examined, implying that teachers
could benefit from interaction with parents in improving their
self-efficacy, regardless of cultural and contextual boundaries. When
considering this finding alone, parents appear to be public allies to
teachers.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge that the evolving landscape of
education, particularly in light of the COVID-19 pandemic, has significantly altered teacher-parent communication patterns and teacher wellbeing. The data analyzed in this study were collected in 2018, predating

pandemic introduced unprecedented challenges for educators, leading
to increased workload, shifts to online or hybrid instruction, and
heightened stress and anxiety (McNicholas et al., 2024; Westphal et al.,
2022). Future research leveraging more recent data, such as the forthcoming TALIS 2024, would be invaluable to explore how these
pandemic-driven shifts have permanently or temporarily altered the
relationships between parent communication time, teacher self-efficacy,
and stress, and the role of professional development in this new
educational landscape.

Limitations and future studies
While this study provides valuable insights into the dual role of
teacher-parent communication and the moderating effects of PD, several
limitations must be acknowledged. First, the results should be interpreted as correlational rather than causational. The observed associations between time spent communicating with parents and teacherreported self-efficacy or stress may reflect bidirectional relationships.
For instance, teachers with higher self-efficacy might proactively engage
with parents, while those experiencing higher stress levels may avoid
such interactions (Aleksić et al., 2024). Future research could address
this limitation by leveraging exogenous events—such as policy changes
or natural experiments—that directly affect teachers’ communication
time, enabling causal inference.
Second, while the study highlights the potential of PD to mitigate
stress, the findings are constrained by the lack of detailed information on
PD content, duration, and quality. Although all PD activities included in
this analysis were related to teacher-parent communication, the absence
of specificity limits our understanding of which aspects of PD are most
effective. Future studies should explore how tailored PD programs and
what components, such as those focusing on conflict resolution, cultural
responsiveness, or boundary-setting, enhance teachers’ communication
skills and reduce stress. Similarly, the measure of communication time
lacks granularity, as it does not capture the purpose, content, or modality (e.g., face-to-face meetings versus digital interactions) of these
exchanges. Investigating how different types of communication impact
teacher outcomes could provide more actionable insights for policymakers and school leaders to guide effective communication.
Third, this study relied on self-reported measures of teacher selfefficacy and stress, which are inherently subjective and susceptible to
individual and cultural interpretation biases (Jerrim & Sims, 2021). For
example, what one teacher perceives as “stressful” may vary significantly across cultural contexts or even within similar educational systems. While we attempted to narrow the scope of stress to parent-related
interactions only, future research could enhance reliability by incorporating objective indicators such as teacher turnover rates, absenteeism,
or measures of teacher effectiveness derived from student outcomes. In
addition, future studies could employ alternative measures for outcomes, such as collective teacher self-efficacy or various emotional and
psychological symptoms (Anderson et al., 2023; Goddard et al., 2000;
Tuxford & Bradley, 2015). For instance, investigating collective
self-efficacy as an outcome could provide insights into how school-wide
patterns of parent engagement contribute to a stronger, more confident
teaching community. Such research could reveal how positive or challenging interactions with parents might collectively influence the
overall morale and perceived capability of school staff, distinct from or
in addition to individual effects.
Fourth, although this study used a large-scale international dataset
encompassing 45 countries, cross-national variability in results suggests
that contextual factors—such as school policies, cultural norms around
parental involvement, and institutional support systems—may shape
how communication affects teachers. For example, while parent-related
stress was significantly associated with communication in most countries, exceptions like Brazil and Saudi Arabia highlight the importance of
cultural norms that prioritize familial trust over frequent engagement.
Conversely, Finland’s high parental expectations may explain why
communication had no significant effect on self-efficacy but exerted the
strongest impact on stress. Future research should investigate these
cross-country differences in greater depth to identify how contextual
factors mediate or moderate these relationships.

Conclusion
Teacher-parent relationships, like all meaningful human connections, are inherently complex. This study underscores that communication between teachers and parents embodies a dual dynamic, which
can enhance teachers’ self-efficacy through collaborative partnership
yet simultaneously exacerbate stress via heightened demands and
emotional labor. These findings caution against simplistic narratives
that reduce parent engagement to either an unqualified good or an
adversarial burden. Overemphasizing parental involvement as an unmitigated benefit risk amplifying stress and burnout, particularly when
teachers lack training to manage contentious interactions. Instead,
policymakers and school leaders need to adopt nuanced strategies that
harmonize these dual forces. By grounding policies in local realities and
equipping teachers with evidence-based tools, education systems can
transform effective and respectful communication between schools and
families from a precarious tightrope into a sustainable pillar of teacher
well-being and student success. Ultimately, the goal is not to eliminate
the inherent tensions of teacher-parent relationships but to recalibrate
them.
