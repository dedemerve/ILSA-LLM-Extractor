Abstract
This study presents a novel approach to improving quality assurance in the Trends
in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS). It utilizes a combination
of neural networks and regression analysis to validate country-level achievement
scores. Concerns over the integrity of TIMSS test scores necessitate robust quality
control methods. The research described here developed and piloted new statistical
indicators to identify countries with apparently unusual results, aiming to enhance
the efficacy of the TIMSS quality assurance process. The methodology leverages
the unique design of TIMSS, which assesses 4th and 8th graders every 4 years, to generate model-based predictions for country-level average scores in math and science. The
comparison of these predictions against actual results supported the feasibility of this
approach. This study also investigated the correlation between large positive residuals
and substantial changes in sample quality or population definition. While no strong
correlations were found, this investigation contributes valuable insights for future
TIMSS assessments.
Keywords: Large-scale assessment, TIMSS quality assurance, Neural networks,
Regression analysis, Educational policy, Cross-country comparisons, Country rankings,
Trend prediction

Introduction
Since 2000, International Large-scale Survey Assessments (ILSA) have become an
increasingly important contributor to education policy discussions and designs
across the globe. There is ample evidence that a broad range of education stakeholders find great utility in primary ILSA results, as well as secondary analyses of those
results (Kirsch & Braun, 2020; Singer et al., 2018). For school-age populations, among
the largest and most influential ILSAs are the: (i) Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS); (ii) Progress in International Reading Literacy Study

(PIRLS), and (iii) Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA). These
assessments each include two sets of instruments: one for measuring a range of cognitive outcomes and one for eliciting information on demographics, background

Unfortunately, political leaders often regard primary ILSA results, such as country
rankings, as indicators of the success (or lack thereof ) of their education policies—a
perspective that is driven, in part, by the focus of the media on those rankings. As the
incentives for improving scores and rankings intensify, there is an elevated risk that
results will be subject to some level of manipulation of procedures or even outright
distortion of data. Clearly, such efforts, if successful, undermine the cross-country
comparability that is key to ILSA utility.
Comprehensive quality control methods are employed at each stage of the workflow
to ensure the credibility and validity of ILSA outcomes (Kirsch et al., 2018; Braun &
Kirsch, 2025). [The following section contains TIMSS-specific details.] Nonetheless,
in a particular administration, it does happen that after reviewing: (i) the auxiliary
data collected during the course of sampling implementation (i.e. the paradata); (ii)
the psychometric analyses of the response data; (iii) and comparisons with the results
from earlier assessments, the current results for a country may appear to be unusually
out of line. Typically, this only involves a very small number of countries, and may not
happen in every cycle. However, when it happens, the centers responsible for operations at the international level, in collaboration with country representatives, launch
an investigation to try to determine the cause of these deviations.
The investigators consider a number of technical indicators, as well as relevant auxiliary information, with the goal of deciding whether the results should be accepted
or challenged. When challenging the results, the more indicators available that offer a
consistent counter-narrative, the stronger the case. This adjudication process is costly
and time-consuming. Further, because of political sensitivities, a preliminary decision to annotate the reported results, or even to reject them entirely, may well involve
some level of diplomatic engagement. In this context, the availability of another credible indicator for consideration would be most welcome.
With this in mind, we developed a family of new statistical indicators of data consistency in ILSA and examined their operating characteristics. The basic methodology
consists of a combination of neural networks and classical regression analysis to generate model-based predictions of individual-level scores which are then aggregated
to obtain country-level, average achievement scores for all participating jurisdictions.
Those predictions are then compared to the observed average scores to generate
country-level residuals. The distribution of these residuals is the basis for the new
indicators.
The empirical results from the present study show some promise for the utility of a
composite indicator in contributing to the current investigative strategy. Importantly,
the indicator is not one-sided; that is, it may show that an apparently unusual outcome
is, in fact, quite reasonable given the patterns in the data as a whole for that country.
However, if it shows that the outcome is markedly inconsistent with those patterns, the
finding can either: (i) support the challenge to the legitimacy of these out-of-line results
or (ii) suggest the need for further investigations.

The patterns referred to above are generated by models that relate individuals’ scores
on a cognitive test to a set of explanatory variables derived from their demographic and
background characteristics, as well as certain contextual factors. In all ILSAs, for almost
all countries, individual differences in the vectors of explanatory variables are strongly
correlated with differences in individual outcomes, and, consequently, the model-based
predictions of country-level mean scores are reasonably close to the country-level
observed scores. However, if there were some upward manipulation of the cognitive
scores (only), then the model-based prediction described above would substantially
underestimate the reported, country-level average score, yielding a large positive residual. Consequently, those countries with unusually large, positive residuals are plausible
candidates for further investigation, in conjunction with other sources of evidence.
We investigate two quality control (QC) indicators in this study. One indicator
(Method 1) takes advantage of a particular feature of TIMSS. Among ILSAs, TIMSS is
unique in that it assesses both 4th and 8th graders every 4 years. Consequently, the 8th
graders at one administration are (approximately) the same cohort that were 4th graders
at the previous administration. The indicator derives from a strategy that takes advantage of this linkage, along with the fact that the respondents at each administration constitute an (approximately) probability sample of the target population. The logic of the
method relies on the putative consistency of the 4th grade and 8th grade results for a
particular cohort. The indicator is based on the residuals obtained by comparing the
predicted 8th grade results to the actual 8th-grade results, focusing on large, positive
residuals.
As detailed below, the second indicator (Method 2) uses 8th grade results in one
administration to predict 8th grade results in the succeeding administration. Note that
Method 1 relies on data from the same cohort, while Method 2 relies on data from different cohorts. The present attention to predicting 8th grade results in math and science
is motivated by the fact that they serve as important bellwethers for the success of education policies aimed at improving elementary STEM education.
To our knowledge, nothing in the literature employs this type of methodology to generate QC indicators. Thus, it nicely complements those indicators currently employed
for QC. As a final step, we evaluate how closely the countries with large, positive residuals align with those identified in the TIMSS report as experiencing substantial changes
in sample quality and/or population definition.

Background on TIMSS
TIMSS is an international, large-scale survey assessment sponsored by the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) and administered every 4 years. It assesses students in the 4th and 8th grades in mathematics
and science. The outcome data are the individuals’ achievement scores on the cognitive assessment.1 These achievement scores are estimates that are derived utilizing
a complex modeling approach that is employed in all major ILSAs (e.g., von Davier
& Sinharay, 2013; von Davier et al., 2009). In addition to the cognitive instruments,

As is true of all ILSAs, substantial resources are dedicated to ensuring all countries
follow the same implementation procedures and QC procedures at each stage of the
TIMSS workflow. In particular, the fidelity of implementation of the school sample
design and the instrument administration procedures is carefully monitored. With
regard to the latter, teams of international monitors are dispatched to each participating country. They observe the TIMSS administration and submit detailed reports,
including notations on any deviations from the protocols (TIMSS 2019, Chap. 7).
Responses to both the cognitive instruments and background questionnaires are subjected to a series of analyses followed by extensive QC checks (TIMSS 2019, Chap.
10–16). Analysts review item statistics, score distributions, etc. Country results are
compared to those of other countries, as well as to past data (if available). Results that
appear to be unusual trigger investigations that typically involve multiple contractors,
as well as country representatives. In some instances, satisfactory explanations are
obtained. In others, there remains sufficient concern about the validity of the results
that additional procedures are invoked.
Our three research questions are:
Research Question 1: What are the characteristics of the tails of the distributions
of the residuals generated by Method 1 (4th grade—> 8th grade) for 8th-grade TIMSS
country-level results in math and science?
Research Question 2: What are the characteristics of the tails of the distributions
of the residuals generated by Method 2 (8th grade—> 8th grade) for 8th-grade TIMSS
country-level results in math and science?
Research Question 3: Using a composite indicator (based on combining the results
from the two methods) for TIMSS math and science (grade 8), what is the degree of
correspondence between countries associated with large positive residuals and countries
identified as having substantial changes in sample quality and/or population definition?

Methodology
RQ 1

As noted above, the first indicator is derived from a comparison of each observed, country-level Grade 8 mean score (math or science) with each predicted, country-level Grade
8 mean score (math or science). The latter is obtained by a two-stage procedure. The
first stage utilizes a neural network undergoing supervised learning with the data from
the ensemble of countries2contributing to the analysis for a particular subject and a specific pair of administrations. The second stage utilizes regression analysis, applied to the
results of Stage 1, to produce the final values of the country-level predictions.
Stage 1: neural networks

Appendix A contains a brief introduction to the type of neural network (NN) employed
in this study. The basic idea is that a NN undergoing supervised learning can identify
patterns linking the full set of input data to the criterion data. In this application, the
input data comprised individual-level variables derived from respondents’ demographic
characteristics and responses to background questions (see Appendix B). The criterion data were the individual’s score (mean plausible values) on either math or science.
Supervised learning typically involves a large number of iterations and, ultimately, yields
a pattern-based predicted criterion score for each individual. These predictions were
aggregated to obtain a country-level, mean predicted score. These country-level mean
predicted scores were differenced from the reported scores for the countries, and the
resulting residuals were recorded.
Note: The data from all countries are incorporated into the analytic data set on which
the neural network was trained. With a substantial number of countries contributing data, the impact of any one country on the final results is rather small. Ideally, a
‘leave-out-one’ strategy akin to cross-validation would be preferred, but would require
substantially more computation. This would constitute an interesting extension of the
present study.
In empirical applications, a NN must be ‘trained’ in order to generate useful results.
This training involves determining the number of hidden layers to be employed, as well
as the types of transformations to be employed as interim results generated in one layer
are passed on to the next layer. For this study, we initially began by training a NN to predict both 2011 4th-grade and 2015 8th-grade student-level math scores simultaneously,
using the observed 2011 4th-grade and 2015 8th-grade student background variables,
respectively.
Leveraging the fitted model, we exploited the fact that—for each country—the 4thgrade and 8th-grade samples represent (approximately) probability samples of the same
cohort. Specifically, we predicted 2015 8th-grade math scores for each country by inputting the 2011 4th-grade background and contextual data while modifying the grade
indicator from 0 (4th grade) to 1 (8th grade). This approach assumes that the relationships between contextual data, grade level, and achievement scores are sufficiently well
captured by the model, such that repurposing 4th-grade context data—while adjusting

In Stage 1 we relied on the ability of NN undergoing supervised learning to ‘discover’
patterns that can yield useful predictions of the criterion scores (e.g. Grade 8 mean math
country-level scores). However, after carrying out Stage 1, we found systematic, substantial underpredictions of the criterion scores. We surmised that these underpredictions
were likely caused by the systematic differences in the distributions of the background
responses between 4th graders (in 2011) and 8th graders (in 2015). An example is the
often-replicated finding of overall lower level of ‘enjoyment’ of math (or science) in students’ self-reports in 8th grade compared to 4th grade. Accordingly, we introduced a
second stage of analysis to account for systematic changes in some of the input variables. Country-specific analyses were implemented since these changes were not uniform
across countries.
Stage 2

For this stage, the country-level residuals from Stage 1 were regressed on a new set of
variables derived from the average differences in certain background responses between
Grade 4 (in one administration) and Grade 8 (in the next administration). We first calculated, for all the background questions, the location shifts (mean differences) between
Grade 4 and Grade 8. We then selected those background questions whose location
shifts correlated with the Stage 1 residuals at the 0.05 significance level or beyond. The
location shifts of these selected background questions were assigned to one of three
matrices, depending on whether the question was an SES question, an attitudinal question with positive wording, or one with negative wording. The background variables in
each matrix are displayed in Appendix D.
We then derived three new variables by extracting the first principal component from
each of the three matrices. These three country-level, derived variables were employed
as predictors in a multiple regression with the Stage 1 country-level residuals as the criterion. The fitted values from this Stage 2 regression were then subtracted from the corresponding Stage 1 residuals to produce a new set of country-level predictions. This set
of predictions were differenced from the country-level observations, yielding a new set
of country-level residuals. Compared to the Stage 1 residuals, these Stage 2 residuals had
markedly improved properties. In particular, the distribution of the Stage 2 residuals was
approximately centered at 0 and more nearly symmetric than were the Stage 1 residuals.
Note: The strategy for obtaining the Stage 2 residuals is analogous to the “re-roughing”
procedure introduced by Tukey (1977) in the context of time series smoothing.
After completing the analysis for predicting 2015 Grade 8 mean country-level math
scores and computing the corresponding residuals, we employed the same procedure
described above to train three additional neural network models: one for predicting
student-level science scores for the 2015 8th-grade cohort (using 2011 4th-grade cohort

RQ2

To answer RQ2, we employed the same methodology as for RQ1, with the difference
being that predictions for Grade 8 in one administration were based on Grade 8 data for
the previous administration. Thus, in this case, the students in the two administrations
corresponded to different cohorts.
RQ3

To provide a partial answer to RQ3, we extracted information from the documentation
in TIMSS technical report appendices that included information on sample quality in
each administration, as well as changes in the target population between administrations. One characteristic is whether there were substantial differences in school participation rates before and after replacement. A second was where the final participation
rate was unusually low. A third was whether private schools were included in the sampling frame. We then looked to match countries with large, positive outliers derived
from the composite indicator (described below), with countries that experienced modest
to substantial changes in the student samples submitted for analysis.

Results for RQ3
The rationale behind RQ1 and RQ2 was to develop and investigate two related, statistical
methods in order to identify countries with unusual results (i.e. large, positive residuals)
that merited further scrutiny. For both RQ1 and RQ2, some countries were identified in
a particular subject in both pairs of years or in both subjects within a particular pair of
years. With RQ1, for example, Chile was identified in Mathematics for both 2011–2015
and 2015–2019, as well as in Science for 2011–2015. With RQ2, Georgia was identified
in both Mathematics and Science for 2011–2015, while Turkey was identified in Mathematics for both 2011–2015 and 2015–2019.
In reviewing the results from both RQ1 and RQ2, we found that although there was
some overlap in countries identified, there were countries called out by one method but
not the other. Since these methods are intended to serve as a first stage screening device,
we decided that an appropriate use of the findings was to combine the results of the two
methods; that is, take the union of countries located in the top quintiles as the ‘screened
set’ of countries to be subjected to further examination.
The matching exercise described in the Methods section was carried out for two pairs
of cycles (2011 and 2015; 2015 and 2019), for both math and science. For all four combinations, we found a modest number of correspondences between the screened set
of countries and countries highlighted for these sampling difficulties. For instance, in
TIMSS 2015 Grade 8, Kazakhstan, Saudi Arabia, and Turkey had exclusion rates exceeding 10%, while Turkey, Quebec, and the United States were flagged for low participation
rates (LaRoche et al., 2015). However, only Kazakhstan, Iran, and Turkey appeared in the
screened set for mathematics, and only Georgia and New Zealand showed overlap for
science.
Similarly, in TIMSS 2019 Grade 8, Saudi Arabia, Kazakhstan, and Russia exhibited
notable exclusion rates and Hong Kong, Norway, and the United States were flagged for
participation concerns (Martin et al., 2020a, 2020b). However, only Iran, Italy, and Turkey appeared in the screened set for mathematics, and only Hong Kong, Italy, and the
United States overlapped for science.
This modest overlap suggests that: (i) the use of the upper quintile may be too ‘liberal’ and (i) the residual-based approach may be capturing issues not solely attributable

Discussion and future research
The goal of this research was to develop a methodology that could distinguish a few
countries with unusual results (i.e. TIMSS 8th grade mean scores in Mathematics or
Science) that could be employed, in conjunction with other indicators, to identify those
countries whose TIMSS cognitive outcomes merited further scrutiny. The RQ1 methodology took advantage of two features of TIMSS: (i) The 4th-grade cohort in one administration is approximately the 8th-grade cohort in the following administration, and (ii)
There is a strong statistical relationship between individual performance on the cognitive instruments of TIMSS and the set of demographic and background characteristics
associated with the individual.
Consequently, if in a particular country there were some substantial degree of deviation from sampling and operational procedures that led to substantial, upward bias in
the outcomes, then the general pattern of the relationship between outcomes and demographics/background variables (as determined by the prediction model estimated using
the data from all participating countries) would be severely disrupted, leading to a predicted country mean score quite different from (i.e. lower than) the observed mean
score. Of course, since the threshold is set solely by a statistical criterion, it should not be
assumed that a country being located in the screen set constitutes a prima facie case for
data quality issues. As noted earlier, much more evidence must be brought to bear.
The rationale for the methodology relies on a reasonable assumption; namely, that if
a country had indeed deviated from standards in ways that affect data quality systematically, it would likely not have also manipulated the demographic/background data in
such a way as to maintain the general pattern observed in the aggregate. On the other
hand, some changes in the sampling plan might not become evident with this methodology. For example, reducing the number of schools sampled in typically lower-performing
sectors (e.g. low SES schools, rural schools) would only come to light through a careful
comparison with the intended plan or by comparison with the obtained samples in previous cycles.
To be of practical value, the estimated prediction model should fit most of the countries reasonably well and the size of the screened set cannot be too large. Indeed, for all
the fitted models the distributions of residuals were generally well-behaved. The upper
quintile was a convenient choice, though it necessarily includes countries where the
model fit is just somewhat poorer, but not due to any manipulation. Thus, we should not
expect more than modest overlap with countries identified through a review of sampling
implementation features.
For RQ1, the methodology was applied to TIMSS data from two pairs of administrations (2011 and 2015; 2015 and 2019) for both math and science. The focus was on
predicting 8th-grade results in the later administration. The outcomes were very encouraging. In all four cases, most countries’ 8th-grade results were reasonably well predicted
by the estimated regression model. For RQ2, we employed a methodology similar to the

Finally, for RQ3, we investigated the degree of correspondence between countries in
the screened set (top quintile from either prediction model) and countries that had been
identified as having challenges with regard to school sample quality and/or changes in
the target population between cycles. We found only modest correspondence, suggesting that further investigation of both the method and indicators of possible workflow
anomalies are called for.
This study lays the foundation for a number of future investigations: (a) A retrospective study in which the results of the RQ1 and RQ2 methodologies are triangulated with
other indicators that were available at the time in order to judge the utility of the new,
composite indicator; (b) A prospective study in which the present approach is applied to
data from TIMSS 2023; (c) Investigation of the correspondence between the presence of
large, positive residuals and other indicators of data integrity; (d) Further refinement of
the prediction models and the selection of countries for screening.

Appendix A: neural networks: theoretical foundations and methodology
In contrast to classical regression modeling, mainly employed for the purpose of “attribution” (Efron, 2020), the twenty-first century has seen the development of an extraordinary collection of algorithms for the purpose of “pure prediction”. One approach, deep
learning, leverages increased computing power and very large databases to operate at
enormous scales, incorporating tens of thousands (or more) of predictors. Deep learning
models (neural networks) fall under the rubric of data-based prediction models.
There are essential differences between these two approaches. Model-based prediction
targets estimation and inference, with the data assumed to be generated from a stochastic process. Neural networks, on the other hand, emphasize efficient implementation
and empirical validation of results, with the data assumed to be generated by a mechanism of unknown dynamics (Breiman, 2001; Hand, 2000). In addition, the goals of these
two approaches are different. Model-based prediction aims to provide a fitted model,
a set of predictors (or a classifier) for the data and its structure. The outputs, such as
marginal effects, signs, and the properties of the etimated model parameters are used to
offer putative explanations of the phenomenon under investigation (Wild & Pfannkuch,
1999). By contrast, most applications of neural networks principally aim to provide accurate predictions for future observations of the phenomenon.
Model development is different for the two approaches. Model-based prediction typically leads to a final model selected from a family of models assumed a priori. However,
regardless of the methods used (supervised or unsupervised, maximum likelihood or
Bayesian, and so on), the learning process for neural networks leads to more than one
model as the learning curve may have various local minima (Ripley, 1996). Neural networks approximate the underlying mapping (or generating) function via iterative learning, rather than using a prior assumed model family, making this approach more flexible
than model-based prediction modeling (Warner & Misra, 1996). Furthermore, neural

Braun et al. Large-scale Assessments in Education

networks are generally more robust to the distributions of errors, outliers, missing data,
and multicollinearity (Hassoun, 1995; DeTienne et al., 2003; Gupta & Lam, 1996, Principle et al., 1999).
Supervised learning and feedforward neural networks

Supervised learning is a machine learning paradigm in which a model is trained on a
labeled dataset; that is, each input is paired with a corresponding output. The objective
is for the model to ‘learn’ a mapping from inputs to outputs that generalizes well to new,
yet unseen data. Training involves minimizing a loss function that quantifies the difference between predicted and actual outputs. This process is typically carried out through
iterative optimization techniques such as gradient descent.
A common architecture used in supervised learning is the feedforward neural network, also known as a deep feedforward network or multilayer perceptron (MLP).
It is one of the simplest types of neural networks. In such networks, data flows in one
direction—from input nodes, through one or more hidden layers, and finally to output
nodes—without any cycles or feedback connections. Each node in one layer is fully connected to nodes in the next layer, but no connections exist between nodes within the
same layer.
To make such networks capable of learning complex relationships, non-linear activation functions are applied at each node. These activation functions govern the transfer of
data between nodes.3 Without non-linearities, the entire network, regardless of depth,
would be equivalent to a linear transformation of the input. Popular activation functions include the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU), defined as ReLU(x) = max (x, 0), and the
sigmoid function, defined as Sigmoid(x) = 1/(1 + exp (−x)). These functions allow the
network to learn non-linear decision boundaries and to approximate a broad class of
functions. In fact, with sufficient capacity and data, a feedforward neural network with
non-linear activations can approximate any Borel measurable function (Goodfellow
et al., 2016, p. 192).4
To prevent overfitting—a situation where a model performs well on training data but
poorly on new data—strategies like cross-validation are often employed. Cross-validation partitions the data into subsets to ensure that the model’s performance is tested on
unseen examples during training, thus promoting better generalizability.
The general function approximation theory

The foundation of supervised learning lies in the assumption that there exists a true but
unknown mapping function—often called the target function—that links inputs to outputs. The goal of machine learning is to approximate this function as closely as possible
using the available data. Supervised learning methods aim to find a function within a
hypothesis space (a well-defined set of possible models that is determined by the network architecture and implementation) that best approximates the target function. This

is similar in spirit to methods like non-parametric regression, where flexibility is retained

A neural network successively approximates the target function by minimizing an
aggregate loss that measures the overall discrepancy between predicted and true outputs. This iterative process, typically performed using backpropagation and gradient
descent, enables the network to successively adjust its internal weights and biases to better align with the target function.
The universal approximation theorem

In theory, a feedforward network with enough hidden layers is a universal approximator
that can be used to approximate any function (Hornik et al., 1989).
“The universal approximation theorem states that a feedforward network with a linear output layer and at least one hidden layer with any “squashing” activation function (such as the logistic sigmoid activation function) can approximate any Borel
measurable function from one finite-dimensional space to another with any desired
non-zero amount of error, provided that the network is given enough hidden units”
(Goodfellow et al., 2016, p.192).
Subsequent universal approximation theorems have been proven for a wider class of
activation functions, including the now commonly used rectified linear unit (ReLU)
(Leshno et al., 1993). Based on these studies, we decided to employ a feedforward neural
network model.
Batch processing and epochs in data analysis

Processing very large datasets poses challenges in terms of both memory usage and computational efficiency. To address these challenges, it is customary to subdivide the dataset into smaller batches, each containing a fixed number of records. The model processes
one batch at a time, computes the loss and updates the network weights accordingly (via
backpropagation). Once all batches have been processed, one epoch is complete—that
is, the model has made a single pass through the entire dataset. For example, with a
batch size of 128 and a dataset of 256,000 records, one epoch for the training data would
comprise 2000 batches.
Typically, a single epoch is insufficient for the model to converge to a satisfactory
solution. Therefore, the training process is repeated over multiple epochs, allowing the
model to iteratively refine its approximation of the target function. In the present implementation, training was continued for as many epochs as necessary until a pre-determined plateau in loss function reduction was reached. This stopping criterion helps to
prevent overfitting and ensures efficient use of computational resources.

with the following architecture:
