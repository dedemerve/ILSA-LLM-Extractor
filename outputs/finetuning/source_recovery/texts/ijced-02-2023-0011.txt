Abstract
Purpose – Given the scientific and practical difficulties inherent in measuring and comparing socioeconomic
deprivation (SED), and the further complexity added in cross national measurements, the main aim of
this paper was to check the validity of SED measures used in PISA 2018 dataset. The SED measure used
in PISA 2018 was the PISA index of economic, social and cultural status abbreviated as the ESCS index.
This assessment was important as PISA analysis is based on variables derived from this instrument and
the ESCS index and these reports influence and reflect international and comparative education policies
and practice.
Design/methodology/approach – This study critically evaluates the socioeconomic status measures in the
PISA 2018 dataset, focusing on their convergent validity and cross-national comparability. Using responses
from over 600,000 students in 73 countries, it examines the validity of SES indicators and their comparability
across countries. The study employs principal component analysis to construct local SES measures and
compares them with the existing Economic, Social, and Cultural Status (ESCS) index. It explores the
relationship between these SES measures and academic achievement in reading, science, and mathematics,
aiming to understand their predictive validity in diverse educational settings. Statistical analyses were
conducted using the IEA’s IDB Analyser and SPSS, ensuring robustness and generalisability across the
diverse participant countries.
Findings – Our research findings challenge the assumed superiority of local measures over broader constructs
like the Economic, Social, and Cultural Status (ESCS). It suggests that standardised measures like ESCS may
provide more reliable predictions of academic achievement across various educational contexts, underscoring
the complex relationship between SES measures and academic performance.
Originality/value – Our novel analysis shows that local and cross-national SED measures are poorly
correlated. Our findings raise questions about the measures’ validity while acknowledging the methodological
challenges. We provide empirical evidence to support ongoing debates on the topic.

1. PISA
The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), a worldwide study by the
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), stands as a pivotal
instrument in the realm of global educational evaluation. Since its inaugural administration in
the year 2000, PISA has evolved into a triennial international assessment, uniquely dedicated
to measuring the literacy in reading, mathematics, and science of 15-year-old students. The
essence of PISA transcends mere academic assessment; it endeavours to provide a
comprehensive and comparative insight into the educational systems of participating
countries, thereby enabling policymakers to make informed decisions aimed at enhancing
educational quality within their respective nations.
PISA’s primary objective is to furnish comparable data that aids educational stakeholders
in both improving domestic educational standards and evaluating the relative performance
of their students against international counterparts. This comparative analysis is essential,
as it allows for a global perspective on educational effectiveness, illuminating diverse

their knowledge of core subjects but also to assess their ability to apply this knowledge in
practical, real-world contexts. This approach underscores a shift from traditional rote
learning to an emphasis on critical thinking and problem-solving skills.
The methodology employed in PISA is noteworthy for its innovative approach to
question framing. The focus is not primarily on the information students have memorised,
but rather on their ability to utilise and apply their acquired knowledge effectively.
This methodology aligns with contemporary educational philosophies that prioritise
analytical skills and real-world applicability over mere factual recall. The dissemination of
PISA results often ignites comprehensive discourse regarding the efficacy of national
education systems. These discussions are instrumental in shaping educational policies
and practices across the globe. They provide a platform for countries to reflect on their
educational strategies, benchmark their progress, and derive inspiration from the successes
of others.
PISA assessments engage a representative sample of 15-year-old students from various
countries, typically on a triennial basis. The most recent cycle conducted in 2022, initially
scheduled for 2021, experienced a postponement to 2022 due to the global disruptions caused
by the COVID-19 pandemic. Each PISA cycle places a concentrated focus on one of the three
core domains—reading, mathematics, and science—thus allowing for an in-depth
examination of each area over time. Beyond these central subjects, PISA also extends its
scope to encompass additional skills and competencies, such as problem-solving and
financial literacy, which are included in specific cycles. This expansion reflects an
acknowledgement of the evolving demands of modern society and the diverse skill sets
required for success within it.
Furthermore, PISA collects extensive background information through detailed
questionnaires. These questionnaires probe into various aspects such as the socioeconomic status of students, their learning habits, and their attitudes towards education.
This rich repository of data facilitates a more nuanced analysis, enabling researchers and
policymakers to unravel the complex web of factors influencing educational outcomes. Such
comprehensive data collection underscores PISA’s role not merely as an assessment tool but
as a critical resource for understanding the multifaceted nature of education and its impact on
student performance globally.

2. Literature review: validity and dimensionality of SES measures in PISA data
for 15-year-Olds
The exploration of socioeconomic status (SES) and its impact on educational achievement
remains a perennial focus in educational research. The rich PISA data serves as one of the
most extensive platforms for researchers exploring this relationship. However, questions
regarding the validity and dimensionality of the socioeconomic measures used in PISA to
assess 15-year-olds have drawn significant attention, often raising concerns about their
efficacy and applicability.
Rutkowski and Rutkowski (2013) for example highlight the challenges inherent in
applying a single, standard measure of SES across the diverse national contexts represented
in PISA. They contend that SES is a complex construct, influenced by the socio-cultural
dynamics of each participating country, thus necessitating more context-sensitive
approaches. Similarly, Pokropek et al. (2017) express concerns about the comparability of
SES indicators across countries, challenging the often-tacit assumption that these measures
operate uniformly across varied cultural and educational systems.
Traditional measures of SES, such as parental occupation and educational level, while
commonly used, are increasingly viewed as insufficient. Avvisati (2020) critically examines

these traditional SES measures utilised in PISA, arguing that they fail to comprehensively
capture the construct’s multi-dimensionality. Engzell (2019) further critiques the limitations
of commonly used indicators like the number of books in the home, suggesting that these
proxies inadequately capture the multifaceted nature of SES.
Numerous studies have delved into the predictive validity of SES measures in relation to
student achievement. Lee et al. (2019), alongside Liu et al. (2022), affirm a predictive
relationship between SES and academic performance. However, according to Harwell et al.
(2017), this association is not as robust as one might initially assume. Supporting this
perspective, Marks and O’Connell (2021) argue that the SES-achievement model in PISA is
fraught with inadequacies, necessitating a re-evaluation of how SES is conceptualised and
measured when explaining educational outcomes. Selvitopu and Kaya (2021) and Sirin (2005)
also contribute to this discourse through meta-analytic methods, examining the relationship
between SES and academic performance, thereby highlighting its complex and often nuanced
nature.
Broadening the discussion beyond traditional measures, studies such as those by Erola
et al. (2021) and Isunget et al. (2021) advocate for the incorporation of genetic variables into the
analysis. They propose that an understanding of social stratification and academic
achievement could be enriched by considering both genetic and environmental factors.
Similarly, Murray (2020) underscores the importance of considering biological variables
alongside SES to provide a more holistic understanding of academic achievement and social
stratification.
Rutkowski and Rutkowski (2018) underscore the importance of enhancing the
comparability of SES measures across nations while adapting them to local contexts to
increase their utility in policy formulation and implementation. This view is echoed by
Sandoval-Hernandez et al. (2019) and Pokropek et al. (2021), who stress the need for a multidimensional approach to measuring SES, implying that the search for a universally
applicable SES measure continues.
In conclusion, the literature unambiguously calls for more nuanced, context-sensitive, and
multi-dimensional approaches to measuring SES in PISA. While traditional measures have
some utility, they fall significantly short in capturing the intricate web of factors constituting
SES that influence academic achievement. These studies collectively emphasise the need for
enhancing the cross-country comparability of SES measures while aligning them with local
contexts. A deeper understanding of these intricacies will better equip educational
researchers and policymakers in interpreting PISA data, contributing to a more equitable
educational landscape globally.
In the literature, there is a notable effort to comprehensively understand each country’s
socioeconomic measures to accurately capture its socioeconomic position (Lynch and Kaplan,
2000). Researchers and scholars delve into various aspects of socioeconomic status (SES)
within different nations, aiming to develop a nuanced understanding of the social, cultural,
and financial capital that defines a country’s position within the global socioeconomic
landscape. By analysing factors such as income distribution, educational attainment (Broer
et al., 2019), wealth indicators, and access to resources like healthcare and sanitation
(Hosseinpoor et al., 2012), these studies seek to create a holistic view of how SES manifests in
diverse national contexts. This detailed examination allows for a more precise assessment of
the unique socioeconomic characteristics and disparities present within each country,
contributing to a deeper comprehension of the complexities surrounding socioeconomic
status on a global scale. To comprehend the multifaceted nature of socioeconomic impacts on
education, our analysis leverages the socioeconomic status index from PISA data, providing
robust evidence of how diverse aspects of socioeconomic backgrounds influence student
achievement globally.

3. The PISA index of economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS)
The Economic, Social, and Cultural Status (ESCS) construct within PISA, while not grounded
in an explicit theoretical foundation as per the PISA questionnaire frameworks, is
operationalised through an index described for facilitative analysis of family background’s
multifaceted impact on educational outcomes. This construct draws on broader literature,
embodying the gradient approach to socio-economic status, emphasizing hierarchical social
positioning relative to resource access, including wealth, power, and status, as detailed by
prominent researchers (Avvisati, 2020; Willms and Tramonte, 2015). Despite critiques
regarding its unidimensional application, ESCS’s practical utility in educational inequality
analysis persists, advocating for a composite measure approach to navigate the intertwined
nature of its components (Deaton, 2002; O’Connell, 2019). The OECD utilises the ESCS index
as a composite measure, comprising of three primary indicators: parental education
(PARED), highest parental occupation (HISEI), and a proprietary index of household
possessions, known as HOMEPOS (OECD, 2018). This multifaceted approach to constructing
the ESCS index aligns with the traditional conceptualization of socioeconomic status, which
commonly encompasses education, occupation, and income.
Figure 1 in the referenced document (OECD, 2020a, p. 39) offers a schematic representation
of the ESCS and its component indicators, elucidating their interrelationships and collective
relevance. The parental education indicator within PISA is derived from students’ responses,
which are used to create an index based on educational qualifications. These qualifications
span a broad spectrum, ranging from no formal education to post-graduate levels, and
are further categorised as primary education, lower secondary, vocational/prevocational
upper secondary, general upper secondary, non-tertiary postsecondary, vocational tertiary,
and theoretically oriented tertiary education. PISA develops three separate indices for this
dimension: one each for the mother and father’s education levels (PARED), and a third
representing the higher education level of either parent.
For the parental occupation dimension, student responses about their parents’
occupations are employed to code indicators such as the father’s and mother’s
occupational statuses, and the highest occupational status among the parents (HISEI).
This approach ensures a comprehensive understanding of the occupational dimension of a
family’s SES.

Given the absence of a direct income-based measure in PISA, household possessions are used
as a surrogate indicator of family wealth. In this regard, students provide information on
the availability of 16 household items, including three items specific to each country, viewed
as relevant markers of family wealth within the context of that country. Additionally,
the number of books and other possessions at home are reported. HOMEPOS, therefore, is a
composite index summarizing all reported household possessions and items.
For the calculation of the ESCS index in PISA 2018, equal weight was attributed to these
three standardised components: PARED, HISEI, and HOMEPOS. In instances where values
for any of these components were missing for certain students, imputation was carried out
using predicted values, supplemented with a random element based on regression analysis
involving the other two variables. In cases where data were missing for more than one of the
three variables, ESCS computation was not feasible, and a missing value was assigned for
that student’s ESCS.
After the imputation process, the three components were standardised across all OECD
countries and partner economies. This standardisation process, post-2015, incorporates equal
contributions from each country or economy, a shift from the previous practice where only
OECD countries were considered. The finalised ESCS variable underwent a transformation,
with a score of 0 denoting the average OECD student and 1 representing the standard
deviation across equally weighted OECD countries (OECD, 2020a, p. 40).
This robust and comprehensive approach to measuring socioeconomic status within PISA
underscores its significance in understanding the diverse socio-economic backgrounds of
students and their potential impact on educational outcomes. However, despite the rigour
aimed at developing the ESCS index several questions have been raised when comparing
socioeconomic deprivation (SED) across regions and countries with varying levels of
development. Consequently, the methodological and practical challenges encountered in
accomplishing this goal have been the topic of extensive scholarly discourse and debate over
a significant period.

4. Rationale for developing a local SES measure
The development of the local-SES measure within the PISA framework presents a compelling
case for advancing our understanding of educational outcomes on a more nuanced, localised
level. The theoretical foundation for incorporating a local-SES measure into PISA is grounded
in the sociology of education, which posits that socioeconomic factors significantly influence
educational access, quality, and outcomes. Bourdieu’s theory of social and cultural capital
highlights how individuals’ backgrounds and the resources available to them can affect their
educational attainment and achievement. Similarly, Coleman’s work on social capital in
education underscores the importance of family and community contexts in shaping
students’ academic performance. By integrating a local-SES measure, PISA acknowledges
the complex, layered impact of socioeconomic factors that operate not just at the individual or
national level, but within localised contexts, providing a richer, more detailed picture of the
educational landscape.
Conceptually, the local-SES measure enhances the PISA framework by recognising
the variability of socioeconomic contexts within countries. It moves beyond traditional,
broad-brush indicators of SES, which often fail to capture the micro-level disparities that
exist within regions, cities, and even neighbourhoods. This measure allows for a more

differentiated analysis of educational outcomes, considering the specific socioeconomic
characteristics that define smaller, localized environments. Conceptually, this aligns with the
growing emphasis on place-based education research, which argues that understanding the
specific context of educational settings is critical for effective policy and practice.
Empirically, the justification for a local-SES measure is supported by numerous studies
demonstrating the significant role of localised socioeconomic factors in shaping educational
success. Research has shown that outcomes such as test scores, graduation rates, and
college attendance rates can vary dramatically within countries based on local economic
conditions, the educational level of the community, and access to educational resources. By
incorporating a local-SES measure, PISA can provide data that is more sensitive to these
variations, offering insights that are more actionable for policymakers and educators at the
regional and local levels. This measure can help identify “pockets of need” where targeted
interventions could be particularly impactful, as well as areas of strength from which best
practices can be drawn.
Furthermore, empirical evidence suggests that local-SES measures can enhance the
predictive validity of educational outcome models. By accounting for the specific
socioeconomic context in which students learn, researchers and policymakers can better
understand the mechanisms through which SES influences educational attainment and
identify more effective strategies for intervention. The development of a local-SES measure
within the PISA framework represents a significant step forward in the quest to understand
and improve educational outcomes globally. Theoretically, it acknowledges the complex
interplay of social and cultural capital in education; conceptually, it recognizes the importance
of localized contexts; and empirically, it promises to enhance our understanding of the
socioeconomic dynamics at play within educational settings. As such, the local-SES measure
can provide a more nuanced, effective tool for diagnosing educational challenges and crafting
targeted policies that address the specific needs of communities around the world.

5. Methods
5.1 Study objectives and research questions
This study undertakes a critical examination of socioeconomic status (SES) measures used in
the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2018 dataset, with a particular
focus on their convergent validity. The research is guided by two main questions.
RQ1. How valid are the socioeconomic measures used in the PISA data?
RQ2. What is the relationship between these two socioeconomic status indices ESCS and
SES_Local and how do they relate to student academic achievement in reading,
science and maths?
5.2 Data source and sample
Utilising the PISA 2018 dataset, the latest available at the time of writing (OECD, 2019a, b, c),
this analysis incorporates responses from 15-year-olds in grades seven and above across
82 countries and partner economies. The dataset, collected by the OECD, represents
approximately 32 million 15-year-olds globally and includes assessments of students’
mathematics, science, and reading literacy, with a particular emphasis on reading proficiency
(OECD, 2019a, b, c).
5.3 Background information collection
In addition to literacy assessments, PISA 2018 gathered extensive background information
through questionnaires targeting students, their parents, teachers, and school principals
(OECD, 2019a, b, c). These questionnaires provided insights into home backgrounds, learning

5.4 Sampling methodology
The PISA survey employed a two-stage stratified sampling strategy. The first stage involved
sampling schools based on the probability proportional to size (PPS) method, factoring in the
number of eligible students. The second stage entailed randomly selecting a sample of
students from each school. The overall participation in PISA 2018 involved over 600,000
students worldwide (OECD, 2019a, b, c) representing approximately 32 million 15-year-olds
in schools in 82 participating countries and economies who participated in the 2-h PISA test
in 2018.
5.5 SES measure construction
We utilised the existing ESCS index provided within the PISA 2018 dataset. This variable
was not directly observed but was inferred from other observed variables (ESCS 5
HISEI þ PAREDINT þ HOMEPOS). To construct a localised measure of SES we applied
principal component analysis (PCA) to three country-specific household items from the PISA
questionnaire, indicative of socioeconomic status (Appendix). The process entailed selecting
relevant categorical variables, transforming them into dummy variables, standardising the
data to ensure that each categorical indicator contributed equally to the analysis (scaling),
and then conducting PCA (Jolliffe and Cadima, 2016). The resulting principal component
scores represented the newly derived SES_LOCAL.

6. Results
Table 1 summarises the number of participants from each country, the minimum and
maximum scores, and the mean and standard deviation of each country’s ESCS and
SES_LOCAL indices. The maximum score of ESCS was for Georgia (Max 5 4.2051),
and the minimum score of ESCS was for Latvia (Min 5 7.7516). The maximum score of
SES_LOCAL was for Denmark (Max 5 3.7562), and the minimum score of SES_LOCAL was
for Belarus (Min 5 12.6255) (See Figure 2a and b).
The correlation coefficient examined within each country shows the relationship between
ESCS and SES_LOCAL. A country-wise summary of the correlation coefficients is in Table 2.
The correlation coefficients did not follow a similar pattern. For certain countries, the values
ranged from moderate to extremely low, and for relatively fewer countries, there was a strong
correlation. The highest correlation coefficients were for Peru (r 5 0.68), Indonesia (r 5 0.64),
and Panama and Indonesia (r 5 0.63). The lowest correlation coefficients were for Romania
(r 5 0.10), Ireland (r 5 0.17), Luxembourg (r 5 0.19), Malaysia (r 5 0.21), Finland and Latvia
(r 5 0.22). Table 2 shows the correlation coefficients of Latin American countries (yellow
colour in Table 2) and Asian countries (green colour in Table 2) were relatively higher. For
many European countries (blue in Table 2), the correlation coefficients were relatively lower
than in other countries (see also Figure 3).
PCA involved creating new variables; one was derived from country-level questions in
the survey, while the other was derived from survey questions at the local level. We expect
if these local indicators truly reflect each country’s national (local) indicators, then the
correlation between the SES_LOCAL and ESCS (SES) index should be high. On the contrary
we discovered a random pattern with countries showing strong, medium, or weak
correlations. A weak correlation coefficient means that these local and national items did
not accurately reflect the local indicators of each country.
The regression analysis aimed to delve deeper into the predictive validity of SES measures
on academic achievement, specifically in the domains of reading, science, and mathematics.
Contrary to initial expectations of Local_SES demonstrating a stronger association with
academic outcomes due to its contextual nature, the findings revealed that ESCS consistently
outperformed Local_SES in predicting achievement across the studied countries.
Additionally, the analysis revealed a common trend across several countries where the
local SES coefficient consistently fell within the narrow range of 0–0.2, indicating a limited
variation in the influence of local socioeconomic factors on academic achievement. This
finding highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the nuanced interplay between
local SES and educational outcomes and suggests that the impact of local SES might be
more constrained than previously assumed in these contexts. In fact, in 11 countries,
Local_SES exhibited a negative predictive effect on academic success. This finding prompts
a revaluation of the assumed superiority of local measures over broad, internationally
recognized constructs. The study underscores the nuanced relationship between SES
measures and academic achievement, suggesting that broader, standardized measures
such as ESCS may offer more robust predictive utility across diverse educational landscapes
(see Figures 4 and 5 below) also see Tables 3 and 4.
7. Discussion and conclusion
The findings of this study call for a critical reassessment of the prevailing assumption
regarding the dominance of localized SES measures in predicting academic performance.

This research has illuminated a complex and nuanced relationship between SES indicators
and academic achievement, underscoring the superior predictive consistency of broader
measures like the ESCS in diverse educational landscapes. While localized SES measures
provide valuable insights, their influence appears more limited than previously believed,
particularly in comparison with standardized, international metrics. Consequently, this study
advocates for a multifaceted approach to SES measurement, essential for a comprehensive
understanding and prediction of academic outcomes in varied educational contexts.
Addressing the scientific and practical challenges of measuring and comparing
Socioeconomic deprivation (SED), this paper primarily aimed to examine the convergent
validity of SED measures utilised in PISA. Given that both local SES measures and ESCS
are intended to measure SES, a strong correlation was anticipated. However, our analysis
reveals a weak correlation between local and cross-national SED measures. There has
been a lack of official guidance explaining this poor correlation, especially why it is
weaker in OECD/richer countries. Our findings suggest that meaningful conclusions
from global SED measures like ESCS, or local measures, may be limited due to their
idiosyncratic nature. This conclusion aligns with issues documented in previous studies
(Eryilmaz et al., 2020; Lee, 2019; Rutkowski and Rutkowski, 2013; Sandoval-Hernandez
et al., 2019). A single, simpler measure like the International Socio-Economic Index of
Occupational Status (ISEI) might be more applicable in a cross-national context. Future
research should explore the potential of ISEI for SED analysis, in contrast to other OECD
education measures. Additionally, the “Comparative Analysis of Social Mobility in
Industrial Nations” (CASMIN) scale, developed in the 1970s within the social stratification
framework (Brauns et al., 2003), merits consideration for capturing educational systems’
effects on social mobility patterns.
The PISA metric for assessing disadvantages combines the ESCS index with locally
specific household items for each country. Our analysis, however, raises concerns about the
validity of this metric, particularly its convergent validity. Additionally, the reliance on selfreported measures provided by 15-year-olds in the ESCS index raises questions about their
awareness and consistency in reporting parental education, occupation, and home
possessions across various cultures.
Additional information on the composition of the HOMEPOS report (OECD, 2018; see
Appendix) indicates that this list predominantly consists of single-item belongings denoting
high social class in each participating country. The criteria for determining high social class,
whether cumulative or based on access to the “highest status” item, remain unclear. For
instance, in Greece, ownership of specific items like a dishwasher and garage indicates an
advantage, while in Thailand and Switzerland, different items serve as equivalents. In Saudi
Arabia, owning Islamic books is considered a “high-status” item, a comparator that may not
exist in other countries’ questionnaires. The variability and potential issues with these
country-specific and local measures, as well as the self-reported nature of the responses, bring
into question the validity of these measures as SES indicators. Our findings, while
highlighting these issues, also acknowledge the inherent methodological challenges in such
measurements.
In conclusion, our study contributes significantly to the ongoing discourse on
socioeconomic constructs. These findings provide a foundation for further exploration,
refinement of measurement strategies, and continued discussion on socioeconomic
constructs within the field.
