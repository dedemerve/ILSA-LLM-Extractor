Abstract
Few of previous reading studies comprehensively examined the contributing factors of
students’ digital reading literacy. To ﬁll this gap, based upon the ecological perspective,
this study aims to investigate which factors from the student, home, and school context
are more important in discriminating high-performing digital readers from non–highperforming digital readers. The data of the Progress in International Reading Literacy
Study 2016 with 74,692 Grade 4 students from 14 countries and economies was
analyzed using the machine learning approach of support vector machine with recursive feature elimination. Results showed that except print reading levels, students’
reading self-efﬁcacy, home resources for learning, talking about what have read in class,
and the number of books in the home are the most inﬂuential contextual factors
contributing to the high performance of digital readers. The selected 20 key contextual
factors render a high prediction power for discriminating digital readers. Our ﬁndings
show that, in general, home-related factors have overarching inﬂuences on children’s
digital reading development; at the school level, instruction-related features are more
inﬂuential than school characteristics.

Introduction
Reading literacy in students’ ﬁrst primary school years is critical to students’ later
academic success (Cooper et al., 2014). In a digital age with increasing developments in
information and communication technologies (ICTs), the concept of reading literacy
should be reconstructed because one’s reading behaviors include both print reading and
digital reading (Coiro, 2003; Leu & Kinzer, 2000). Thanks to the affordable and
convenient access to ICTs, digital reading has been increasingly popular for students to
acquire information nowadays (Daniel & Woody, 2013; Leu et al., 2013; Murnane
et al., 2012; Parkes et al., 2015), which calls for more research speciﬁc to digital reading
to better examine its unique contextual features.
Contextual factors of reading literacy have been widely examined in the past several
decades. However, reading-related contextual factors were mostly investigated in terms
of print reading (e.g., Hawes & Plourde, 2005; Petscher, 2010; Taylor et al., 2003), and
relatively fewer studies examined those speciﬁc to digital reading. In addition, the
existing research often examined digital reading using a relatively small sample without
accounting for a large number of contextual factors (e.g., Coiro, 2011; Gil-Flores et al.,
2012; Hahnel et al., 2016; Lim & Jung, 2019). It is desired that digital reading-related
factors be examined using a broader sample within a holistic contextual framework.
Fortunately, given the new reading requirements in the digital age, some international
large-scale assessment programs, for example, the Progress in International Reading
Literacy Study (PIRLS) and the Programme for International Student Assessment
(PISA), expanded their reading framework by including digital reading. These programs aim to provide not only students’ reading performance but also diagnostic
information of contextual factors contributing to reading. However, very few, if at all,
studies accounted for a wide coverage of digital reading-related contextual factors at
student, school, and home levels. Moreover, it is still under-investigated that what
factors affect digital reading most. Given the gaps in previous research, this study aims
to identify the most inﬂuential contextual factors discriminating high-performing
digital readers from non–high-performing digital readers using the data of PIRLS
2016 with a machine learning approach—support vector machine (SVM) with recursive feature elimination (RFE).

Digital Reading and Print Reading
Although digital reading is a new concept without an explicit deﬁnition, it differs from
print reading in regard to a wide range of characteristics (Ackerman & Goldsmith,
2011; Ackerman & Lauterman, 2012; Eshet-Alkalai & Geri, 2007; Lee & Wu, 2012).

The major difference between digital and print reading is that digital reading involves a
much more complex reading context than print reading (Leu et al., 2013). For example,
the presentation of text differs greatly between the digital and the printed settings.
Digital text is more likely to be presented in a sparing, multimodal, interactive, and
experiential way. For example, dynamic elements such as video and audio clips and
pop-up windows can be incorporated in digital reading to visually attract readers’
interest, which is however infeasible for print reading. Another difference between the
two is the non-linearity of digital reading (Coiro et al., 2011). Contrast to printed text
which is typically read in a linear and sequential fashion, digital text is often distributed
across multiple pages and readers are required to create their own paths to reconﬁgure
and search through a network of multiple text, which imposes cognitive load on readers
(DeStefano & LeFevre, 2007). In addition, due to the complexity of text presentation in
digital reading, readers need to be able to maintain their focus on locating the targeted
information through multiple web pages without being distracted by irrelevant or other
interesting information, which requires their self-regulatory strategies and additional
comprehension of the usefulness of unseen text during digital reading (Mullis &
Martin, 2015). Moreover, online information is of a big volume and there are often no
human ﬁlters to control the quality of digital text. Readers therefore need to be able to
evaluate the credibility and suitability of text in digital reading (Anne Britt & Gabrys,
2001; OECD, 2015; Strømsø et al., 2008), which calls for their critical thinking skills.
Furthermore, it was believed that readers tend to have a feeling of disorientation and
show more difﬁculties in navigating through digital text because no physical text exists
in digital reading (Armitage et al., 2004; Cangoz & Altun, 2012). Regarding reading
habits, it was reported that digital text is often read in a slower pace than printed text
(Altonen et al., 2011; Daniel & Woody, 2013). In summary, due to the complexity of
digital reading contexts, readers might demonstrate different reading behaviors and are
expected to use different strategies to locate and comprehend information in digital
settings. As a result, previous ﬁndings on how contextual factors shape reading might
not directly apply to digital reading settings and it is worthwhile to investigate the
contextual effects speciﬁc to digital reading.

Theoretical Perspective
Inspired by some pioneering studies examining reading-related contextual factors
through an ecological lens (e.g., Martin & Lazendic, 2018), this study adopted the
ecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner, 1992) as a theoretical perspective. The
theory stresses that students’ learning outcomes are a function of multiple contextual
factors, and a holistic model incorporating many contextual factors is needed to account
for learning outcomes. More speciﬁcally, human development is affected by different
types of environmental systems. Each system has different layers and diverse processes, which affect human development outcomes. Regarding educational outcomes,
students’ competences are inﬂuenced by their maturing biology, which closely interacts
with their immediate family and school environments. In the ecological systems model,

there are ﬁve subsystems and we particularly used the ﬁrst two systems closest to
students, microsystem and mesosystem, as the theoretical guidance for our study. The
microsystem, the most proximal layer to students, involves factors such as students’
and their caregivers’ biological or innate characteristics, family- and school-related
factors, and their peers as well as the interplay between these factors. For example,
students’ academic outcomes might be affected by their innate attributes such as
academic self-efﬁcacy (Martin et al., 2012), which might be also affected by other
student attributes and family factors (Caprara et al., 2011; Weiser & Riggio, 2010). This
indicates that students’ academic outcomes are shaped by the interactions between their
innate attributes and the immediate environmental factors. The mesosystem is a larger
system which consists of all the interconnections between different parts of students’
microsystems. For example, how students’ families and schools interact with each other
also plays a role in affecting their academic outcomes. As such, in terms of the
ecological systems perspective, students’ innate attributes, family related, and schoolrelated factors need to be analyzed as a whole to identify how they contribute to
educational outcomes. The ecological perspective was adopted by a large body of
studies examining how contextual factors affect educational outcomes (e.g., Dotterer &
Lowe, 2011; Martin et al., 2012; Martin & Lazendic, 2018).
In addition, our study was also theoretically guided by the Pirls 2016 context
assessment framework (Mullis & Martin, 2015). In the framework, students’ reading
literacy was conceptualized as being shaped by contextual factors mainly from student,
home, and school contexts, which aligns the ecological perspective. Concretely, the
student context mainly contains students’ reading dispositions and behaviors, such as
reading motivation and reading self-concept. The home context involves factors related
to home resources, parents’ expectations and support and students’ early literacy
activities. The school context consists of factors related to school characteristics,
teachers, principals, and school climate. In our study, we posit that all the factors within
the aforementioned three contexts might affect students’digital reading literacy (see
Figure 1), so we can identify the most inﬂuential ones from these factors.

Inﬂuential Factors of Digital Reading
In this section, the inﬂuential factors of digital reading discussed in previous studies are
brieﬂy reviewed. We introduce the inﬂuential contextual factors of general reading
followed by how they differ between print and digital reading if applicable.

Student Context
According to the Pirls 2016 context assessment framework, factors within the student
context are student characteristics and attitudes toward learning (e.g., student motivation and conﬁdence). One of the most prominent student dispositions affecting
reading is reading self-efﬁcacy, which refers to the extent to which a reader believes that
he or she would have good performance on a reading task (Schiefele et al., 2012). In

previous studies, it was consistently found that lower reading self-efﬁcacy was associated with lower reading achievement (Chapman et al., 2000; Lee & Jonson-Reid,
2016; Susperreguy et al., 2018). In addition to reading self-efﬁcacy, reading motivation
and self-concept are also essential to students’ reading success (Chapman & Tunmer,
1997). It was believed that students’ intrinsic motivation is more helpful than extrinsic
motivation for their reading success (Becker et al., 2010). These reading dispositions
were also found to be effective for digital reading (Coiro, 2011; Popham, 2009).
Speciﬁc to digital reading, inﬂuential dispositions also include students’ attitudes
toward ICT (Lee & Wu, 2012). For example, it was found that higher levels of ICT or
Internet self-efﬁcacy, better attitudes toward computers and stronger conﬁdence in
high-level ICT tasks are associated with students’ higher digital competence or digital
reading performance (Hatlevik et al., 2015; Lee & Wu, 2012; Tsai & Tsai, 2003), and
negative attitudes toward ICT are associated with lower digital reading proﬁciency
(Naumann & Sälzer, 2017).
Students’ readiness to learn, which refers to students’ physiological readiness and
prerequisite knowledge to engage in the content, is another crucial student context
factor affecting reading suggested by the Pirls 2016 assessment framework. It was
found that nutritional problems seriously affected children’s ability to learn and sleep
deprivation was associated with lower achievement (Dewald et al., 2010; Taras, 2005).

However, it should be noted that despite being student context factors, these factors
might overlap students’ home context factors such as students’ family socioeconomic
status (SES). For example, students from lower SES families tend to have more food
and sleeping problems (Buckhalt, 2011). These factors therefore should not be investigated and interpreted separately from factors within other contextual levels.
Furthermore, students’ reading literacy behaviors were also found to be inﬂuential
predictors of reading performance. It was found that how often students read and the
degree to which students read for fun contributed to students’ reading ability (De
Naeghel et al., 2012; Mol & Bus, 2011). The relationships between reading dispositions
and reading literacy behaviors are reciprocal. That is, students’ positive reading attitudes might promote their positive reading behaviors, which in turn consolidate
reading dispositions (Mullis & Martin, 2015). In terms of digital reading, students’
reading literacy behaviors also include their online reading activities such as reading
email, chatting online, reading news, and using dictionary. Lee and Wu (2012) found
that students’ online reading activities positively predicted their digital reading literacy,
which served as mediators in explaining the gender difference in digital reading literacy. However, online reading behaviors might be correlated with their family and
school resources and access to ICT. It is thus possible that students with easier access to
ICT read online more often.
Another student context factor examined by most psychoeducational studies is
gender. Their ﬁndings consistently showed that girls showed higher reading performance than boys (Brozo et al., 2014; Lynn & Mikk, 2009). A study analyzing 10-year
PISA data across different countries also suggested that girls scored higher than boys in
reading in all participating countries, and the gender difference in reading was three
times larger than that in mathematics (Stoet & Geary, 2013). Girls’ advantages of
reading might be due to that girls have stronger involvement in language-related
activities than boys (Lynn & Mikk, 2009). Speciﬁc to digital reading, it was found that
girls prefer print reading more than boys, whereas boys are more satisﬁed with digital
reading than girls (Liu & Huang, 2008). However, girls still demonstrate advantages in
digital reading performance over boys, which can be attributable to their online reading
activities and ICT use at home for leisure (Cheung et al., 2013). Nevertheless, girls’
advantage in digital reading is smaller than that in print reading, which might be due to
boys’ advantages in computer-related activities (e.g., video gaming; Borgonovi, 2016).

Home Context
Home context factors are another set of contributing factors of reading proﬁciency,
which can be mainly categorized as family characteristics (e.g., parents’ education and
occupation levels, SES, language, immigration background), early literacy activities
and parents’ attitudes toward reading. PIRLS uses home resources for learning as an
indicator of students’ family SES. Home resources for learning were revealed as a
strong factor affecting academic achievement in the literature (Sirin, 2005). A wide
coverage of home resources was found to be inﬂuential for students’ reading literacy

(e.g., number of books in the home [Park, 2008], access and use of ICT at home
[Chiong & Shuler, 2010; Lee et al., 2009], family income [Dahl & Lochner, 2012], and
home internet use [Jackson et al., 2006]). In the digital age, ICT use at home is perhaps
particularly helpful for digital reading literacy development and it was found to be a
mediator accounting for the effects of other contextual factors on digital reading
(Borgonovi, 2016; Cheung et al., 2013). For example, in their study analyzing PISA
data, Chiao and Chiu (2018) found that students using ICT for the informational
purpose and the social-networking purpose mediate the impact of family SES on
student achievement in reading. Regarding other family characteristics, it was found
that immigrant students and students who do not speak the test language were disadvantaged in reading (Howie et al., 2008; Meunier, 2011).
In addition, early literacy activities, such as exposure to print and shared storybook
reading by parents, were found to be another important contributing factor of reading
by numerous studies (e.g., Cunningham & Stanovich, 1997; Scarborough et al., 1991).
A study analyzing PIRLS data found that early literacy activities positively affected
reading achievement in almost all participating countries (Mullis & Prendergast, 2017).
Notably, its effects on reading is long-lasting even in students’ later life (Cunningham
& Stanovich, 1997; De Jong & Leseman, 2001), and the stable association between
early literacy activities and reading has also been established for digital reading (Notten
& Becker, 2017).
Finally, with regard to parents’ attitudes toward reading, it was found that parents
showing children love of reading helps shape children’s reading motivation and attitudes (Strommen & Mates, 2004). As a result, students might see reading as a form of
recreation and be more positive on reading (Baker et al., 1997).

School Context
School context factors are mainly related to school characteristics and school climate.
The former refers to physical conditions of schools (e.g., school location, SES, resources, number of teachers, enrollment size) and the latter refers to the dynamics in
teaching and learning and the school environment and atmosphere (e.g., instructional
engagement, disciplinary atmosphere, safety, school emphasis on academic success).
Among the factors related to school characteristics, school SES was uniformly found to
be an inﬂuential factor of reading by a plethora of studies (e.g., Cairns &
Areepattamannil, 2019; Ma & Klinger, 2000). With respect to school location, it
was found that students from schools in urban areas had higher academic achievement
than students from schools in rural areas as urban schools were more likely to provide
students with more resources and a more supportive environment (e.g., Lubienski &
Lubienski, 2006; Owoeye & Yara, 2011; Woods & Wolke, 2004). For school resources,
like home resources, ICT use at school is associated with student learning (Hu et al.,
2018). According to PISA 2009 results, a portion of digital reading achievement
variance might be explained by ICT-related variables such as ICT use at school (OECD,
2011). However, Lee and Wu (2012)’s study showed that school ICT availability

demonstrated no effect on both print reading and digital reading, which might be due to
that students’ online reading activities at school were constrained by the planned
instruction despite the abundant school ICT resources.
Regarding school climate, school emphasis on academic success and school discipline and safety are two prominent factors affecting students’ academic achievement.
It was found that school emphasis on academic success has positive effect on reading in
large-scale assessments (Martin et al., 2013), elementary schools (Goddard et al., 2000)
and high schools (Hoy et al., 2006), and a safe and disciplined school also facilitates
student learning (Reynolds et al., 2017; Maxwell et al., 2017). Another inﬂuential
school climate factor is student bullying, which was found to negatively affect students’
reading achievement (Konishi et al., 2010), physical and psychological health
(Kowalski & Limber, 2013), and their sense of school belonging (Glew et al., 2008). In
summary, the above ﬁndings speak to the importance of building a positive school
climate for students’ reading success.

Research Objective
This study aims to identify key factors contributing to digital reading with an international large-scale assessment dataset (i.e., PIRLS 2016). Our study differs from
previous research and contributes to the literature in the following ways. First, it is a
large-scale analysis grounded in multiple countries with a large sample size, which
improves the generalizability of the ﬁndings. Second, our study has a wide coverage of
contextual factors conceptualized at different levels (i.e., student context, home
context, and school context) and measured from different perspectives (i.e., students,
teachers, parents, and principals), which makes the identiﬁed key factors more
comprehensive and reliable. Finally, given the large sample size and high-dimensional
data, we use a machine learning approach with cross-validation to iteratively select the
key factors, which improves the validity of our ﬁndings.

Methods
Data
The data of PIRLS 2016 digital reading (ePIRLS; https://timssandpirls.bc.edu/) were
used in this study. PIRLS collects both the reading performance data from fourth-grade
students and the contextual survey data from students, parents, teachers, and school
principals. ePIRLS is an innovative computer-based assessment which uses a simulated
Internet environment to measure digital reading literacy and shares the same assessment
framework and quality assurance procedures with PIRLS. A total of 14 countries and
economies including Sweden, Chinese Taipei, United States, United Arab Emirates,
Canada, Denmark, Georgia, Ireland, Israel, Italy, Norway, Portugal, Singapore, and
Slovenia participated in both PIRLS and ePIRLS. Students from these countries and
economies constitute the sample used in our study, which involves 74,692 students

from 2875 schools. In PIRLS 2016, the ﬁnal reading achievement scores for digital
reading were scaled by different item response theory models (TIMSS & PIRLS
International Study Center, 2017). Having digital reading scores, PIRLS used cutoff
scores of 625, 550, 475, and 400 to develop four international benchmarks of advanced,
high, intermediate, and low digital reading levels. In our study, students with advanced
and high levels are considered as high-performing digital readers, and students with
intermediate and low levels are considered as non–high-performing digital readers,
which results in a dichotomous target. In the ﬁnal sample, there are 33,130 highperforming digital readers and 41,505 non–high-performing digital readers.

Contextual Features
It should be noted that because digital reading and print reading share the same
comprehension processes, print reading levels are used as a feature for predicting digital
reading. In this study, we used a total of 79 contextual features from the school, teacher,
home, and student questionnaires for predicting students’ reading levels. Some of these
features were their original measures, while some were derived features based on item
response modeling. It should be noted that the PIRLS datasets include a variety of
variables (e.g., reading achievement scores, demographic information, responses on the
original test and questionnaire items, and derived scale scores for latent constructs
based on a set of individual items). Given that most individual items were designed to
measure a higher-level construct, we used the derived scale scores instead of the
original item scores for those constructs measured by a set of individual items for
analysis. In addition, we only selected the factors considered important or potentially
useful in the literature. Readers who are interested in more details of these factors can
consult Mullis and Martin (2015).

Analytic Strategy
The data of PIRLS 2016 used in our study was obtained by merging several different
datasets responded by students, teachers, parents, and school principals, which is of
extreme complexity given the relationships between numerous variables. In addition,
the purpose of our study is to identify the key factors contributing to digital reading.
Unlike conventional multivariate statistical analysis (e.g., hierarchical linear modeling,
structural equation modeling), our study does not assume any strong preconceptions or
hypotheses. In this sense, instead of a multivariate statistical approach, a machine
learning approach, which is capable of generating insightful information and detecting
underlying patterns from big data, might be more appropriate for data analysis. More
speciﬁcally, we used a supervised learning method, SVM, for classifying students as
high- or non–high-performing digital readers. Supervised learning tries to infer a
function that maps an input to an output based on labeled training data with inputoutput pairs. For a supervised learning problem, the outcome category of each sample
of the training data is known before learning the model. In our case, students’ digital

1772

Journal of Educational Computing Research 60(7)

reading levels are their labels (i.e., output) and their contextual factors are features
associated with the label (i.e., input). It should be noted that there are numerous
supervised learning methods for classiﬁcation, and we selected SVM given that its
advantages ﬁt the characteristics of our dataset and our research purpose. Speciﬁcally,
in terms of the data characteristics, the data used in our study is high-dimensional with a
large sample size, and some variables in the dataset might be correlated with each other
(e.g., family SES is correlated with parents’ educational and occupational levels).
Compared with other supervised learning methods, SVM is particularly effective in
dealing with high-dimensional data. This is because SVM does not use all data points
but only uses some of them in the high-dimensional space to build a boundary for
classiﬁcation (further elaboration is given below). Therefore, for SVM, removing other
data points in the space does not change the decision boundary, which can be seen as an
automatic regularization for optimization. This property of SVM also makes it work
well when some features are correlated, which is often the case for high-dimensional
datasets. In addition, with respect to our research purpose, this study aims to identify the
key contextual factors contributing to top-performers in digital reading. We, therefore,
used SVM combined with RFE, a widely used feature selection algorithm, to identify
the contextual features that collectively affect students’ digital reading levels. By using
SVM with RFE, we were able to test the prediction accuracy of models with different
feature subsets with SVM as the underlying supervised learning algorithm. Moreover,
SVM is a generalizable and robust technique because it uses partial training data while
many of its competitors often use all the training data for learning. In other words, the
predictive model built by SVM is more likely to work well on unseen test datasets. This
is a desired feature for our study since the selected key factors can only be deemed valid
if they are also generalizable in other settings.

SVM with RFE
Support vector machine is a supervised machine learning algorithm, which is used to
learn the underlying rules of contextual factors associating with digital reading performance and predict new cases as either high- or non–high-performing digital readers
in our study. Technically, SVM learns the data by ﬁnding a hyperplane best dividing all
cases into two classes. In SVM, a hyperplane can be considered the best line or decision
boundary in the data space for classiﬁcation. The right hyperplane is decided by the
support vectors, which are the samples closest to the hyperplane. Concretely, given a
training dataset of n data points ðxi ,yi Þ, i ¼ 1; 2,…,n, where yi are either 1 or -1
(i.e., high- or non–high-performing readers) and xi are multidimensional vectors which
indicate contextual factors in our study, SVM tries to ﬁnd a hyperplane with maximum
margin which optimally separates observations xi with yi ¼ 1 from observations xi with
yi ¼ 1. As a result, the distance between the closest vectors from either class and the
hyperplane could be maximized (see Figure 2 for a graphic intuition). This hyperplane
can be represented by the equation: wx  b ¼ 0 where w indicates the normal vector to

the hyperplane and b indicates the bias. The prediction by SVM can be represented by

Support vector machine is the model for supervised learning which cannot be
directly used for feature selection. It was incorporated within the algorithm of RFE for
ranking the feature importance. Recursive feature elimination is a wrapper-type approach that wraps a machine learning algorithm as the core for feature selection. It
searches for a set of candidate feature subset sizes in a backward way with an iterative
process and determines the optimal feature subset size through the comparison in
prediction performance across models built with different feature subsets. Therefore, to
use SVM with RFE, a set of candidate feature subset sizes need to be pre-speciﬁed, and
then SVM is ﬁtted on the training data and evaluated on the test data with all features
included for learning. Meanwhile, all the features of the data are ranked by their relative
feature importance in differentiating high-performing digital readers from non–highperforming digital readers. Feature importance in SVM can be indicated by the weight
vector w. The squared value of individual weight of each feature is calculated as their
corresponding feature importance. Suppose there is a total of n features and for each
pre-speciﬁed feature subset size k ðk ≤ nÞ, SVM is ﬁtted on the training data and
evaluated on the test data again. However, for both training and test data, according to
the feature importance ranking from the model with all features, only the k most
important features are retained, and the other features are not used in the current round.
The prediction performance of the model with the k most important features can be
obtained. This procedure is iteratively conducted to build different models corresponding to different feature subset sizes. Having these models, the optimal feature
subset size can be determined by selecting the best-performing model (i.e., the model

Analytic Procedure
We ﬁrst randomly split the original dataset into the training dataset (80%) and the test
dataset (20%). In the datasets, many contextual factors have a large amount of missing
values given that the questions might not apply to the respondents or they failed to give
a response. As such, we applied the k-nearest neighbor imputation (kNN) to impute the
missing values of each contextual feature. In addition, we normalized the data by
standardizing all features with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. After missing
value imputation and data normalization, the training data was analyzed by the SVM
with RFE to identify the key features. For model evaluation, we used the ﬁve-fold
cross-validation to evaluate the model with the selected feature subset size. In our study,
we arbitrarily set a feature subset size of 20 for the purpose of demonstrating key
features. Concretely, the ﬁve-fold cross-validation ﬁrst randomly shufﬂed the data and
then split it into ﬁve folds. Each of the ﬁve folds was taken as the test dataset and the

Results
Prediction Performance of the Top 20 Features
The AUC score for the top 20 features identiﬁed by SVM with RFE was 89.00%,
indicating a high prediction performance. Table 1 presents the results of ﬁve-fold crossvalidation with the top 20 features for training the model. It can be seen that the SVM
algorithm with the top 20 features demonstrated a high prediction performance, with an
average AUC score of 90.01%, an average sensitivity of 80.65% and an average
speciﬁcity of 83.51%. These results validated the importance of the top 20 features for
predicting students’ digital reading literacy. Regarding the test performance, the results
showed that the top 20 features also demonstrated good performance on the test dataset,
with an AUC score of 90.30%, an accuracy score of 82.54%, a sensitivity score of
80.73%, and a speciﬁcity score of 83.98%.

Discussion
In this study, we stress that unlike the majority of studies on digital reading, the
theoretical reasoning of reading-related contextual effects in our study is mostly at the
three main contextual levels rather than individual variables, which makes it possible to
identify the key factors at each level and the relative importance of the three contexts to
digital reading. Therefore, the key ﬁndings are discussed with respect to each context
below. For a clear-cut presentation of the key features, we reorganized the selected
features by contexts in Table 3. It can be seen that there is only one factor—pre-primary
educational programs—does not lie into the three aforementioned contexts. In PIRLS
2016, the effect of pre-primary educational programs on reading literacy should be
discussed at the national and community level. Although our ﬁnding on this factor is
consistent with previous studies (e.g., Berlinski et al., 2009; Pholphirul, 2017), the
policies on pre-primary educational programs vary across countries, provinces, and
communities, which are beyond the scope of this study. We therefore focus on those
factors within the three main contexts in the discussion.

Home-Related Factors
For digital reading, home-related factors are predominant in characterizing highperforming digital readers given that nine out of the top 20 key factors, and seven
out of the top 10 key factors are home-related (see Table 3). Concretely, these features
can be categorized as three types: home resources supporting reading, parental involvement in children’s reading development and parents’ reading attitudes and habits.
They can be considered as key components of the dynamic process of academic
socialization in the home context. Speciﬁcally, parental inﬂuences on children’s academic development in the early years can be conceptualized as two perspectives:
“what parents do” and “who parents are” (Taylor et al., 2004). In the home context,
“what parents do” focuses on parental behaviors while “who parents are” emphasizes
parental traits in socializing children’s school-related development. Speciﬁc to the key
home-related factors identiﬁed in our study, parental involvement in children’s reading
development stands for “what parents do” and “who parents are” can be characterized
as home resources supporting reading and parents’ reading attitudes and habits. These
two aspects of parental impact on reading collectively contribute to children’s digital
reading development.
In terms of “who parents are,” family SES is an integrated measure of parents’
educational and occupational background and home resources. The measure of home
resources for learning was used as an indicator of family SES in PIRLS 2016 (Mullis &
Martin, 2015). Our ﬁnding on home resources for learning strongly aligns with the
well-established link of family SES to reading achievement (e.g., Park, 2008; Sirin,

devices were particularly inﬂuential for digital reading. This ﬁnding is not surprising
given that the association between the number of books at home and students’ academic
achievement was uniformly established in the literature (Wößmann, 2003). It should be
noted that the number of books at home was used as a proxy variable for family SES in
many studies (Park, 2008), and therefore its role in promoting digital reading can be
complicated. It is possible that given easy access to books at home, children tend to read

more often, or families with more books could provide their children with more
educational resources, which in turn promotes digital reading ability. Regarding the role
of digital devices at home, it might be due to that access to digital devices enables
children who cannot yet read printed text to construct meaning from digital text (Lynch
& Redpath, 2014), which contributes to their overall literacy development (Neumann &
Neumann, 2014). In addition, it was believed that children are able to gain “knowledge
in the operational, cultural, and critical dimensions of literacy” through a multiliteracy
way supported by mobile digital devices (Wong, 2015). Overall, we contend that the
effects of different variables on home resources should not be interpreted separately
because children’s literacy development beneﬁts from home resources in direct, indirect or integrated ways. Another important aspect of “who parents are” for shaping
children’s digital reading development is parents’ reading attitudes and habits. Consistent with previous studies (e.g., Strommen & Mates, 2004), our results conﬁrmed
that how parents think of reading and their own reading habits affected children’s digital
reading development. However, parents’ reading attitudes and habits might be shaped
by other factors such as their socioeconomic and cultural background and individual
experiences. These factors as a whole constitute “who parents are” and might indirectly
affect children’s digital reading development through “what parents do.” For example,
parents who like reading or consider reading important might be more likely to create a
more literate home environment where children have easier access to different literacy
activities.
Regarding the “what parents do” perspective, our ﬁndings highlighted the impact of
early literacy tasks, which highly require parental involvement and efforts. This ﬁnding
echoes the importance of early literacy activities at home for children’s academic
development revealed by a large body of previous studies (e.g., Mullis & Prendergast,
2017; Notten & Becker, 2017). Moreover, it was found that parents’ involvement in
children’s homework was a predictor of digital reading performance. Typically, it was
believed that there is a negative association between parents’ homework-related
practice and children’s academic performance development (Silinskas et al., 2013,
2015). In the context of our study, it is possible that parents would devote more time and
efforts in helping with their children’s homework when they perceived that their
children showed difﬁculties in reading or were not able to ﬁnish reading-related assignments autonomously. However, if true, parents’ home-related practices should not
be considered as a contributing factor to digital reading literacy development. Alternatively, they might be caused by the “evocative impact” of children’s reading
performance (Silinskas et al., 2013).
Conforming to the academic socialization model proposed by Taylor et al. (2004),
the key factors pertinent to “who parents are” and “what parents do” might affect digital
reading development through an intergenerational process, where parental traits and
home background features affect digital reading performance through the mediating
effects of parental behaviors. In this sense, we stress that parents from lower SES
families should take more remedial practices in helping their children’s digital reading
development to buffer the negative inﬂuences of socioeconomic disadvantages.

School-Related Factors
Six of the top 20 key features are school-related factors. Interestingly, most schoolrelated key factors are associated with classroom instruction. This implies that how
reading is instructed in school might be more important than the school socialpsychological environment or school characteristics. Concretely, compared with
other instruction-related factors, students talking about what they have read in reading
classes (ASBR02C) showed remarkably higher importance. Unsurprisingly, talking is
often perceived as the foundation of literacy and it was believed that “reading and
writing ﬂoat on a sea of talk” (Britton, 1983, p. 11). Asking students to talk about what
they have read can be seen as a teaching strategy for reading, and its effectiveness for
promoting students’ reading performance was also validated by some empirical studies.
For example, students were found to show improvements in reading-related tasks, class
engagement and post-test reading scores when they were taught with the think aloud
strategy by teachers (Sudiati et al., 2018). Compared with the talking-based teaching
strategy, the other three instruction-related factors are much less inﬂuential because of
their low rankings of feature importance. Speciﬁcally, the factor of classroom instruction limited by student attributes (ATBGSLI) characterizes the extent to which
teaching in reading classes is affected by students’ preparedness and readiness to learn,
such as students’ nutritional and sleeping problems, non-mastery skills and absence
from school. This factor, however, is associated with students’ family background
given that students’ preparedness and readiness to learn is largely supported by their
parents. As we discussed earlier, the home-related factors show overarching inﬂuences
on digital reading, which might have accounted for partly of its effect. Likewise, the
factor of instruction affected by reading resource shortages (ACBGRRS) might overlap
the feature of school characteristics—school SES by student background (ACDG03). It
is reasonable to posit that socioeconomically disadvantaged schools are more likely to
be handicapped by resource shortages, and a portion of the effect of ACBGRRS might
be accounted for by the school SES. Regarding the school instruction days
(ACBG07A), it is the least inﬂuential factor among the selected key features. While it is
commonly accepted that increased instructional time beneﬁts students’ academic
achievement, its effect varies across different subjects and contexts. For example, a
recent review on instructional time by Yeşil Dağlı (2019) pointed out that the effect of
an additional school day before testing is relatively small despite it contributing to a
higher average score in reading. In addition, a non-linear relationship exists between
instructional time and academic achievement, implying a ceiling effect of instructional
time (Yeşil Dağlı, 2019).

Student-Related Factors
Among the three student-related contextual factors (print reading levels are not
considered as a contextual factor in our study), students’ reading self-efﬁcacy
(ASBGSCR) was found to be the strongest predictor of high-performing digital

Chen et al.

1785

readers. This ﬁnding is consistent with a plenty of previous ﬁndings on the strong effect
of reading self-efﬁcacy on print reading performance (e.g., Chapman et al., 2000; Lee &
Jonson-Reid, 2016; Susperreguy, et al., 2018). Despite the explicit direct link of reading
self-efﬁcacy to print reading performance, the role of reading self-efﬁcacy affecting
digital reading literacy development deserves further investigation. It should be noted
that in PIRLS 2016, the measure of reading self-efﬁcacy applied to both digital reading
and print reading. However, it is still unclear that if digital reading and print reading
share the same motivational process. It is possible that reading self-efﬁcacy for digital
reading includes additional motivational components related to ICT use, and novel
measures on digital reading self-efﬁcacy are thus needed. In addition, according to
previous studies, home-related factors such as students’ family SES and early literacy
activities were found to be associated with students’ academic self-efﬁcacy (e.g., Kim,
2014; Schnulz, 2005; Sonnenschein & Munsterman, 2002), and students’ academic
self-efﬁcacy mediated the links of contextual factors such as classroom environment,
parental involvement and peer victimization to academic achievement (Fast et al.,
2010; Weiser & Riggio, 2010; Thijs & Verkuyten, 2008). Therefore, it is reasonable to
posit that the interplay of various contextual factors shapes students’ reading selfefﬁcacy, which in turn contributes to digital reading performance. In other words, we
contend that the role of reading self-efﬁcacy in affecting digital reading not be interpreted in isolation from the other contextual factors. With respect to the other two
student-related factors, their effects are less inﬂuential. As we discussed earlier, students’ nutritional problems (ASBG07B) and absence from school (ASBG06) are
closely related to their family background. Thus, the relatively low feature importance
of these features might be explained by the overarching effects of other contextual
factors.
Generally, the top 20 contextual factors for digital reading identiﬁed in our studies
demonstrated high prediction performance with a test accuracy score of 83% and a test
AUC score of 90%. By knowing a student’s proﬁle on these key features, we can
conﬁdently predict if he or she is able to perform a digital reading task well. In the
above, we discussed some key ﬁndings with respect to each context. Our ﬁndings also
call in to the question that which context is more important for children’s digital reading
literacy development. Obviously, the home context accepts most high-ranking factors,
implying its important role for children’s digital reading success. However, it should be
noted that except digital devices in the home (ASBGDDH), none of the key factors
identiﬁed in our study is ICT-related, which seems to contradict the previous ﬁnding
that ICT access and usage and ICT-related attitudes signiﬁcantly predict students’
digital reading performance (e.g., Borgonovi, 2016; Chiao & Chiu, 2018; Cheung et al.,
2013; Chiong & Shuler, 2010; Lee et al., 2009). It is worth mentioning that in previous
relevant studies, ICT-related variables were pre-conceptualized as inﬂuential predictors
and they were included in the statistical model by hypotheses. Although those studies
accounted for a set of covariates such as SES and print reading performance to control
for the confounding effects, we suspect that ICT-related predictors were signiﬁcant with
very small effect size. For example, in their study on digital reading-related contextual

1786

Journal of Educational Computing Research 60(7)

factors with hierarchical linear modeling, ICT-related variables along with SES, gender
and metacognition variables only accounted for 5% of the student-level digital reading
score variance as a whole. Informed by the literature, family SES or home resources for
learning typically has much larger effect size than the other contextual factors, which
indicates that the effects of ICT-related variables might be trivial. In this sense, our
study offers a distinct perspective to disentangle the most critical determinants from a
wide coverage of contextual factors.

Limitations
Inevitably, there are several limitations in our study which might be of implications for
future studies. First, although we used a large sample covering 14 countries and
economies in different continents, the PIRLS 2016 data, like all other international
large-scale assessment data, is cross-sectional, which is limited in establishing causal
relationships between contextual factors and digital reading. Some key factors identiﬁed in our study measured what children did prior to primary school. Future studies
are therefore suggested to validate the effectiveness of these features by tracking how
they affect digital reading over a period of time with longitudinal data. Second, in the
realm of data mining, there exist numerous powerful techniques and algorithms for
selecting key features for predictive analyses. Although we tried to articulate the
rationale of using SVM with RFE for the purpose of our study, how capable it is
compared with other data mining techniques and whether they deliver the same set of
key features are still worthy of further investigation. Third, limited by our research
purpose and the analytical approach, this study failed to identify how the key contextual
factors interacted with each other in contributing to digital reading development. As we
discussed, the indirect, hierarchical or even non-linear associations between the key
factors and digital reading should be examined in future studies. Unlike conventional
statistical approaches, machine learning approaches are often limited in interpretability.
Adapted or novel data science techniques are needed to examine interaction effects
between contextual factors of digital reading. This could improve the interpretability
and the generalizability of the ﬁndings of this study. Finally, it should be noted that
different assessment programs focus on different populations. Given our research
purpose and the scope of PIRLS 2016 data, our study was conducted in the context of
primary education. Therefore, our ﬁndings are limited to the stage of late childhood. We
encourage future studies to examine how the effects of the key features on digital
reading change across different stages of one’s life (e.g., childhood, adolescence,
adulthood).

Author Biographies
Fu Chen is an assistant professor of Data Science in Education in the Faculty of
Education at the University of Macau. His research interests include three distinct but
related areas: educational data mining, learning analytics, and data science applications
in computer-based assessments. The overarching theme of his research work is developing novel methodologies to understand and optimise student learning by analyzing both structured and unstructured learner data in a wide range of educational
settings.

Alfred Sakyi is a senior manager at the Research Branch, Alberta Education, Government of Alberta. He obtained his PhD in Measurement and Evaluation from the
University of Toronto and has been working in the Government of Alberta for more
than 24 years. His work focuses on using quantitative and data mining techniques to
investigate the contextual effects of student academic achievement for Alberta students.
Ying Cui is a professor in Educational Psychology in the Department of Educational
Psychology at the University of Alberta. Her research goals are to continuously support
rigorous quantitative methods in applied social and educational research, and to develop innovative quantitative methods to solve complex, real-world problems. Her
background and training are in statistics, educational measurement, and quantitative
theories and applications. Her research has focused primarily on investigating different
psychometric issues related to educational assessments through the analysis and interpretation of student test data, educational data mining of student process data to
extract useful information that helps better understand student problem solving processes and learning analytics for the early detection of students who are at risk of course
failure.
