A B S T R A C T  
This study aims to examine one of the key contextual constructs measured in ILSAs, socioeco­
nomic status, within a single national context. Using PISA data from Sweden, this study in­
vestigates whether the items used to indicate socioeconomic status can be used to create a 
construct of socioeconomic status that is applicable to multiple cycles of PISA data in Sweden. MI 
testing of the 2000 CFA solution across all PISA cycles demonstrates invariance at the metric 
level. Once the alignment optimization method was applied to the measurement model, the so­
lution fell within the thresholds for acceptable measurement non-invariance. Therefore, this study 
posits that items retained in the PISA questionnaire since 2000 can be used to tailor a model of 
SES for Sweden.   
1. Introduction 
International Large Scale Assessments (ILSAs) have increasingly come to dominate the educational landscape. ILSA results strongly 
influence political discourse and decisions about education. Further, the results of these international assessments are used by poli­
ticians to legitimise educational plans (N´ovoa & Yariv-Mashal, 2003). Due to the profound consequences for the development of 
educational policy, researchers utilizing data generated by international assessments should pay extra attention to the provenance of 
their measurement instruments. In the case of secondary analyses, the data collection protocol is designed and determined prior to the 
secondary analysis being carried out (Shadish, Cook, & Campbell, 2001). Consequently, it is imperative that the data we use in 
comparative analysis is well representative of cultural contexts that it claims to compare and explain. 
1.1. Socioeconomic status and educational outcomes 
For many decades, socioeconomic status (SES) has had well-documented explanatory power in regards to outcomes in the 
educational sciences (i.e. Coleman, 1966). Broadly describing the ranking of individuals and families on a hierarchy according to their 
control of various commodities such as wealth, power, or social status, SES has become one of the commonly used contextual variables 
in statistical analysis in the social and educational sciences (Mueller & Parcel, 1981; Sirin, 2005; White, 1982). The financial ability of 
higher socioeconomic status parents to support learning and early cognitive development in the home (Schulz, 2005) is key to the 
inevitable link between coming from an advantaged SES background and excellency in academic performance. Despite the ubiquity of 
this relationship, variation in the magnitude of the effect of student background on outcomes has been noted between countries 
(Freeman, Machin, & Viarengo, 2011; Hanushek, Woessmann, Schivardi, & Pistaferri, 2011) and also between deployed measures

The importance of accurate measures of SES in ILSAs goes beyond the use of such measures to conduct secondary analyses using 
international data. ILSAs use large batteries of test items and cannot logistically test all participants on every item. As such, data from 
student background questionnaires is used to create achievement estimates for various subpopulations (see Rutkowski & Rutkowski, 
2010). This makes an accurate measure of SES integral to the identification of the very phenomena we use it to explain. 
Approaches to capturing SES have varied over time. Sirin (2005), in his meta-analysis of studies of SES and academic achievement 
between 1990 and 2000, noted that SES has three traditional components, parental income, parental education, and parental occu­
pation, with a fourth component, home resources, being less commonly deployed. Counter to such a multi-component SES mea­
surement, Goldthorpe (1996) argues that such a blanket approach fails to take into account the advances and reforms in society and 
education, including widening participation in higher education and a corresponding shift of the social capital associated with varying 
tiers of education (Bourdieu, 1998). Accordingly, in considering the consistency of measurements of SES, one needs to account for 
changes to the social context studied. 
Sweden has witnessed substantial politically induced educational restructuring following the passing of several reforms to the 
School law in the mid-1990s. These reforms included increased marketization, relocation of educational resources and control, the 
growth in the state-financed independent school sector, and parental choice in school admissions. Contemporaneously to the 
implementation of wide-ranging reforms to the Swedish school system, Swedish society was undergoing significant demographic 
changes. In line with the nation’s social democratic tradition, Sweden welcomed over 600,000 refugees for resettlement between 1980 
and 2017 (Migrationsverket, 2018). This shift in the demographic composition is mirrored in the doubling of the proportion of school 
children eligible for language support (Skolverket, 2018a, 2018b). When these substantial changes to the Swedish educational context 
are considered in conjunction with findings from the international assessments, for example, that the effect size of individual and 
school socioeconomic background on science achievement in Sweden increased significantly between the 2006 and 2015 cycles of 
PISA (Skolverket, 2016), it is clear that thorough measurement and conceptualisation of SES in Sweden is essential and merits close 
attention. 
1.2. Measuring socioeconomic status in international assessments 
ILSAs such as PISA are prominent sources of educational performance and contextual data (Gustafsson & Ros´en, 2014). This data 
allows for the comparison of educational systems at single time points, and functions as a progress check or an indicator of a school 
systems’ health for regularly participating countries. Socioeconomic status is perhaps the most commonly included background 
variable collected in ILSAs, which is in line which its multi-functionality (White, 1982). As such, comparable and reliable measures of 
socioeconomic status are of vital importance for ILSAs (i.e. OECD, 2017b), and therefore the validation of the construct is of especial 
concern. 
The SES construct in ILSAs is derived from questionnaire items administered to participants. As noted in a previous review of the 
determinants of socioeconomic inequality in student performance in international comparative studies (Strietholt et al., 2019), the 
typical approach to measuring SES in secondary analyses of ILSA data is by using indices of SES provided by the organizing bodies of 
the ILSA. An alternate procedure is adopting one or more individual survey items, (e.g. using the number of books in the home as a 
proxy, or creating an index of SES or educational capital). Using ILSA-provided indices of SES in secondary analyses raises a concern in 
cross-cultural contexts, whereby items that indicate differential levels of wealth in one country are ubiquitous in another, and items 
considered luxuries in one are basic infrastructure in another. This issue has been criticised in the literature (e.g. Filmer & Pritchett, 
1999). Due to the intrinsic socioeconomic variation between nations, there is an inherent challenge in designing questionnaires to 
capture comparable data across multiple countries. Scales used to indicate student background should account for differing cultural 
contexts (Rutkowski & Rutkowski, 2010, 2013). 
1.2.1. PISA’s index of economic, social and cultural status 
The OECD operationalises socioeconomic status in the PISA studies through the computation of the index of economic, social, and 
cultural status (ESCS). ESCS is compiled through principle component analysis (PCA) of parental education, highest parental occu­
pation, and home possessions (OECD, 2017b). Of these indicators, parental education can be considered a stable aspect of SES as it is 
“typically established at an early age and tends to remain the same over time” (Sirin, 2005, p. 419) and correlates with family income. 
Parental occupation indicates the prestige and culture of a household and the potential for social and economic resources that are 
available to the student. ESCS’s home resources component functions as a proxy for familial wealth, as it is not possible to collect data 
on household income (OECD, 2017b). This approach is consistent across PISA cycles from 2003 onwards. However, PISA 2000 
calculated ESCS slightly differently in separating home possessions into three discreet indices (family wealth, cultural possessions, and 
home educational resources) (OECD, 2017b). Several steps are taken to establish the components of ESCS. 
Indices of maternal and paternal education are created by recoding nationally determined responses to questions on parental 
education, according to the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) 1997 (OECD, 2017b). The index of the highest 
level of parental education (HISCED) is taken from the higher of the two parental education responses. The HISCED variable is further 
recoded to the estimated number of years in schooling (PARED), which is country-specific (see ‘Mapping of ISCED to years’ (OECD, 
2017b, p. 435)). 
Parental occupation is situated in open-ended questions about parental job titles and processes (OECD, 2017a). Responses are 
coded according to the International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO), and indices of the mother’s, the father’s, and the 
higher of the two occupational statuses are calculated. In each of the indices, “the higher ISEI scores indicate higher levels of

PISA. However, the ISCO was updated in 2008. As such, PISA 2012 and 2015 utilized the 2008 version of this scale, while the 
2000–2009 cycles of PISA used the 1988 version. 
Finally, the HOMEPOS index is a summary index of the responses to all the household and possession items utilized in a given cycle, 
with the addition of an item recording how many books are present in a student’s home (2009, OECD, 2005, OECD, 2012, 2014, 
2017b) (for a summary of the common home possession items, see Table 1). The number of items deployed to measure family wealth in 
PISA varies by cycle. From the 2006 cycle of PISA onwards, the background questionnaire included space for up to three further 
country-specific items, which were “seen as appropriate measures of family wealth within the country’s context” (OECD, 2017b, p. 
300). The inclusion and exclusion of items deployed in the student questionnaire to indicate the home possessions index (HOMEPOS) is 
designed to reflect technical, economic, and social changes in participating countries (OECD, 2017b). It is also important to note that 
the estimation processes have changed in recent cycles for non-OECD countries (OECD, 2017b). 
In instances where one of the three valid responses necessary to compute ESCS is missing, a predicted value for the missing response 
and a random component based on a regression of the two valid responses are used to estimate ESCS (2009, OECD, 2005, 2012, 2014, 
2017b). ESCS is not computed when there is more than one missing component: in these cases, a missing value is assigned. Once the 
three components of ESCS are established, “all three components were standardised for OECD countries, and partner coun­
tries/economies with an OECD mean of zero and a standard deviation of one” (OECD, 2017b, p. 339). It is important to note that the 
most recent cycles use data from all participating countries, and thus “all countries and economies contribute equally to the estimation 
of ESCS scores” (OECD, 2017b, p. 341). In contrast, in earlier cycles of PISA (prior to 2012), only data from OECD counties was used in 
the PCA and the estimation of ESCS. 
The components of ESCS have all been subject to criticism in the literature. Schulz (2005) reported that inaccurate student 
reporting of parental education levels and occupation are of concern in the construction and validity of ESCS. This observation was 
paralleled in studies of other ILSAs (i.e. Rutkowski & Rutkowski, 2010). Scholarly criticism of the HOMEPOS index indicates that this 
index is also problematic, in as far as it attempts to create a structure of a facet of SES across multiple national contexts. Rutkowski and 
Rutkowski (2013) studied the properties of the ESCS measure in PISA 2009. They found that subscales within the HOMEPOS index 
were ill-fitting for the majority of participating countries, suggesting that “the items hypothesized to measure the constructs that 
comprise the home possessions scale are likely not correct for most educational systems” (p. 268). Caution in blindly adopting the 
OECD’s measure of SES in secondary analysis can be arguably championed by these findings, particularly in single country studies as 
the variation of the items used to identify these constructs over time can undermine their usefulness in making comparisons. 
1.3. Research questions 
The purpose of the current study was firstly to identify perennially deployed indicators of SES in PISA, and secondly to investigate 
how those might be used to create a bespoke model of SES for Sweden. The study draws on the nationally representative data from six 
cycles of PISA between 2000 and 2015 to assess whether a measurement model of SES refined to fit the 2000 sample is statistically 
valid for five subsequent cycles of PISA. Unlike previous contributions to the literature which have focused on the measurement 
properties of the existing ESCS indicator, this paper builds a measurement model of SES which is tailored to Sweden’s national context.

While earlier studies have explored SES measurement in ILSA data within single data cycles, this paper seeks to create a measurement 
model and assess the invariance of the model across time though the deployment of both Measurement Invariance testing and 
alignment analysis. This paper aims to answer the following research questions:  
1 Which questionnaire items asked in the measurement of ESCS in PISA have appeared repeatedly? Has the average response to these 
items changed over time in Sweden?  
2 Can these repeated items be used to create a Sweden specific measurement model of SES? Is it appropriate to make a cross-time 
comparison of SES in Sweden using this model? 
2. Method 
2.1. Data source and participants 
This analysis utilized data from the OECD’s PISA suite of studies. The data included Swedish students drawn from the six cycles of 
PISA (approximately 4500 individuals per cycle). Within PISA, samples are drawn using a two-stage stratified sampling design (OECD, 
2014). In the first stage, schools are sampled, with the probability proportionated to the number of 15 year olds enrolled in the sampled 
school. In the second stage, students are sampled within sampled schools, with a target cluster size of 35 students per sampled school 
(OECD, 2014). The final number of participants was some 26,043 across the six cycles of PISA, once missing were excluded. 
2.1.1. Indicator selection 
In order to make a comparison of the SES measures deployed in PISA across time, the constancy of items appearing in contextual 
questionnaires was the qualifier for inclusion in this study. Consultation of technical documents pertaining to the various cycles of PISA 
(see Table 1) revealed that PISA deploys 11 repeated items across each cycle. The indicator of parental education and was oper­
ationalised consistently over time and was thus also included in the analysis. Parental occupation, while a constant item in the student 
survey and a constant component of ESCS across all cycles of PISA, was excluded from the analysis. The reasons for this were two-fold: 
firstly, the high theoretical correlation between parental education and occupation (one begets the other); secondly, the challenge of 
rationalizing the data down to a manageable number of categories. 
In preparing data for this study, the data was processed in SPSS. The questionnaire items of home possessions, namely possession of 
“a desk,” “a dedicated study space,” “books to help with school work (textbooks),” “educational software,” “a room of one’s own,” “a 
domestic internet connection,” “classical literature,” “poetry,” and “works of art” were dichotomous variables and coded as “no” and 
“yes”. The variable “the number of books in the home” was a 6-scaled variable and coded categorically as 0–10, 11–25, 26–100, 
101–200, 201–500, and more than 500, corresponding with the available responses offered from 2003 onward. 
2.2. Analytical method 
2.2.1. Measuring change across time 
In the first step, the means of the selected items were calculated and compared across the cycles between 2000 and 2015 (see 
Appendix A). Change in the responses to the questionnaire items was assessed by examining changes in the mean response. The 
Cohen’s d was calculated for the response to each of the items between 2000 and 2015. In interpreting the observed changes, a value of 
.2 is held as small, .5 as medium, .8 as large, and 1.3 as very large (Cohen, 1988; Rosenthal, 1996). 
2.2.2. Confirmatory factor analysis 
The second analytical approach taken in this study was confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) (Brown, 2015). CFA is a theory-driven 
approach to measurement modelling which allows for researcher specification of relationships among error variance. The CFA 
framework also offers sufficient modelling flexibility to test for various sources of model invariance. When assessing model fit, the χ2 
goodness-of-fit test, the Root Mean Square, Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) and 
Comparative fit index (CFI) were used. The first three of these model fit statistics are absolute fit indices indicating how well a model 
fits the sample data. The RMSEA is sensitive to the number of estimated parameters in the model and thus regarded as “one of the most 
informative fit indices” (Diamantopoulos & Siguaw, 2000, p. 85). In addition to favouring parsimony insofar as choosing models with 
fewer parameters, the RMSEA statistic is calculated with a confidence interval surrounding its value, allowing for more precise testing 
of the null hypothesis. Good fit is indicated by an RMSEA of .07, with an upper limit to the confidence interval of less than .08 (Hooper, 
Coughlan, & Mullen, 2008). When interpreting the SRMR, a value of 0 is held to be perfectly fitting, and values of less than .05 are 
deemed well-fitting (Hooper et al., 2008). The incremental fit index consulted in assessing the fit of the models in this analysis is the 
CFI, for which values of greater than .95 indicate good fit (Hooper et al., 2008). 
2.2.3. Measurement invariance testing 
The essential question as to whether perennially deployed questionnaire items can be used to model a structure of socioeconomic 
status over time developed through CFA requires the determination of measurement invariance. Measurement invariance testing is a 
stepwise procedure to evaluate whether a model is rightly applicable to multiple groups. Model constraints are successively exposed, 
and the model fit is compared under the three constraint conditions, namely, identical factor structure (configural invariance), equality 
of factor loading (metric invariance), and equality of indicator intercepts (scalar invariance).

In the absence of measurement invariance, “the finding of a between-group difference cannot be unambiguously interpreted” 
(Cheung & Rensvold, 2002, pp. 233–234). Therefore, the legitimacy of the comparisons across any PISA cycles, for example, is 
dependent on the level of measurement invariance. As Brown (2015) summarises, “it is not useful to compare groups on aspects of the 
latent factors (factor variances, factor covariances, latent means) without first ascertaining that the latent factors measure the same 
constructs in the same fashion in each group” (p. 262). In this endeavour, invariance of factor loadings is particularly important. 
Mplus (Muth´en & Muth´en, 1998-2019; Muth´en and Muth´en, 1998) simplifies the tedious step-wise measurement invariance testing 
procedure and makes possible the testing of equal form, equal factor loadings, and equal intercepts in one model run using the MODEL 
= CONFIGUARL METRIC SCALAR command. This procedure automatically conducts χ2 difference testing and provides unstandard­
ized parameter estimates for the three models. However, standardized estimates and modification indices are unavailable when using 
the command (Brown, 2015). 
2.2.4. Alignment analysis 
The alignment technique assesses the degree of measurement equivalence across large numbers of groups and subgroups within 
populations (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014; Munck, Barber, & Torney-Purta, 2017). It “does not assume measurement invariance and 
can estimate the factor mean and variance parameters in each group while discovering the most optimal measurement invariance 
pattern” (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014, p. 496). Accordingly, it is capable of estimating a factor score in the face of invariance in some 
of the groups. Additionally, Asparouhov and Muth´en (2014) noted that “as a by-product, information about the degree of measurement 
invariance can also be provided. After the alignment estimation is completed, a detailed analysis can be done to determine which 
measurement parameters are approximately invariant and which are not” (p. 499). In this analysis, the cycles of PISA are interpreted as 
fixed modes of variation in the model. The alignment analysis method has been deployed successfully in multiple studies seeking to 
create measurement models across multiple countries using large-scale international data (Glassow, Rolfe, & Yang Hansen, in press; 
Munck et al., 2017). A particular concern in building measurement models generated from PISA data is that refining CFA models using 
modification indices can generate very many, sometimes conflicting, suggested changes when a large number of groups are included. 
This leads to a scalar model which is far from the true model (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014). Applying the alignment approach allows 
for building a model based on recommendations for a single cycle and subsequently testing its suitability for all cycles of data. 
2.3. Analysis plan 
A measurement model of socioeconomic status was built and evaluated in several stages. First, a review of the SES items that were 
used in PISA was conducted to identify which common items (i.e., excluding national adaptations) were replicated across contextual 
questionnaires for the various cycles of PISA. Second, the means of these items were compared over PISA cycles and assessed to see 
whether they exhibited significant change. Third, a measurement model was constructed using CFA. Modification indices for the 2000 
cycle were consulted to refine the model and identify a well-fitting factor structure. Fourth, the measurement invariance of the final 
model identified in the third stage was tested. Finally, an alignment analysis was run to test whether item level invariance could be 
attributed to the overall factor mean and intercept invariance. 
The modelling process was conducted in Mplus (Muth´en & Muth´en, 1998-2017; Muth´en and Muth´en, 1998). To account for the 
cluster effect of the sample and to correct the standard error estimation, the “COMPLEX” option in model types in Mplus was used. PISA 
cycles were used as groups, and a model solution was produced for each cycle. Missing data was handled by Mplus’ use of full in­
formation maximum likelihood (FIML) estimates of missing data generated using the EM algorithm (Muth´en, Muth´en, & Asparouhov,

3. Results 
3.1. Change in items over time 
In the first stage of the analysis, the means were calculated and compared for the common items in each PISA cycle (tabulated in 
Appendix A). The Cohen’s d captures the size of the mean difference between each pair of items across PISA cycles. 
Close examination of the changes in the item means for Sweden (see Fig. 1) revealed small declines in the prevalence of textbooks, 
the number of books in the home, and poetry. Internet connectivity saw a moderate increase, which is in line with the expansion of the 
internet globally (Skinner, Biscope, & Poland, 2003; The World Bank, 2018). Sweden was an early adopter of internet-based systems, 
and most civil society interactions are conducted through internet portals. It has a significant tech industry, so this high preponderance 
of internet connections is unsurprising. 
It is important to note that the scale used to measure the number of books in the home in the PISA background questionnaire 
changed between 2000 and 2003. Two other items that are linked to the books in the home measure (in as far as they are most 
commonly consumed in book form) are the possession of poetry and the possession of classical literature. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the 
three items exhibited similar directionality in their change. The significance of the changes to the mean of the indictors varied. For 
example, minor differences in effect size were noted for possession of a desk. The lack of significant effect sizes implies that the items 
are stable over time. 
3.2. Establishing the measurement model 
The initial model (Model 1) in the analysis was a simple CFA, examining the relationship between the latent variable SES and its 
indicators from the PISA dataset. A separate model was run for each PISA cycle. Modification indices were requested in the output. The 
models displayed acceptable fit in terms of absolute fit, but not in terms of incremental fit (see Appendix B). 
In the 2000 cycle, classic literature and poetry had the strongest loading on the SES factor, with books the third strongest loading 
item. This pattern of loadings was replicated in all bar the 2003 cycle. These items have somewhat of conceptual overlap, with classic 
literature and poetry both forming part of the PISA index of cultural possessions. The items all share a physical overlap, with classic 
literature and poetry both generally existing in book format. Given these overlaps, and the poor incremental fit of the model, it was 
prudent to examine the modification indices generated in the Mplus output prior to the respecification of the model. 
Examining the modification indices output of the initial model for the 2000 cycle, four groups of indicators were identified. In 
Model 2, a five-indicator model was specified. Parental education remained a separate indicator, while the remaining indicators were 
parcelled to create four new variables: economic indicators of space (study space, desk, and room), cultural capital (classic literature, 
art, poetry, and books), and computing resources (educational software and internet). The model failed to demonstrate good incre­
mental fit in 4 of the 6 cycles (see Appendix C).

3.3. Measurement invariance of correlating variables model 
Measurement Invariance testing revealed that both the configural and the metric invariance models held satisfactory model fits (see 
Table 3), but the Scalar invariance model did not. Focusing on the χ2 contributions to the metric model (see Table 4), we can see that 
the model was best fitting for the 2009 cycle and 2003 fits the worst. A similar pattern was observed for the scalar model. 
An advantage of using the MODEL = CONFIGUARL METRIC SCALAR command in Mplus is that it provides comparisons of the 
models automatically. While the fits were similar for the configural and the metric model, the χ2 difference between the configural and 
metric models was significant (χ2 diff (20) = 266.739 p = .000). Therefore, for parsimony and usefulness, the metric invariance model 
was preferred. Accordingly, the result of this analysis implied that the model had weak factoral invariance across the six cycles of PISA. 
As such, further steps in investigating the invariance of Model 3 were warranted. 
3.4. Alignment analysis of correlating variables model 
Having achieved well-fitting configural and metric invariance in the measurement invariance testing stage of this analysis, 
alignment analysis of the identified measurement model was conducted. The alignment method takes the configural model as its point 
of departure and estimates the most optimal pattern of measurement invariance in the measurement model across the six cycles of PISA 
(Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014). The model fit remains the same as the underlying configural model, and the resultant model validates 
application of the model structure determined by the 2000 cycle to further cycles of PISA data. 
Rather than allowing the alignment model to automatically fix the alignment to the first group in the model (in this case the 2000 
cycle of PISA) and erroneously assume that this group had the best fitting model, an initial alignment model with a free alignment 
approach was specified. Examining the estimated factor means produced in the output, the smallest factor mean was identified for the 
2003 cycle of PISA. As such, the model was re-run, with the alignment fixed to the 2003 cycle. 
The group parameter invariance for the intercepts and loadings is summarized in Table 5. R2 statistics indicate the degree of non- 
invariance, which can be absorbed into the group-specific factor means and variances for SES. As values close to 1 represent higher 
degrees of absorbable non-invariance (Muth´en & Asparouhov, 2017), we can interpret these results as suggesting that the invariance in 
the intercept of cultural capital should be absorbed into the overall factor mean and variance for SES. When examining the weighted 
average estimates (see Table 6) across the invariant groups, parental education had the largest estimate of the intercept (invariant in 3 
out of 6 cycles), while cultural capital displayed the highest estimate of the loading (invariant in all cycles). The proportion of 
non-invariance in the model is 21.67 %. This level of non-invariance falls within the limit suggested by Muth´en and Asparouhov (2014)

3.5. Monte Carlo simulations of alignment analysis of correlating variables model 
While the proportion of non-invariance in the aligned model met the threshold for trustworthiness, confirmation of these results 
was sought through the conducting of a Monte Carlo (MC) simulation. As the average group size across the six cycles of PISA was 4300 
students, the MC simulations were conducted for two group sizes, n = 5000 and n = 15,000, each with 500 replications (see Table 8). 
The MC simulation tests the correlation between the actual and the estimated factor means for the various cycles of PISA. In order to 
confirm the trustworthiness of the alignment analysis results, a near-perfect correlation of .98 is required (Muth´en & Asparouhov, 
2014). The estimated average factor means generated by the MC simulations are well above the .98 level for both n = 5000 (r = .993 for 
factor mean; r = .974 for factor variance) and n = 15,000 (r = .997 for factor mean; r = .987 for factor variance). The MC simulation 
results, therefore, demonstrate that the aligned model of socioeconomic status is well suited to all six cycles of Swedish PISA data, 
despite the existence of non-invariance. 
4. Discussion & conclusions 
By investigating the measurement properties of repeatedly deployed questionnaire items used to indicate socioeconomic status in 
PISA, this study provides convincing evidence that the continual inclusion of these items is warranted in the case of Sweden. This study 
proposed using confirmatory factor analysis to create a measurement model of socioeconomic status which was applicable and well- 
fitting for Swedish PISA data over a 15 year period, and to test the properties of this model using measurement invariance and 
alignment analysis. As noted in the literature, socioeconomic status is a ubiquitous predictor of achievement (e.g. Schulz, 2005), and 
functions as a key contextual variable in analysis of educational data (Mueller & Parcel, 1981; Sirin, 2005; White, 1982). However, 
there is also a noted variation in the strength of this relationship dependent on the exact measure of SES (Freeman & Viarengo, 2014), 
and some conflict on the result of societal change on the appropriateness of measures of socioeconomic status (i.e. Goldthorpe, 1996). 
This study investigated two research questions. Firstly, whether the average response to repeatedly deployed PISA SES items had 
changed over time in Sweden. Secondly, the study explored whether a Sweden-specific model of SES could be created from these items 
and whether it is statistically justified to make a cross-time comparison of SES based on the identified model. 
When investigating student responses to questionnaire items, small to moderate changes were observed in the average response 
patterns for half of the items included in the PISA questionnaires to measure SES. Textbooks, poetry, books, a dictionary, and 
educational software displayed small declines, and internet connectivity had a moderate increase in average response. The items 
displaying small but significant declines in average response are linked by medium, naturally occurring in printed book form, which 
may be linked to increased digitalisation. 
As a highly developed OECD member state, it is perhaps unsurprising that the average response to many of the questionnaire items 
was high. Closely examining those relating to the economic situation of a student’s home (i.e. a desk, a place to study, and a room of 
one’s own), we can see that these items were near universal, with mean responses of over .9, in 2000. The possession of a place to study 
and a room of one’s own declined negligibly over time, and the larger but still negligible decline in students reporting possession of a 
desk is likely due to changing patterns of work in an increasingly digitalised world, rather than changes in material circumstances. 
Digitisation is perhaps the driving force behind changes to student reports of owning the items which indicate computing resources. 
While Sweden was an early adopter of the internet, student reports of having a home internet connection saw a moderate increase 
across the PISA datasets, to the point of near total saturation (98 % of students in 2015 had internet at home). Despite this increased 
availability of online resources, there was no significant decline in educational software, posing the question as to whether Swedish

The stability of so many of the items that reoccur in the measurement of SES in PISA for Sweden mitigates the concerns of 
Goldthorpe (1996) that a measurement of SES that includes multiple components does not account for social changes over time. The 
consistency of student reports of possessing the various indicators of socioeconomic status implies that the socioeconomic conditions of 
Swedish families have remained broadly stable since the turn of the millennium. The stability of these indicators should be reassuring 
to both researchers using PISA data, and policy-makers who derive educational change from their findings. 
While the literature is unequivocal in the suggestion that SES is positively related to achievement (see Schulz, 2005), the nature of 
the indices deployed in ILSAs, notably PISA’s ESCS index, has been questioned (Rutkowski & Rutkowski, 2010, 2013). In the second 
phase of the analysis, a measurement model of SES was built for Sweden using the repeated SES items. The use of CFA and mea­
surement invariance to investigate the properties of SES in PISA is in line with the previous literature (e.g. Rutkowski & Rutkowski, 
2013). However, this study takes a within-country, across-time approach, and is novel in utilising a process of model refinement by 
consulting modification indices to create a model that deployed parcelled items and correlations in its structure. 
The final model developed through consultation of modification indices, model M3, was found to possess metric invariance. The 
weak factorial invariance allows us to assume that the factor loadings of the five items (cultural capital, economic indicators of space, 
study literature, computing resources, and parental education) are similar across the six cycles of PISA and that differences in the factor 
variance and invariance is not a result of the PISA cycle used. The lack of scalar invariance does not allow for confidence in the 
measurement properties of the SES construct across time as the item intercepts differ across time. Addressing this lack of confidence in 
the measurement model through the use of alignment analysis is a small but growing approach to validating measurement models (i.e. 
Glassow et al., in press; Munck et al., 2017). 
The finding that the alignment of the final model is trustworthy offers compelling justification for building a Swedish model of SES 
using PISA data. Alignment analysis provided an alternative approach to proceeding with a model that has not shown strong factorial 
invariance using more traditional methods. This series of analyses suggest that there is some degree of legitimacy in retaining items in 
questionnaires across the various cycles of PISA. 
Alignment analysis has already been acknowledged as a useful method for evaluating and refining attitudinal scales in ILSAs across 
cultures and cohorts (e.g. Munck et al., 2017). This study extends the literature by applying the alignment optimization method to 
improve the SES measure across time. The findings of this study thus have some interesting implications for data collection in in­
ternational assessments. The fluctuation in the items administered to indicate home possessions reflects a purposeful updating of the 
measures of home background to both reflect considerable advances in the ICT sector and better indicate within and between country 
variation in home possessions (OECD, 2017a). Subsequently, changes in the components of ESCS, in addition to the estimation method 
(as outlined in Section 1.2.1 of this paper) make ESCS non-comparable across PISA cycles. By deconstructing ESCS to create a refined 
SES measure, this study offers empirical support for the retention of legacy home possession items and the use of such items to create a 
temporally stable SES construct. 
4.1. Limitations of the study and recommendations for future research 
This study was limited in that it only features data from one country. As such, unlike other studies on the measurement properties of 
SES in ILSAs (e.g. Rutkowski & Rutkowski, 2013), the findings are not generalizable to other economies. The aim of this study to 
investigate changes in the items used to form the measurement model of SES over time is somewhat limited by the ambition to use data 
from 6 cycles of PISA. The 2003 cycle of PISA administered considerably fewer items in the student questionnaire, the inclusion of 
which might have added nuance to the analysis. 
While a natural future development of this study would be to extend it to a wider group of economies, a more compelling direction 
would be to apply its analyses of Swedish achievement data in PISA. Although the ESCS index partly determined achievement scores in 
PISA, the use of a customised country-specific model of SES in data analysis could provide researchers and policy-makers a more in- 
depth understanding of the manifestation of socioeconomic inequalities in educational outcomes in Sweden.
