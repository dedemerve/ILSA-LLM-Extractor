This study compares eleven widely used machine learning algorithms to identify the most accurate and 
comprehensive method for assessing children’s reading fluency. Our fluency framework integrates three key 
dimensions: accuracy in word decoding, reading speed (words per minute), and prosody, which captures 
appropriate pausing and intonation during reading. Audio recordings from 2nd and 3rd grade students across 144 
Brazilian schools were transcribed using the advanced Whisper ASR system, enabling automated extraction of 
ﬂuency features. The research objective was to determine which algorithm best predicts fluency, considering 
diverse evaluation setups including binary classification (fluent versus nonfluent), multiclass classification 
(diﬀerentiated fluency levels), and regression analysis to estimate continuous fluency scores. Among the eleven 
models evaluated, Logistic Regression achieved the highest overall performance in the classification experiments, 
demonstrating superior precision and accuracy. Ensemble methods such as Gradient Boosting and Random Forest 
also yielded robust results, particularly in regression analyses where they eﬀectively captured the variability 
in expert fluency ratings. Notably, reading speed emerged as the most critical indicator of fluency across 
all experiments, consistently outweighing contributions from accuracy and prosody. Nevertheless, prosody 
maintained signiﬁcant importance, especially when fluency was modeled as a continuous variable, emphasizing 
the value of expressive reading. These findings suggest that while high accuracy in word decoding is fundamental, 
interventions that enhance reading speed and incorporate prosodic features can oﬀer more nuanced support for 
reading development. This work provides educators with insights into scalable, automated, and cost-effective 
approaches to monitoring and improving reading fluency.

According to do Valle et al. (2022), there are still few approaches

provide continuous, consistent, and accessible information to teachers.
A promising solution to assessing reading fluency is using automatic 
speech recognition (ASR) technologies. Although the primary goal of 
ASR systems is to convert audio into text, recent studies have shown 
that it is possible to build systems for reading fluency assessment us­
ing this approach (Ribeiro et al., 2019). According to de Assis et al. 
(2022), automating large-scale reading fluency assessments can reduce 
costs, execution time, and the impact of human subjectivity in grading 
activities.
However, it is important to note that using ASR for oral reading as­
sessment in children faces several challenges, as children have specific 
acoustic characteristics that make the evaluation process diﬃcult, since 
many of these systems are trained with adult speech. Children’s reading 
also tends to be slower, with possible repetitions and false starts, mak­
ing the transcription process more challenging. The quality of the audio 
and background noise, as well as the child’s behavior, can also produce 
audio with unexpected and unpredictable speech or noise (de Assis et 
al., 2022).
Recently, the Whisper model, developed by OpenAI, has emerged 
as a promising solution to the limitations faced by automatic speech 
recognition systems. Whisper is a general speech recognition model, 
trained on a large multilingual dataset collected from the web. Its fea­
tures include voice activity detection, multilingual transcription with 
punctuation, and translation into English (Gris et al., 2023).
Even so, it is essential to consider the limitations and the need for 
continuous adaptations. Reading fluency assessment depends on the ef­
fective integration of these tools with existing educational practices, 
ensuring that they can meet the specific and complex demands of learn­
ing contexts.
In this context, this study aims to compare existing machine learning 
models to identify the best approach to assessing reading fluency based 
on transcriptions of readings performed by Whisper. This study em­
ployed audio recordings of children’s readings from 2nd and 3rd grades 
from 144 public and private schools across all regions of Brazil.
2. Background works
According to the systematic review by Bhardwaj et al. (2022), the 
ﬁeld of voice recognition research in the context of children is still open, 
as despite an increasing amount of work in this area, it focuses on adult 
speech.
An example of an approach using an ASR system with children for 
reading assessment, in the context of Portuguese speech can be seen in 
the work of Silva et al. (2019). This study presents a computational ap­
proach called Talk2Me, which can be understood in three main stages: 
1) Audio collection; 2) Audio processing; and 3) Result generation. How­
ever, their approach is limited to assessing the accuracy of word reading, 
which is classified as correct or incorrect, and does not evaluate read­
ing speed or prosody, the latter being the dimension that, according to 
Machado and Freitag (2019), most evidences comprehension of what is 
being read.
Another example of work focused solely on the dimension of accu­
racy in assessing children’s reading is demonstrated in de Assis et al. 
(2022), which focuses on the process of reading pseudowords. The au­
thor develops a feature extraction process from texts, creating methods 
to calculate the Number of Words Read and the Number of Words Read 
Correctly in a 60-second audio segment.
New machine learning techniques have signiﬁcantly improved the 
development of speech recognition models with few labeled data, as 
demonstrated in the study by Gris et al. (2021). This study uses Wav2vec 
2.0, a self-supervised pre-trained model based on a modern type of neu­
ral networks known as Transformers, to validate the development of an 
ASR system in Portuguese using a dataset with only 1 hour of transcribed 
speech.
2.1. Reading fluency assessment
Automating fluency evaluation through ASR and machine learning 
oﬀers multiple benefits, including reducing teacher workload by han­
dling listening and counting tasks. Research supports that such technol­
ogy alleviates the burden of monitoring reading practice (Ja’afar et al., 
2021). Automation saves time during assessments and allows for more 
frequent testing, enabling timely identification of reading diﬃculties 
and more responsive instruction. Additionally, automated assessments 
provide consistency and objectivity, ensuring unbiased results. Stud­
ies indicate that automated ORF assessments can closely match human 
scoring (Bolaños et al., 2013). Reliable fluency data empowers teachers 
to make informed instructional decisions, such as grouping students or 
oﬀering individual feedback. Furthermore, automation can enhance stu­
dent motivation, as reading with a computer or tablet often feels more 
engaging and less stressful than traditional one-on-one evaluations.
In the research by Rodrigues et al. (2023), a process for assessing 
reading fluency in the context of Portuguese speaking is described. In 
the described process, the Wav2vec2 ASR model is used, where the audio 
undergoes a preprocessing stage involving the segmentation of speech 
segments. After the transcriptions are completed, they are reassembled 
and then go through a post-processing stage. This stage includes several 
steps to ensure the quality of the comparison, such as aligning the tran­
scribed text with the original text and merging and separating words, 
which occurs due to students’ reading characteristics, such as very slow 
or rushed readings.
Considering reading fluency for other languages, the work by Yıldız 
et al. (2024) evaluated the accuracy of reading assessments of 120 Turk­
ish 4th-grade students conducted by an automatic speech recognition 
system compared to human evaluators. The analysis showed that the 
diﬀerence between the evaluations from the two sources was minimal, 
with an excellent level of agreement.
Lin et al. (2019) assessed the reading accuracy of Taiwanese students 
using a speech recognition e-book system, finding that the system’s ac­
curacy was comparable to human evaluations, although they did not 
classify readers as good or poor. Moby.Read is an oral reading fluency 
assessment system with artificial intelligence, developed in the USA. 
Bernstein et al. (2020) found correlations of 0.96 for accuracy, 0.92 for 
comprehension, and 0.88 for expression when comparing the system 
with human evaluations. Moby.Read is notable for assessing all three 
components of reading fluency.
However, despite the progress that these ASR systems have made, 
Gris et al. (2023) states that the patterns found in the training pro­
cess of these models make them non-generalizable to other datasets, 
which necessitates fine-tuning. Fine-tuning uses a smaller set of labeled 
data to accurately perform the task, and even then, it can still make ba­
sic errors when evaluated on datasets with diﬀerent characteristics and 
conditions. Additionally, most of these ASR systems are trained with 
normalized texts, where punctuation is removed and texts are all in 
lowercase or uppercase, which once again makes it diﬃcult to evalu­
ate prosody.

2.2. Whisper ASR
Whisper, OpenAI’s advanced automatic speech recognition (ASR) 
model, has attracted attention for its near human-level accuracy on adult 
speech and robustness in noisy environments. However, several studies 
have noted signiﬁcant limitations when applying Whisper to transcribe 
children’s speech. For example, because its training data is predomi­
nantly composed of adult audio, Whisper does not fully capture the 
unique acoustic and linguistic characteristics of children’s speech—such 
as higher pitch, evolving articulation, and frequent disfluencies. Attia et 
al. (2024) report that while Whisper achieves a word error rate (WER) of 
approximately 13% on children’s speech, fine-tuning with child-specific 
data can reduce this rate to around 9%. Despite this improvement, the 
model still struggles with both inter-speaker and intra-speaker variabil­
ity inherent in child speech.
Another concern is the model’s handling of disfluencies and reading 
mistakes common among young readers. Its powerful language decoder 
may ``auto-correct'' errors—transcribing what the child likely intended 
to say rather than the exact spoken words—which can mask genuine 
reading diﬃculties (Johansson, 2024). This issue is compounded in real 
classroom environments with background noise, where performance can 
further degrade. In a comparative study, Molenaar et al. (2023) found 
that although Whisper’s raw transcription accuracy was high, a tra­
ditional Kaldi-based system augmented with passage-specific language 
models outperformed it in detecting reading errors. This suggests that 
without additional mechanisms such as forced alignment or confidence 
scoring, Whisper’s general transcription strength may inadvertently con­
ceal errors critical for accurate fluency assessment.
Additionally, Whisper was originally presented by Radford et al. 
(2022) and trained on an extensive dataset of over 680,000 hours of la­
beled audio across more than 90 languages—with approximately 9,000 
hours in Portuguese—making it a robust option for speech recognition 
in Portuguese (Gris et al., 2023). Beyond transcription, the model is 
capable of predicting punctuation automatically due to its training on 
non-normalized texts. Gris et al. (2023) note signiﬁcant advancements 
in punctuation prediction in Portuguese compared to previous models; 
however, research speciﬁcally addressing this capability remains scarce.
Overall, while Whisper shows promise due to its overall accuracy 
and noise robustness, its eﬀectiveness in specialized applications-
such as transcribing children’s speech and predicting punctuation in 
Portuguese—requires further refinement and targeted investigation. 
Within this context, there is currently a scenario where applications fo­
cus on the dimension of accuracy, which, in itself, does not encompass 
the evaluation of fluency as a whole.
2.3. Machine learning approaches
Machine learning (ML) has gained prominence in reading fluency 
assessment, with researchers employing various classifiers to evaluate 
oral reading fluency (ORF) and detect reading diﬃculties (Matsuki et 
al., 2016; Raatikainen et al., 2021). Ensemble methods, such as Ran­
dom Forest and XGBoost, have demonstrated superior accuracy and 
generalizability compared to single classifiers, particularly in analyzing 
children’s reading errors and predicting reading disabilities (Psyridou et 
al., 2023; Žabkar et al., 2023). While simpler models like Logistic Re­
gression and Naive Bayes remain valuable due to their interpretability, 
boosting algorithms tend to excel in complex tasks, including dyslexia 
detection using eye-tracking or prosodic features (Raatikainen et al., 
2021; Foldnes et al., 2024).
The success of ML models depends on high-quality datasets, includ­
ing standardized assessments like DIBELS and GORT, as well as longi­
tudinal tracking data from technology-based platforms (Matsuki et al., 
2016; Psyridou et al., 2023). Multimodal approaches, incorporating eye­
tracking and audio analysis, provide deeper insights into reading fluency 
by capturing fixation patterns, pause durations, and prosody metrics 
(Raatikainen et al., 2021; Žabkar et al., 2023). These features enhance 
classification accuracy, especially when combined with demographic 
variables and standardized test scores, allowing models to diﬀerentiate 
ﬂuent from dysfluent readers more eﬀectively (Matsuki et al., 2016).
Despite these advancements, ML-based reading assessments face 
challenges, including data variability, label quality, and scalability is­
sues (Matsuki et al., 2016). Eye-tracking studies, while informative, 
are costly and time-intensive, limiting widespread adoption (Žabkar et 
al., 2023). Additionally, ensuring model interpretability remains criti­
cal for educational use, as complex deep learning methods often lack 
transparency compared to tree-based ensembles (Foldnes et al., 2024). 
Addressing fairness and bias in ML applications also requires diverse 
training data to ensure equitable outcomes across linguistic and cultural 
groups (Psyridou et al., 2023).
Therefore, this work proposes the application of a machine learn­
ing approach that involves using the Whisper ASR system to transcribe 
reading audio of texts, rather than just isolated words. This approach in­
cludes a process for extracting features from these transcriptions capable 
of capturing variables related to reading speed and prosody, in addi­
tion to accuracy. It also aims to implement supervised machine learning 
models to automate the fluency assessment process, not only to classify 
readings as good/bad but also to evaluate them at diﬀerent levels of 
ﬂuency.
3. Materials and methods
This research was developed based on data collected by Escribo dur­
ing regular School activities in 144 schools, both public and private, 
across all regions of Brazil. Students read ``O Lobo e o Veado'' short story. 
The test was recorded and sent to a database for evaluation of how the 
intervention influenced the development of the children’s reading skills.
That said, this study aims to use the recorded reading audios to seek 
a method for automating the evaluation of fluency through machine 
learning. Therefore, we will proceed with the explanation of the method 
used in this research.
3.1. Steps of the machine learning approach
The present study presents a machine learning approach that essen­
tially involves: 1) Transcription of the collected audio using Whisper; 2) 
Extraction of fluency features from the transcribed texts; and 3) Appli­
cation of machine learning models to predict fluency.
During this process, experimental steps that complement the ma­
chine processes are also performed, such as selecting the audio to be 
transcribed, developing the fluency score from human evaluations, fil­
tering the data, and categorizing the target variable. For all stages in­
volving code production, the Google Colaboratory platform and Python 
programming language were used.
3.2. Understanding and preparing the data
3.2.1. Selection of audio files
The data selection for the study was carried out in two distinct phases 
to ensure a representative sample with the necessary quality for further 
analysis. Initially, 600 audio recordings were randomly selected. This 
decision was based on the available research time and operational costs, 
taking into account the duration of each recording.
3.2.2. Transcription of readings
With the sample of 600 audio files defined, the transcription process 
began using the automatic speech recognition system Whisper (Radford 
et al., 2022). After initial tests, it was identified that a 3-minute reading 
audio from our sample takes about 2 minutes to transcribe, considering 
the use of the large-v3 model. For the entire dataset, this process could 
take 20 hours.
As an open-source project, various other projects have been built 
based on Whisper. Among them, one of the tested projects was WhisperX

3.2.3. Feature extraction from readings
With the transcriptions completed, we had a set of text files to pro­
cess in that stage of generating independent variables for our model. As 
explained earlier, the fluency assessment had three dimensions: accu­
racy, speed, and prosody. Therefore, the reading features to be extracted 
were related to these three dimensions, as described in Table 1.
As described in Table 1, the Accuracy and Prosody reading variables 
will be obtained through the comparison of the transcribed text with the 
original text that the child was asked to read. The Speed variable will 
be obtained from the timestamps of the first and last words read, after 
the Accuracy extraction process, considering only the correct words.
Before starting the variable extraction step in the code, a word equal­
ization step was performed to ensure that item-by-item comparison is 
consistently self-regulating and to avoid errors during reading compar­
isons. The code used for word equalization is publicly available and can 
be accessed on a GitHub page.1
Since the comparison process is done item by item, it is notable that 
this method is quite eﬀective in avoiding comparison errors that may 
arise from reading mistakes, transcription errors, word repetitions, or 
even nonsensical speech lost in the middle of the reading. It preserves 
only the words that match the original text, thus allowing a better ex­
traction of fluency characteristics.
After performing the word equalization, the first characteristic to be 
extracted was Prosody. For this, the transcribed texts and the original 
text underwent a tokenization process, breaking them into an array of 
words. Any word that has a comma or period, symbolizing pauses made 
during reading, was stored in a new array for comparison. The original 
text has a total of 20 words accompanied by a comma or period, which 
then represents the maximum possible score in the Prosody variable.
Once the Prosody scores were extracted, all commas and periods 
were removed from the texts to avoid interfering with the word-by-word 
comparison for Accuracy extraction. Again, a new array was created to 
store only the words identified as accurate, and a counter was used to 
count them. Since the original text contains 153 words, this is also the 
maximum score for the Accuracy variable.
The Speed variable is obtained from the array generated in the Ac­
curacy process. The time of the last word minus the time of the first 
word in the array is considered the total reading time, thus avoiding 
counting time where the student delays starting the reading or where 
the recording continues even after the text has been completed. How­
ever, pauses during reading were not considered in this exclusion. The 
ﬁnal value for the Speed variable is obtained by rounding the following 
calculation: Speed = Accuracy / (Total Reading Time / 60).

3.2.4. Development of the target variable
This stage of the process aims to generate the dependent variable of 
our model, which we want to predict. For this purpose, the 600 selected 
audios were also evaluated by a specialist in the field, with the goal of 
measuring the students’ fluency.
The specialist evaluated each child’s reading by examining several 
key aspects of their performance according to the rubrics. They looked 
at how eﬀectively the reader used expression and varied volume to make 
the text sound natural, rather than simply reading word by word. Phras­
ing, whether the child was able to recognize sentence boundaries and 
modulate intonation appropriately, rather than segmenting the text into 
unnaturally short phrases, was also rated. Additionally, they assessed 
the smoothness of the reading, checking for excessive pauses, hesita­
tions, or repeated attempts at diﬃcult words or phrases. Finally, the 
rhythm of the reading was evaluated to determine if the child main­
tained a steady, conversational pace or if the speed was uneven. This 
holistic approach allowed the raters to identify both the strengths and 
areas for improvement in each child’s reading performance.
The evaluator also used tags to mark categories in the audios, such 
as: presence of other people’s speech, very low audio, very poor reading, 
many background noises, did not read, etc. A tag is added whenever a 
problem is identified in at least one segment of the reading.
Each of the human fluency evaluation categories was included in 
the table, with scores from 1 to 4, as well as the mentioned tags, with 
values of 0 for the absence of the tag or 1 for the presence of the tag in 
the reading.
For the supervised learning analysis, it is necessary to have a labeled 
target variable that needs to be predicted. The target variable used for 
this work is obtained by summing the scores given by the evaluator 
divided by 1.6, so that the maximum score (16) became 10.
With the table construction completed, the data understanding and 
preparation stage is finalized, proceeding to the modeling and evalua­
tion stage.
3.3. Modeling and evaluation
The modeling and evaluation stage of the machine learning ap­
proach encompasses three experiments: 01) Binary Classification; 02) 
Multiclass Classification; and 03) Regression. Each of the experiments 
involves the steps of categorizing the target variable based on the type 
of supervised learning addressed in each experiment, filtering related 
to the tags added during the human evaluation process, and applying 
the models and evaluating the generated metrics. For all three experi­
ments, the Independent Variables (Accuracy, Speed, and Prosody) were 
assigned to a variable ``X'' and the Dependent Variable (Fluency) was 
assigned to a variable ``y''. This is a standard procedure in supervised 
learning.
Various algorithms aimed at generating predictive models have been 
proposed in the literature of machine learning and data science. For our 
research, we selected eleven of the most common and popular algo­
rithms used in various applications and areas: Logistic Regression (LR), 
Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support 
Vector Machine (SVM), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), Decision Tree (DT), 
Gradient Boosting (GB), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Naive Bayes 
(NB), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and Categorical 
Boosting (CatBoost).
For the proper application of model training, a hyperparameter grid 
was used employing the Grid Search technique to find the best param­
eter combination for each of the algorithms used. After training the 
models, an evaluation of each model’s performance metrics is carried 
out. The metrics used for this evaluation include the confusion matrix, 
accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and the ROC curve.
The process then underwent a second round, where all techniques 
were executed again, this time on a version of the dataset after filter­
ing out audios tagged with issues. The reasons for exclusion included: 
muted audio (n = 8), extremely low volume (n = 44), uncomprehensi­

3.3.1. Experiments description
For the execution of binary classification, the fluency score variable 
was binarized, where values less than or equal to 7.5 were categorized 
as 0 (nonfluent, n = 283) and values greater than 7.5 were categorized 
as 1 (fluent, n = 230). The score of 7.5 was chosen as the cutoff value 
because it provided the best division between classes. Considering the 
ﬁltered dataset, class 0 has 152 readings, and class 1 has 175 readings.
The StratifiedKFold cross-validation technique was chosen for model 
training, as it maintains the class proportions in each of the generated 
subsets. The parameters used for cross-validation were: n_splits = 5, 
shuﬄe = true, and random_state = 42. The hyperparameter optimiza­
tion process for binary classification was defined based on the AUC (Area 
Under the Curve) value.
For multiclass classification, the fluency score variable was catego­
rized into 3 classes: values less than or equal to 5 were categorized as 0 
(nonfluent, n = 170), values greater than 5 and less than or equal to 
7.5 as 1 (partially fluent, n = 113), and values greater than 7.5 as 2 (flu­
ent, n = 230). Considering the filtered dataset, class 0 has 67 readings, 
class 1 has 85 readings, and class 2 has 175 readings.
The process follows the same steps as in Experiment 01, with the 
division of dependent and independent variables, training using strati­
ﬁed cross-validation, executing the 11 classification models, using Grid 
Search to find the best parameter combinations, this time based on ac­
curacy, and displaying the metrics that will be used for performance 
analysis of the models in this experiment.
In the Regression Analysis, no categorization is done on the target 
variable; it remains a continuous variable with values up to 10. Regard­
ing the algorithms, the regression versions of the ones from the previous 
experiments were used, except for Logistic Regression, which needed to 
be changed to Linear Regression, and Naive Bayes, which was removed 
from the process since it is only used for classification, totaling 10 re­
gression algorithms.
The Grid Search technique is once again used to optimize hyperpa­
rameters, now using Negative Mean Squared Error as the metric, and 
ﬁnally, the metrics of Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Squared Error 
(MSE), and R2, which represents the proportion of variance in the de­
pendent variable, are generated. From these metrics, the best regression 
models for the context of this work will be estimated.
4. Results
4.1. Experiment 01: binary classification
For the first experiment in this research, 11 diﬀerent classification 
algorithms were used. Each algorithm was executed on a version of the 
complete dataset with 513 labeled audios assessed by the evaluator and 
another version of the dataset after filtering out audios tagged with is­

5. Discussion
5.1. Analysis of the most relevant models
Considering the first experiment, binary classification, the best over­
all result was achieved by Logistic Regression and Gradient Boosting 
with the filtered dataset. Both of these models reached an accuracy of 
0.82, precision of 0.84, recall of 0.82, and F1-Score of 0.83. Although 
Logistic Regression and LightGBM for the complete dataset achieved 
higher accuracy (0.83), we understand that precision is the most impor­
tant metric for our context.
The precision metric indicates the proportion of correct positive pre­
dictions relative to the total number of positive predictions made by the 
model, which is more relevant in our case as it is important to minimize 
false positives. Ensuring that the model correctly identifies positive cases 
(fluent readers) is more important than merely achieving high overall 
accuracy. This is crucial to avoid misclassifying nonfluent students as 
ﬂuent, which could lead to diﬃculties in pedagogical interventions and 
the proper learning process for the child.
Additionally, although Logistic Regression for the complete dataset 
has good accuracy and precision values, it achieved only 0.77 in the 
recall metric. In our context, recall indicates how well the model is able 
to identify all fluent students. Even though it is possible for the educator 
to quickly identify if a student is incorrectly classified as nonfluent and 
the consequences may not be as problematic as the opposite case, it is 
still important to have balanced recall rates with other metrics. Since 
this is one of the lowest among the evaluated models, this model was 
not considered one of the best.
For the multiclass classification analysis, it is noticeable from the 
generated confusion matrices that splitting the previous class 0 (in bi­
nary classification) into two classes worsened the performance of the 
models. This is likely due to the reduced number of readings in the 
dataset. For the complete dataset, we have: class 0 with 170 readings, 
class 1 with 113 readings, and class 2 with 230 readings. In the filtered 
dataset, these numbers decrease to 67, 85, and 175.
Nevertheless, we proceeded with the evaluation of the classifiers, 
and for multiclass classification, the standout model is Logistic Regres­
sion for the complete dataset with Accuracy = 0.75, Precision = 0.73, 
Recall = 0.75, and F1-Score = 0.73. Although other models achieved 
the same precision, it was Logistic Regression that performed best for 
the other metrics, thus being the best model considering the overall per­
formance across the 3 classes. We believe the performance of the filtered 
dataset was likely worse due to the overall reduction in the dataset size, 
which hampers model training.
Despite the inherent diﬃculty of classifiers in achieving good perfor­
mance for multiclass classification, we view this analysis as important 
and promising. Developing the ability to identify diﬀerent levels of flu­
ency can be more signiﬁcant than merely classifying as good or poor, 
thus enabling more individualized pedagogical choices.
Finally, we applied regression analysis, where both the complete and 
ﬁltered datasets were considered. In this analysis, 10 diﬀerent regression 
algorithms were evaluated. The best models identified were CatBoost 
with MAE = 1.09, MSE = 2.37, and R2 = 0.71, and Random Forest 
with MAE = 1.09, MSE = 2.38, and R2 = 0.71. Both models exhibited 
the lowest mean absolute error and the lowest mean squared error, indi­
cating high precision in their predictions. Additionally, their coeﬃcient 
of determination (R2) was higher than that of the other models, demon­
strating that they explain a greater proportion of the variability in the 
data.
5.2. Most important fluency characteristics
We will now discuss the most important characteristics that influ­
enced the performance of the models in the binary classification, mul­
ticlass classification, and regression experiments, considering only the 
models that demonstrated the best performances.
In the binary classification experiment, the best models found were 
Logistic Regression and Gradient Boosting, both with similar perfor­
mance. For the Logistic Regression, we used its coeﬃcients, which indi­
cate the relative importance of each variable for the model’s decision­
making: Speed = 0.1017, Prosody = 0.0094, and Accuracy = 0.0006. 
In the Gradient Boosting model, the values were obtained based on the 
gain during the tree splits: Speed = 0.8963, Accuracy = 0.0641, and 
Prosody = 0.0396.
The analysis of the models reveals that the speed variable is the 
most influential in predicting reading fluency. In the Logistic Regres­
sion model, speed had the greatest impact, followed by prosody, which 
had a signiﬁcantly lower weight, while accuracy was the least relevant 
variable. Similarly, in Gradient Boosting, speed also stood out, while 
accuracy and prosody contributed to a lesser extent.
In the multiclass classification experiment, the model that demon­
strated the best performance was again Logistic Regression, whose co­
eﬃcients are: Speed = 0.1250, Prosody = 0.0632, and Accuracy = 
0.0134. As in the binary experiment, speed was the most influential 
variable, but prosody played a more signiﬁcant role, while accuracy 
maintained a lower influence.
In the regression experiment, the models CatBoost and Random For­
est were used, which presented very close results in their metrics. The 
analysis of these models confirms the predominance of the speed vari­
able in predicting reading fluency. In the Random Forest model, speed 
was the most influential variable, with a value of 0.884774, while ac­
curacy and prosody had signiﬁcantly lower importances, with values 
of 0.058298 and 0.056929, respectively. CatBoost also reinforced this 
conclusion, highlighting speed with a value of 55.369010, followed by 
prosody with 23.407274 and accuracy with 21.223717.
The analysis of the results from the three experiments reveals a clear 
and consistent pattern: reading speed is the most important variable 
in predicting fluency, both in classification contexts (binary and multi­
class) and in regression.
This alignment between classification and regression results suggests 
that reading speed is not only an indicator of fluency in categorical con­
texts but also translates into a quantitative measure in regression, where 
the fluency score provided by experts can be eﬀectively predicted based 
on speed. Accuracy, while essential, seems to play a secondary role in 
determining fluency, indicating that a reader may not be perfectly pre­
cise but can still maintain a fluent reading.
Prosody, which reflects aspects such as correct pauses and intona­
tion, had an increasing influence in the regression models, especially 
in CatBoost, where the importance was substantial (23.407274). This 
suggests that, while speed remains the primary factor, the way a stu­
dent modulates their reading is also relevant and can influence overall 
ﬂuency more profoundly as the analysis becomes more detailed.
These findings oﬀer practical insights for educators. Interventions 
that prioritize increasing reading speed, without neglecting prosody, 
may have a more signiﬁcant impact on improving fluency. Additionally, 
a sole focus on accuracy may not be suﬃcient to promote overall flu­
ency, suggesting that a balance between accuracy, speed, and prosody 
is fundamental for reading development.

In the regression model, each minor fluctuation in students’ read­
ing expressiveness contributes to a continuous fluency score, allowing 
prosody to have a greater cumulative impact on the outcome. Unlike 
classification—which assigns readers to discrete categories—regression 
can more finely capture nuances like pauses, intonation, and pitch vari­
ations, translating them into incremental increases or decreases in the 
ﬂuency score (Miller & Schwanenflugel, 2008). Thus, even if a student’s 
speed is moderately high, well-placed pauses and expressive intonation 
can lead to a comparatively higher overall rating. This mechanism high­
lights that prosody plays a critical supporting role in shaping expert 
judgments of fluency, especially when those judgments are entered on 
a spectrum rather than binned into broad categories.
In contrast, classification imposes threshold-based decisions (e.g., 
“nonfluent,'' ``partially fluent,'' ``fluent''), often governed by speed due 
to its strong separation power between proficient and struggling read­
ers. A child who achieves a certain rate might automatically be labeled 
“fluent,'' overshadowing their actual expressiveness or lack thereof. 
This means that students with slightly slower reading rates but strong 
prosodic skills risk being classified in a lower category, even though they 
may receive higher continuous fluency scores in a regression framework. 
Consequently, the rigid cut points in classification can diminish the ob­
served importance of prosody, whereas in regression, prosodic qualities 
retain a more direct influence on the final fluency rating.
5.3. Literature connections
Our findings reinforce the importance of speed, prosody, and ac­
curacy as critical components of reading fluency, aligning well with 
classic and contemporary research on the subject. Early fluency models 
emphasize that reading fluency constitutes three key pillars—accuracy, 
rate, and prosody—each contributing distinctly to comprehension (Hud­
son et al., 2005; Samuels, 1979). However, our results indicate that 
speed (rate) has the most substantial impact on predicting overall flu­
ency scores, suggesting a dominant role of reading rate in distinguishing 
ﬂuent from less fluent readers in the lower elementary grades. This ob­
servation aligns with research showing that rate typically emerges as the 
ﬁrst noticeable factor when students transition from laborious decoding 
to more automatic, fluid reading (Hudson et al., 2005).
Although accuracy was expected to be a strong contributor to flu­
ency, a growing body of evidence suggests that once children reach 
a baseline level of decoding proficiency, further gains in fluency of­
ten manifest most visibly in reading speed (do Valle et al., 2022; 
Machado & Freitag, 2019). This may be because minimal decoding er­
rors (high accuracy) can still coexist with slow reading rates. Moreover, 
previous large-scale assessments focusing heavily on words-correct-per­
minute (WCPM) measures, such as the studies by Silva et al. (2019) and 
Carchedi et al. (2021), also report a strong association between rate and 
overall fluency scores. Our study extends this perspective by confirming 
that, in a multivariate machine learning context—where speed, prosody, 
and accuracy are modeled together—speed dominates the predictive 
models for fluency, even when prosody and accuracy are explicitly in­
cluded.
At the same time, our work underscores the nuanced role of prosody. 
While earlier research suggests prosody is a qualitative metric often 
overlooked in large-scale assessments (Machado & Freitag, 2019), we 
found that prosody’s impact on fluency is more evident when assessed on 
a continuous scale (regression) rather than in discrete classification con­
texts. This partially corroborates the view that once students cross a cer­
tain threshold for decoding and speed, expressive reading (i.e., prosody) 
emerges as a more critical diﬀerentiator of advanced fluency skills (Hud­
son et al., 2005; Samuels, 1979). In other words, children who already 
read quickly can distinguish themselves further by properly segmenting 
text into phrases, modulating intonation, and pausing appropriately-
all prosodic elements that signal better text comprehension (Machado & 
Freitag, 2019). Our findings thus align with prior studies emphasizing 
that while speed often defines the broad contours of fluency, prosody 
contributes meaningfully to how well students ultimately comprehend 
and engage with texts (Ribeiro et al., 2019; de Assis et al., 2022).
Taken together, this study’s results and prior literature suggest a 
layered progression in fluency development: early readers must mas­
ter accuracy and decoding to form a foundation; speed then becomes a 
pivotal driver of overall fluency perception; and prosody grows in sig­
niﬁcance as children’s readings become increasingly automatic. Future 
research could delve into longitudinal data to explore how these vari­
ables shift in relative importance from first through fifth grades, and 
how automated assessment tools might integrate additional nuanced 
acoustic features to capture prosody more precisely.
6. Conclusion
6.1. Highlights
This study set out to compare how established ML models—such as 
Logistic Regression, Gradient Boosting, and Random Forest—perform 
in automatically assessing children’s reading fluency across speed, ac­
curacy, and prosody. By systematically examining the algorithms, we 
provide insights into which existing methods are most accurate and 
robust for this educational context. The process involved using audio 
transcription with automatic speech recognition systems, extracting flu­
ency features from the transcribed texts, and applying machine learning 
models for fluency prediction.
One of the contributions of this work is the demonstration of the 
use of the ASR tool Whisper, speciﬁcally its modified version WhisperX. 
This tool was valuable because, in addition to having a Word Error Rate 
(WER) of 4.1 for Portuguese, making it accurate in transcriptions, it 
is fast, generates timestamps for each word, and assigns punctuation 
appropriately based on the reading, which allowed for the proper ex­
traction of features such as accuracy, speed, and prosody.
Additionally, the proposed method’s structure, which presents a ma­
chine learning approach for assessing fluency, rather than just a direct 
comparison between extracted metrics and human evaluations, is em­
phasized. This represents another step towards large-scale automation 
of fluency assessment, providing real-time information to teachers.
Moreover, the study highlights which algorithms perform best in 
diﬀerent test cases and their eﬀectiveness, allowing future researchers 
to test new approaches and algorithms and compare their results with 
those presented here, thus continuing to advance the field.
6.2. Limitations and future works
The main limitation of the study is that the audio recordings were not 
controlled. Teachers recorded the students reading in the regular school 
setting, thus external noises (e.g. other children speaking, people walk­
ing in the hallway, etc) may have interfered with the analysis. Future 
studies may try to control the recording ensuring that there are no in­
terruptions or other children speaking while a reading session is taking 
place, reminding the child to read aloud, training teachers to handle the 
situation properly to avoid talking during the recording, and ensuring 
the reading is recorded.
Increasing the quantity and variety of fluency features extracted from 
the text could contribute to the training and performance of the models. 
A process for identifying speakers could also be explored to diﬀerenti­
ate automatically between moments when the teacher or other children 
are speaking during the reading, thus allowing for the removal of these 
sections and avoiding transcription problems. Finally, more varied test­
ing of machine learning algorithms and even deep learning approaches 
could be applied to determine if, even with the current conditions of this 
research, the application of a specific model or approach could improve 
performance.

6.3. Implications for educators
Our findings indicate that speed (i.e., reading rate) consistently 
emerges as the strongest predictor of overall fluency, yet accuracy and 
especially prosody still play crucial supporting roles—particularly when 
a child’s fluency is evaluated on a continuous scale. Teachers and school 
administrators aiming to foster holistic reading skills should integrate 
activities that build automatic word recognition (supporting speed) 
while also modeling expressive reading through intonation, pauses, and 
phrasing (reinforcing prosody) to ensure comprehension is not sacri­
ﬁced.
Selecting Diﬀerent ML Models in Various Contexts:
• Binary Classification (Fluent vs. Non-Fluent): Where classrooms 
need a quick screening tool with minimal false positives, Logis­
tic Regression or Gradient Boosting can be eﬀective. Logistic Re­
gression is more interpretable, which helps educators understand 
which features—speed, accuracy, or prosody—push a student into 
the ``fluent'' category.
• Multiclass Classification (Diﬀerentiated Levels of Fluency): For ed­
ucators who wish to group students at multiple proficiency levels, 
Random Forest or CatBoost showed robust performance. They can 
segment readers into ``nonfluent,'' ``partially fluent,'' and ``fluent'' 
groups, allowing nuanced intervention strategies.
• Regression (Continuous Fluency Scoring): When scoring and rank­
ing students on a continuum, Random Forest and CatBoost out­
performed other models in explaining variance. This approach is 
particularly beneficial for schools interested in detailed, ongoing 
progress-monitoring.
In resource-limited settings where interpretability is paramount and 
data may be less abundant, a simpler yet transparent model (e.g., Lo­
gistic Regression) might suﬃce for a broad fluency classification. For 
schools equipped with robust computing resources and larger datasets, 
ensemble methods (e.g., Random Forest, CatBoost) can yield more nu­
anced insights into each student’s reading strengths. Ultimately, the 
model choice may depend on the school’s hardware constraints, the 
number of audio samples available, and the level of diagnostic gran­
ularity desired.
