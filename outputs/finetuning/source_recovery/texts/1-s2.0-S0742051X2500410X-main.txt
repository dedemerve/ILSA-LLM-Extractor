This study investigates key factors of multicultural teaching self-efficacy (MTSE) among U.S. secondary teachers
using nationally representative data from the 2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS).
Grounded in Bandura’s social cognitive theory, we analyzed factors reflecting professional preparation, school
climate, and teacher sociodemographics, using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic
regression, a machine learning technique. Results revealed six significant factors associated with MTSE:
perceived preparedness in multilingual and multicultural teaching through initial teacher education (ITE),
language-specific training, pedagogy-focused professional development (PD), inclusive school practices, and
teacher race/ethnicity and gender. Findings underscore the critical role of ITE in multicultural/multilingual
education, with language-specific training making a distinct contribution to MTSE. Pedagogical PD and schoolwide diversity-promoting practices were also related to MTSE. The results underscore the importance of a diverse
teaching workforce and the value of leveraging teachers’ lived experiences and identity-informed knowledge to
enrich professional learning of all educators.

1. Introduction
The growing multicultural and multilingual diversity in K–12 classrooms globally makes it essential to prepare teachers to effectively
support diverse student populations for achieving equitable and inclusive education. This urgency is particularly pronounced in the U.S.,
where multilingual learners (MLs) represent one of the fastest-growing
student populations. Yet a troubling gap persists between these classroom realities and teacher preparedness. Many educators report feeling
inadequately prepared for diversity, often approaching it through a
deficit lens that associates cultural and linguistic differences with risk
and challenge (Rowan et al., 2021). The consequences of this preparation gap extend far beyond individual classrooms. When teachers lack
confidence in diverse settings, it contributes to professional burnout,
elevated attrition rates, and ultimately, worsening teacher shortages (cf.
Mahalingappa, 2023). These challenges also restrict students’ opportunities for high-quality learning, particularly for those from minoritized
backgrounds, and may reinforce existing educational inequalities.
Addressing these issues begins with improved teacher preparation
and ongoing professional support, underscoring the importance of

understanding and fostering teachers’ self-efficacy in multicultural
classrooms. This efficacy, conceptualized as multicultural teaching selfefficacy (MTSE), represents a critical but understudied aspect of teacher
effectiveness in diverse classrooms. Grounded in social cognitive theory
(Bandura, 1986), MTSE refers to teachers’ beliefs in their capability to
teach effectively in culturally diverse classrooms (Mou et al., 2024).
MTSE includes their perceived belief in their capacity to recognize and
respond to diverse student strengths and needs, adapt pedagogical
practices, and create inclusive learning environments. The practical
implications of MTSE are significant. Teachers with robust MTSE engage
more confidently with diverse learners and implement culturally and
linguistically relevant, responsive, and sustaining practices that foster
equitable outcomes (Argüello-Gutiérrez et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2024;
Siwatu et al., 2015; Tucker et al., 2005; Young & Young, 2023; Zee &
Koomen, 2016). Conversely, limited MTSE can lead to implicit biases
and reliance on traditional, transmissive pedagogies that fail to equitably support all students (Rowan et al., 2021). Even teachers who are
theoretically aware of diversity-related issues may struggle to translate
their awareness into effective instructional practice without sufficient
self-efficacy.

Despite growing recognition of its importance, MTSE remains
understudied, particularly among in-service teachers. The broad benefits of teacher self-efficacy are well documented across domains.
Research consistently shows that self-efficacious teachers are more
likely to implement effective instructional strategies (Morris et al.,
2017), adopt innovative practices to meet diverse student needs (Cho
et al., 2020; Mahalingappa, 2023; Tschannen-Moran & Hoy, 2001), and
demonstrate greater resilience and commitment to the profession (Zee &
Koomen, 2016; Zhou et al., 2024). However, much of this research focuses on general teaching contexts, overlooking the unique demands of
teaching in culturally and linguistically diverse classrooms (Cheng et al.,
2024; Geerlings et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2022; Schwarzenthal et al., 2023).
Given that self-efficacy is inherently domain-specific, findings from
general teaching efficacy research may not be automatically applied to
multicultural teaching contexts without focused investigation.
This study addresses the critical gap by examining U.S. secondary
teachers’ (grades 7–9) multicultural teaching self-efficacy using nationally representative data from the 2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS). The U.S. offers a particularly compelling
context for this investigation. Its long history of immigration and its
often-contentious language policy landscape, especially the widespread
use of English-only mandates across many states (García & Sung, 2018;
La Salle et al., 2020), shape the linguistic and cultural realities of
classrooms. Additionally, a persistent demographic mismatch exists
between a largely White, monolingual teaching workforce and an
increasingly diverse student population (Dunham et al., 2022; Hansen-Thomas et al., 2016). Together, these factors create unique conditions for understanding the development of MTSE. A focused national
analysis is crucial, as self-efficacy is strongly influenced by local
educational contexts and policies (Klassen et al., 2011), making the U.S.
a critical case study for understanding this global educational concern.
We employed Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator
(LASSO, Tibshirani, 1996) logistic regression, a machine learning
technique that enhances model interpretability and predictive accuracy
by identifying the most relevant variables. Guided by social cognitive
theory (Bandura, 1986) and informed by an extensive body of conceptual and empirical literature, we analyze a comprehensive set of factors
across three domains: professional preparation and training, school
climate and support structures, and teacher sociodemographics.
Through this comprehensive approach, our findings will inform targeted
improvements in teacher education and professional development that
foster educators’ confidence and competence in diverse classroom settings. We investigate the following research questions:

According to social cognitive theory, teachers’ self-efficacy enhance
their goal setting, strategy selection, and persistence in overcoming
challenges (Bandura, 1997; Woolfolk Hoy et al., 2009). In diverse
classrooms, stronger MTSE is associated with setting higher learning
expectations for multilingual and minoritized learners (Callaway, 2017;
Siwatu, 2007), greater willingness to implement and refine culturally
and linguistically sustaining practices (Paris & Alim, 2017), and
increased resilience when initial attempts fall short (Holzberger et al.,
2013; Zee & Koomen, 2016). Teachers with strong MTSE tend to
interpret setbacks as opportunity to adjust their pedagogy rather than as
signals to abandon it, which supports the enactment of responsive and
adaptive practice. This pathway helps explain consistent empirical links
between MTSE and richer instructional repertoires in multicultural
settings and close the belief-practice gap (Siwatu, 2011).
This theoretical framework structures our analysis of MTSE development by organizing the associated factors around the three interconnected domains of Bandura’s model: teacher preparation and
professional development (behavioral factors), school climate and
institutional support (environmental factors), and individual teacher
characteristics (personal factors).
2.1. Teacher education and professional development
Both initial teacher education (ITE) and ongoing professional
development (PD) are foundational to the development of MTSE.
Effectively addressing classroom diversity is a complex pedagogical task
that demands comprehensive preparation and sustained professional
training (OECD, 2019a).
Initial Teacher Education. Teachers who enter the profession with
strong preparation are more likely to demonstrate higher self-efficacy
(Morris et al., 2017). The knowledge and skills acquired during initial
training can influence teaching differently than those developed
through subsequent in-service experience (Tschannen-Moran et al.,
1998). We examine ITE through two complementary dimensions: formal
coursework and training and teachers’ self-perceived preparedness to
teach in multicultural and multilingual classrooms.
Formal Education and Training. Despite growing awareness of the
need to prepare teachers for diversity, evidence on the effectiveness of
ITE in this domain remains limited and inconsistent (Koukoulidis et al.,
2024). A critical gap exists in how ITE programs address linguistic diversity, particularly in recognizing, responding to, advocating for, and
adapting instruction to meet multilingual learners’ needs.
Language-specific preparation, such as specialized training in English as
an additional language (EAL) or bilingual education, is often marginalized, even within broader multicultural training. This “poverty of
language learning” (Pettit, 2011, p. 128) has tangible consequences.
Nearly one-third of U.S. secondary teachers in PISA 2018 expressed a
strong need for additional EAL training. Many teachers report low
confidence in meeting the needs of MLs (Zhou et al., 2024). The impact
extends beyond individual confidence: teacher turnover is significantly
higher in schools serving large concentrations of MLs (cf. Sutcher et al.,
2019). To address this gap, we examine how both general multicultural
training and language-specific preparation during ITE relate to MTSE
development.
Perceived Preparedness. In addition to formal coursework, teachers’
perceived preparedness provides important insight into the quality and
impact of their ITE experience (OECD, 2019a). Teachers who believe
they acquired relevant skills and knowledge during training tend to
develop stronger self-efficacy (Darling-Hammond et al., 2002; Gorski
et al., 2012; Morris et al., 2017). Conversely, those who feel underprepared are more likely to experience reduced confidence, potentially
contributing to burnout, attrition, and broader teacher shortages (cf.
Mahalingappa, 2023). While this association has been established
generally, its specific relevance to multicultural teaching contexts

2. Teacher self-efficacy in multicultural classrooms and
contributing factors
Understanding how teachers develop MTSE and identifying its key
factors is essential for equipping educators to effectively navigate
culturally and linguistically diverse classrooms. This study is grounded
in Bandura’s social cognitive theory, which provides a comprehensive
framework for understanding how teachers develop confidence in their
ability to effectively teach in multicultural and multilingual classrooms.
The theory conceptualizes human functioning as the product of dynamic
reciprocal interactions among three sets of factors: personal characteristics (including cognitive, affective, and biological events), behavioral
patterns, and environmental influences (Bandura, 1986). This triadic
reciprocity suggests that teacher self-efficacy is not merely an individual
trait but is shaped by the complex interplay between teachers’ personal

Professional Development. PD is one of the most frequently studied
dimensions related to teacher self-efficacy. According to Bandura
(1977), PD can enhance self-efficacy through three primary mechanisms: mastery experiences (direct practice), vicarious learning (observation of others), and social persuasion (constructive feedback).
Research consistently shows that high-quality, targeted PD is associated
with stronger teaching efficacy across various domains, including
multicultural education. However, most existing studies focus on
pre-service contexts (Ash & Maguire, 2023). The relationship between
in-service PD and MTSE remains understudied and largely addressed
through qualitative approaches (Agirdag et al., 2016). Quantitative
research in this area is still emerging, with a few notable contributions.
For example, Choi and Lee (2020) found that multicultural education PD
significantly contributed to MTSE among secondary teachers in South
Korea and the United States. Similarly, Cheng et al. (2024) reported a
positive link between such PD and MTSE in U.S. non-STEM secondary
teachers. However, these studies did not disaggregate different forms of
multicultural education PD. Building on these findings, we distinguish
between two complementary forms of PD: PD in instruction-focused
multicultural/multilingual teaching and interpersonal-focused PD in
cross-cultural communication.1 This distinction allows for more
nuanced understanding of how different types of professional learning
contribute to MTSE development.

School Diversity Practices. Beyond the structural composition of the
student body, how schools respond to and engage with diversity may be
a more salient factor in shaping teacher self-efficacy in multicultural
contexts (Schwarzenthal et al., 2023). Whole-school diversity practices,
such as inclusive pedagogical and curricular approaches, can foster a
climate of critical reflection and cultural openness (Parkhouse et al.,
2019). Teachers working in culturally responsive school environments
may build MTSE through vicarious experiences, including learning from
established practices and role models who effectively engage with student diversity (Schwarzenthal et al., 2023). Supporting this perspective,
Cheng et al. (2024) found that the implementation of diversity practices
was positively associated with MTSE, particularly among STEM
teachers.
2.2.2. Instructional support systems: feedback and autonomy
Feedback Mechanisms. Feedback represents a powerful mechanism
for building self-efficacy through social persuasion (Bandura, 1977).
Schools with robust feedback cultures, characterized by professional
learning communities and systematic feedback networks, can foster
professional growth and instructional improvement (Choi, 2023; OECD,
2020). That said, the relationship between feedback and teacher
self-efficacy is complex and multifaceted. While feedback from diverse
sources can contribute to enhanced self-efficacy, the absence of feedback
or a broader culture of constructive feedback can undermine it (Clark &
Newberry, 2019; OECD, 2020; Smith et al., 2019). The effects of feedback are not uniformly positive; rather, the source of verbal persuasion
seems to play a crucial role (Tschannen-Moran et al., 1998). Peer feedback from colleagues showed stronger associations with teacher
self-efficacy than feedback from administrators or external evaluators
(OECD, 2020; O’Neill & Stephenson, 2012). Thus, we consider multiple
sources of instructional feedback, including input from colleagues,
school leaders, and external observers, not only to assess whether
feedback contributes to MTSE, but also to examine the distinct contribution of each source.
Professional Autonomy. Teachers’ sense of autonomy is a key
element of professional agency, providing teachers with freedom to set
instructional goals, select teaching methods, and apply educational
strategies (OECD, 2021). Instructional autonomy empowers teachers to
take ownership of classroom decisions and outcomes (Skaalvik &
Skaalvik, 2014). While underexplored, existing studies suggest that
greater instructional autonomy is associated with strong teaching
self-efficacy (OECD, 2020; Tschannen-Moran et al., 1998). In multicultural contexts, the potential effect of autonomy may be even more
pronounced. When provided with sufficient autonomy, teachers are
more likely to develop confidence in navigating student diversity and
implementing adaptive teaching practices. This relationship is supported by Choi and Mao (2021)’s cross-national study involving 47
countries, which found that empirical evidence multicultural PD was
less likely to translate into practice without adequate instructional
autonomy.

2.2. School climate and institutional support
While individual teachers remain central to effective education, their
practices and beliefs should be understood within the broader school
context that shapes teaching and learning conditions (Tschannen-Moran
et al., 1998; Vanbuel & Van den Branden, 2021). Understanding context
is crucial in multicultural and multilingual settings, where institutional
power dynamics often pose distinct challenges for students from
non-dominant backgrounds (Jang & Brutt-Griffler, 2023). Despite its
importance, relatively few studies have examined MTSE in relation to
school-level factors and existing findings are mixed (Cheng et al., 2024;
Romijn et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2024).
2.2.1. Multicultural school climate
Student Diversity. Previous studies have found mixed results
regarding the link between student diversity and teacher self-efficacy.
Some evidence suggests that teachers working in more diverse environments report higher levels of diversity-related efficacy, possibly due
to increased opportunities for direct engagement and adaptive learning.
This trend has been observed across various conceptualizations of student diversity and across cultural contexts, including studies of first- and
second-generation immigrant populations across 46 OECD countries
(Schwarzenthal et al., 2023), ethnic diversity in the Netherlands
(Geerlings et al., 2018), and broader sociocultural diversity, including
socioeconomic status, linguistic variation, and cultural distance, in England, Italy, the Netherlands, and Poland (Romijn et al., 2020). Yet not
all findings support this relationship. Gutentag et al. (2018) found no
significant association between the proportion of immigrant students
and immigration-related efficacy among Israeli primary and secondary
teachers. To extend this line of inquiry, we operationalize student diversity through the proportion of MLs in U.S. secondary classrooms,
allowing us to explore its relationship with MTSE in a context-specific
way.

3. Methods
3.1. Participants and data
This study utilized data from the 2018 U.S. TALIS, administered by
the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD).
TALIS is designed to provide internationally comparable data on
teaching practices, instructional methods, and learning environments of
lower-secondary teachers (corresponding to grades 7, 8, and 9 in the U.
S.) in regular schools in 48 countries and economies (NCES, 2020;
OECD, 2019b). The present analysis included all 2,561 teachers from
165 schools who participated in the U.S. TALIS 2018. Of these, 66 %
identified as female and 34 % as male. On average, participants were 43
years old and had approximately 14 years of teaching experience.
3.2. Measures
Outcome variable. Teachers’ multicultural teaching self-efficacy
(MTSE) was measured using five items. Teachers rated the extent to
which they felt capable of performing the following tasks in culturally
diverse classrooms: (1) coping with the challenges of a multicultural
classroom, (2) adapting their teaching to the cultural diversity of students, (3) ensuring that students with and without a migrant background work together, (4) raising students’ awareness of cultural
differences, and (5) reducing ethnic stereotyping among students.

total variance. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency
(Cronbach’s α = .83). Higher scores indicate greater perceived instructional autonomy.
Sociodemographic factors. Teacher-level variables included race/
ethnicity (1 = racially/ethnically minoritized teachers, 0 = White
teachers), gender (1 = female, 0 = male), years of teaching experience
(continuous), and highest level of education attained (1 = master’s degree or higher, 0 = bachelor’s degree or lower).

3.3. Statistical analysis
We conducted a one-way repeated measures Analysis of Variance
(ANOVA) to examine differences across the five MTSE subscales, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc comparisons to identify specific
pairwise differences. Although the TALIS data are cross-sectional, this
analytic approach was appropriate because the five subscales were
measured concurrently within each respondent.
Then, we employed Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator
(LASSO; Tibshirani, 1996) logistic regression for variable selection to
identify the most influential variables of MTSE. LASSO logistic regression applies an L1 (Manhattan norm) penalty to the loss function, systematically shrinking some regression coefficients toward zero while
retaining those with substantial predictive contributions (Hastie et al.,
2015; Tibshirani, 1996). This regularization approach effectively differentiates between significant and non-significant variables while
reducing overfitting risk.
LASSO logistic regression was used as an alternative to traditional
regression and subset selection methods because of its ability to perform
both variable selection and regularization (Tibshirani, 1996). In models
with many interrelated variables, conventional approaches often suffer
from overfitting and multicollinearity, limiting interpretability and
generalizability (Hastie et al., 2015). LASSO mitigates these issues by
penalizing the absolute size of coefficients, shrinking less relevant ones
toward zero and retaining only the most informative features. This
yields a more parsimonious and interpretable model, particularly useful
for identifying key factors (Zou & Hastie, 2005) associated with multicultural teaching self-efficacy.
We included sixteen candidate variables based on theoretical relevance and prior literature. The use of LASSO with this number of variables is justifiable when considering the concept of events per variable
(EPV). A commonly recommended threshold is at least ten events (i.e.,
outcome = 1) and ten non-events (i.e., outcome = 0) per variable,
suggesting a minimum of 320 observations for sixteen variables in a
balanced binary outcome (Peduzzi et al., 1996). In our analytic sample,
we had 1,016 teachers were classified into the high MTSE category
(events) and 1,031 into the low MTSE category (non-events). This corresponds to approximately 64 events and 64 non-events per variable,
yielding an EPV ratio well above the recommended minimum. While
LASSO can perform reasonably well with lower EPV, especially for
variable selection rather than inference, simulation studies have shown
that performance tends to decline in terms of stability and accuracy as
EPV decreases (van Smeden et al., 2019). Importantly, LASSO tends to
exclude weaker variables by shrinking their coefficients to zero, thereby
reducing the risk of overfitting - even when EPV guidelines are only
moderately met.
To mitigate overfitting and assess model generalizability, we
randomly split the dataset into a training set (n = 1,281, 50 %) and a test
set (n = 1,280, 50 %). Model selection was optimized using three
different tuning strategies to ensure robust variable selection and model
generalizability: (1) cross-validation (CV) LASSO using 10-fold CV over
a grid of 100 penalty values (λ) to minimize test deviance using the
minimum CV error (CV-min) rule (Friedman et al., 2010); (2) minimum
Bayesian Information Criterion (minBIC) LASSO (Zhang et al., 2010);
and (3) Adaptive LASSO, using initial ridge estimates as adaptive
weights (Zou, 2006). These methods were implemented and compared
using Stata’s lasso suite (Version 17; lasso logit), with evaluations based
on model fit, predictive accuracy, parsimony, post-selection deviance
and deviance ratio, variable selection stability, interpretability, and
computational efficiency. The optimal model was selected based on
these comprehensive evaluation criteria.

4.1. Teachers’ perceived multicultural teaching self-efficacy
A total of 1,016 teachers were classified in the high MTSE group,
while 1,031 were classified in the low MTSE group. Lower secondary
teachers reported a relatively high level of MTSE, with an average selfefficacy score of 11.33 (SD = 2.29), and a range from 4.88 to 15.26 (see
Table 2). While overall confidence in teaching in multicultural classrooms was strong, a one-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed statistically significant differences across the five MTSE sub-scales, F(4,
8165) = 36.51, p < .001. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni
correction indicated that teachers reported significantly higher selfefficacy in reducing ethnic stereotyping among students (M = 2.96,
SD = .82) compared to raising awareness of cultural differences (M =
2.78, SD = .85), t = 10.54, p < .001. The estimated effect size for this
contrast was small, d = .22. Confidence was also relatively higher for
ensuring that students with and without a migrant background work
together (M = 2.87, SD = .87). These findings suggest that teachers feel
more confident managing interpersonal dynamics and addressing visible
forms of bias than engaging in the pedagogical practices necessary for
deeper multicultural teaching or adapting instruction to diverse student
needs.

Convergent Variable Selection. To identify variables most strongly

Notably, language-specialized training during ITE emerged as a
critical leverage point for strengthening MTSE. It carried substantially
greater weight than general multicultural preparation, which was not
statistically significant. This pattern offers compelling empirical support
for a necessary language turn in teacher education. Language-informed
and multilingual perspectives should be embedded across all teacher
training, rather than being limited to language specialists. This shift
aligns with the view that knowledge about language and language
learning should be “standard knowledge for all teachers” (Nieto & Bode,
2018, p. 194). It also reinforces longstanding concerns about inadequate
language preparation in teacher education. Over a decade ago, Pettit
(2011) highlighted in-service teachers’ limited understanding of language learning and instruction. Lucas and Villegas (2013) also called for
linguistically responsive teaching, noting the frequent neglect of linguistic issues within culturally responsive or closely related teaching
approaches. Despite these calls, students’ linguistic capitals remain
undervalued and neglected (Jang & Brutt-Griffler, 2019). Our findings
suggest that while general multicultural education is important, it may
be insufficient without explicit and sustained attention to the multilingual realities of today’s classrooms. Every teacher needs to regard linguistic and cultural diversity as central to their professional
responsibilities, not as peripheral concerns.

4.2.4. Sensitivity analyses
As a sensitivity check, we tested alternative thresholds using TALISdefined cut-points (≥10 %, ≥30 %, and ≥60 %). Results remained
consistent across thresholds in both effect direction and statistical significance. Full results of these sensitivity analyses are provided in the
Supplementary Materials (Table S1). In an additional exploratory
sensitivity analysis, we estimated linear LASSO models using the
continuous MTSE score, despite its non-normal distribution. These
models selected a somewhat larger set of factors (9–11 across selection
criteria) than the logistic LASSO models, although the factors were
consistently retained. Model fit was modest (R2 = 0.09–0.14), but the
stability of key associations reinforces the robustness of the primary
findings. Penalized ordinal logistic regression was not pursued, as the
MTSE construct does not represent a true ordinal scale.

5.2. Pedagogically grounded professional development over awareness
building
Teachers’ ongoing engagement in PD was positively associated with
their MTSE. This aligns with prior research linking multicultural education PD to greater teacher self-efficacy in diverse classrooms (Cheng
et al., 2024; Choi & Lee, 2020). More importantly, our results emphasize
that it is the nature of PD, not merely participation, that is critical.
Participation in multicultural or multilingual teaching PD was positively
associated with higher MTSE, whereas PD in cross-cultural communication showed no significant relationship. These findings mirror
observed patterns in teachers’ sense of efficacy in multicultural classrooms, showing greater teacher confidence in managing interpersonal
dynamics and addressing overt bias than in developing students’ cultural awareness and adapting instruction to diversity. Such a contrast
suggests that pedagogically grounded PD or training directly aligned
with classroom instruction might contribute more effectively to MTSE
than general cultural competence training.
This distinction aligns with Gorski’s (2009) categorization of
multicultural PD into different approaches, ranging from liberal
frameworks focusing on cultural sensitivity or celebrating diversity to
more critical approaches centered on developing multicultural competence and addressing systemic inequities through pedagogy. Gorski reported that the latter, which involves equipping teachers with concrete
instructional strategies, is more likely to build efficacy. While interpersonal or awareness-based PD may support broader understanding, it
may not adequately prepare teachers for specific pedagogical challenges, thus failing to translate into teaching-specific self-efficacy. In
short, developing MTSE requires structural support through targeted PD
that moves beyond awareness-building to focus on concrete instructional strategies for culturally and linguistically sustaining teaching.

5. Discussion and conclusion
The current study identified six key factors significantly associated
with secondary teachers’ multicultural teaching self-efficacy, spanning
initial preparation, ongoing professional development, multicultural
school climate, and sociodemographic characteristics. Teachers reported notably higher MTSE when they: (1) felt well-prepared for
multilingual and multicultural teaching at the outset of their careers, (2)
received formal training in EAL or bilingual education, (3) participated
in PD focused on multilingual and multicultural teaching, (4) worked in
schools committed to inclusive practices, (5) identified as racially/
ethnically minoritized, and (6) identified as female.
5.1. Initial teacher education and the critical language turn
Among all variables, in-service teachers’ perceived preparedness—how well they believed their ITE equipped them to teach in
multicultural and multilingual classrooms—was most strongly associated with MTSE. Additionally, specialized training in EAL or bilingual
education during ITE was significantly linked to higher self-efficacy.
These findings highlight the foundational and enduring role of early
preparation in contributing to teachers’ confidence for diverse classrooms. Longitudinal research by Künsting et al. (2016) similarly shows
that perceived preparedness in the first year of teaching predicted
instructional quality even a decade later. This becomes particularly
critical given broader concerns around teacher retention and equity.
Nearly 50 % of U.S. teachers leave the profession within their first five
years and a trend particularly evident in schools serving ethnically and
socioeconomically minoritized students (cf. Young & Young, 2023).

5.3. Leveraging the experience- and identity-informed knowledge of
teachers
Teachers’ racial/ethnic background was positively associated with
MTSE. Specifically, teachers from racially/ethnically minoritized groups
reported higher self-efficacy than their White counterparts. This pattern
is particularly salient given the persistent cultural and linguistic
mismatch between an increasingly diverse student population and a
teaching workforce that remains predominantly White, monolingual,
and middle-class (Dunham et al., 2022; Hansen-Thomas et al., 2016).
Research indicates that mainstream teachers often perceive

culturally and linguistically diverse students as fundamentally other or
different from themselves. (Gallagher & Scrivner, 2024). This perception of otherness may contribute to lower MTSE among teachers from
dominant backgrounds. In contrast, the higher levels of MTSE reported
by racially/ethnically minoritized teachers likely reflects their lived
experiences navigating fluid cultural and linguistic identities that often
mirror those of their diverse students. These teachers bring cultural and
linguistic assets to their practice and often share personal understanding
of marginalization, enabling them to connect more authentically and
confidently with diverse students.
Female teachers demonstrated higher levels of MTSE compared to
their male counterparts. This pattern appears to relate to distinct professional and social experiences within the education profession. Despite
comprising the majority of the teaching workforce, women continue to
navigate gendered expectations and power structures embedded within
the profession. As Perera et al. (2019) note, gender socialization often
results in differential opportunities for professional skill development.
This structural positioning may allow them to better align their perspectives with the realities faced by diverse students.
These demographic findings reinforce the importance of diversifying
the teaching workforce. Equally important is acknowledging and
leveraging the lived experience- and identity-informed knowledge and
perspectives of current racially/ethnically minoritized teachers to
enrich the professional learning of all educators. Rather than treating
demographic differences as static or deterministic, these teachers should
be recognized critical professional assets in enhancing multicultural and
multilingual teaching within both preparation programs and in-service
school contexts.

Although we identified a significant relationship between initial
language-specific training and MTSE, the TALIS dataset lacked information on language-related PD. Consequently, we were unable to
determine whether similar benefits of specialized training extend to inservice professional training. Moreover, student-level factors are known
to significantly influence teachers’ self-efficacy (Morris et al., 2017), but
were not included in our analysis due to data limitations. Further
investigation is needed to include these variables and explore MTSE
more holistically.
Our study revealed varying effects of different content areas within
teacher preparation, both in ITE and PD. While we did not examine the
mechanisms behind these differences, prior research highlights that PD
effectiveness depends not only on content, though content is a key
driver, but also on format, duration, and delivery method (cf. Schwarzenthal et al., 2023). To complement our findings and offer more concrete guidance for supporting teacher development, investigation is
needed into which specific combinations of these features optimize
MTSE development across different teacher preparation and professional development contexts.
The absence of a significant relationship between feedback culture
and MTSE may point to issues with feedback quality. Research suggests
that harsh, vague, or non-constructive feedback can undermine selfefficacy, particularly when the source lacks credibility or trustworthiness (Bandura, 1986; Tschannen-Moran et al., 1998). In our study,
feedback was measured using a binary indicator of whether teachers
received input from particular sources, which may have overlooked
qualitative dimensions. Examining both the sources and the quality of
feedback would offer a more comprehensive understanding of its role in
supporting MTSE development. As such, the null findings should be
interpreted with caution, as they may reflect measurement limitations
rather than a true absence of association.
Similarly, a non-significant effect of instructional autonomy may
reflect its role as a mediator rather than a direct factor of MTSE. For
example, Choi and Mao (2021) found that autonomy mediated the
relationship between PD in multicultural education and MTSE. Investigating such pathways could clarify its function more fully. Furthermore,
the general indicators used to assess classroom autonomy in this study
may not capture the specific aspects most relevant to multicultural
teaching contexts. While our study measured instructional autonomy as
teachers’ perceived control over instructional decisions, recent research
has emphasized another critical dimension, namely, teacher autonomous motivation. This form of autonomy has been shown to support
both professional efficacy and psychological well-being (cf. Aelterman
et al., 2019; Katz & Moè, 2024; Reeve & Cheon, 2014). Future research
might therefore examine multiple dimensions of teacher autonomy to
better understand how they interact with MTSE. As with feedback, this
null result may also stem from measurement limitations and should be
interpreted with caution rather than assumed to reflect a true absence of
effect.
While teachers’ self-efficacy is crucial to their practice, translating it
into effective classroom instruction remains challenging. Investigating
how teachers enact their MTSE in daily practice, the conditions that
facilitate this process, and how they adapt instruction to respond to
cultural and linguistic diversity would enhance our understanding of its
practical impact.
Although teachers are nested within schools, we did not explicitly
account for clustering during the variable selection process because the
LASSO logistic regression used in this study does not readily support
multilevel modeling. As a result, the standard errors may be underestimated, and the estimates may be influenced by within-school
dependence. We acknowledge this as a methodological limitation.

5.4. Cultivating systematic school responses to diversity
Beyond individual characteristics and professional training, the
broader school environment was strongly associated with higher levels
of MTSE. Teachers in schools with strong diversity practices reported
higher levels of self-efficacy. These practices included supporting student identity-based activities, organizing multicultural events,
addressing discrimination, and integrating global perspectives into the
curriculum. Importantly, the contrast with the non-significant effect of
school demographics affirms that what matters is not how diverse a
school is but how schools systematically respond to and engage with
such diversity. This underscores the critical role of intentional, schoollevel initiatives and structures in fostering inclusive practices that support teachers’ MTSE. Without such support, even well-intentioned educators may struggle to teach effectively in diverse classrooms (Ji et al.,
2025; Koukoulidis et al., 2024).
5.5. Limitations and future research directions
Our findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations.
First, while self-efficacy is best understood through both the quantity
and quality of individuals’ experiences (Morris et al., 2017), our study
relied primarily on cross-sectional survey data, which limits our understanding of the qualitative aspects and developmental nature of
teachers’ experiences. Additionally, the exploratory nature of the data
and analyses precludes any inference of causality. To address these
limitations, future research would benefit from mixed-methods or longitudinal designs that examine not only whether teachers have these
experiences, but also how they engage with them and how such
engagement contributes to sustained growth in MTSE.
While preparing teachers for multicultural and multilingual classrooms is a significant global concern, our study focuses specifically on
the U.S. context. Given the country’s unique immigration history and
policy landscape surrounding cultural and linguistic diversity, as discussed earlier, our findings may not be directly transferable to other
national contexts. Future research should pursue cross-national
comparative studies or nation-specific analyses to better understand

Lastly, we highlight the potential value of implementing comprehensive diversity practices to actively engage with and celebrate cultural
and linguistic diversity. In parallel, efforts to diversify the teaching

intentional strategies to recognize, support, and leverage the cultural

to the profession. By addressing these areas, educational systems may be
better positioned to support the development of teachers’ MTSE, ultimately enhancing educational outcomes for all students in our increasingly diverse society.

5.6. Implications for policy and practice
This research reveals several important implications for educational
policy and practice. First, the association between language-specific
preparation and MTSE suggest that teacher preparation programs
could benefit from prioritizing language-informed pedagogical approaches throughout their curricula, moving beyond treating linguistic
diversity as a specialized concern. Lucas and Villegas’s (2013) framework for linguistically responsive teaching (LRT) offers a robust foundation for such preparation, emphasizing key components: valuing
linguistic diversity and advocating for MLs, developing sociolinguistic
awareness, applying principles of second language (L2) principles,
analyzing classroom language demands, and implementing scaffolding
strategies.
Building on this foundation, our findings on the role of school diversity practices point to the potential value of preparing teachers not
only for classroom-level responsiveness, but also for school-wide and
systemic advocacy. Teacher education programs could consider
fostering teachers’ capacity to participate in collective advocacy efforts
that advance linguistically responsive practices institution wide. This
could include engaging teachers in critical reflection and analysis of
structural inequities embedded in school policies, curricula, and practices, while collaboratively working toward institutional change. Such
preparation may help cultivate a shared responsibility for creating and
sustaining equitable learning environments, while also potentially supporting teachers in responding more effectively to student diversity
within their classrooms.
Furthermore, we suggest expanding beyond traditional L2 approaches to incorporate translingual pedagogies that recognize and
validate how MLs naturally use their complete linguistic repertoires as
instructional resources. This entails equipping teachers not only to
analyze discipline-specific language demands and design responsive
scaffolds, but also to strategically leverage students’ full linguistic repertoires as assets in instruction. Grounded in both individual pedagogical development and collaborative, systemic engagement, this
expanded approach could better position teachers to enact adaptive and
equity-oriented instruction across diverse educational contexts.
Second, our finding of a positive association between pedagogicallyfocused PD and MTSE suggests that school districts might consider
investing in pedagogically grounded PD that is directly applicable to
multicultural and multilingual classroom contexts, rather than relying
on general cultural awareness training. This approach aligns with Gorski’s (2009) framework for critical multicultural competency, which
emphasizes pedagogical knowledge for diversity, understanding systemic inequities, and action-oriented strategies for inclusive curriculum
and assessment.
Building on Gorski’s framework and our findings, effective PD could
usefully incorporate Bandura’s (1977) mechanisms for building
self-efficacy: mastery experiences, vicarious learning, and social
persuasion. Structurally, programs should offer sustained,
job-embedded mastery experiences that allow teachers to apply and
refine pedagogical content knowledge for diversity within their own
classrooms. These experiences might include analyzing curricula for
cultural and linguistic demands, implementing adaptive instructional
strategies, and designing scaffolded instruction. The extended LRT
framework can serve as a meaningful guide for shaping this in-service
professional learning. These mastery experiences should be coupled
with critically reflective professional learning communities that facilitate vicarious learning through peer observation and provide constructive feedback. Through this integrated and practice-embedded
