A B S T R A C T  
Search engine users, when presented with multiple web search results, must be able to flexibly navigate 
dependent on the type of search tasks. Users who demonstrate inappropriate navigation behavior would likely 
fail to obtain target information. However, very few studies have examined users’ web navigation patterns and 
their effectiveness in completing different types of search tasks in multi-layered hypertext environments. To fill 
these research gaps, we used data composing a sample of 1408 adults in the United States and the United 
Kingdom who completed both a specific information-locating task and an amorphous information-evaluating 
task when participating in the Programme for the International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) 
2012. We extracted participants’ clickstream data as well as their performance on the two tasks from recorded 
web navigation logs. Results from a series of latent class analyses and full-path sequence analyses showed five 
distinct behavioral patterns when participants performed the two tasks: of which, Flimsy, Breadth-first, Laborious, 
and Sampling patterns were revealed in both tasks, while the Satisficing pattern emerged exclusively in the 
specific information-locating task. Regarding pattern effectiveness, the Sampling pattern group outperformed 
other navigation pattern groups in the amorphous information-evaluating task, whereas the Satisficing pattern 
group performed best in the specific information-locating task.   
1. Introduction 
In today’s global information society, searching for information on 
the World Wide Web (WWW) to solve real-world problems has 
increasingly evolved into an indispensable part of people’s everyday life. 
Internet World Stats (2021) estimates that 65.6% of the total population 
around the world are Internet users and a large proportion of these 
people frequently utilize the WWW for information-seeking purposes. 
For example, people who have medical problems may use Internet 
search engines to initiate search queries for treatments. After entering 
search terms in a selected search engine (e.g., Google) and sending them 
off, people are usually exposed to a so-called search engine results page 
(SERP) with a list of search result links. Diverse search results contain a 
mixture of information with variant specificities such as timeliness and 
authority, which makes online information at different levels of use­
fulness, suitability, and trustworthiness to information problems. In 
consideration of their information needs such as acquiring the most 
professional and/or the most up-to-date medical advice, web users 
navigate search results to identify target information (Brand-Gruwel 
et al., 2009; Walraven et al., 2009). 
An important distinction can be made between single- and multi- 
layered hypertext environments in which web information is distrib­
uted (Chang & Chen, 2011; Cho & Afflerbach, 2017). In the case of 
single-layered hypertext environments, information is visibly displayed 
on homepages after web users click on search results links on SERPs. In 
contrast, navigating for desired information in multi-layered hypertext 
environments is more complex because the information is typically 
distributed through multi-layered hypertexts. This means that some 
information, which might be both helpful and necessary to solve infor­
mation problems, is hidden beneath a series of hypertexts. As a result, 
web users engage in a multi-layered navigation process to access in­

information distribution (Basso et al., 2016; Tierney, 1994). To sum­
marize, navigating web search results involves selecting and accessing a 
series of web pages that are either directly or indirectly linked to SERPs 
(Blom et al., 2018). Web users’ behavioral patterns that orient them­
selves in diverse web search results to acquire target information (which 
we labeled “web navigation patterns”) are the subject of the current 
research. 
Past research that investigated how web users deal with multiple 
web search results can be primarily grouped into two categories. One 
category is stopping rules use during web information search (e.g., 
Browne et al., 2007; Browne & Pitts, 2004; Pennington & Kelton, 2016). 
Instead of modeling how people seek information, stopping rules answer 
why people terminate their search behavior at some point. Different 
standards that web users invoke to assess the sufficiency of the infor­
mation gathered and to terminate their information search behavior are 
referred to as stopping rules (Browne et al., 2007; Pennington & Kelton, 
2016). Given the implicitness of stopping rules, past studies, with a 
comparatively small number of participants, usually gathered written or 
oral answers to open-ended questions and coded individual responses 
into stopping rules (Browne et al., 2007; Pennington & Kelton, 2016). 
The second category is information foraging on the Web (e.g., List & 
Alexander, 2017; Pirolli, 2005; Reader & Payne, 2007; Wilkinson et al., 
2012). Drawing upon theories in evolutionary biology, research on on­
line text navigation has revealed how people navigate multiple 
non-hyperlinked texts (List & Alexander, 2017) and single-layered hy­
pertexts (e.g., Reader & Payne, 2007; Wilkinson et al., 2012). As 
compared to research on stopping rules, the research on information 
foraging on the Web studied information foraging patterns according to 
manifest source access behaviors. That is, analyzing navigation behav­
iors, such as detecting sequential patterns or computing association 
rules, is of particular importance for identifying information foraging 
patterns (Eichmann et al., 2020; Gabadinho et al., 2011; Liu et al., 
2004). 
Yet, a thorough review of the literature on web search results navi­
gation suggests that relatively limited work has examined web naviga­
tion patterns in multi-layered hypertext environments (e.g., Juvina & 
van Oostendorp, 2006; List & Alexander, 2017; Liu et al., 2004). Espe­
cially, it is still largely unknown what navigation patterns are likely to 
emerge in multi-layered hypertext environments and which specific 
navigation patterns are beneficial regarding different types of search 
tasks. According to theories and research within the field of information 
foraging on the Web, this study analyzed adults’ clickstream data from 
navigation logs of two web information search tasks in multi-layered 
hypertext environments. The purposes of the current study are 
twofold: 1) to identify adults’ web navigation patterns based on their 
sequences of actions for completing two types of search tasks, and 2) to 
examine how identified web navigation patterns are associated with 
adults’ tasks performance. 
1.1. Web search results navigation 
Over the past three decades, much research attention has been paid 
to web information search (e.g., Browne & Pitts, 2004; Browne et al., 
2007; List & Alexander, 2017; Puntambekar & Stylianou, 2005; Tsai 
et al., 2012). Navigating web search results is a crucial step to web in­
formation search, given that from the large number of web pages 
returned by search engines, only a limited set contains information that 
is adequate for search tasks (Kammerer & Gerjets, 2014). In addition, 
most homepages connected to SERP links are not only nested as pieces of 
information within sites, but they also embed many hypertexts that lead 
to other embedded web pages to provide further explanation, excep­
tions, examples, or increasingly sophisticated knowledge (Cho & 
Afflerbach, 2017). 
Compared to web information search process wherein web users 
primarily need to decide what search keywords are suitable for defined 
search task scenarios (Cho, 2014), web search results navigation centers 
exclusively on SERPs-relevant processes. It describes any movement 
away from SERPs toward one of the sites listed (Jenkins et al., 2003; 
Lawless & Schrader, 2008). Typically, scanning and skimming SERPs 
lead one to selectively click on some links so that web users could dive 
deep into relevant web pages information (Brand-Gruwel et al., 2017; 
Walraven et al., 2009). Later, web users may stay on the currently dis­
played homepage and move into hidden web pages by clicking on hy­
pertexts that are nested within the homepage. A grouping of the 
homepage and its nested web pages that jointly provide expository in­
formation is generically referred to as an information source (Connolly 
& Thorn, 1987), which is equivalent to what some have termed as an 
information patch (Pirolli, 2005). In contrast, in single-layered hyper­
text environments, each homepage is considered as an information 
source in the absence of nested web pages. Afterward, according to 
navigation decisions (e.g., rejecting or adopting visited information 
sources), web users might return to SERPs and move to a different in­
formation source or they would alternatively terminate their underway 
search (Salmer´on & García, 2011; Tu et al., 2008). Therefore, web 
search results navigation can be described as a situation that involves 
choosing and approaching between a large number of alternative web 
pages from SERPs in an interconnected information space. 
In this study, we conceive web navigation patterns as different 
streams of concrete action sequences of navigating web pages (Savo­
lainen, 2016). In this regard, web navigation patterns provide an ac­
count for how web users, while navigating multiple information sources, 
adaptively choose to shift to other links in which to continue searching 
for information or to terminate information acquisition process after 
information-seeking activities within chosen links. These clickable links 
might be within the same information source or across different infor­
mation sources. Accordingly, web navigation patterns can be unraveled 
by investigating the series of links that web users click on when they 
filter out desired information from accessible web pages (Bates, 1979). 
In the below, we reviewed web navigation patterns established in pre­
vious studies. 
1.1.1. Web navigation patterns in single-layered hypertext environments 
A prominent theory accounting for web users’ navigation behavior 
on search results is the information foraging theory (IFT; Pirolli & Card, 
1999), which has been proposed for decades in the field of information 
foraging on the Web. IFT assumes that behavioral mechanisms used by 
human ancestors to forage for food are a valuable resource for under­
standing how people forage for online information (Pirolli, 2005; Pirolli 
& Card, 1999). A central component of IFT is the information patch 
model, which explains how long a user ought to stay in an information 
patch before moving on to other information patches. The model pre­
dicts that web users constantly assess web pages’ potential usefulness 
and the cost of browsing them when foraging in an information patch; 
when the rate of gain from the current information patch falls below 
some tolerated rate, web users will move to a new information patch 
(Dennis & Taylor, 2006). In line with this prediction, when navigating 
multiple information patches, web users are expected to maximize the 
value of information gained as a function of navigation cost (Pirolli, 
2005). In other words, web users would have to balance the improved 
information accuracy, completeness, and usefulness against acquisition 
costs (e.g., increased time, effort, or cognitive resources). As such, 
different web navigation patterns that respond to multiple web search 
results produced by search engines can be understood within the 
framework of the cost of activities versus the value of information 
(Pirolli & Card, 1995). 
Drawing on IFT, prior studies have recognized some web navigation 
patterns to be descriptive of individual navigation behavior in single- 
layered hypertext environments. For example, Reader and Payne 
(2007) asked adult participants to read the information contained in 16 
web pages and, based on the given information, write a short essay on 
the human heart. Each web page can be easily accessed using the cor­
responding link from a menu frame. Unlike multi-layered hypertext

environments, conventional digital texts instead of hypertexts were 
displayed on each web page. The authors identified two distinct navi­
gation patterns of online information, Sampling and Satisficing. The 
Sampling pattern is characterized by adults visiting all available links 
from the menu frame in an effort to identify and select the best infor­
mation patch, according to some criteria. When adopting the Satisficing 
pattern, adults do not compare information from all available links in 
the menu, but sequentially evaluate them only until one is considered as 
being “good enough”. Importantly, information navigation with the 
Satisficing approach involves no explicit comparison of information 
sources. Rather, it is used to denote behaviors that seek “good enough” 
rather than optimal solutions (Wilkinson et al., 2012). In a more recent 
study, List and Alexander (2017) emphasized that the Sampling and 
Satisficing navigation patterns are distinguished from one another in 
what criteria people utilize to judge information acceptability. People 
with the Satisficing navigation pattern evaluate information using a 
personally determined acceptability threshold, which is set before 
navigation and monitored during navigation. In contrast, people 
exhibiting the Sampling navigation pattern may adopt higher criteria, 
resulting in comparing available information patches to one another. 
Empirically, Satisficing is a much more commonly occurring pattern 
than Sampling. For example, Reader and Payne (2007) found 68% of 
people to be satisficers, 12% to be samplers, and the rest (20%) to follow 
neither the Sampling nor the Satisficing browsing pattern. In a follow-up 
study, Wilkinson et al. (2012) found a comparable distribution of nav­
igation patterns (Satisficing: 76.7%; Sampling: 6.7%; neither pattern: 
16.7%). However, the occurrence of one or the other navigation pattern 
depends on external factors (List & Alexander, 2017; Reader & Payne, 
2007). For example, Reader and Payne (2007) found that providing brief 
summaries of information sources content before navigation led to the 
Sampling becoming the majority pattern. In addition, List and Alexander 
(2017) found the manifestation of the Satisficing pattern, rather than the 
Sampling pattern, when adults navigate information that conforms to 
task demands in non-hyperlinked text environments. The absence of the 
Sampling pattern may be due to two factors. First, without time limita­
tion to tasks, people may feel free to access information patches, rather 
than feel pressured to identify an optimal information patch. Second, 
when responding to open-ended tasks (i.e., allowing for multiple, 
potentially valid responses), people may have been less aimed at pin­
pointing an optimal information patch. They both suggest that 
task-relevant features affect the occurrence of Satisficing and Sampling 
navigation patterns. 
1.1.2. Web navigation patterns in multi-layered hypertext environments 
Unlike the Satisficing and Sampling navigation patterns established in 
single-layered hypertext environments, a number of different web nav­
igation patterns have been identified in multi-layered hypertext envi­
ronments (e.g., Jenkins et al., 2003; Juvina & van Oostendorp, 2006). 
For example, Jenkins et al. (2003) examined how combinations of 
domain expertise and web expertise were associated with different web 
information navigation patterns. Along with a qualitative (i.e., 
think-aloud) research method, the authors captured web users’ thoughts 
concurrent with performing information-seeking tasks. They found that 
people with high domain expertise and high web expertise carried out 
Depth-first searches and people with low domain expertise and low web 
expertise followed a Breadth-first search pattern. This finding was in 
accord with the general results of Marchionini et al. (1993). In addition, 
Juvina and van Oostendorp (2006) first identified 19 first-order metrics 
such as path length, back button usage, and relative amount of revisits 
with the aid of weblogging data. Then, they ran a principal component 
analysis with equamax rotation on the first-order metrics. As such, they 
recognized four types of navigation patterns: Flimsy, Content-focused, 
Laborious, and Divergent. Explanations of these patterns have been pre­
sented in Table 1. 
1.1.3. Comparisons of different web navigation patterns 
Although web navigation patterns summarized herein were identi­
fied by different studies, all of them describe behavioral actions for 
seeking out online information. Analyzing their similarities and differ­
ences can provide a clear picture of what is already known about web 
navigation patterns and what still needs further exploration. 
The patterns identified in multi-layered hypertext environments 
generally differentiate people as either accessing information sources 
sequentially or as engaging in more deliberative source access (Chen 
et al., 2006; List & Alexander, 2017; Macgregor, 1999). Concerning the 
first category, it incorporates Breadth-first (Jenkins et al., 2003) and 
Content-focus (Juvina & van Oostendorp, 2006) patterns. In contrast, 
Depth-first (Jenkins et al., 2003) and Laborious (Juvina & van Oos­
tendorp, 2006) belong to the second category. A comparison between 
the patterns within each category showed that Breadth-first and Depth-­
first are more coarse-grained classifications. Conversely, Content-focus 
and Laborious are more fine-grained descriptions of navigation behavior. 
Those with a Depth-first pattern navigate information sources in depth; 
those exhibiting a Laborious pattern not only explore information sour­
ces deeply but also display a high revisitation rate (Juvina & van Oos­
tendorp, 2006). Thus, we expect that Laborious is a sub-profile of the 
Depth-first pattern. 
Regarding Sampling and Satisficing, we expect that some web users 
exhibit such patterns when navigating web search results in multi- 
layered hypertext environments. This is because in multi-layered hy­
pertext environments web users also need to find an optimal trade-off 
between cognitive effort and efficient outcome. Since IFT is proposed 
in consideration of the conflicting goal of effectiveness (e.g., acquiring 
credible and appropriate information) and efficiency (e.g., no wasting 
effort on information that is not needed) during web information search, 
it is suited for explaining how web users navigate multiple web search 
results for target information in multi-layered hypertext environments 
as well. Sampling characterizes systematical navigation across all the 
available information sources, whereas Satisficing indicates a more se­
lective and adaptive navigation pattern among information sources (List 
& Alexander, 2017). However, concerning a specific information source, 
both Sampling and Satisficing exhibit intensive navigation of the infor­
mation source. In this sense, they, together with Laborious, are 
sub-profiles of the Depth-first pattern. On the other hand, Breadth-first 
could be a superficial pattern of Sampling since the former characterizes 
systematical but not intensive navigation whereas the latter typifies

Another way to group web navigation patterns is to investigate their 
effectiveness in acquiring the needed information. Users with a Breadth-

and Depth-first have been confirmed as ineffective and effective patterns 
separately (Jenkins et al., 2003; Thatcher, 2008). However, there is

Laborious, and Divergent patterns, which are combinations of two

1.2. Web information search task types 
In addition to the importance of manifest action sequences in iden­
tifying web navigation patterns and their effectiveness, the choice of 
web navigation patterns and their optimality must be understood in 
terms of their interactions with task types (Etcheverry et al., 2012; 
Savolainen, 2018). Different types of search tasks characterize diverse 
scenarios that web users face, and, to some extent, determine challenges 
to be overcome throughout web information search process (Lazonder & 
Rouet, 2008; Wirth et al., 2016). However, there is limited knowledge 
about web navigation patterns that web users exhibit when engaging in 
a given task and their optimality for the task. To develop a framework 
for web navigation patterns in multi-layered hypertext environments, 
we began with the identification of important search task types. 
In a series of studies on the relationship between web search 
behavior and task type, the existence of two major types of search task 
was established: “information-locating task” and “information-evalu­
ating task” (Wirth et al., 2016). The two types of search tasks differ in 
terms of task intention (Li & Belkin, 2008; Xie, 1998). In conducting an 
information-locating task, users find information that meets task de­
mands from a range of information sources (Walraven et al., 2009). 
However, information-evaluating tasks are usually more complex 
because web users need to not only locate particular information but 
also use a higher level of references to form judgments of information 
quality. 
The present study focused on specific information-locating tasks and 
amorphous 
information-evaluating 
tasks. 
Particularly, 
specific 
information-locating tasks require users to find target information ac­
cording to given explicit demands. Constituent elements of the desired 
information can be clearly recognized and operationalized (J´a˜nez & 
Rosales, 2016; Liu & Kim, 2013; MacMullin & Taylor, 1984). An 
example of specific information-locating tasks is ‘Which online shop 
offers drugs that could be used to treat the common cold in children and 
could be delivered in two days?’ This task asks users to find an online 
shop and it clearly describes the requirements for the online shop. Its 
search process is straightforward as once web users find an information 
source that satisfies the search requirements, the entire activity ends 
(Walraven et al., 2009). In contrast, search goals of amorphous 
information-evaluating tasks are ambiguous, resulting in great difficulty 
in appraising information from different sources. Accordingly, people 
usually act based on their personal senses (J´a˜nez & Rosales, 2016; Liu & 
Kim, 2013; MacMullin & Taylor, 1984) and initiate an iterative search 
process. For example, in performing this task ‘Which website do you 
think provides the most reliable treatment information for a common 
cold in children?’ web users are asked to evaluate the reliability of 
website information. They do not know what evaluation criteria they 
need to use and must decide for themselves what cues they might rely on 
to judge reliability while navigating multiple search outcomes. Thus, 
web users are likely to navigate multiple times iteratively to adjust their 
criteria till they identify the most satisfying alternative. 
1.3. Hypotheses and research questions 
This study aims at examining what web navigation patterns emerge 
when adults encounter specific information-locating and amorphous 
information-evaluating tasks in multi-layered hypertext environments 
and how they are associated with task performance. According to dif­
ferences between Satisficing and Sampling (List & Alexander, 2017; 
Reader & Payne, 2007; Wilkinson et al., 2012), we may expect that they 
differentially appear in specific information-locating and amorphous 
information-evaluating tasks. Given that specific information-locating 
tasks clearly specify each requirement for target information, web 
users are expected to navigate web search results in light of explicit 
acceptability criteria (Satisficing). By comparison, web users are ex­
pected to involve constant comparison of information sources to distill 
quality cues (Sampling) considering their ambiguous mental represen­
tation of desired information in amorphous information-evaluating 
tasks. Unlike Satisficing and Sampling, there is no evidence for the 
different appearance of Breadth-first and Depth-first patterns in specific 
information-locating and amorphous information-evaluating tasks. 
Therefore, our hypotheses are as follows:  
1. Satisficing is an effective web navigation pattern in specific 
information-locating tasks.  
2. Sampling is an effective web navigation pattern in amorphous 
information-evaluating tasks.  
3. Breadth-first is an ineffective web navigation pattern in specific 
information-locating and amorphous information-evaluating tasks.  
4. Depth-first is an effective web navigation pattern in specific 
information-locating and amorphous information-evaluating tasks. 
In contrast, neither the occurrence nor the effectiveness of Flimsy, 
Content-focus, Laborious, and Divergent patterns could be confirmed 
because of limited evidence from the literature. Thus, this study pursues 
to identify web navigation patterns for specific information-locating and 
amorphous information-evaluating tasks in multi-layered hypertext 
environments. In addition, this study seeks to reveal the effectiveness of 
the identified web navigation patterns in prompting people to acquire 
target information. Thus, we formulate the following two research 
questions:  
1. What web navigation patterns could be recognized by examining full 
navigation paths while people solve specific information-locating 
and amorphous information-evaluating tasks?  
2. How effective are different web navigation patterns in leading to 
success in specific information-locating and amorphous information- 
evaluating tasks, respectively? 
2. Methods 
2.1. Sample 
We used data from the problem-solving in technology-rich environ­
ments (PSTRE) assessment of the Programme for the International 
Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) 2012 cycle conducted by the 
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). In 
2012, 81,744 adults between the ages of 16 and 65 from 17 countries 
participated in the PIAAC test (OECD, 2013). The adult participants 
were randomly assigned to two of three cognitive modules, each of 
which comprised either literacy, numeracy, or PSTRE tasks (OECD, 
2013). The PSTRE competency was assessed via 14 computerized tasks 
among which two tasks named digital photography book purchase and 
sprained ankle – reliable/trustworthy site were developed to mimic 
real-life problems that people are likely to encounter while using the 
technology of Internet (OECD, 2019a). For simplicity, the two tasks were 
abbreviated as the book purchase task and the reliable site task, 
respectively. To complete both tasks, adults were exposed to mock

patterns identified in the United States could be recovered in another

country, English as the test language) to the United States. In addition, 
participants (N = 1182) from the United Kingdom displayed a similar 
PSTRE proficiency level with those from the United States, which may

without any navigation (i.e., guessing), but they cannot answer the book 
purchase task if they do not navigate at all. Given our interests in web 
navigation patterns, we removed participants who pressed the next 
button before clicking on any links on a SERP. The final sample of N =
1408 participants (494 from the United States and 914 from the United 
Kingdom) was included for data analysis, of which 619 (43.96%) were 
male (237 from the United States and 532 from the United Kingdom). 
2.2. Measures 
Both the book purchase task and the reliable site task simulate web 
search results navigation scenarios, together with corresponding SERPs 
(OECD, 2019b, pp. 72–73). Given that the two tasks are confidential and 
cannot be disclosed, we took a released sample task (i.e., “Job Search 
Part 1”) as an illustration of the task environment comprising a mock 
Google-like SERP interface (see Appendix B), homepages, and nested 
web pages (OECD, n. d.). The “Job Search Part 1” task can be accessed 
via the OECD website (https://piaac-logdata.tba-hosting.de/). As 
described in the stem of the task, located on the left side of the mock 
Google-like SERP interface, participants took the role of a job seeker and 
aimed to find a site that does not require registering or paying a fee. 
Some browser buttons such as back, forward, and bookmark as well as a 
link menu were displayed on the right side of the interface. Each web 
search result contained a title, a lead sentence explaining the content of 
the web page, and a URL. If participants click the second link (i.e., “Work 
Link”) of the SERP, they would be directed to the connected homepage. 
To discover the specific contents of the homepage, they would click the 
“Learn More” button and access the nested web page. It should be noted 
that, as opposed to navigating the open WWW where users have access 
to an enormous amount of web pages, the task setting of the present 
study was a restricted information space. This means that only a limited 
number of homepages and nested web pages are available for partici­
pants’ information navigation activities. 
In the book purchase task, participants took over the role of online 
consumers trying to figure out the best book choice for their friend’s 
birthday. Before navigating the SERP with six links, participants were 
provided with three requirements for the desirable book, including 1) 
the shipping date should be within two weeks, 2) the target audience 
should be beginners of photography, and 3) the price should be less than 
$40. Given that key characteristics of the target book have been clearly 
and separately defined, we considered this task to be an exemplar of 
specific information-locating tasks. To solve the book purchase task 
properly, participants need to browse web pages relevant to the six links 
and locate the essential information. For example, it is likely that only 
part information such as book price and/or shipping date is displayed on 
a homepage within an information source. Under this condition, web 
users should leave the homepage with the desire to seek out additional 
information about its intended audience, which might be clarified on 
nested web pages of the information source. 
In the reliable site task, participants were asked to recommend an 
optimal information source among five optional websites to their 
friends, which provided the most reliable and trustworthy treatment 
information for a sprained ankle. Unlike the book purchase task, eval­
uation criteria associated with reliability and trustworthiness were not 
mentioned in the reliable site task. Key distinguishers of the quality of 
available information sources were the authority of information pro­
viders and purposes of shared information. For example, information 
from sources edited by specialists in sports medicine is more reliable and 
trustworthy compared to those edited by laypersons (i.e., non-experts in 
sports medicine). Also, information from sources sharing and spreading 
neutral knowledge is more trustworthy than those aiming to sell or 
promote particular products. This task required participants to judge 
information quality and its goal was perceived as being of high ambi­
guity. Thus, this task is regarded as an exemplar of amorphous 
information-evaluating tasks. Participants needed to click on links listed 
on the SERP and browse contained information toward the optimal in­
formation source. 
Both tasks required participants to navigate homepages, as well as 
the nested web pages, to acquire the essential information to support 
task completion. Information embedded in SERP links is distributed in 
two-layered hypertext environments. In particular, in the book purchase 
task, the six links on the SERP were connected to six homepages and five 
of them had nested web pages (see Table 2). Only one information 
source is optimal in terms of satisfying all three requirements. In the 
reliable site task, the five links were associated with five homepages 
respectively and three of them had nested web pages (see Table 2). 
Likewise, there is only one information source that provides the most 
reliable and trustworthy information. Participants’ responses to both 
tasks were dichotomously scored (0 for incorrect and 1 for correct). 
2.3. Data preparation 
The clickstream data were extracted from the task log files in the 
PIAAC 2012 database (OECD, 2017). Variables recorded in the log files 
(e.g., “Event Type” and “Event Description”) have been elaborated by 
Goldhammer et al. (2020). A sample clickstream data file demonstrating 
how a participant navigated for the most reliable treatment information 
to solve the reliable site task is shown in Appendix C. In this study, we 
only collected the information of accessed web pages from rows with 
TEXTLINK in the “Event Type” variable and deleted the redundant click 
data (e.g., rows with BUTTON and TOOLBAR in the “Event Type” var­
iable). As such, for the data file shown in Appendix C, we learned that 
the participant spent about 1 min on the reliable site task, and the web 
pages she/he browsed sequentially were: H1 → H2 → N2 → H3 → N3 → 
H4 → H5 → H2 → N2. That is, this participant browsed the second 
homepage and its nested web page twice and all other web pages once.

one for the book purchase task and another for the reliable site task. The 
following analyses were performed for the two resulting subsets of the 
data separately for each country. 
We first conducted LCA to identify groups that displayed different 
navigation patterns. As a model-based approach, LCA can be used to 
identify latent classes of participants who share similar profiles of

responses to a set of observed variables (McCutcheon, 1987). In LCA, a 
discrete instead of a continuous latent variable is assumed to explain the 
associations between observed variables. For example, Greiff et al. 
(2018) applied LCA to divide students into different exploration strategy 
groups based on their exploration behavior in computer-based complex 
problem-solving tasks. The observed variables in our analysis were 
amounts of visits denoting how many times each web page was browsed 
by a participant in each task. We performed an exploratory LCA of each 
task for each country in Mplus 7 (Muth´en & Muth´en, 1998-2012). 
To determine the optimal number of classes for each task, we 
compared models based on absolute and relative fit indices, class attri­
bution probabilities, and the Lo-Mendell-Rubin test (LMR; Lo et al., 
2001). To determine absolute fit, we consulted the Pearson chi-squared 
(χ2) statistic and the Likelihood-Ratio χ2 statistic. As a general rule, the 
higher the number of classes, the smaller the χ2 values and the better the 
absolute fit. However, a χ2/df ratio below 2 is usually considered an 
indication of adequate absolute fit in large samples. To determine 
relative fit, we relied on the Akaike information criterion (AIC; Akaike, 
1987), Bayesian information criterion (BIC; Schwartz, 1978), and the 
sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC). As relative fit indices, AIC, BIC, and 
aBIC do not have absolute values for acceptable model fit exist, but the 
lower the respective value the better the model fit. Thus, among several 
competing models the one with the lowest values is preferred. In addi­
tion to evaluating the absolute and relative fit values, we inspected class 
attribution probabilities (Greiff et al., 2018). Class attribution proba­
bilities indicate the certainty with which participants can be assigned to 
the corresponding classes. Attribution probabilities should not fall 
below 0.80 in any class. Finally, we also employed the LMR test, which 
provides a direct test of the null hypothesis that specifying an additional 
latent class will not improve absolute model fit. 
We then applied full-path sequence analysis to identify typical 
behavioral patterns that best characterize the set of sequences of each 
class (i.e., navigation pattern groups) in the respective tasks. Full-path 
sequence analysis works in two main steps. In the first stage, it pre­
pares a sorted list of candidate representative sequences without caring 
for redundancy, which will be handled in the second stage (Gabadinho 
et al., 2011). It requires users to specify a representativeness criterion for 
the first stage and a similarity measure for evaluating redundancy in the 
second one. We followed the procedure described in Gabadinho et al. 
(2011) and used neighborhood density as a representativeness criterion 
for sorting candidate representative sequences. Neighborhood density, 
as compared to using the medoid of a set of sequences, allowed for more 
than one representative sequence and yielded better coverage (Gaba­
dinho et al., 2011). Once the representative sequences were identified, 
we chose the optimal matching between sequences of spells (OMspell) al­
gorithm (Studer & Ritschard, 2014) to measure similarities between 
sequences. Regarding the OMspell algorithm, Studer and Ritschard 
(2016) conducted a simulation study to compare it with the other eight 
algorithms and found it to be most sensitive to pairs of sequences. To 
control the size of the representative set, we limited the size of the 
candidate list so that the cumulated frequency of the retained distinct 
candidates covered at least one-fourth of sequences in each class as the 
total coverage (Gabadinho et al., 2011). All the analyses were imple­
mented using the package TraMineR (Gabadinho et al., 2011) in R (R 
Core Team, 2021). A graphical tool for visualizing the selected repre­
sentative sequences is also included in the package TraMineR. In this 
study, we labeled different classes as navigation pattern groups based on 
typical browsing sequences. 
In responding to the effectiveness of web navigation patterns, task- 
specific correctness across pattern groups was calculated as an index 
of effectiveness. Pearson’s χ2 test was applied to investigate whether 
task correctness was related to web navigation patterns; that is, whether 
the frequency of participants who succeeded in tasks was equally 
distributed across navigation pattern groups. Post-hoc pairwise testing 
after Pearson’s χ2 test was implemented to identify the pattern that 
performed best in the respective tasks. These analyses were carried out 
with the package rcompanion (Mangiafico, 2021) in R (R Core Team, 
2021).

3.1. Web navigation patterns identification 
3.1.1. Web navigation patterns of the book purchase task 
We first divided participants from the United States into different 
web navigation pattern groups based on their navigation behavior in the 
book purchase task. Results of exploratory LCA of the accessed web 
pages in the book purchase task are shown in Table 3. Regarding ab­
solute fit, all solutions from two to seven classes fulfilled the condition of 
a χ2/df ratio below 2. In terms of relative fit, BIC was the lowest for the 
five-class solution, whereas AIC and aBIC indicated that the seven-class 
solution was the most appropriate. Of note, one class attribution prob­

were extracted. In addition, the LMR test revealed a significantly better 
fit of the five-class model than the four-class model (Δχ2 = 125.784; df =
12; p = .010) and an insignificantly better fit for the six-class model in 
comparison with the five-class solution as a more parsimonious model 
(Δχ2 = −77.720; df = 12; p = .989). Taking these results together, the 
five-class solution was suggested as the most appropriate classification 
for the book purchase task.

3.5. Web navigation patterns comparison 
With regard to the occurrence rate of different web navigation pat­
terns in the two countries, the results in Table 7 show a comparable 
distribution for the book purchase task. Regarding the reliable site task, 
however, most participants from the United States (N = 319) navigated 
with a Sampling pattern; in contrast, Breadth-first is the most frequently 
observed pattern for the United Kingdom (N = 630). After identifying 
web navigation patterns in the book purchase and reliable site tasks, the 
task-specific correctness across different navigation pattern groups was 
calculated (see Table 7). Pearson’s χ2 test revealed significant differ­
ences in group correctness for the book purchase task (United States: χ2 
= 336.38, df = 4, p < .001; United Kingdom: χ2 = 468.40, df = 4, p <
.001) as well as for the reliable site task (United States: χ2 = 50.73, df =
3, p < .001; United Kingdom: χ2 = 83.28, df = 2, p < .001). These results 
supported that participants’ task performance differed significantly 
depending upon their web navigation patterns. Post-hoc pairwise testing 
showed that the Satisficing pattern group obtained the highest task 
correctness in the book purchase task (United States: 96.05%; United 
Kingdom: 93.55%), while it is the Sampling pattern group that achieved 
the highest task correctness in the reliable site task (United States: 
92.45%; United Kingdom: 92.90%). 
4. Discussion 
In this study, we set out to identify web navigation patterns when 
web users perform specific information-locating and amorphous 
information-evaluating tasks in two-layered hypertext environments. 
Our findings of web navigation patterns and their effectiveness in 
acquiring desired information add to the growing theory and empirical 
knowledge base concerning web search results navigation in web in­
formation search. 
4.1. Satisficing and Sampling patterns 
Our findings verified the first two hypotheses: Satisficing is an 
effective navigation pattern in specific information-locating tasks, while 
Sampling is an effective navigation pattern in amorphous information- 
evaluating tasks. In addition, the reproducibility of Satisficing and 
Sampling navigation patterns was confirmed with participants from two 
different countries. 
Satisficing as a task-specific pattern emerged in the specific 
information-locating task (i.e., the book purchase task) rather than the 
amorphous information-evaluating task (i.e., the reliable site task). 
When responding to the specific information-locating task, web users 
who demonstrated the Satisficing pattern matched source content to 
information demands and put an end to the task once they found an 
information source that met their threshold for acceptability. Thus, the 
Satisficing pattern is likely sufficient to select the globally optimal in­
formation source when users are confronted with tasks that require web 
users to locate information and lend themselves to explicit information 
demands. However, when performing the amorphous information- 
evaluating task, it was rarely enough to just sequentially and individu­
ally evaluate each information source. Without clear criteria for the 
reliability and trustworthiness of online information, it seems infeasible 
for web users to directly decide whether to accept or reject an infor­
mation source. In this case, web users are expected to gather as much 
evidence as possible to justify their hypothesized options. This potential 
mechanism was described as higher standards for source quality 
involved in the Sampling pattern, as compared to the use of well- 
established principles in the Satisficing pattern (List & Alexander, 
2017). In contrast, the Sampling pattern associated with visiting all

4.2. Breadth-first and depth-first patterns 
Our findings confirmed the third and fourth hypotheses regarding 
the emergence and the effectiveness of Breadth-first and Depth-first pat­
terns in specific information-locating tasks and amorphous information- 
evaluating tasks. Aligned with our hypothesis, we found that Breadth- 
first was an ineffective pattern in both tasks, and adults with different 
sub-profiles of the Depth-first pattern performed better than those with 
the Breadth-first pattern. 
Breadth-first, as a robust web navigation pattern over different 
country contexts (i.e., the United States and the United Kingdom), 
characterized participants as merely accessing homepages and consis­
tently skimming nested web pages. Without taking information con­
tained in nested web pages into consideration, information gathered 
from homepages was rarely sufficient to correctly judge the relevance 
and quality of information sources. This web navigation pattern is 
entirely consistent with observed navigation behaviors of people with 
low domain expertise and low web expertise in prior studies (Jenkins 
et al., 2003; Marchionini et al., 1993). This indicates that information 
embedded within homepages is sometimes disregarded by adults, sup­
porting the findings from Kuiper et al. (2008) and Bråten et al. (2016). A 
possible explanation for this neglect is that some web users may fail to 
realize the importance of hypertexts in providing vital information for 
task completion but rather consider them as part of ordinary semantic

In this study, we did not label a specific pattern as a Depth-first

adults with Sampling, Satisficing, and Laborious patterns were well

or irregularly (i.e., Laborious) navigate nested web pages. Such an 
intensive exploration was a criterion of the Depth-first navigation pattern 
defined in earlier studies (Jenkins et al., 2003; Marchionini, 1993). A 
test of the effectiveness of Sampling, Satisficing, and Laborious patterns 
showed mixed results. Although adults with these patterns performed 
better than those with the Breadth-first pattern, not all forms of Depth-­
first pattern were equally effective: Laborious was less effective in both 
tasks in comparison with Sampling and Satisficing patterns. 
4.3. Extreme navigation patterns 
Two extreme web navigation patterns were further identified when 
adult participants conducted the two search tasks: Flimsy and Laborious. 
Flimsy and Laborious were first proposed by Herder and Juvina (2004) as 
predictors of users’ disorientation in web navigation and later studies 
have reported them as two navigation patterns (e.g., Gwizdka & Spence, 
2007; Juvina & van Oostendorp, 2006). However, most, if not all, of 
these studies gathered single unit measures (e.g., counts of visits to web 
pages) or combinations of these measures (e.g., a small number of pages 
visited and a high frequency of back button use) rather than full navi­
gation sequences analyzed in the present study. 
Flimsy in this study reveals that some web users abandoned naviga­
tion right after browsing only one or a few web pages, despite accessed 
pages being largely insufficient to guide their options (Gwizdka & 
Spence, 2007; Juvina & van Oostendorp, 2006). This behavioral ten­
dency is aligned with the Flimsy navigation described by previous 
studies (e.g., Gwizdka & Spence, 2007; Juvina & van Oostendorp, 2006). 
This pattern might be sparked by adults’ internal factors (e.g., uninter­
ested in tasks, low motivation) and/or externally driven by features of 
tasks (e.g., unfamiliar topics involved). To construct a deeper under­
standing of the Flimsy navigation pattern, we examined the average 
response time of all navigation pattern groups and found that the Flimsy 
pattern group spent less time than other groups (see Appendix E). 
However, the average response time was far longer than 5 s which was 
used as a constant threshold for the minimum amount of time needed to 
provide a valid response to a task (e.g., Goldhammer et al., 2016; Wise & 
Kong, 2005). In this respect, the Flimsy pattern is different from disen­
gaged test-taking behavior, though being disengaged is common in 
low-stakes assessments such as the PIAAC 2012 (Goldhammer et al., 
2016; Ulitzsch et al., 2021). On the other hand, Weeks et al. (2016) 
noted that task-specific timing thresholds for judging test-taking 
engagement in the PIAAC literacy and numeracy tasks ranged from 20 
to 30 s, which were notably higher than the constant threshold of 5 s. In 
this regard, more research is needed on response time thresholds for the 
two search tasks to explicitly distinguish the Flimsy pattern and disen­
gaged test-taking behavior. 
However, users who adopted the Laborious navigation pattern 
showed extremely long navigation paths (i.e., sequences) including both 
homepages and nested web pages. In addition, homepages, as well as 
their nested web pages, were double-checked repeatedly by the Labo­
rious pattern group. This could be an expression that these users invested 
much effort to complete the two tasks (Eichmann et al., 2020; Juvina & 
van Oostendorp, 2006). However, the Laborious pattern that character­
ized users with a seemingly perseverant behavior cannot always guar­
antee success in web navigation. Likewise, Gwizdka and Spence (2007) 
found that highly Laborious paths were far from the optimal web navi­
gation path and associated with task failure. Given that the total time on 
each task was found to be one of the most effective indicators of 
disorientation (Gwizdka & Spence, 2007), users who demonstrated a 
Laborious navigation pattern might get lost in multiple information 
sources because of low reading skills. Those with low reading skills 
failed to interpret and connect important ideas presented on different 
web pages (e.g., Hahnel et al., 2016, 2018) and thus repeatedly visited 
them. 
4.4. Implications 
To summarize, the results show the appropriateness of Satisficing and 
Sampling for portraying navigation patterns in two-layered hypertext 
environments. For the field of hypertext navigation, no novel pattern 
that is not aligned with previous studies was identified in this study. 
Such a result, however, should be considered carefully since both 
limited information space and two-layered hypertext environments in 
this study may restrict observable navigation behavior (Hahnel et al., 
2016). As suggested by Hahnel et al. (2018), navigation can be seen as a 
constructive process, depending on behavioral actions of web users, 
cognitive processing of information, and external constraints of digital 
environments. In line with this perspective, the present study attempted 
to disclose online information processing by examining adults’ naviga­
tion patterns in two-layered hypertext environments. In total, the nav­
igation paths can contribute to our understanding of how adults manage 
their value-cost analyses under different circumstances, and hence 
provide further insight into adults’ information processing (e.g., Hahnel 
et al., 2018; Juvina & van Oostendorp, 2006; Rouet & Le Bigot, 2007). 
The point is that adults stop processing online information when the cost 
of further navigation outweighs the value of additional information and 
the threshold of information acceptability is met. Information accept­
ability criteria, as well as a trade-off between navigation effort and 
search outcome, guide navigation behavior and contribute to a large 
extent to the success of performing a search task (List & Alexander, 
2017). In addition to the type of search tasks, reading skills are expected 
to be an important factor of online information processing (Hahnel et al., 
2018; Juvina & van Oostendorp, 2006). A more valuable investigation of 
reasons why adults navigate web search results in a particular way 
should be a task for future research, especially skill determinants of 
sub-optimal navigation patterns (i.e., Breadth-first, Flimsy, and 
Laborious). 
5. Limitations 
This study has several limitations that are worth noting. First, we 
studied only two types of search tasks with one task for each type. A 
single task for each type warns us not to overgeneralize the findings of 
the study to the Web context. Indeed, the computer-based Google-like 
SERP simulations in this study cannot capture the complexity of real- 
SERP navigation in practice. For example, Hahnel et al. (2020) inves­
tigated how specific website attributes affect users’ task processing 
behavior. They found that web users responded differently depending 
on the attractiveness and quality of target and non-target search results 
in search situations. A target search result denotes the optimal solution 
for a search task in terms of both information relevance and credibility. 
Non-target search results of a search task, in contrast, are characterized 
by some flaws and shortcomings in information relevance and/or 
credibility (e.g., they may address a different audience or mainly deliver 
commercial intentions). The present study did not explore how adults’ 
web navigation patterns vary across different combinations of infor­
mation attractiveness and quality of target and non-target search results. 
One direction for future research may be to incorporate varied website 
attributes in efforts to further elaborate on how web navigation patterns 
vary based on the attributes of target and non-target search results. 
Second, the current study did not involve any personal characteris­
tics (e.g., age, gender, and educational background) to further explain 
the differences between the navigation pattern groups. A potentially 
interesting direction for future research might be examining whether 
personal characteristics affect web navigation pattern choice. For

example, Etcheverry et al. (2012) found that older adults performed less 
well than younger adults when required to locate navigation links. Given 
their difficulty in identifying relevant navigation links in web pages, 
older adults might be more likely to adopt the Breadth-first pattern 
compared to younger adults. Further empirical research is needed to 
understand how users’ personal characteristics are associated with 
adopting and implementing web navigation patterns. 
Lastly, we did not exclude possible effects of task position on web 
navigation patterns identification. The amorphous information- 
evaluating task was always administered first to the participants, fol­
lowed by the specific information-locating task (OECD, 2019a). 
Different administration positions might affect the time available for 
responding to the two tasks. When performing the amorphous 
information-evaluating task, participants might invest a vast amount of 
time to obtain the optimal information source for each web information 
search task. However, some participants might be not able to inspect all 
available information sources due to perceived time pressure when 
responding to the specific information-locating task. This potential ef­
fect of task position on the recognition of task-specific navigation pat­
terns should be disentangled in future research. 
6. Conclusion 
Web navigation has become an integral part of adults’ personal, 
academic, and professional life. Given that search engines likely return 
all the relevant information including unfitting, inaccurate, or 
misleading information together with optimal information, being able to 
tactically navigate for desirable information becomes particularly 
salient for adults. However, relatively limited work has examined the 
patterns that web users exhibit when navigating multiple search results. 
This study thus aims to 1) identify web navigation patterns for a specific 
information-locating task and an amorphous information-evaluating 
task presented in multi-layered hypertext environments and 2) 
compare the effectiveness of web navigation patterns in task perfor­
mance. A series of analyses of clickstream data from navigation logs 
demonstrated four global patterns (i.e., Flimsy, Breadth-first, Laborious, 
and Sampling) between two tasks and one unique pattern (i.e., Satisficing) 
for the specific information-locating task. Moreover, the most effective 
pattern varied across two tasks: Sampling for the specific information- 
evaluating task and Satisficing for the amorphous information-locating 
task. Overall, the present study has been one of the first attempts to 
thoroughly investigate web users’ navigation patterns in relation to 
different types of web navigation tasks (i.e., specific information- 
locating and amorphous information-evaluating) in the adult popula­
tion. The findings of this study lay the groundwork for future research 
into the examination of the effectiveness of web navigation patterns 
across various tasks in multi-layered hypertext environments.
