Abstract Using an international education database, PISA
2012, this study compares 15-year-old students’ mathematics anxiety and its relationships with educational issues
among five Asia Pacific economies (Indonesia, Korea,
Malaysia, Shanghai, & Singapore), using descriptive, figural, partial correlative, multiple regressive, and factor
analysis methods. The main variable, math anxiety, is
made up primarily from six of ten items around student
worry, tense, nervousness, or helplessness for mathematics
difficulties, homework, tough problems, or poor grades.
New findings present a perspective of student math anxiety
and its impacts across economies with three different
models: math anxiety has strong negative correlations with
student standardized test scores, interests, and knowledge
of mathematics in all economies; it has reverse relations
with student abilities, importance, self-attribution in the
high and low achievement economies; and it has weak
correlations with teaching methods, parents’ influences, or
friends’ performances. This study also discusses a series of
questions for further study in mathematics learning.

Introduction
Mathematics anxiety (M-Anxiety) refers to a multidimensional psychological construct that involves complex factors, such as feelings of pressure, performance inadequacy,
and test anxiety that interfere with manipulation of numbers and solving mathematical problems in a wide variety
of ordinary life and academic situations (Kvedere, 2014).
M-Anxiety is characterized by feelings of apprehension
and tension concerning manipulation of numbers and
completion of mathematical problems in various contexts
(Richardson & Suinn, 1972). Others have explained a fivefactor structure of M-Anxiety: anxiety toward evaluation,
temporality, understanding of mathematical problems,
numbers and operations, and mathematical situations in
real life (Garcia-Santillan et al., 2016).
The impacts of M-Anxiety on student learning are rather
negative. As most literature concluded, math standardized
test scores and M-Anxiety had a moderate and negative
relationship (Villavicencio & Bernardo, 2016). M-Anxiety
was particularly a strong predictor of low math achievement (Gunderson et al., 2018; Hwang et al., 2016), skills
learning (Wang & Tsai, 2017), performance (Xu & Huang,
2018), and value, cost, or self-learning (Li & Zheng, 2018).
Students who have high M-Anxiety would avoid situations
in which they had to perform mathematical calculations.
Math avoidance results in less competency, exposure to
math practice, leaving students more anxious and mathematically unprepared (Ashcraft & Moore, 2009). M-Anxiety causes students to move away from lessons, to increase

the feelings of panic, and to hate learning mathematics
(Sevgi & Arslan, 2020). A school counseling group intervention showed reducing M-Anxiety increases achievement (Ruff & Boes, 2014).
What are the characteristics of M-Anxiety? Some
scholars have considered that gender and school grades
influence their anxiety levels (Bruce, 2016; Zhou et al.,
2017). Females had M-Anxiety significantly higher than
males, and private school students had lower M-Anxiety
than those of public school students (Lowe & Ang, 2016),
while others saw no differences (Garcia-Santillan et al.,
2018). Low SES students were disadvantaged in mathematics learning and higher family SES compensates for
their mathematics anxiety (Chiao & Chiu, 2018; Fan et al.,
2016).
Students’ learning styles, self-efficacy, abilities, and
efforts also influence their M-Anxiety level (Aksu et al.,
2016; Ngui & Lay, 2018). Test anxiety is significantly
correlated with self-efficacy and attachment styles and can
be predicted by dismissing, fearfulness, and secure
attachment styles (Li & Zheng, 2018). In an attribution
model, student performance avoidance and goal perspective predicted that students with high M-Anxiety use more
self-protective coping strategies (Skaalvik, 2018), and
motivation and attitudes had a direct impact on M-Anxiety
(Lin et al., 2017). The causes of M-Anxiety included lack
of self-confidence, fear of failure, ineffective learning
practices, and non-engagement of students (Finlayson,
2014). Student intrinsic motivation, help-seeking behavior,
and efforts of self were more important (Federici &
Skaalvik, 2014). However, the correlations between
M-Anxiety and self-regulated learning behaviors were not
strong (Guo & Wei, 2019).
The SEM model revealed inverse influence from school,
parental, family, friend, and anxiety aspects (Dong et al.,
2019; Hendron & Kearney, 2016). M-Anxiety was associated with goal structure and instruction in the math
classroom (Chang & Lin, 2019). Effects of the situated
learning were significant because students had less
M-Anxiety after the situated instruction (Chin et al., 2016).
Teachers improved understanding and devised strategies
that enabled students to overcome difficulties and anxiety
(Kwan, 2016). Teachers’ use of brief-guided imagery did
not appear to temporarily reduce M-Anxiety (Henslee &
Klein, 2017). Parents’ math attitudes, talk, and input were
also related to children’s math knowledge and their anxiety
level (Eason & Levine, 2017; Iacopini & Hayden, 2017).
Based on the triadic reciprocity model, Jameson (2014)
indicated that student level of math self-concept, rather
than the environmental factors, was a strong predictor of
M-Anxiety.
Research discussions in this field had different or
unclear results in M-Anxiety levels among different

substantial research has done in the area of M-Anxiety,
much work is needed to fully comprehend the distinctions

Because M-Anxiety is widespread and tied to poor math

overcome it (Beilock & Willingham, 2014).
The Program for International Student Assessment
(PISA) comprehensively measures the literacy performance of 15-year-old students. It assesses how students
analyze, reason, and interpret mathematical problems.
PISA analyzed factors of students and schools related to
mathematics self-efficacy (Usta, 2016); provided evidence
for helping educators and policy makers tackle the massive
problem of math literacy from different gender and
socioeconomic status (Thien, 2016). It also examined
relationships among socioeconomic status, self-efficacy,
and achievement using advanced statistical tools (Kalaycioglu, 2015). Most PISA participants think that the impact
of PISA is rather high, and nearly half of them believe that
PISA results are reliable (Ioannidou et al., 2017).
In this study, M-Anxiety is defined as six items of students’ attitudes toward math learning instead of ten items
on the 2012 PISA assessment. Because schools and students in Asia Pacific region vary widely, the findings in this
region would be helpful references for international
schools and educators (Evans et al., 2015; Hall, 2019; Kim,
2016). We chose five economies (PISA’s term: a
unit/country/city/area) in the Asia–Pacific region with the
top and bottom mathematics achievement scores for a
cross-national comparison of M-Anxiety. Mathematical
anxiety is affected by cultural background, and the effects
are different according to the origin of the subject to be
studied (Brown et al., 2020). The cross-national comparisons and discussions in this study provide information that
can help educators, teachers, parents, students, and
researchers to identify M-Anxiety to actively foster students’ positive motivations, attitudes, and performances.

Methodology
The primary data source was the Student Questionnaire of
PISA 2012. The economies were selected in the Asia
Pacific region with mathematics performance at two
extremes on PISA (OECD, 2014). The sample group is
composed of 17,284 fifteen-year-old students, effective

data, from five economies, including 20.5% Indonesian,
19.3% Korean, 19.3% Malaysian, 19.8% Chinese-Shanghai, and 21.1% Singaporean. From all 65 economies in the
report of PISA, according to mathematics performance
(Order number, Mean of test score), the top-performing
economies included China-Shanghai (No.1, M = 613),
Singapore (No.2, M = 573), and Korea (No.5, M = 554);
yet Malaysia (No.52, M = 421) and Indonesia (No.64,
M = 375), close to the bottom of the achievement mean
list. International database, PISA, questionnaires, data, and
how to use data technology methods are published online,
and the data for research are encouraged by administer
OECD (https://www.oecd.org/pisa). PISA 2012 Student
Questionnaire has actual three sub-questionnaires (-A, -B,
& -C), and each includes 2/3 items. Form-A does not
include the main variable—ST42 Anxiety for mathematics
study; so, students who filled in form-A were excluded
from analyses. This had the effect of dropping effective
cases from 26,575 to 17,284 students.
In PISA 2012, one question (ST42) with 10 items in
Student Questionnaire asks students’ attitudes toward
mathematics learning. Through a factor analysis of the 10
items, we gained two factors: M-Anxiety (mainly related to
6 items) and mathematics confidence (4 items), and this
study focuses on examining the first factor, M-Anxiety
with no correlations with the second factor.
All analyses are around relationships of the student
M-Anxiety with assorted educational variables. Most
variables were means of the items around a pertinent
question or issue of mathematics learning.
Based on the PISA 2012 User Guide, we used the
Sampling Weights (SW) and Design Effects (DE) procedures to ensure that the samples are representative of the
population. Student weight values were obtained by
dividing the mean of SW and DE for each unit of PISA
(Marsh & Yeung, 1998). Descriptive methods were used to
compare the six items and the Factor of M-Anxiety among
the five economies. Figures used to compare M-Anxiety
between male and female students and to reveal the relationships between ESCS and M-Anxiety. Partial correlations were applied by controlling for the influences of
gender and ESCS to find the relationships of M-Anxiety
and multiple educational factors of students, parents and
peers, including ability, test score, importance, interest,
self-attribution, parents’ attitudes, and friends’ activities.
Multiple regressions were conducted to predict student
M-Anxiety by nine factors of teachers’ performance and
students’ activities.

Results
Comparisons of M-Anxiety Among the Five
Economies
There were large differences among the six items and five
economies. The means of Worry_poor_grade and
Worry_difficulty were the highest, and all means were
higher than midpoint (2.50), especially some means of
Malaysia, Korea, and Singapore (Ms [ 3.00). It seemed
that most students often worried about the level of difficulty of mathematics in class or about poor grades in
mathematics, those will (term in the 1st & 10th items of
Table 1) approach, and abstract or unclear worries threaten
students more. Tense_homework and Feel_helpless were
not so high, and all the scores were below the midpoint,
especially those in Shanghai and Singapore. Doing homework did not have much pressure on most students and
working on an existing math problem also did not increase
helplessness. Get_nervous and Am_not_good were around
the midpoint, which means that most students had a midlevel sense of self-evaluation at doing all math problems.
Taking an overview of the above results and values of the
factor M-Anxiety, it was found that most students worry
about imaginary poor grades in mathematics, or the possible math difficulties, but not as much about the existing
problems or homework. Although there were differences
among the economies, more students worried about test
scores or math difficulties, but they were not so anxious
about particular practice itself that they may ask someone
or get helps (Tables 2, 3).
Generally speaking, students in poor achievement
economies had higher math anxiety than those with top
achievement scores: means of M-Anxiety in Malaysia and
Indonesia were positive, but in three high achievement
economies, they were negative. In all comparisons of
items, students in Shanghai and Singapore had very low
scores, but some Korean students’ scores of the same items
were not low. Only Tense_homework and Get_nervous
showed obvious differences between top and bottom math
score economies. Worry_poor_grade presented unclear
results. Indonesian and Malaysian students did not show
high values, which perhaps showed that they did not worry
about poor mathematics grades impact their future works
or lives.

Partial Correlations Between the M-Anxiety
and Assorted Educational Variables
Figures 1 and 2 show that students’ gender and ESCS
affected the M-Anxiety to different degrees; therefore, we
used the partial correlations and controlling the influences
of student gender and ESCS, to indicate better or more
accurate relationships between M-Anxiety and the educational variables.
The results in Table 4 show that student M-Anxiety had
significant and negative relationships with three variables
in all economies: Test-score, Interests, and Familiarity.
Although the correlation coefficients were somewhat different (the correlations were stronger in Shanghai and
Singapore), all results were similarly negative and significant at ps \ 0.001 level. This means that those students
with poor mathematics achievement, low interests in math,
or poor understanding or mastery of math concepts,

formulas, and compositions tended to have higher
M-Anxiety. This finding is consistent in all economies,
without exception. However, there were also three variables that showed opposite results between economies with
top and bottom math achievement scores: Abilities,
Importance, and Attribution. In Shanghai, Singapore, and
Korea, students who evaluated their own ability to solve
problems high, considered math study very important, or
attributed math performance to themselves tended to have
lower M-Anxiety. These effects did not appear in Malaysian and Indonesian students; they even showed inverse
effects. The last two variables, Parents’ attitudes and
Friends’ performances in math, did not show influences on
students in Shanghai and Singapore but had positive correlations with student M-Anxiety in Malaysia, Indonesia,
and Korea, meaning that these influences were adverse and
students showed increased M-Anxiety in the three
economies.
Multiple Regressions Predict M-Anxiety by Nine
Factors of Teacher and Student Activities
Table 5 presents how the four student activities and five
teaching methods in mathematics classes predicted student
M-Anxiety. First, we can see that Shanghai and Singaporean prediction rates were not low (to a nation, prediction R square higher than 10% signifies an important
variable for education fields), but Indonesian and Malaysian prediction rates were low, meaning that these nine
activities do not predict student M-Anxiety well in these
two economies. In Table 5, only one factor was significantly predictive in all five economies: S_efforts negatively
predicted student M-Anxiety. It showed a large effect in
Shanghai, Singapore, and Korea (ps \ 0.001) but not as
strong in the other two economies (ps \ 0.05). Students’
math activities outside school played a role in reducing
M-Anxiety in Shanghai, but not in Malaysia and Indonesia;
and last, students with high M-Anxiety were more likely to
pay their attentions to math learning in three economies.

Discussions and Implications
More attention should be paid to issues of intersectionality
that is how the interplay of race, gender, socioeconomic
status, language, and so on affects math learning as well as
students’ math learning experience in particular contexts
(Edward et al., 2011). Besides students’ much lower math
anxiety in higher achievement economies than in lower
achievement economies, this study presents current states
of 15-year-old student M-Anxiety and the relationships
among various educational issues across five economies
with top and bottom math achievement scores in Asia
Pacific region.
One of the main findings is as follows: all students are
more worried about the imaginary oncoming difficulties
that will course bad grades, which they have no control of.
However, they felt less tense about the homework or the
relatively obvious obstacles in math learning, which they
could easily seek for help (Table 3). This result tends to

the correlational levels were different, and students’
interests in math had stronger negative relationships with
M-Anxiety in those higher achievement economies. This
study attests that M-Anxiety is more closely related to
student own behaviors or attitudes but less to external
factors.
Second, different or opposite results were found in the
mathematics test score top and bottom economies: student
M-Anxiety is negatively related with their beliefs of math
importance, self-attribution, and abilities in the higher
achievement economies, but not in the lower achievement
economies. The study also revealed that in the higher
achievement economies, female students had higher
M-Anxiety than males’ and the M-Anxiety decreased as
ESCS increased; but these situations did not show in lower
achievement economies (Figs. 1 and 2).

Students’ cultural backgrounds and learning styles are
considered crucial by many scholars for improving academic achievement (Dilworth & Brown, 2001). New
findings offer some meaningful suggestions to policy
makers and teachers. Studies have explored possibilities of
environment that can help students to overcome their
mathematics anxiety (Bicer et al., 2020). Policy makers
should offer not only mathematics curricula but also
instructions about how to reduce students’ anxiety and
provide good environment based on characters of area,
district, and school. New findings of this study also suggest
mathematics teachers show more concerns and provide
assistances to students, particular supporting resources for
students’ homework and difficult problem solutions to
reduce student M-Anxiety for good performances. Math
teachers can ease students’ worries on their imaginary
challenges and difficulties that might result in poor scores,
by encouraging students to make efforts in math learning
so as to master the knowledge and skills. As the findings
indicated, although educators cannot change student genders and SECS, they can help them to reduce M-Anxiety
and overcome their academic barriers, especially for those
higher achievement economies. Improving the methods of
teaching mathematics terms/concepts seems to be more
effective in helping reduce student M-Anxiety (Beilock &
Willingham, 2014).
More studies and wider range of investigations are
needed to confirm and verify the applications within educational contexts and the local math education environment
and practices are important factors to be considered. At a
national level, M-Anxiety was found to be associated with
mathematics performance though relatively complicated.
There was a stronger association between M-Anxiety and
math performance in European countries compared to
Asian countries (Kalaycioğlu, 2015). The results of this
study discovered differences between higher and lower
achievement economies or learning environment, and these
findings may explain some conflicts in previous studies
related to student gender, ESCS, the importance of math
study, attributions to oneself, and abilities of learning.
Among the comparisons, same as higher achievement
economies, results from Korean students are somewhat
different from those from Shanghai’s and Singaporean.
Besides students themselves, more reports on M-Anxiety
from their teachers, parents, and peers should be included
in further studies and discussions.

In summary, this study of cross-national comparisons
confirmed that M-Anxiety is negatively correlated with
student math performances in both higher and lower math
achievement countries. Students might frustrate themselves
with imaginary math difficulties rather than the existing
difficulties that they can easily get help with. Student
M-Anxiety in top score countries is more affected by other
learning factors than those in bottom score countries.
M-Anxiety also has three relationship models with other
learning issues: It is negatively correlated with math
achievement, interest, and familiarity of math knowledge
in all countries; and it is differences or opposite relations
with math importance, self-attribution, and abilities
between the top and bottom achievement score countries;
but it does not have solid relations with the influences of
teacher, parent, and friends. Further studies on student
anxiety in mathematics should add a cross-national and
multi-level perspective to the current state of educational
research. The limitations are connected to the scope of the
questionnaires and student self-report items included. PISA
2012 Student Questionnaire do not include anxiety for
other subject learning or peer considerations. Test anxiety
is important in all aspects of life, and reducing anxiety can
lead to more successful behavior and performance. Future
research of student M-Anxiety should include students’
peer groups and other content areas, as well as the context
of culture, tradition, society, and education in their
respective nations.
