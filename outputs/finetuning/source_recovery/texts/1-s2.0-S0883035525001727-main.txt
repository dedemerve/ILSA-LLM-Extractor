Moderated mediation analysis

Guided by the job demands-resources model, a moderated mediation analysis was conducted to
explore the relationship between teachers’ perceived instructional leadership and well-being,
with teachers’ workload stress as the mediator and autonomy support as the moderator, using
data from 46,563 secondary school teachers in the PISA 2022 survey. The results revealed that
instructional leadership significantly positively predicted teacher well-being across 15 participating countries and regions, and this relationship was partially mediated by teachers’ workload
stress. Perceived autonomy support was found to moderate the detrimental effect of workload
stress on teacher well-being. The implications of these findings are discussed.

1. Introduction
Over the past decade, work stress among teachers stemming from the intertwined demands of instructional and administrative
workloads has increased significantly (Bellibas et al., 2024; Creagh et al., 2023; OECD, 2020). Simultaneously, teachers’ professional
autonomy has diminished (Elo & Nygren-Landgärds, 2021), and both trends are strongly linked to declines in their overall well-being
(Oxley et al., 2024). Substantial empirical research shows that multiple pedagogical reforms (Jerrim & Sims, 2022), stricter
accountability systems (Bellibas et al., 2024), and growing uncertainties (Klassen & Chiu, 2011; Yin et al., 2023) have further
intensified teachers’ workload stress. Consequently, this increased stress leads to a notable decline in teachers’ job satisfaction
(Admiraal & Roberg, 2023; Collie et al., 2012), and an elevated likelihood of professional attrition (Collie, 2023; Skaalvik & Skaalvik,
2018). The persistent deterioration of teachers’ working conditions and well-being demands immediate attention from policymakers
and practitioners (Viac & Fraser, 2020; Yin et al., 2018, 2016). Thus, there is an urgent need to devise leadership strategies to improve
teachers’ working environments, ensuring such improvements neither exacerbate workloads nor compromise their professional
well-being.
Among various school leadership practices, instructional leadership, which focuses on teaching and learning outcomes, has
garnered significant attention over the past few decades (Hallinger et al., 2020; Liu & Hallinger, 2024). Recent studies reveal that
instructional leadership practices are essential for creating a positive and supportive school climate (Akgöz et al., 2024; Dutta &

Instructional leadership has been prevalent in primary and secondary schools for decades, which remains one of the most widely
adopted leadership styles due to its emphasis on instructional effectiveness (Hallinger et al., 2020). However, much of the existing
research in this domain has predominantly focused on its impact on student learning outcomes, while overlooking its effects on teacher
well-being, a critical area for institutional improvement in many schools. A growing consensus acknowledges that the association
between instructional leadership practices and teacher well-being remains underexplored in empirical literature (Chen et al., 2024; Liu
et al., 2024). Particularly, the omission of working condition factors and underlying mechanisms may lead to misleading conclusions
about the relationship between leadership practices and teacher-related outcomes (Liu & Hallinger, 2024). Empirical studies examining how instructional leadership influences teacher well-being are scarce, with a notable gap in understanding the underlying
mechanisms, particularly from an international perspective. Against this backdrop, this study aims to investigate the relationship
between instructional leadership practices and teacher well-being, using data from 15 countries and regions that participated in the
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022 Teacher Questionnaire survey.
2. Literature review
2.1. Instructional leadership and teacher well-being
Research on instructional leadership emerged in the mid-20th century amid a wave of organizational reforms spurred by the
effective school movement (Bossert et al., 1982; Hallinger & Murphy, 1985), which sought to initiate the transformation of school
principals’ roles from administrators to instructional leaders (Hallinger et al., 2020). Since then, substantial research has focused on
the impact of instructional leadership on teaching and learning outcomes (Goldring & Greenfield, 2002; Hallinger et al., 2013).
Broadly defined, instructional leadership refers to “school leadership intended to influence school and classroom teaching and learning
processes” (Hallinger et al., 2020), with the core objective of improving learning for all students. Current literature highlights that
effective instructional leadership practices are characterized by framing a shared school vision and providing incentives for teachers
(Hallinger & Murphy, 1985; Lee & Kim, 2016), supporting teacher development through professional learning communities (Liu et al.,
2024), delivering constructive feedback to improve teaching quality (Ovando, 2005), and demonstrating a sustained commitment to
enhancing instructional excellence (Bellibas et al., 2021). All of these practices are likely to contribute to teacher well-being.
Teacher well-being, a multifaceted construct composed of several dimensions (Schleicher, 2018), includes (a) physical well-being,
(b) psychological well-being, and (c) social well-being (Hascher & Waber, 2021). It is defined as teachers’ responses to the cognitive,
emotional, health, and social conditions pertaining to their work and profession (Viac & Fraser, 2020). These dimensions comprehensively embody teacher well-being, a critical foundation for teachers’ development in school settings (Sadick & Issa, 2017). By
synthesizing the operationalized definition and the multidimensional framework, this study conceptualizes teacher well-being as
comprising three facets: physical, psychological, and social well-being.
The investigation into how instructional leadership practices influence teachers’ well-being is a critical yet underdeveloped area
within educational research. Most prior studies have concentrated primarily on the impact of instructional leadership on teachers’ job
satisfaction (Dami et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024; Skaalvik, 2020), a related but narrow dimension that, although related, does not fully
capture the multifaceted nature of teacher well-being. This narrow focus limits our comprehension of the broader impact of
instructional leadership on teachers’ overall well-being in the workplace. While there has been research on the relationships between
school leadership styles, such as distributed leadership (Bellibas et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022), transformational leadership (Khan,
2023), servant leadership (Zhang et al., 2023), and teacher well-being, the specific role of instructional leadership in shaping this
relationship remains underexplored.
Although the empirical evidence on the relationship between instructional leadership and teacher well-being remains scarce,
several studies have revealed a mixed association (Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). Some research highlights positive connections,
primarily because instructional leadership focuses on boosting teachers’ self-efficacy and professional learning community (Liu et al.,
2024), through creating an environment that supports teachers’ professional growth, which has a beneficial influence on teachers’
well-being (Liu & Hallinger, 2018). Conversely, some research has indicated that instructional leadership may lead to psychological
distress among teachers owing to the neglect of teaching autonomy (Chen et al., 2024). When teachers perceive their professional
autonomy being undermined, it can be detrimental for their well-being. Although the issue of teacher autonomy should not be ignored,
the benefits resulting from enhancing teacher self-efficacy and creating a supportive work environment for teachers through
instructional leadership generally outweigh the potential negative effects (Karakus et al., 2024). Thus, we expected that teachers’
perceived instructional leadership plays a significant role as a positive predictor of teachers’ well-being.
2.2. The theoretical mechanism of how instructional leadership affects teachers’ well-being
The job demands-resources (JD-R) model (Demerouti et al., 2001) provides a theoretical framework for this study, aiming to
explore how instructional leadership influences teachers’ well-being and the potential roles of teachers’ workload stress and perceived

autonomy support within this relationship. In educational settings, the JD-R model has been increasingly applied to examine predictors of teacher well-being (Yin et al., 2018, 2016). This model categorizes working conditions into two fundamental components:
job demands and job resources. Job demands refer to those aspects of the job that necessitate sustained physical, cognitive, or
emotional exertion (e.g., excessive workload, work-related stress, job insecurity), which are linked to specific physiological and
psychological costs (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). In contrast, job resources cover the physical, psychological, social, and organizational aspects of the job that are essential for achieving work goals, mitigating job demands, and fostering personal growth (Demerouti
et al., 2001). For teachers, these resources include both individual and organizational supportive capital, with teachers’ perceived
autonomy support emerging as a particularly relevant construct in this study (Collie et al., 2018; Collie & Martin, 2017).
Teacher workload stress, conceptualized as an indicator of job demands, aligns with the health impairment pathway in the JD-R
model (Demerouti & Bakker, 2023). Poorly designed job demands, such as excessive workloads, may trigger heightened exhaustion,
which in turn negatively affects teachers’ well-being. Conversely, teachers’ perceived autonomy support, identified as a core job
resource, operates along the motivational pathway. It addresses teachers’ basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and
relatedness (Ryan & Deci, 2000). By fulfilling these needs, it mitigates work disengagement and enhances well-being (Ostermeier et al.,
2023; Skaalvik, 2020), reflecting the model’s described motivational process. Hence, teacher workload stress is hypothesized to be a
negative predictor of well-being, while perceived autonomy support is posited as a positive predictor.
Drawing on the JD-R model’s pairing interaction process (Demerouti et al., 2012), this study hypothesizes that teachers’ perceived
autonomy support buffers the impact of workload stress on their well-being. As theorized, job resources moderate the relationship
between job demands and work outcomes (Bakker et al., 2007). In the teaching context, high levels of perceived autonomy support are
expected to mitigate the detrimental effects of workload stress on well-being (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). This interplay highlights the
complex yet critical role of perceived autonomy support in alleviating the negative impacts of workload stress on teachers’ well-being.
2.3. The mediating role of teacher workload stress
As a core component of work stress, workload stress is defined as employees’ psychological responses to stressors arising from the
perceived volume of work in the workplace (Bellibas et al., 2024; Lennox & Wu, 2018). Among the various factors affecting teacher
well-being, workload has been identified as one of the most significant job stressors (Jerrim & Sims, 2021). Extant research consistently
demonstrates the detrimental impact of workload stress on teacher well-being. For instance, Bellibas et al. (2024) confirmed that both
teaching workloads (e.g., lesson preparation, curriculum reform) and administrative workload stressors (e.g., data tracking, administrative tasks) negatively influenced teacher commitment via teacher well-being in lower secondary schools across 47 jurisdictions.
Cross-national studies further reveal strong and persistent associations between elevated workload stress and adverse psychological
outcomes, including depression (Greenglass et al., 2003), burnout (Van Droogenbroeck et al., 2014), and emotional exhaustion
(Bakker et al., 2004; Houkes et al., 2003).
Given that teachers’ workload and work-related stress are influenced by school accountability systems, instructional leadership
likely exerts a significant direct impact on teachers’ workload stress (Zhan et al., 2023). Research indicates that effective instructional
leaders can strike a balance between administrative and instructional activities, minimizing unnecessary non-instructional tasks for
teachers (Grissom et al., 2013). By providing instructional guidance and timely feedback, they enable teachers to plan lessons more
effectively and reduce time spent on unproductive activities (Thessin, 2019). Consequently, higher levels of instructional leadership
may correlate with lower workload stress for teachers. However, in some cases, instructional leadership practices designed to promote
all students’ learning could inadvertently increase teachers’ workload (Chen et al., 2024). In particular, when administrators establish
higher instructional standards, teachers may need to allocate more time to differentiated instruction to meet students’ diverse needs.
Nevertheless, the overall impact of instructional leadership on teacher workload stress hinges largely on the efficacy of leadership
practices (Liu et al., 2024). Effective instructional leadership can balance high-performance demands with manageable workloads,
thereby mitigating work-related stress to the optimal level.
As a job demand assessed by the JD-R model (Bakker et al., 2007), workload stress may negatively predict teacher well-being,
which represents a health impairment process (Demerouti et al., 2001). Alongside this process, it is evident that when instructional
leadership is effectively executed, the workload stress perceived by teachers is likely to diminish. Drawing on the JD-R model, we
proposed that workload stress acts as a mediator between instructional leadership and teachers’ well-being.
2.4. The moderating role of teacher autonomy support
Teacher autonomy support constitutes a multifaceted and intricate construct which reflects teachers’ proficiency and the freedom
to make independent decisions within the realm of their profession (Ay & Boz, 2022). It is typically decomposed into teachers’
perceived autonomy in four crucial aspects of their work: class teaching, school mode of operating, staff development, and curriculum
development (Friedman, 1999). In school settings, teacher autonomy support can be defined as the extent to which teachers perceive
they have autonomy in their work and the capacity to independently determine their actions related to their work within the specific
contextual constraints of their work environment (Paulsrud & Wermke, 2020).
While instructional leadership may indirectly influence autonomy support, these constructs are conceptually distinct: the former
focuses on enhancing instructional quality, whereas the latter emphasizes fostering teachers’ self-determined motivation (Ryan &
Deci, 2000). As posited by the buffering hypothesis of the JD-R model (Bakker et al., 2014), when teachers perceived high levels of
autonomy support, it stimulates the development of intrinsic motivation by satisfying basic psychological needs (Bao et al., 2022).
This, in turn, has a buffering effect on teacher workload stress and their well-being. Recent empirical studies have revealed that

teachers’ perceived autonomy support is positively correlated with their overall well-being (Collie & Martin, 2017; Ebersold et al.,
2019), suggesting that higher levels of autonomy support are associated with greater job satisfaction and psychological well-being
among teachers (Oduro et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2022). Similarly, some research demonstrated a positive link between teacher autonomy support and organizational commitment (Collie et al., 2020), indicating that autonomy in the workplace enhances teachers’
contentment with their jobs. On the flip side, some studies have shown that teacher autonomy support is negatively associated with
work stress (Nie et al., 2015), as well as work disengagement and emotional exhaustion (Collie et al., 2018), highlighting its protective
role against the detrimental effects of work-related stress.
Drawing on the literature and the JD-R model, a hypothesis can be formulated as follows: teacher autonomy support moderates the
relationship between teacher workload stress and teachers’ well-being. When teachers have a greater degree of autonomy in their
work, they may be better equipped to cope with the demands of heavy workloads, thereby maintaining or even improving their wellbeing. Conversely, in the absence of sufficient autonomy support, the negative impact of workload stress on teacher well-being may be
exacerbated. Thus, we expect that autonomy support acts as a buffer between workload stress and teachers’ well-being.
2.5. The present research
Building on the theoretical framework of the JD-R model (Demerouti et al., 2001) and the empirical evidence mentioned earlier
(Hallinger et al., 2018; Liu & Hallinger, 2024), we hypothesize that teachers’ autonomy support is positively associated with their
well-being and acts as a buffer between teacher workload stress and well-being. Conversely, the workload stress that teachers bear is
detrimental to their well-being and may trigger negative outcomes. Hence, a moderated mediation model will be validated by testing
three major hypotheses:
H1. Teachers’ perceived instructional leadership exerts a positive direct effect on teachers’ well-being.
H2. Teachers’ workload stress acts as a mediator between perceived instructional leadership and teachers’ well-being.
H3. Teachers’ perceived autonomy support moderates the relationship between teachers’ workload stress and their well-being.
Guided by these assumptions, we constructed a moderated mediation model to examine the relationships among teachers’
perceived instructional leadership, workload stress, autonomy support, and teachers’ well-being (see Fig. 1).
3. Methods
3.1. Participants
The data used in this study came from the eighth cycle of Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), administered in
2022 by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). The Teacher Questionnaire covers teachers’ demographic, socio-demographic characteristics, beliefs, attitudes and teaching practices. The data of teachers and principals were
collected. Both the School Questionnaire and the Teacher Questionnaire address the scale of instructional leadership, but we prefer to
use teachers’ responses since the latter tends to be more reliable, drawing on previous studies (Bellibas et al., 2024; Hallinger & Chen,
2015). Furthermore, the teacher-level variables used in this study, including teachers’ workload stress and autonomy support, were
only available in the Teacher Questionnaire. Therefore, in this study, the teacher-level variables were employed.
Computer-based Teacher Questionnaire in PISA 2022 was administered in 18 countries and regions. Teachers were sampled
systematically from a comprehensive national and regional list either as a mathematics teacher or as a general non-mathematics
teacher in each PISA-eligible school (OECD, 2024). PISA assessment adopted a two-stage stratified sampling design, aiming to

3.2. Measures
3.2.1. Instructional leadership
Instructional leadership served as the explanatory variable in this study. Teachers were asked to assess the frequency of their
principals’ engagement in instructional leadership activities via the Teacher Questionnaire. This construct was measured using seven
items (OECD, 2024), such as “My principal collaborated with teachers to solve classroom discipline problems.”, “My principal
observed instruction in the classroom.”, “My principal provided feedback to teachers based on his/her observations.”, etc. Responses
were recorded on a 4-point Likert scale, where 1 indicated “never or rarely” and 4 indicated “very often”. The reliability coefficients
(Cronbach’s alpha) for the instructional leadership scale across participating countries and regions ranged from 0.861 to 0.943, all
exceeding the 0.70 threshold (Nunnally, 1978) for acceptability.
3.2.2. Teacher well-being
Teacher well-being is a multidimensional construct that encompasses teachers’ psychological, physical, and social well-being. The
scale is a 20-item measure of three dimensions of teachers’ occupational well-being: psychological (perceptions of positive mental and
emotional feelings in school, such as “I started the school day feeling fresh and rested.”), physical (indicators related to physical health,
e.g., absence of disease, normal physical functioning, and biological healthy), and social (enjoyable interactions with the principal and
colleagues in the workplace) well-being. Similarly, prior research has also integrated the psychological, physical, and social aspects of
teachers’ well-being into a composite construct for analysis, which has been proven to be valid in understanding the overall well-being
of teachers (Hascher & Waber, 2021; Viac & Fraser, 2020). Given the heterogeneity of teacher well-being measurements, the items
included in the scale and corresponding parameter estimates were uniformly standardized to account for differing response scales and
to simplify interpretability (Kovich et al., 2023). To investigate potential higher-order constructs of the teacher well-being scale,
confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to validate the second-order factor structure comprising three dimensions
(Kolenikov, 2009; Mankin et al., 2018), and the results in Table 2 showed that it had a good data fit (CFI> 0.90, TLI> 0.90, RMSEA<
0.06, SRMR< 0.05). The reliability coefficients of the teacher well-being scale were above 0.80 in all participating countries and
regions, which implies a high internal consistency.
3.2.3. Teacher workload stress
Teacher workload stress was measured using a 9-item scale (Jerrim & Sims, 2021) that asked respondents about the extent to which
the following sources of stress were present in their work: “having too little time for lesson preparation”, “having too many lessons to
teach”, “having too much marking”, “having too much administrative work to do”, “maintaining classroom discipline”, “being
intimidated or verbally abused by students”, “addressing parent or guardian concerns”, and “modifying lessons for students with
special needs”, etc. Respondents rated each item on a 4-point Likert scale, with options including “not at all”, “to some extent”, “quite a
bit” and “a lot”. The reliability coefficients of the teacher workload stress scale in our sample ranged from 0.77 to 0.88.
3.2.4. Teacher autonomy support
Teacher autonomy support was measured by seven items that cover strategies for fostering teacher autonomy in professional
contexts, including “determining course content”, “selecting teaching methods”, “determining the amount of homework to be
assigned”, “turning down non-teaching tasks (e.g., counselling students, communicating with parents, administrative work)”, etc. Each
item utilized a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “no control” to “full control”. The teacher autonomy support scale demonstrated high
reliability across all participating countries and regions, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients exceeding 0.70.
3.2.5. Control variables
During the construction of the moderated mediation model and testing of the hypothesis, control variables were selected in tandem
with prior research (Bellibas et al., 2024; Hascher & Waber, 2021). The control variables involved in the model included the gender
and years of work experience, as these background variables may be related to teacher well-being. In the measurement model, gender
is defined as a dummy variable, where 1 denoted female and 0 denoted male. Concurrently, work experience is defined as a continuous
variable that represents the total number of years of teaching experience.
3.3. Data analytics
The data analysis of this study was conducted in two distinct phases. In the first phase, we calculated the means, standard deviations and correlations of the main variables within the model for descriptive statistics. Subsequent to that, we performed a
confirmatory factor analysis and a model goodness-of-fit test concerning teachers’ well-being. Additionally, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were utilized to assess the reliability of the measurement scales. In the second phase, referring to the methodologies provided
by previous studies (Hayes, 2013, 2015; Preacher et al., 2007), a moderated mediation analysis using structural equation modelling

4.1. Descriptive statistics and reliabilities
In the initial phase, we started our analysis by performing the results of descriptive statistics for the key variables. As shown in
Table 1, teachers in Hong Kong (China) (M=− 0.870) and Macao (China) (M= − 0.675) reported below-average levels of well-being.
With respect to instructional leadership, teachers in Hong Kong (China) (M= − 0.171) and South Korea (M= − 0.091) perceived their
school principals as having relatively lower effectiveness in facilitating teaching and learning. In terms of perceived autonomy support,
teachers in Hong Kong (China) (M= − 0.609) and Macao (China) (M= − 0.536) reported relatively low levels. Additionally, teachers
from Brazil (M = 0.295) and Morocco (M = 0.257) perceived higher levels of workload stress. Regarding the correlation magnitudes,

We conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to explore the multidimensional structure of teacher well-being. As shown in
Table 2, the goodness-of-fit indices of the CFA model indicate a satisfactory model fit, aligning with established cut-off criteria (Hu &

adequacy of the proposed model.
Table 3 presents the reliability coefficients of the scales employed in this study. The results reveal that the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for all scales have met the established threshold of 0.70 (Nunnally, 1978), suggesting that these constructs exhibit acceptable
levels of reliability.
4.2. Hypothesis testing
The first hypothesis proposes that there is a positive relationship between perceived instructional leadership and teacher wellbeing. We tested the hypotheses in each participating country and region using a moderated mediation model that had been validated to exhibit acceptable model fits (CFI> 0.90, TLI> 0.90, RMSEA< 0.06, SRMR< 0.05). Table 4 presents the results of the
moderated mediation analyses, including the standardized estimates of the direct and indirect effects.
As depicted in Table 4, the results reveal that instructional leadership practices significantly and positively predicted teachers’ wellbeing, and the direct effect of instructional leadership was far greater than the indirect effect through teacher workload stress, after
controlling for teacher background characteristics. The results were consistent across all participating countries and regions, indicating
that instructional leadership practices had significant and positive impacts on teachers’ well-being in the secondary school context.
Thus, Hypothesis 1 was supported.
Hypothesis 2 aims to reveal whether and to what extent teacher workload stress exerts a mediating role in the relationship between
instructional leadership and teacher well-being. As shown in Table 4, the results show that teacher workload stress significantly
mediated the relationship between instructional leadership and teacher well-being in most participating countries and regions, except
for Kosovo. Since instructional leadership had a significant direct effect on teacher well-being, the mediating role of teacher workload
stress was partial. This finding confirmed the mediating relationship between instructional leadership and teacher well-being, with
teacher workload stress as a mediator, which provided partial support for Hypothesis 2.
We subsequently constructed regression slope diagrams (Hayes, 2015) to visualize the moderating effect of teachers’ perceived
autonomy support. As displayed in Fig. 2, the slope analysis revealed that the impact of teacher workload stress on their well-being was
weakened under conditions of high autonomy support compared with low autonomy support. Teachers’ well-being was optimized in
situations characterized by low workload stress and high autonomy support, whereas it reached its lowest point in situation marked by
high workload stress and low autonomy support. This implies that within the organizational contexts, the detrimental effect of teacher
workload stress on their well-being can be buffered to some extent when teachers perceive a higher level of autonomy support.
Hypothesis 3 examines whether teacher autonomy support moderates the effect of instructional leadership on teacher well-being
via teacher workload stress. As shown in Table 5, the results reveal that teacher autonomy support significantly moderates the indirect

with Morocco being an exception. Furthermore, significant conditional effects were observed across all three levels of autonomy
support, with a few exceptions. The findings show that when teacher autonomy support is at a high level, instructional leadership
exerts a stronger indirect effect on teacher well-being through workload stress. Conversely, when teachers perceive low levels of
autonomy support, the indirect effect of instructional leadership on teacher well-being via teacher workload stress diminishes. These

5. Discussion
5.1. Implications for theory
Prior studies have demonstrated that instructional leadership practices are crucial for cultivating a positive school climate
(Hallinger et al., 2017), facilitating teacher collaboration (Goddard et al., 2015), enhancing teachers’ self-efficacy and collective efficacy (Karacabey et al., 2022), promoting professional learning communities (Liu & Hallinger, 2024), and strengthening teachers’
commitment to their schools (Hallinger et al., 2018). However, empirical research on the link between instructional leadership and
teacher well-being remains scarce. The results of this study confirm that instructional leadership practices significantly and positively
predict teacher well-being, aligning with prior findings on transformational leadership (Li et al., 2024), authentic leadership (Shie &
Chang, 2022), distributed leadership (Bellibas et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022) and a case study on instructional leadership in Confucian
cultural contexts (Liu et al., 2024). The unique contribution of this study lies in its cross-cultural exploration, providing international
evidence that instructional leadership enhances teacher well-being across diverse educational settings.
The findings of this research illuminate the indirect effect of instructional leadership on teacher well-being by identifying the
mediating role of teacher workload stress. As hypothesized, our analysis confirms that teacher workload stress mediates the relationship between instructional leadership and teacher well-being. Consistent with the JD-R model (Demerouti et al., 2001), teacher
workload stress, as a job demand, is more likely to trigger the health impairment process (Demerouti & Bakker, 2023), thereby exerting
a deleterious impact on teacher well-being. The results revealed that the teachers who participated in the survey in Brazil, Hong Kong
(China), Macao (China), and Morocco perceived above-average levels of workload stress. Notably, perceived instructional leadership
practices significantly alleviated the dilemma caused by the escalating workload stress, highlighting the critical role of school leadership in mitigating excessive workload burdens on teachers.
This study corroborates the finding that in most participating countries and regions, teacher autonomy support moderates the
relationship between teacher workload stress and teacher well-being. As a job resource in the JD-R model (Bakker et al., 2014;
Demerouti et al., 2001), autonomy support plays a significant role in enhancing teacher well-being through the motivational path. This
aligns with prior research identifying autonomy support as an operative mechanism to alleviate work-related stress (Bellibas et al.,
2024; Hascher & Waber, 2021). Our analysis shows that teacher autonomy support buffers the effect of workload stress on teacher
well-being, such that school environments where principals foster shared visions and empower teachers with decision-making autonomy are conducive to reducing teaching and administrative stress, ultimately contributing to the improvement of teacher
well-being.
This study extends existing literature by demonstrating that sustained increases in workload stress can erode teacher well-being,
while teacher autonomy support plays a significant buffering role in mitigating the health impairment effects of workload stress.
Notably, the results for Malaysia, Kosovo, and South Korea were statistically insignificant. One plausible explanation is that highly
competitive educational environments may attenuate the buffering effect of teacher autonomy support (Hsieh et al., 2024). Recent
research highlights that the role of teacher autonomy in centralized cultural contexts may manifest differently across countries and

5.2. Implications for practice
This research holds significant implications for policymakers and practitioners. First, our findings urge policymakers to fully
leverage the potential of school leaders. Supporting instructional leadership has become a foundational pillar in education policies
across regions like Hong Kong (China), Singapore, and South Korea (Kim & Lee, 2020; Yin et al., 2022). As confirmed here, promoting
instructional leadership practices is a viable strategy to enhance teacher well-being. While school leaders’ primary mandate is to
improve teaching and learning quality, broader objectives like teacher well-being must also be prioritized. Well-being, as a core
objective of school operations, represents a universal value that school leaders should systematically pursue (McCallum & Price, 2010).
Alongside fostering student well-being, maintaining the well-being of teacher communities is equally critical within the educational
ecosystem.
Second, our research provides empirical support for the potential of instructional leadership practices to enhance teacher wellbeing by alleviating workload-related stress. As many countries and regions prioritize improvement in teachers’ well-being, unravelling the layers of teachers’ work-related stress has become critical (OECD, 2023). In the current era characterized by digitalization
and artificial intelligence, school environments have undergone profound transformations, exposing teachers to heightened challenges
and pressures amid these evolving work conditions. To address these issues, school leaders should prioritize reducing the stress
stemming from excessive non-teaching workloads. There is an urgent need to cultivate a collaborative school environment that
actively supports teachers’ physical, psychological and social well-being.
Third, our findings hold specific relevance for regions such as Hong Kong (China) and Macao (China), where teachers’ perceived
autonomy support remains suboptimal. A major critique of traditional instructional leadership is its reliance on a “heroic leader”
archetype, which can relegate teachers to passive executors of instructional policies (Evans, 2022). To address this, we propose that
education systems in these regions empower teachers with greater professional autonomy, which is a critical factor in fostering their
proactive engagement with instructional practices. In this context, autonomy-supportive leadership can help mitigate the
high-pressure workplace culture, amplify teacher voice, and thereby create enabling conditions for enhancing teachers’ well-being
more effectively.
5.3. Limitations
This study has several limitations that merit attention for future research. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data utilized in this
research restricts causal inference. To address this, future studies could adopt longitudinal or randomized controlled trial designs to

establish causality. Second, this study focuses solely on teachers’ perceptions of instructional leadership, excluding evaluations from
other stakeholders (e.g., principals, instructional supervisors, and department chairs). Future research should incorporate evaluations
from these roles. Finally, findings rely on PISA 2022 variable conceptualizations, which may be influenced by scale construction. While
the measures demonstrated reliability, integrating multi-source data in future research will strengthen validity.
6. Conclusion
The burgeoning literature on educational leadership has provided substantial evidence that teachers’ well-being is significantly
influenced by the leadership practices of school principals. However, efforts to determine how instructional leadership affects teacher
well-being through underlying mechanisms remain underexplored. Under the framework of the JD-R model, this study employed a
large-scale international dataset encompassing 15 participating countries and regions to explore the relationship between perceived
instructional leadership and teacher well-being. The results revealed that, after accounting for teacher background characteristics,
perceived instructional leadership had a significant and positive impact on teacher well-being across all contexts. This consistency
suggests a robust positive association between instructional leadership and teacher well-being, regardless of socioeconomic or cultural
factors. Our findings provide international evidence that effective instructional leadership practices are beneficial for fostering teacher
well-being, highlighting the urgent need for school leaders to strive to promote such practices by alleviating teachers’ workload
burdens and providing support for teachers’ autonomy needs, thereby enhancing teacher well-being. The significance of this study lies
in its revelation of the mechanisms through which instructional leadership practices contribute to teacher well-being, achieved by
balancing job demands and job resources.
