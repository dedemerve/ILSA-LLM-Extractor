Measurement invariance of non-cognitive measures in TIMSS across 
countries and across time. An application and comparison of Multigroup 
Confirmatory Factor Analysis, Bayesian approximate measurement 
invariance and alignment optimization approach 
Sebastian Wurster 
Institute of Educational Research at Johannes Gutenberg-University, Mainz, Germany   
A R T I C L E I N F O  
Keywords: 
Measurement invariance 
Non-cognitive measures 
Large-scale assessments 
School effectiveness 
Educational effectiveness 
A B S T R A C T  
Measurement invariance is a key topic in international large-scale assessments (ILSA). The study investigates 
measurement invariance across countries and over time of noncognitive measures in TIMSS, as well as comparing 
different approaches of measurement invariance analyses. Multigroup Confirmatory Factor Analysis (MGCFA) is 
the standard procedure to test exact measurement invariance. Due to criticism of this approach in the context of 
ILSAs, the novel approaches alignment optimization and Bayesian approximate measurement invariance method 
were applied. Principal, teacher, and student data of the TIMSS cycles 2007, 2011 and 2015 of 26 countries was 
used. MGCFA and Bayesian approximate measurement invariance analyses indicates configural invariance in all 
analyses across countries and a variety of invariance levels (mostly metric) over time. The results of the align­
ment optimization method show for most scales that latent means are comparable across countries - under the 
assumption of approximate measurement invariance   
1. Introduction 
Non-cognitive measures are an important part of school effectiveness 
research and international large-scale assessments (ILSA) (Creemers & 
Kyriakides, 2008; Kuger, Klieme, Jude, & Kaplan, 2016). There are 
different non-cognitive measures, such as attitudes or motivation of 
students, characteristics of teaching and teachers, or characteristics of 
the school, like the school climate. While most of the public interest 
focuses on student achievement, other measures are necessary to reach 
the full potential of ILSAs to inform policymakers about the context and 
preconditions of student achievement within and between different 
educational systems. Educational effectiveness research aims to inves­
tigate how educational outcomes, like student achievement, differ be­
tween different educational systems, schools, and classrooms as well as 
which conditions are relevant for these outcomes. ILSAs provide infor­
mation about educational effectiveness of different educational systems 
at different levels (e.g. system level, school level, classroom level), and 
since there are several cycles of the same ILSA, trends that occur over 
time can be measured. Comparing non-cognitive and cognitive measures 
across countries or measurement occasions brings about methodological 
challenges. The most important challenge is employing the same 
measures in all countries and at all measurement occasions—this can be 
difficult as measures have to be translated and might be understood 
differently across cultures and over time (van de Vijver, Jude, & Kuger, 
2019). Meaningful comparisons would require measurement invariance 
across countries and over time (Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014; van de 
Vijver, Avvisati, et al., 2019). Measurement invariance can be best 
described in that the constructs measured at different measurement 
points in time and/or in different groups (e.g. countries) have the same 
“meaning”. 
Currently, Multigroup Confirmatory Factor Analysis (MGCFA) is the 
standard procedure of measurement invariance analyses for question­
naire measures (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016; Vandenberg & Lance, 
2000). However, MGCFA was recently criticized – especially in the 
context of ILSA –, and new procedures have been proposed and dis­
cussed (Avvisati, Le Donn´e, & Paccagnella, 2019; Greiff & Scherer,

Despite the importance of measurement invariance as a necessary

2. Non-cognitive measures in international large-scale 
assessments 
The TIMS-study (Mullis et al., 2016), administered by the Interna­
tional Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA), 
and the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), 
administered by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and 
Development (OECD) (OECD, 2019), are two important ILSAs. The aim 
of ILSAs is to provide information for policymakers to make effective 
decisions, e.g. in educational reforms (Beaton, Martin, & Mullis, 1997; 
Rutkowski, Thompson, & Rutkowski, 2020) and to provide extensive 
data for scientific work (Johansson, 2016; Strietholt & Scherer, 2018). 
For example, TIMSS assesses mathematical and science skills of fourth- 
and eighth-grade students and evaluates other key indicators of educa­
tional effectiveness at classroom, school, and country-level from surveys 
of students, teachers, parents, and principals every four years since 1995 
(Mullis et al., 2016). This enables participating countries to monitor 
trends in these indicators, especially to track changes between fourth- 
and eighth-grade student cohorts. Results of ILSAs are used by govern­
ments for monitoring and are integrated into educational policy (Tobin, 
Nugroho, & Lietz, 2016). 
ILSAs claim to provide key indicators of educational systems (e.g. 
Mullis & Martin, 2013; OECD, 2013). Besides a wide range of student 
achievement data, they provide indicators of the functioning, practices, 
equipment, and attitudes within school systems. In TIMSS, the selection 
of non-cognitive measures is based on the “context questionnaire 
framework” (Hooper, Mullis, & Martin, 2015). The framework contains 
all non-cognitive measures and is organized along different contexts of 
learning: national and community, home, school, classroom, and stu­
dent characteristics and attitudes toward learning (Mullis & Martin, 
2013). One example is the School Emphasis on Academic Success scale, 
which measures principals’, teachers’, parents’, and students’ expecta­
tions and practices to reach academic success. A non-cognitive measure 
representing student characteristics is, for example, the Students like 
Learning Mathematics scale. 
By means of non-cognitive measures, key issues of educational 
effectiveness and equity can be addressed. Non-cognitive educational 
outcome measures, like student motivation or attitudes, and other so- 
called “soft skills”, are equally important to cognitive outcomes, like 
student achievement (Kuger et al., 2016; Seidel, 2008). Non-cognitive 
measures have both a direct and indirect impact on student achieve­
ment and are therefore part of ILSAs (Mullis & Martin, 2013). For 
example, motivation of students is an important non-cognitive construct 
in ILSAs and associated with student achievement (He et al., 2019). 
Furthermore, non-cognitive measures are, in themselves, measures of 
educational effectiveness (Creemers & Kyriakides, 2008; van der Wal & 
Waslander, 2007) and central part of the goals of schooling (Fend, 
2009). 
Data of ILSAs are also an important source of scientific work 
(Johansson, 2016; Strietholt & Scherer, 2018). Reviews of literature 
about PISA (Hopfenbeck et al., 2018) and the Progress in International 
Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS) (Lenkeit, Chan, Hopfenbeck, & Baird, 
2015) shows that an increasing number of articles is based on secondary 
analyses, including measures of the context questionnaire. Recent ex­
amples of research based on TIMSS show applications of trend analyses 
of different non-cognitive measures across countries and over time. For 
example, research on associations between measures of teacher effec­
tiveness and student achievement in mathematics (Burroughs et al., 
2019), the development of a socio-economic index for TIMSS trend 
analyses (Broer, Bai, & Fonseca, 2019), or the investigation of clusters of 
student motivation (Michaelides, Brown, Ekl¨of, & Papanastasiou, 2019). 
One assumption implicitly made or, in some cases, explicitly mentioned 
is measurement invariance (Michaelides et al., 2019). 
The importance of valid comparisons across countries and in trend 
analyses through time is obvious. A significant amount of financial and 
timely resources is needed for participation in ILSAs, since decisions in 
educational policy are based on ILSA results (Rutkowski et al., 2020; 
Tobin et al., 2016) as well as much research is based on ILSA data 
(Hopfenbeck et al., 2018). Valid comparisons are challenged by three 
possible biases (van de Vijver, Jude, et al., 2019; van de Vijver & Leung, 
1997): (1) the construct bias is present, if the construct has a different 
meaning in different groups, e.g. different definition and understanding 
of happiness in Western and East Asian cultures (Boer, Hanke, & He, 
2018); (2) the method bias describes incomparability due to differences 
in sampling, administration of the test instruments, or mode of admin­
istration, e.g. the survey of different age cohorts, the use of different data 
collection modes or if the coverage of the school-going population dif­
fers systematically across countries, and (3) the item bias means that the 
meaning of an item is different in at least one of the groups, e.g. if stu­
dents coming from different cultures do not equally likely endorse an 
item despite they have the same level of the latent trait. Therefore, a 
huge effort is put into the development of measurement instruments, e. 
g. the involvement of experts and translators from each country in the 
sample in the item development process and translation processes as 
well as field tests of the instruments (Martin, Mullis, & Hooper, 2016; 
van de Vijver, Jude, et al., 2019). Despite this effort, it is necessary to 
investigate the appropriateness of comparisons across countries and 
over time of non-cognitive measures derived from the context ques­
tionnaires in ILSAs concerning content validity and psychometric 
comparability. 
3. Measurement invariance in international large-scale 
assessments 
Measurement invariance is – besides the content validity – a key 
topic in ILSAs because meaningful comparisons across countries and/or 
over time require measurement invariance of the measures of interest 
(Cieciuch, Davidov, Schmidt, & Algesheimer, 2019; Desa, van de Vijver, 
Carstens, & Schulz, 2019; Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014). Conceptually, if 
measurement invariance is present, the constructs measured in different 
groups (e.g. countries and/or different measurement points in time) are 
assumed to have the same “meaning”, and no construct bias or item bias 
occur. Technically, measurement invariance testing involves examining 
whether the parameters for factor loadings, intercepts and/or residual 
variances are equal across groups (Meredith, 1993). On the one hand, 
measurement invariance is a necessary precondition to compare scales

On the other hand, measurement invariance analyses offer the possi­

sions by analyzing non-invariant items. Measurement invariance 
analysis is an analytic tool to investigate, if the constructs measured in 
ILSAs are invariant and the preconditions for further analyses are given. 
Furthermore, it is a tool for investigating sources of non-invariance. 
3.1. Methods of measurement invariance testing 
Modern methods to analyze measurement invariance emerged from 
two traditions (Khorramdel, Pokropek, & van Rijn, 2020; Reise, Wida­
man, & Pugh, 1993): The first tradition focuses on differential item 
functioning (DIF) and was developed in the field of educational and 
psychological assessment; nowadays, it refers to item response theory 
(IRT). The second tradition is based on the confirmatory factor analysis 
(CFA) modeling approach and emerged in the context of cross-cultural 
studies, especially to analyze (latent) non-cognitive measures. In the 
context of ILSAs, both traditions are used. This paper follows the 
CFA-based approach because it is more common for questionnaires. 
Currently, Multigroup Confirmatory Factor Analysis (MGCFA) is the 
standard procedure to investigate measurement invariance in the CFA 
framework (Boer et al., 2018; Putnick & Bornstein, 2016; Vandenberg & 
Lance, 2000). Different levels of measurement invariance are distin­
guished; these are tested by a gradual, step by step addition of re­
strictions to a confirmatory factor model. Configural invariance is the 
weakest level of measurement invariance. If the items represent the 
same (latent) construct, it is present, and, therefore, the structure of the 
factors (i.e., number of factors, pattern of item-to-factor assignment) is 
the same across groups. The next level is metric measurement invari­
ance. Here, the strength of the relationship between items and latent 
construct (factor loadings) is additionally equal across groups. The same 
construct is measured in all groups, and each item carries the same 
relative weight in defining the construct. With metric measurement 
invariance, correlations between constructs can be analyzed across 
groups, but latent group mean comparisons can be biased. The next level 
of scalar measurement invariance is necessary to compare group means. 
In this case, the items have the same origin and metric (intercept) in all 
groups. Thus, a comparison of mean values across groups is possible. In 
summary, the requirements of MGCFA to reach scalar invariance are 
strict and in practice, especially in ILSAs, difficult to reach (Byrne & van 
de Vijver, 2017; Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014). One approach to deal with 
this challenge is to relax the assumptions for single or a few 
non-invariant parameters. This is referred to as partial measurement 
invariance (Byrne, Shavelson, & Muth´en, 1989). In simulation studies, 
the latent mean would be estimated very well, if the non-invariant items 
were known (Pokropek, Davidov, & Schmidt, 2019). However, the free 
estimation of certain non-invariant parameters is also associated with 
difficulties in interpretation and a lack of established criteria about the 
degree of partial measurement invariance that can be tolerated (Greiff & 
Scherer, 2018; Putnick & Bornstein, 2016). Furthermore, it is considered 
impractical in the context of ILSAs because of the large number of 
groups, a very high number of models must be tested (Byrne & van de 
Vijver, 2017). 
For these reasons, the MGCFA approach was criticized in the context 
of ILSAs, and new procedures have been proposed and discussed 
(Avvisati et al., 2019; Greiff & Scherer, 2018; Van De Schoot et al., 2015; 
van de Vijver, Avvisati, et al., 2019). Apart from weaknesses of the 
MGCFA approach regarding its use with many groups or measurement 
points, the question arises whether the assumptions of a strict parameter 
estimation, such as that made in MGCFA, to test exact measurement 
invariance are appropriate in the context of ILSAs. This is especially true 
for scalar measurement invariance as a prerequisite for the comparison 
of latent mean values. To deal with this challenge, methods for 
approximate measurement invariance were proposed: the Bayesian 
approximate measurement invariance, which allows for a “wiggle room” 
in parameter estimation (Van De Schoot et al., 2013), and the alignment 
optimization method to minimize non-invariance in parameter estima­
tion (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014). Both approaches should lead to 
approximate measurement invariance and therefore comparable means 
and variances across groups. Bayesian approximate measurement 
invariance testing follows Meredith’s (1993) approach to different levels 
of measurement invariance, like the MGCFA, but with the difference that 
approximate rather than exact parameter equivalence is the goal (Van 
De Schoot et al., 2013). In the alignment optimization method, no 
distinction is made between these levels (e.g. metric and scalar invari­
ance) because it follows a different approach of reaching approximate 
invariance (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014). Both approaches are pre­
sented below. 
Bayesian approximate measurement invariance is similar to MGCFA in 
the sense of testing different levels of invariance, but it is based on less 
strict assumptions about differences in parameters between groups (Van 
De Schoot et al., 2013). Those differences in parameters across groups 
must not be exactly equivalent but are assumed to be near zero allowing 
for a “wiggle room” (Van De Schoot et al., 2013). Bayesian estimation is 
based on priors, which are specified by the researcher before the ana­
lyses. Prior distributions represent the researchers’ knowledge about the 
parameters of the model, like that the difference in the intercepts is near, 
but not exactly zero (Van De Schoot et al., 2013). Allowing trivial dif­
ferences between groups in parameter estimation is managed by speci­
fying a small amount of variance in the prior distribution of the group 
difference parameter (usually a normal distribution with a mean of 
zero). The goal is to keep the differences at a minimum to ensure an 
approximately invariant and therefore comparable construct. For an 
in-depth description of the method, see Van De Schoot et al. (2013), and 
Seddig and Leitg¨ob (2018). 
Another approach of establishing approximate measurement 
invariance is the alignment optimization method (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 
2014). The main idea is to estimate latent means and variances by 
tolerating small differences in the parameters between the groups. 
Similar to partial measurement invariance for in the alignment 
approach, it is sufficient, if the majority of the parameters are invariant 
and only small differences exist, while the MGCFA assumes and tests for 
an exact equivalence of the parameters. To reach the goal of comparing 
latent group means, two steps are required: In the first step, an unre­
stricted configural model is estimated across all groups. The mean values 
of the construct are fixed to 0, and the variances are fixed to 1 to esti­
mate the parameters. In the second step, this configural model is opti­
mized step by step with the goal of minimizing the non-invariance in the 
mean values and variances of the construct for all groups. Therefore, a 
so-called “loss function” is used, which is similar to factor rotation in 
Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) (for a detailed description, see 
Asparouhov and Muth´en (2014)). The goal and final step in the opti­
mization is a minimum of non-invariance between the groups. This is 
achieved, when many parameters are approximately invariant and only 
a few parameters show large non-invariance. The result is the best 
possible estimation of latent means to compare different groups with the 
given data. The model fit is the same as in the configural model in 
MGCFA. Muth´en and Asparouhov (2014, p. 168) state, “The advantage 
of the alignment method is that metric and scalar invariance are not 
required. Instead, factor means are made comparable while minimizing 
measurement non-invariance.” The alignment procedure can be esti­
mated by (frequentist) maximum likelihood estimation or by Bayesian 
estimation. For an in-depth description of the alignment method, see 
Asparouhov and Muth´en (2014) as well as Byrne and van de Vijver 
(2017). It should be noted that there is further research needed on the 
criteria when a solution of the alignment optimization enables mean­
ingful comparisons of group means (van de Vijver, Avvisati, et al., 
2019). 
Strengths and weaknesses of all three methods are discussed in Kim, 
Cao, Wang, and Nguyen (2017), Pokropek et al. (2019), and van de

3.2. Research on measurement invariance of non-cognitive measures in 
international large-scale assessments 
Despite the necessity of invariant constructs and the repeated call for 
investigation of measurement invariance, non-cognitive measures in 
ILSAs are rarely tested for invariance (Braeken & Bl¨omeke, 2016; He 
et al., 2019; Hopfenbeck et al., 2018; Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014). 
Furthermore, van de Vijver et al. (2019) states, with reference to Boer 
et al. (2018), that many studies neither acknowledge nor discuss po­
tential threats to comparability across groups or use single items ana­
lyses, with which measurement invariance cannot be assessed. 
In the context of TIMSS, measurement invariance of non-cognitive 
measures is also rarely tested. A few studies investigate both student 
attitudes and motivation. The measurement invariance across 29 
countries of the scales, students value science, students like learning 
science, and students’ sense of belonging to school, was recently 
analyzed with MGCFA based on TIMSS 2015 (He et al., 2019); all scales 
reached metric invariance. The analyses were also conducted for com­
parable PISA scales with similar results. Bofah and Hannula (2015) also 
reports metric invariance for several scales, e.g. the students like 
mathematics scale across five African countries with MGCFA based on 
TIMSS 2011 data. The study by Marsh et al. (2013) is based on TIMSS 
2007 data of 4 Arab-speaking and 4 English-speaking countries and uses 
MGCFA, too. The authors report overall poor model fit and metric 
invariance respectively partial scalar invariance after controlling for 
method effects by negatively worded items (Marsh et al., 2013). Hans­
son and Gustafsson (2013) found, based on TIMSS 2003, a lack of 
measurement invariance of socio-economic status across different 
groups with migration background even within one country (Sweden). 
Another study investigated different approaches for scaling the “home 
resources for learning index” with TIMSS 2011 data and reports different 
degrees of measurement invariance depending on the scaling method 
(Wendt, Kasper, & Trendtel, 2017). To my best knowledge, no study 
testing measurement invariance over time is available. Since TIMSS 
2015, trend results from the cycles 2011–2015 are reported. Therefore, a 
linking procedure is used to establish a common metric for the trend 
scales (Martin et al., 2016). Insights into measurement invariance are 
not reported. 
Existing research on measurement invariance of non-cognitive 
measures in ILSAs in the field of education is very limited, and further 
research is needed. Similar to results of studies based on TIMSS, other 
studies (e.g. Caro, Sandoval-Hern´andez, & Lüdtke, 2014; Çetin, 2010; 
Lakin, 2012; Schulte, Nonte, & Schwippert, 2013; Segeritz & Pant, 
2013) mostly show a lack of exact scalar invariance for non-cognitive 
measures and, therefore, limitations of country mean comparisons or 
trend analyses, too. However, some studies point to different approaches 
to deal with the lack of scalar invariance, like Exploratory Structure 
Equation Modeling in a study about teaching quality based on PISA 2012 
data (Scherer, Nilsen, & Jansen, 2016), or Bayesian approximate 
invariance with Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 
data (He & Kubacka, 2015). 
Studies in other large-scale survey contexts, like the European Social 
Survey (ESS) or the International Civics and Citizenship Education 
Study, support evidence regarding the feasibility of the novel ap­
proaches to test measurement invariance. While in MGCFA analyses, 
scalar invariance is not reached, the alignment optimization method (e. 
g. Byrne & van de Vijver, 2017; Cieciuch et al., 2019; Munck, Barber, & 
Torney-Purta, 2018) or Bayesian approximate measurement invariance 
approach (e.g. Desa et al., 2019; Rudnev et al., 2020; Seddig & Leitg¨ob, 
2018) show promising results to establish at least approximate 
invariance. 
4. Research questions 
Measurement invariance is an essential precondition for unbiased 
comparisons of groups, like countries, and the investigation of change 
over time. But non-cognitive measures in ILSAs are rarely tested for 
measurement invariance, especially measures at classroom- and school- 
level, as well as invariance over time. Furthermore, several different 
methods exist to analyze measurement invariance, but so far, very few 
studies exist comparing these methods with real data. The goals of the 
study are to apply and compare different approaches of measurement 
invariance analysis and to go beyond the MGCFA by applying both 
Bayesian approximate measurement invariance and the alignment 
optimization method for approximate measurement invariance to 
answer the question, whether there are differences in the results of 
measurement invariance, and to explore the possibilities of comparing 
non-cognitive measures across countries and over time. 
5. Method 
5.1. Sample 
The study focuses on non-cognitive measures at different levels of the 
school system (principal/school level, teacher level, student level). The 
selection of the constructs was based on two criteria: (a) a comparison 
over time, and (b) a comparison over the largest possible number of 
countries. The sample includes 26 countries that participated in the 
TIMSS cycles 2007, 2011, and 2015, fourth grade, in: Australia, Chinese 
Taipei, The Czech Republic, Denmark, England, Georgia, Germany, 
Hong Kong, Hungary, The Islamic Republic of Iran, Italy, Japan, 
Kazakhstan, Kuwait, Lithuania, Morocco, The Netherlands, New Zea­
land, Norway, Qatar, The Russian Federation, Singapore, The Slovak 
Republic, Slovenia, Sweden, and The United States. Data from the 
principal, teacher, and student questionnaires were used to analyze 
measurement invariance of non-cognitive measures. For analyses over 
time, a pooled dataset of TIMSS cycles 2007, 2011, and 2015 was used. 
Depending on the analysis, the sample size varies: N = 105,739 – 
120,315 (student questionnaire), N = 7665 – 8411 (teacher question­
naire), and N = 4117 – 4389 (principal questionnaire). 
5.2. Measures 
Due to its cyclical approach, the development of the context ques­
tionnaires is a successive, step-by-step modification of previous TIMSS 
cycles (Hooper et al., 2015). The modification should maintain conti­
nuity with previous assessments; at the same time, it should keep the 
questions up- to- date with recent research and policy innovations. 
Consequently, only a small number of constructs have identical items 
over time. The TIMSS constructs “School Emphasis on Academic Success” 
(principal and teacher questionnaire), “Students Confident in Mathematics 
(SCM)” scale, and “Students Confident in Science (SCS)” scale (student 
questionnaire) fulfilled the criteria, making a comparison over time as 
well as across a large number of countries possible. 
The TIMSS construct “School Emphasis on Academic Success” is 
measured by seven items, in each cycle from 2007 to 2015, using a five- 
point Likert scale (ranging from very high, high, medium, low, to very 
low): (1) “Teachers’ understanding of the school’s curricular goals”, (2) 
“Teachers’ degree of success in implementing the school’s curriculum”, 
(3) “Teachers’ expectations for student achievement”, (4) “Parental 
involvement in school activities”, (5) “Parental commitment to ensure

The TIMSS “Students Confident in Mathematics (SCM)” scale and

science.”. Students were asked to what extent they agree (or disagree) to 
the items on a four-point Likert scale (“agree a lot”, “agree a little”, 
“disagree a little”, and “disagree a lot”). 
5.3. Analytic strategies 
Initially, measurement invariance was tested across 26 countries for 
all constructs of each TIMSS cycle separately (invariance testing across 
countries). Therefore, different strategies in analyzing measurement 
invariance were applied to show both the similarities and differences 
between the following approaches: MGCFA modeling, Bayesian 
approximate measurement invariance approach, and alignment opti­
mization method; the same series of models was tested using these three 
methods. Next, measurement invariance over time, in the TIMSS cycles 
2007, 2011, and 2015, was investigated, with all constructs being 
analyzed independently for each country (invariance testing over time). 
Again, all three approaches of measurement invariance testing were 
used. 
In the traditional MGCFA approach for exact measurement invari­
ance, all levels of invariance were tested using the following steps: First, 
configural (same items belong to the same construct); second, metric 
(equal factor loadings); and third, scalar (equal factor loading and equal 
intercepts). If scalar invariance does not hold, a model with partial 
measurement invariance could be established or groups, which are not 
fully or partially scalar invariant, could be deleted. These steps were not 
used because in the literature, there is no reliable information about the 
degree of partial measurement invariance that can be tolerated (Putnick 
& Bornstein, 2016), and its application is impractical in the context of 
ILSAs, e.g. because of the large number of groups (Byrne & van de Vijver, 
2017); furthermore, with the alignment optimization method, a more 
suitable method for this case is also used (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014). 
The MGCFA approach follows a step-by-step evaluation. In each step, 
two nested models are compared, e.g. the metric and scalar model. 
Several evaluation criteria for the model comparison are proposed: the 
likelihood ratio test (χ 2-test) and model fit indices, such as the CFI 
(Comparative Fit Index), the SRMR (Standardized Root Mean Square 
Residual), and the RMSEA (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation) 
(Putnick & Bornstein, 2016). According to well-established cut-off 
scores, as proposed by Chen (2007) or Cheung and Rensvold (2002), the 
next higher level of measurement invariance is reached, if the change in 
model fit indices does not exceed the following differences: ΔCFI ≤
−0.01, ΔRMSEA ≤−0.015, ΔSRMR ≤−0.03 (metric invariance) and −
0.015 (scalar invariance). A significant result of the χ 2-test indicates that 
the stricter level of invariance is not reached. However, it is sensitive to 
sample size and in larger samples, as in ILSAs, it is very sensitive to 
minor and unimportant deviations (Cheung & Rensvold, 2002; Meade, 
Johnson, & Braddy, 2008; Putnick & Bornstein, 2016; Rutkowski & 
Svetina, 2014). Therefore, model fit indices are often preferable in this 
context (Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014), and both, model fit indices and the 
χ 2-test, are presented. In the case of ILSAs, the model fit indices were 
also found to be too strict (Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014). Based on 
simulation studies, Rutkowski and Svetina (2014) propose more liberal 
model evaluation criteria for establishing measurement invariance in 
ILSAs for unidimensional scales: To evaluate the overall model fit, they 
propose CFI ≥.95, SRMR ≤.11 and RMSEA ≤.10. The model fit indices 
should be interpreted in conjunction, and, in the case of inconsistencies, 
the CFI should be preferred. For model comparisons, Rutkowski and 
Svetina (2014) propose the cut-off scores: ΔCFI ≤−0.02 and ΔRMSEA 
≤−0.03 for configural versus metric invariance, and ΔCFI ≤−0.01 and 
ΔRMSEA ≤−0.01 for metric versus scalar invariance. 
The software R (R Core Team, 2020) with the package lavaan 0.6–7 
(Rosseel, 2012) was used for the MGCFA analyses. The R package intsvy 
(Caro & Biecek, 2017) was used in part for data handling. Statistical 
modeling of ILSA data requires the use of sample weights because of the 
complexity of the survey structure. The different groups require 
different sample weights (Foy, 2017; Rutkowski, Gonzalez, Joncas, & 
Davier, 2010): the school weight (SCHWGT) was used for principal 
questionnaire data, the teacher weight (TCHWGT) was used for teacher 
questionnaire data, and the senate weight (SENWGT) was used for stu­
dent questionnaire data. Regarding student data, the total weight 
(TOTWGT) was not used because each country should be treated equally 
in cross-country analyses, and the TIMSS senate weight provides a 
transformation of the total weight that results in a weighted sample size 
of 500 in each country to avoid the comparison of groups with small and 
large sample size. Unfortunately, an equivalent sample weight is not 
available for principal and teacher data. To deal with missing data, the 
full information maximum likelihood estimation (FIML) was used. The 
robust maximum-likelihood (MLR) estimation was used, to take 
non-normality for some variables and countries into account. 
The Bayesian approximate measurement invariance analyses were 
performed with software R with the package blavaan 0.3–10 (Merkle & 
Rosseel, 2018). Allowing for trivial differences between groups in 
parameter estimation, a prior variance (“wiggle room”) of ~N(0,0.01) 
for all differences between loadings and intercepts was used; all other 
parameters were estimated by a non-informative prior. The Markov 
Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) estimation was used with the Gibbs sampler 
Stan and blavaan default settings and multicore option. Model evalua­
tion criterion is the posterior predictive p-value (PPP) with PPP < .05, 
indicating a worse fit, and a PPP-value around 0.5 an excellent-fitting 
model (Muth´en & Asparouhov, 2012). For model comparisons, the 
Deviance Information Criterion (DIC), Widely Applicable Information 
Criterion (WAIC), and Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO) were used 
(Merkle & Rosseel, 2018; Vehtari, Gelman, & Gabry, 2017). The 
computation time represents a challenge in applying Bayesian approx­
imate measurement invariance analyses, especially in the context of 
ILSAs with their many groups and large sample sizes. For example, a 
configural model with principal data is estimated in under one hour, 
while estimating a more complex model takes more than 24h - the 
estimation of more extensive models (e.g. with teacher or student data) 
takes even longer. Similar computation time is also reported for Mplus 
software (Kim et al., 2017). The software and hardware set-up used 
(Windows 64-bit, multicore CPU 2.60 GHz, 12 GB RAM) could not es­
timate the evaluation criteria for models with extensive student data. 
Therefore, for student data, Bayesian approximate measurement 
invariance testing was not applied. 
The alignment optimization method establishes approximate mea­
surement invariance with the goal of valid group mean comparison 
despite the presence of a tolerable degree of non-invariance. Within the 
alignment approach, the decision about the possibility of comparing 
latent means is mainly based on the proportion of non-invariance. 
Asparouhov and Muth´en (2014) propose a limit of 25% of 
non-invariant parameters as an acceptable proportion of non-invariance 
for factor loadings and intercepts. Furthermore, Asparouhov and 
Muth´en (2014) propose an R2 measure as an indicator of the variation of 
the estimated parameters across groups in the configural model, which 
is explained by the variation in the factor mean and factor variance 
across groups. R2 values of 1 indicate a high degree of invariance and 
values near 0 indicate non-invariance (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014). 
Both evaluation criteria are calculated separately for item loadings and 
intercepts. To conduct the alignment method, the invariance alignment 
function of the sirt package version 3.9–4 (Robitzsch, 2020) in software

6. Results 
6.1. Measurement invariance across countries 
In a first step, measurement invariance of the School Emphasis on 
Academic Success scale (principal and teacher questionnaire), and the 
Students confident in Mathematics/Science scales across 26 countries 
were analyzed for each TIMSS cycle separately. The measurement 
invariance analyses of the School Emphasis on Academic Success scale 
show very similar results for the principal and teacher data (Table 1). All 
MGCFA models indicate overall poor model fit. For example, the CFI is 
between .71 and .80, and thus well below the cut-off values suggested by 
Hu and Bentler (1999) as well as Rutkowski and Svetina (2014). Despite 
the poor overall model fit, all results of measurement invariance ana­
lyses are shown as example application and comparison of different 
approaches. According to the cut-off scores proposed by Rutkowski and 
Svetina (2014), only configural invariance is reached because ΔCFI >
−0.02 and ΔRMSEA > −0.03. Only TIMSS 2015 principal data indicate 
metric invariance. According to the non-significant χ 2-test and ΔCFI, but 
not ΔRMSEA, also TIMSS 2015 teacher data indicate metric invariance. 
Similar to MGCFA results, Bayesian approximate measurement 
invariance testing also indicates a configural level for all scales 
(Table 2). The PPP is well below the proposed cut-off score.05 and in­
dicates a poor fitting model. Again, despite the poor model fit, the in­
formation criteria are interpreted for demonstration reasons. The DIC 
and LOO suggest in almost all cases configural level of invariance 
because of the smallest values at this level. The WAIC is not reported due 
to calculation issues, and blavaan advised to refer to LOO instead. 
In contrast, the results of the alignment method provide evidence for 
comparable latent means for some TIMSS cycles (Table 2). According to 
the results of the alignment optimization method, a group mean com­
parison across countries is possible with TIMSS 2015 principal data and 
TIMSS 2011 and 2015 teacher data. The R2 measure of the loadings and 
intercepts is near to 1, and the proportion of non-invariance is below the 
25% cut-off point of non-invariant parameters proposed by Asparouhov 
and Muth´en (2014). A comparison across countries seems not possible 
with TIMSS 2007 teacher data because 64% of the intercepts are 
non-invariant, and this exceeds by far the 25% cut-off point. The R2 
measure of the loadings of TIMSS 2007 and 2011 principal data in­
dicates that a substantial part of the variation in the factor loadings 
could not be explained by the estimated variation in the factor means 
and variances across countries. This points to a lower degree of 
invariance. 
The MGCFA analyses of the Students Confident in Mathematics (SCM) 
scale show an acceptable but not good fit of the configural model 
(Table 3). An exception is the TIMSS 2007 cycle, where the configural 
model of the scale failed to converge. In contrast, the Students Confident 
in Science (SCS) scale has no acceptable fit in all TIMSS cycles - both 
scales only reach the configural level of measurement invariance for all 
TIMSS cycles. 
The alignment method indicates that approximate measurement 
invariance is established. The proportion of non-invariance is 0% in all 
models and R2 is near 1, which implies at least an acceptable estimation 
of approximate non-invariance as well (Table 4). However, no estab­
lished guidelines for interpreting these values exist. 
The results show different conclusions that can be drawn from the 
different methods. According to MGCFA analyses, exact measurement 
invariance is not reached; all constructs failed to reach metric invari­
ance. Therefore, neither correlational analyses nor comparison of 
country level means are possible. Another issue is the poor overall model 
fit of the School Emphasis on Academic Success scale and the Students 
Confident in Science scale. Bayesian approximate measurement invari­
ance testing supports these findings. In contrast, the results of the 
alignment method suggest (for the student scales and for some TIMSS 
cycles for School Emphasis on Academic Success scale) that latent means 
are comparable across countries under the assumption of approximate 
measurement invariance.

neous analysis of countries and points in time was not conducted 
because of the poor model fit in previous analyses across countries.

7. Discussion 
The aim of the paper was to investigate measurement invariance of 
non-cognitive measures, used in TIMSS across countries and over time, 
as well as comparing different approaches of measurement invariance 
analyses. In line with previous findings (Bofah & Hannula, 2015; He 
et al., 2019; He & Kubacka, 2015; Marsh et al., 2013), most of the 
non-cognitive measures in TIMSS do not reach higher levels of invari­
ance across countries when using the traditional MGCFA method of 
exact invariance testing. However, in this study, mostly only configural 
level is reached with one exception. In contrast, the results of MGCFA 
measurement analyses over time show – depending on the scale, coun­
try, and dataset – scalar invariance for some countries, but mostly metric 
invariance as well as configural invariance, especially with principal and 
teacher data. Relying on MGCFA alone implies that an unbiased com­
parison of latent means across countries, and in almost all cases over 
time, is not possible with the constructs under study. 
Due to limited opportunities in comparing latent means across 
countries and over time by MGCFA, novel methods of measurement 
analysis were applied. The comparison of different approaches shows 
heterogeneous results. While MGCFA and Bayesian approximate mea­
surement invariance (depending on the scales under investigation) 
indicate configural invariance in all analyses across countries and 
different invariance levels (mostly metric) over time, the results of the 
alignment optimization method indicates for student scales, and for 
some TIMSS cycles for School Emphasis on Academic Success scale, that 
latent means are comparable across countries - under the assumption of 
approximate measurement invariance. 
In this study, no simulation data were used but real data with an 
unknown (true) degree of invariance. Therefore, an argumentative 
interpretation approach is applied, which is based on the results of the 
practical application of different methods. The results show that the 
assumptions of the MGCFA method are difficult to meet with real ILSA 
data, which is consistent with previous research (e.g. Byrne & van de 
Vijver, 2017; Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014; van de Vijver, Avvisati, et al., 
2019). The Bayesian approximate invariance approach shows limita­
tions in establishing approximate measurement invariance with the 
given data as well. The use of Bayesian approximate invariance testing 
requires a lot of computational power and computation time. Therefore, 
models with extensive student data could not be estimated in this study 
with the available technical equipment, which is a limitation in the 
practical application of the method. This issue is reported in other 
studies, too (Kim et al., 2017). This might change in the future or could 
be solved using high-performance computing. In contrast, the alignment 
optimization approach established approximate invariance in the 
models over time in almost all countries under study. However, in about 
a quarter of the models across countries, the proportion of 
non-invariance was too high for adequate latent mean comparisons. 
Although alignment optimizations offer the opportunity to establish 
approximate measurement invariance, they cannot handle a high degree 
of non-invariance (Kim et al., 2017; Pokropek et al., 2019), and this 
might be the case here. This points to its feasibility especially in cases 
with a smaller amount of violations of invariance, which is more likely 
with a smaller number of groups or measurement occasions, as the re­
sults of the analyses over time show. Furthermore, researchers need to 
be careful because the alignment optimization provides a good align­
ment solution despite a poor model fit of the scale. Another issue could 
occur because of poor content validity of the instruments, which might 
be masked by approximate measurement invariance. This could be the 
case for example if the interpretation of words or an item is culturally 
sensitive or if the translation is poor. In this case, a purely psychometric 
approach is not sufficient. 
The results illustrate that researchers need to check for measurement 
invariance prior to comparing (latent) means of countries and/or be­
tween measurement occasions; this is not limited to comparisons across 
countries, but also applies to trend analyses over time. In particular, the 
assumptions of exact measurement invariance as analyzed by the 
MGCFA are difficult to meet with real ILSA data with many countries (e. 
g. Byrne & van de Vijver, 2017; Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014; van de 
Vijver, Avvisati, et al., 2019), but also approximate measurement 
invariance as assessed by Bayesian approximate invariance testing 
seems not easy to meet with real ILSA data as well. In contrast, at least in 
most cases, the alignment approach can establish approximate invari­
ance. This might be because of the more explorative character of the 
alignment optimization approach with the goal to establish approximate 
measurement invariance, whereas MGCFA and Bayesian approximate 
invariance testing follow a confirmatory approach. Nevertheless, evi­
dence of the functioning of all three methods is provided by simulation 
studies (Kim et al., 2017; Pokropek et al., 2019). In conclusion, re­
searchers must decide in the context of their analyses, which method 
they rely on and whether approximate invariance and alignment opti­
mization is acceptable. With regard to ILSAs, approximate invariance is 
widely accepted and proposed as a solution for dealing with a lack of 
invariance (e.g. Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014; Van De Schoot et al., 
2013; van de Vijver, Avvisati, et al., 2019). Alignment optimization 
seems to be a promising approach for (latent) factor mean comparisons 
(Kim et al., 2017), when approximate invariance and the exploratory 
approach is accepted. Nevertheless, a careful and thorough investigation 
of the results is necessary, since Bayesian approximate measurement 
invariance as well as alignment optimization method are not sufficient 
in all conditions, such as substantial non-invariance of many items (Kim 
et al., 2017; Pokropek et al., 2019). 
ILSAs must deal with a lot of different challenges (van de Vijver, 
Jude, et al., 2019). Several different proposals are made as to how to 
deal with non-invariance in the context of ILSAs (e.g. Desa et al., 2019; 
van de Vijver, Avvisati, et al., 2019). Besides the use of partial invariance 
or approximate invariance, an important proposal focuses on the 
investigation of the sources of non-invariance; this could be done by 
identifying non-invariant parameters and groups. Groups causing 
non-invariance could be excluded from the analysis, or different com­
parable groups could be investigated separately. Novel methods of 
multilevel mixture modeling or latent class analyses are proposed to 
identify country subsets, for which meaningful comparisons are appro­
priate (Kim et al., 2017; van de Vijver, Avvisati, et al., 2019). Another 
approach is to identify clusters of invariant items (Schulze & Pohl, 
2021). Also, the lack of measurement invariance is an important result 
(Greiff & Scherer, 2018). Non-invariant items provide important infor­
mation for the revision and development of questionnaires. Further­
more, they allow analyses as to why certain items have a different 
meaning in different countries or provide an insight into how this 
meaning changes over time. 
Another finding worth mentioning is the poor overall model fit of the 
constructs in a substantial number of models. Therefore, all results of 
measurement invariance analyses are shown in an example application 
for demonstration purposes. The findings might be affected by reducing 
the number of items due to the small number of overlapping items over 
time. In this context, Van de Vijver et al. (2019) concludes on the basis of 
applications of measurement analyses and simulation studies that short 
scales – with only three or four items – should be avoided in situations 
with large non-invariance. However, the optimal number of items is still 
unclear. Another issue might be negatively worded items, which could 
explain the poor fit (Marsh et al., 2013). This might change in the future 
when optimizing the instruments. Nevertheless, much more effort is 
needed to develop an appropriate measurement model for in-depth 
analyses of causes of non-invariance and by drawing on the

theoretical background of the construct. In TIMSS, most scales are 
treated as unidimensional constructs (Martin et al., 2016), but it remains 
unclear, if – among other issues, such as the wording – a multidimen­
sional scale would be more appropriate, as Marsh et al. (2013) state for 
TIMSS student motivation and self-concept scales. Although it is deemed 
necessary to (continually) improve measurement instruments, a funda­
mental change of these instruments may lead to uncertainty or the 
impossibility in measuring trends appropriately – this is an unwanted 
side effect. 
The study has some limitations. Despite different scales in different 
TIMSS cycles and with the analysis of different questionnaires, only a 
small selection of non-cognitive measures available in TIMSS was 
investigated. Other scales might result in different levels of measure­
ment invariance. As mentioned before, only overlapping items from the 
TIMSS cycles 2007, 2011, and 2015 were analyzed. The scales were 
revised during the last TIMSS cycles and are now longer to optimize 
reliability and content coverage (Hooper, 2016) – whether this leads to 
improvements in measurement invariance must be investigated. 
Another issue is that no sample weights, which accounts for the different 
sample sizes in the countries like the senate weight in the student data, 
are provided for principal and teacher data. 
The aim of this study was to explore and to describe ways to test 
measurement invariance – but an in-depth analysis to explain non- 
invariance is also important. Therefore, follow-up analyses should 
locate the source of non-invariance and investigate why non-invariance 
occurs. Furthermore, future studies should take the multilevel structure 
of data into account because invariance can occur at different levels (Jak 
& Jorgensen, 2017). Other methods of measurement invariance ana­
lyses, such as multilevel CFA and CFA mixture modeling, should also be 
considered (Kim et al., 2017; Pokropek et al., 2019; van de Vijver, 
Avvisati, et al., 2019). Within the – generally rather exploratory – 
alignment approach, a more confirmatory method was proposed 
recently (alignment-within-CFA approach), which combines alignment 
optimization and CFA (Marsh et al., 2018). Another approach is based 
on multilevel methods with random item parameters instead of the fixed 
mode in all models used in this study (Muth´en & Asparouhov, 2018). But 
it is not possible to take complex survey data into account, and this 
method is therefore not preferable in all cases of analyzing ILSAs 
(Muth´en & Asparouhov, 2018). In conclusion, the choice of the appro­
priate method remains a challenge. So far, no conclusive guidance for 
applied researchers is available. In general, also more research is needed 
to identify appropriate model evaluation criteria for the novel ap­
proaches, e.g. the development of valid decision criteria as well as 
appropriate R2 values and the proportion of non-invariant parameters in 
the alignment optimization approach or the choice of prior in Bayesian 
approximate invariance testing (van de Vijver, Avvisati, et al., 2019). 
Another important and interesting perspective is to integrate the ana­
lyses across countries and over time. Furthermore, different groups 
within countries (e.g. migrant background) should be taken into 
consideration (Hansson & Gustafsson, 2013; Segeritz & Pant, 2013). 
Internationally comparable data were and still are a great challenge 
to deal with in research. New methods of investigating and dealing with 
measurement invariance are promising, even though they cannot solve 
all the problems that arise in the context of comparing means across 
groups in ILSAs. Nevertheless, the TIMSS cycles provide a rich data set 
that – when using appropriate methods – is useful for international 
comparisons of groups and subgroups of surveyed countries and of 
groups and subgroups within countries over time.
