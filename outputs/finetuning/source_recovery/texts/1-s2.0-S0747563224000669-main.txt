A B S T R A C T

Handling editor: Nicolae Nistor

Strong claims have regularly been made in the popular media about the alleged negative effects of young
people’s language use in written exchanges via digital media (what is known as textese). This is especially
believed to affect their reading and writing skills. However, the scientific research literature has hardly
confirmed such effects. Its conclusions are generally based on cross-sectional comparisons between individuals
who differ in how much they exchange written messages via digital media. In contrast, the present study examines to what extent per-country changes in the prevalence of online chatting coincide with changes in the
reading literacy of consecutive student cohorts. This approach presents a novel perspective. The findings relate to
data from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) surveys conducted during 2000–2018.
The data analysis showed a strong correlation between variations in per-country patterns regarding the spread of
online chatting during 2000–2018 and changes in the reading literacy of 15-year-olds. Countries with low
chatting prevalence in 2009 showed substantial improvement in reading literacy from 2000 to 2009. These
trends typically reversed to declines when after 2009, online chatting prevalence sharply increased in those
countries. A contrasting pattern applied to countries with high chatting prevalence in 2009 and decelerating
growth in online chatting afterward.

1. Introduction
With increased communication through digital media, adolescents in
particular have developed a specific form of language use in written
digital exchanges. Usually referred to as textese, it resembles the language in telegrams and newspaper headlines. It is characterized by high
density of content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs),
frequent omission of function words (prepositions, pronouns, auxiliary
verbs, and articles), use of specific abbreviations, and unorthodox
spelling. Various types of expressions that had their origins in textese
have now become part of general language use. Examples include abbreviations such as LOL, OMG, and WTF and the use of numbers as
homophones (e.g., 1derful, 4ever, up2d8).
Textese first developed in the exchange of text messages via oldschool mobile phones. It is reminiscent of the communication style in
telegraphy, where charges were levied per word. In addition, the use of
letters is minimized, creating extremely concise words and expressions.
This approach stems from the initial 160-character limit for text messages and the subsequent introduction of fees for each message sent. The
sentences in textese are typically short and of low syntactic complexity

(Verheijen, 2019). Its use has been widely criticized in popular media,
especially because of its alleged harmful effects on students’ reading and
writing skills (Crystal, 2008). The criticisms mainly concern the disregard of formal spelling and grammar rules.
The use of textese extends beyond texting. It is also highly prevalent
in online chatting, even though the restrictions and fees of texting do not
apply. Since the turn of the century, online chatting has become a major
means for exchanging written messages, especially among youth (OECD,
2021). It must be considered a key source of exposure to textese (Verheijen, 2019).
This study addresses the relation between increased online chatting
and changes in the reading literacy of 15-year-olds at the national level.
In the past decades, online chatting has gained popularity among successive student cohorts in various parts of the world. Exposure to syntactically low-complexity language has gradually increased at varying
rates across countries. Findings are reported on the correlation between
per-country trend changes in online chatting and reading literacy among
15-year-olds from 39 countries on five different continents.

2. Theoretical background and prior research
2.1. Popular media versus academic research on textese effects
Despite concerns expressed in the popular media, scientific studies
have hardly confirmed the alleged effects of textese on reading and
writing skills (Verheijen, 2019; Zebroff & Kaufman, 2016). However, the
basic premise underlying this paper is that academic research has
overlooked one important pathway through which increased textese use
may have affected young people’s reading skills.
Research addressing the effects of textese typically involves comparisons between individuals who communicate to a greater or lesser
extent via digital media. Perhaps most compelling are the findings of a
rigorously conducted intervention study by Wood et al. (2011). In this
study, 9- to 10-year-olds who had never owned a mobile phone were
given access to one for 10 weeks. The children who were randomly
assigned to the experimental group received access to a mobile phone,
including instruction in exchanging text messages. However, after 10
weeks this group showed no significant differences from the control
group on a battery of nine language tests.
Thus, the question of alleged harmful effects may seem settled, as
even a randomized controlled trial showed no harmful effects. The PISA
surveys of 2009 and 2018 also showed that in most countries, students
who engaged frequently in online chatting performed somewhat better
on the reading literacy test (Luyten, 2022). It seems plausible that
thanks to digital media, today’s youth read and write more than ever
before. Conceivably, frequent exposure to textese is more beneficial than
sporadic exposure to formally correct and syntactically more complex
language.
Still, the main idea behind the present study is that the mainstream
research approach is not well aligned with the concerns expressed in
popular media by teachers, parents, and newspaper columnists. They
contend that because of increased textese use and exposure, current
student generations read and write less well than past generations.
The approach in the present study is more closely aligned with this
interpretation and presents a novel perspective. Comparison of a large
group of countries makes it possible to examine whether variations in
the speed at which the exchange of written messages through digital
media spread among 15-year-olds are associated with changes in
reading literacy. In 2009, for example, online chatting was already
common practice among 15-year-olds in most Western countries, while
this was not yet the case in Asia and Latin America. In Western countries,
the growth in online chatting slowed down after 2009, whereas it
accelerated in other parts of the world (Luyten, 2023).
The relationship outlined in popular media (implicitly) describes an
effect of changing environments. Such effects do not manifest as correlations at the individual level, but are reflected in correlations at higher
levels of aggregation. An example is the effect of gasoline and diesel cars
on respiratory problems. This effect does not translate into an
individual-level correlation. Within a shared environment, people who

Fig. 1 displays the conceptual model on which this study is based,
guiding the subsequent data analysis. The central hypothesis posits a
negative correlation between per-country trend changes in online
chatting and in reading literacy. Specifically, it is anticipated that increases in online chatting prevalence coincide with decreasing reading

This study does not explicitly address the notion that 15-year-olds
have become increasingly exposed to syntactically stripped-down language over the years due to online chatting. However, the model posits
that changes in awareness of effective reading strategies and reading
fiction habits play a mediating role in the relationship between online
chatting and reading literacy. The model specifies a negative correlation
between online chatting and awareness of reading strategies. It also
includes a negative correlation between changes in online chatting
trends and reading fiction trends. At the same time, a positive correlation is expected between changes in reading fiction habits and in reading
literacy and between awareness of effective reading strategies and
reading literacy. The subsequent sections discuss the theoretical underpinnings and empirical considerations that underlie and support the
conceptual model.
It is important to note that data on reading strategies were collected
only in PISA 2009; 2018, making it impossible to assess trend changes
for this variable. Consequently, the data analysis exclusively involves
the change in awareness of reading strategies during the 2009–2018
period.
2.2.1. Reading strategies and reading onscreen
Until the turn of the century, nearly all text was read from paper.
Over the course of two decades, computers and smartphones have
largely replaced printed sources, such as books, newspapers, and magazines as the main media for transferring written information. Nowadays, texts are mainly distributed via the internet and read onscreen,
frequently on small smartphone displays, and often in complex networks
of information pages (Hahnel et al., 2016; OECD, 2019a).
According to scholars such as Carr (2010) and Wolf (2018), the
spread of digital media has encouraged the use of superficial reading
strategies, such as skimming and scanning, while discouraging more
thorough and time-consuming approaches. This also applies to online
chatting, as all messages are read onscreen. Moreover, the syntactically

Recent meta-analyses have confirmed that reading comprehension

A command of useful reading strategies is instrumental in text understanding and information use. The ability to comprehend and use
information from a text requires a thorough understanding of when and
how to employ various reading strategies. For example, students must
realize when it is appropriate to employ scanning techniques to extract
key information swiftly, or to engage in sustained and comprehensive
reading when the task requires a deeper understanding of the text
(Chambers Cantrell et al., 2010; OECD, 2019a). The finding that countries with positive changes in the awareness of useful reading strategies
also show positive trends for reading literacy seems in line with this
(Luyten, 2022).
Comprehension of a text is not a passive process. Rather, it is an
interactive dynamic involving the text, prior knowledge, context, and
the reader’s motives and goals. This intricate interplay underscores the
need for readers to employ diverse strategies to construct understanding
effectively and highlights the active role of the reader. Successful
reading involves active engagement aimed at constructing meaning
from the text, making connections between prior knowledge, the text
itself, and the broader context in which the reading occurs (Artelt et al.,
2001; Kintsch, 1998).
Reading in task-oriented settings requires not only a comprehensive
understanding of the text, but also an awareness of the task at hand
(Vidal-Abarca et al., 2010). For example, when faced with the task of
understanding and remembering the information in a text, underlining
important fragments would be a useful strategy. In contrast, simply
reading the text aloud would be a poor choice.

Findings from PISA 2009; 2018 indicated that reading print and
fiction in particular correlate positively with reading literacy scores. The
PISA 2018 survey showed that 15-year-old individuals who frequently
read books in print score higher on reading literacy (OECD, 2021;
Analyzing PISA 2009 data, Jerrim and Moss (2019) also reported a
strong and positive link between the frequency of reading fiction (such
as novels, narratives, and stories) and reading literacy.
Reading fiction typically involves prolonged and intense reading.
This alone likely promotes reading literacy. It also seems plausible that
increases in online chatting correlate negatively with changes regarding
reading fiction. As individuals increasingly exchange brief and informal
messages, the motivation and ability to delve into lengthy and concentrated reading experiences may steadily diminish.
Based on their review of the research literature, Jerrim and Moss
(2019) suggested six mechanisms to explain why reading fiction in
particular holds great significance for fostering the reading skills of
young individuals.
1. Time commitment: Reading a work of fiction from beginning to end
demands substantial time investment. The necessity for sustained
commitment surpasses the casual perusal associated with newspapers or magazines, let alone reading (ultra)short online chat messages, or posts on internet forums.
2. Cognitive demands: Extended narrative texts, such as fiction, impose
cognitive demands on readers. Exposure to diverse vocabulary,
varied syntactic structures, and deeper lexicosemantic networks may
actively promote the development of new competencies, enhancing
the reader’s capacity to navigate more complex textual content.
3. Deep reading and concentration: In the digital era, “deep reading”
characterized by intense engagement with (fictional) narratives is
declining (Wolf, 2018). However, the ability to engage in deep
reading seems crucial for developing young people’s sustained concentration and focus. Consequently, this suggests a “fiction effect”
stimulating meaningful thought and reflection instead of superficial
information-gathering through brief text skimming.
4. Engagement and academic outcomes: Certain types of text, particularly fiction, are likely to be more captivating to readers than others
(e.g., magazines), potentially exerting a more significant influence
on young people’s academic outcomes.

separately (2000–2009 and 2009–2018) and in a rather exploratory
manner. The current study, on the other hand, covers the entire
2000–2018 period integrally and with the explicit aim of theoretically
underpinning the statistical associations.
Research on individual student data in PISA has reported a strong
correlation between reading literacy scores and the frequency of fiction
reading (Jerrim & Moss, 2019). Thus, one would expect that if 15-year-olds in a country read less and less fiction over the years, this coincides
with declining reading literacy. The same applies to knowledge of
reading strategies. This study provides an empirical test of these
expectations.
It may seem perfectly plausible that increased fiction reading and
awareness of reading strategies coincide with improved reading literacy
over time, but this is by no means inevitable. The correlation between
wealth and obesity may serve as an example. In that case, the correlation
is negative at the individual level, but positive at the country level.
People with low incomes are more likely to be obese, but the prevalence
of obesity per country actually increases as prosperity increases.
The PISA data are well suited to explore correlations between percountry changes over time in student achievement and other changes
at the national level. Despite the scarcity of such research, the present
study stands out in its focus on correlations between per-country
changes.
Online chatting has by now become very popular among adolescents
(and the population in general) in many countries. Yet, little research
has been published that specifically focuses on the relationship between
chatting and reading literacy. However, research into the effects of
textese shows that online chatting is one of the most common ways
young people use textese.
The OECD report 21st-Century Readers discussed in detail the
changing reading habits of 15-year-olds (OECD, 2021). This showed,
among other things, that online chatting in particular increased sharply
between 2009 and 2018. The report further stated that students who
often read books on paper score high on reading literacy. This also applies to data aggregated at the country level. The national reading literacy averages are higher in countries where many students read books
on paper. However, these were cross-sectional findings that provide only
a snapshot. Instead, this study focuses on relationships between trend
changes at the country level. Research into the effects of textese
certainly does not show that those who often write and read textese are
poor readers. On the other hand, that line of research focuses exclusively
on differences between individuals, while this study conceives the effect
of online chatting as one of changing environments.

2.2.3. The rise of online chatting
Within the landscape of computer-mediated communication, online
chatting is definitely one of the most widely used means for exchanging
written messages (Verheijen, 2019). It involves real-time exchange of
short and informal messages in an atmosphere that resembles
face-to-face conversation. In comparison to other forms of
reading-related use of digital media (such as exchanging email,
participating in forum discussions, and looking up practical information), the rise of online chatting has been remarkably strong in the
second decade of the 21st century (OECD, 2021). The PISA data show
that in 2009, over 30% of 15-year-olds engaged in online chatting on a
daily basis in the 39 countries included in the present study. By 2018,
this had increased to over 70%. However, the growth patterns vary
considerably among countries (Luyten, 2023).
Midway through the second decade of the 21st century, the three
most popular online chat services (WhatsApp, WeChat, and Telegram)
each had hundreds of millions of active users (Burns, 2016; Custer,
2016; Kim, 2015). Before the advent of smartphones, MSN Messenger
(also known as Windows Live Messenger) was the most widely used
online chat platform. Released in 1999, it had acquired over 300 million
users within a decade (Protalinski, 2009). In 2014, however, it was
discontinued (Warren, 2014). By then, smartphone-based services such
as WhatsApp had taken over.
Globally, WhatsApp is probably the most widely used tool for online
chatting. WeChat (released in 2011) is mostly used in China. Telegram
(released in 2013) is particularly popular in Eastern Europe and the
Middle East. Kakao Talk (released in 2010) is widely used in South
Korea. Line (released in 2011) is particularly popular in Indonesia,
Japan, and Thailand (Lukman, 2013).
Over the years, the nature of online chatting has changed in certain
respects. Initially, it exclusively involved exchanging written messages.
In recent years, the possibilities for exchanging graphics, short films, and
such have expanded considerably. However, this is a relatively new
development that started only towards the end of the period covered by
this study (2000–2018). But even today (2024), the exchange of written
messages is still at the core of the most commonly used online chatting
tools. This sets them apart from other popular instant messaging apps
such as Snapchat and TikTok. These tools are primarily used to exchange
images and short videos. Written comments here merely supplement the
visual messages. In any case, it seems unlikely that the effect of online
chatting on reading literacy has become less negative with the expansion
of the graphic options. The opposite seems more likely.

Correlations between trend changes are much more difficult to
dismiss as statistical artifacts than cross-sectional correlations. This also
goes for correlations between straightforward growth scores. In that
case, an alternative explanation requires a variable that can account for
the correlation between the changes. The most likely candidates will be
other changes that occurred simultaneously. In the case of correlations
between trend changes (i.e., shifts in changes), the search for alternative
explanations becomes even more challenging.
The phenomenon under study hardly suits an experimental approach
or even rigorous quasi-experimental methods such as interrupted time
series analysis. The explanatory variable (per-country growth in online
chatting prevalence) defies deliberate manipulation. Unlike policy
measures (such as tax regulations or speed limits), this is a largely
autonomous development. The focus on correlations between trend
changes, therefore, offers quite a viable approach to assess the countrylevel relationship between online chatting and reading literacy.

3.1. Research objectives
The present study focuses on trend changes during 2000–2018. The
main research question addressed is:
To what extent do trend changes in per-country online chatting
prevalence correlate with trend changes in PISA reading literacy scores?
Trend changes denote changes in growth during 2009–2018
compared to 2000–2009. Negative values indicate less growth during
2009–2018 than during 2000–2009. Positive values indicate more
growth during 2009–2018. Three distinct scenarios can produce negative trend changes: decline during 2009–2018 after initial growth during
2000–2009, stronger decline during 2009–2018 compared to the prior
period, and weaker growth during 2009–2018 compared to the prior
period. Likewise, the following scenarios may produce positive trend
changes: growth after initial decline, stronger growth during
2009–2018, and weaker decline during 2009–2018.

further details on the operationalization of the three explanatory variables in the conceptual model.

3.2.2. Reading literacy assessments
The conceptual framework guiding the development of the PISA
reading tests has been revised from time to time. Reading in digital
environments and drawing on multiple resources was specifically
addressed in the 2018 assessment. The 2018 definition of reading literacy was: “Reading literacy is understanding, using, evaluating
reflecting on and engaging with texts in order to achieve one’s goals, to
develop one’s knowledge and potential and to participate in society”
(OECD, 2019a, p. 28).
The entire PISA assessment takes about 3 h. This includes cognitive
tests on reading, mathematics, and science, a short questionnaire, and
some breaks. The cognitive tests make up the main part (approximately
2 h). The reading test includes various text types (e.g., narratives, arguments, and instructions) and addresses four main reading processes,
namely, reading fluently, locating information, understanding, and
evaluating/reflecting.
Despite adaptations across the years, the test design allows for the
assessment of trends in reading literacy over nearly 2 decades
(Schleicher, 2019). Another example of changes over time is the shift
from paper-pencil assessments to computer-based assessments in 2015.
This required adjustments for “mode effects” to ensure comparability
across years, as the computer versions yield lower percentages of correct
responses for most items (Jerrim et al., 2018; OECD, 2017; Robitzsch
et al., 2020). Among the 39 countries in this study, paper-pencil assessments were still used in Albania and Indonesia in 2015. Both
countries switched to computer-based assessments in 2018. Argentina
(not participating in 2015) was the only country that made use of
paper-pencil assessments in 2018.

3.2.4. Absence of missing data
It is important to note that there were no missing data at the country
level, although for reading strategies only changes during 2009–2018
could be determined. Apart from this, a trend change value was available for every variable and country, including the control variables. All
countries participated in 2000/2002, 2009, and 2018. For some countries, values were missing in intermediate years. This did not hinder the
estimation of trend changes. Due to nonresponse, a small to moderate
percentage of respondents participating in the PISA surveys had missing
values per country and year. However, yearly averages and percentages
could always be obtained.
3.3. Data analysis
3.3.1. General approach
The primary findings to be reported are the correlations between percountry trend changes in Fig. 1 variables. In the next step, path analysis
was applied to assess the empirical validity of the conceptual model. In
order to test the robustness of the conceptual model, further analyses
assessed to what extent the path coefficients change when additional
variables are included in the model. This involved 15 variables, which
relate to societal changes (e.g., internet availability), sample characteristics (e.g., coverage of the entire population of 15-year-olds),
educational changes (e.g., student-teacher ratios), and other reading
activities and attitudes (e.g., reading magazines and newspapers). See
Table D1 (Appendix D) for a complete list. The analyses assessed
whether any of these variables show statistically significant relationships with one or more variables in the conceptual model. The model
would fall short of robustness if the relationships between the four core
variables changed substantially when including additional variables.

3.2.3. Prevalence of chatting, reading fiction and awareness of reading
strategies
Information on online chatting is based on self-reports. Students
were asked to indicate how often they engaged in a number of ICT activities. The chatting prevalence data involve per-country percentages of
students involved in chatting several times a day. Information on the
prevalence of online chatting was lacking in the 2000/2002 PISA surveys. However, it seems realistic to assume that in 2000 this was close to
zero in every country. Even though chat platforms did exist in 2000,
getting access to the internet was not nearly as easy as it is today. The
rate of information exchange was also limited. Internet connections
were typically established on desktop computers, using modems and
analog telephone lines. In order to chat online, 15-year-olds had to
overcome considerably more obstacles in 2000 than their peers in later
years. Consequently, the 2009 levels are presumed to indicate growth
during 2000–2009. Changes in growth between the periods before and
after 2009 represent the trend changes.
Likewise, data on reading fiction relate to self-reports. Students
indicated how often they engaged in reading certain materials because
they wanted to (e.g., newspapers, magazines, comic books). Data on
reading fiction involve percentages of students reporting that they read
fiction at least once a month (e.g., novels, narratives, stories). These data
were collected in 2000/2002, 2009, and 2018.

3.3.2. Assessing trend changes
In the present study, the data points for the explanatory variables did
not match the reading literacy data. Five to seven reading literacy
measurements were available per country, but there were only two or
three measurements for online chatting, reading fiction, and online
chatting. In order to deal with this limitation, per-country trend changes
were determined. Next, the correlations between these trend changes
were computed.
In the data analysis, 2009 was a crucial year. Trend changes were
determined by comparing changes before and after 2009. The 2009
survey was the first to be conducted after the introduction of the iPhone
and WhatsApp. The rapid spread of smartphones after the iPhone
strongly stimulated the increase of written communication through
digital media (Verheijen, 2019). Within a few years after its release,
WhatsApp grew to be the most popular messaging app in the world, with
hundreds of millions of users (Kim, 2015). In the years after the launch
of WhatsApp, alternative chat services such as Kakao-talk, Line, and

trend changes were computed by subtracting changes in the period up to

less than for 2000/2002–2009, this resulted in a negative trend change.

during 2000–2009. This approach was also used for reading fiction. In
addition, it was applied for six control variables on reading attitudes and
reading activities.
No information on online chatting was collected in 2000/2002.
Assuming that in 2000 the percentage of 15-year-olds chatting several
times a day was close to zero in all countries, the 2009 percentages are
considered to express growth during 2000–2009. In the next period,
chatting kept increasing in nearly all countries. Negative trend changes
for online chatting thus indicate decreasing growth and positive ones
indicate increasing growth.
During 2000–2009, reading fiction was on the rise in most countries.
Negative trend changes for this variable primarily denote a decrease
during 2009–2018 after an increase in the previous years. Positive trend
changes mostly indicate the opposite (i.e., some increase after a previous
decline).
Data on awareness of reading strategies were only collected in 2009
and 2018. Trend changes for this variable could not be assessed. Only
change during 2009–2018 is included in the data analysis.

4. Results

3.3.3. Country weights
The countries included in this study vary greatly in size. The United
States is the largest. Each year, U.S.A. samples represent about 3.4
million students. In contrast, in countries such as Iceland and
Luxembourg the total population of 15-year-olds hardly amounts to
5000 individuals. To account for this, weights were applied. Countries
were weighted by the total number of students the national samples
represented. This quantity was averaged over the number of surveys
involved. Finally, the country weights were standardized, so that the
average across countries equaled one. Thus, miscalculation of standard
errors – and consequently, of statistical significance – was prevented.
Appendix A details how the country weights were standardized.
Table B3 (Appendix B) lists the total numbers of students and standardized weights for each country.
If all countries carried the same weight (following a one country –
one vote rule), this would cause European countries to dominate the
overall picture. Of the 39 countries involved, 24 (i.e., 61%) were European. However, they only contributed 31% of the students represented
by the 39 national samples (see Table 1).

contrasting trend changes in reading literacy. The main purpose is to
demonstrate the research approach and facilitate the interpretation of
findings presented later on. For South Korea, the reading literacy trend

5. Conclusion and discussion
This section discusses the methodological strengths and weaknesses
of the present study. It also addresses the theoretical relevance of the
findings. Finally, it considers its implications from a policy perspective.
5.1. Methodological considerations
The present study focuses on correlations between trend changes and
shows that per-country accelerations of online chatting after 2009
coincided with decelerations in reading literacy trends. It is difficult to
dismiss such a correlation as a mere statistical artifact. Nevertheless,
future studies could provide more detailed information on changes in
chatting prevalence over time. The relationship between online chatting
and reading skills could therefore be examined more closely. However, it
remains a challenge to apply rigorous research designs to assess the
causal effects of largely autonomous changes. An interrupted time series
design requires a well-defined cutoff point. This hardly applies to online
chatting. The date of introduction is not particularly relevant; rather,
what matters is the hardly predictable pace of its spread.
Additional analyses were conducted to check if potentially confounding variables available in the PISA datasets could account for the
observed correlations between trend changes. This data-driven
approach only yielded moderate adaptations of the findings reported
in Fig. 4. Thus, a number of factors can be precluded as alternative explanations for the reported correlations between declining reading literacy, online chatting, reading fiction, and awareness of useful reading
strategies during 2000–2018. These factors include societal changes
(regarding immigrant percentages, internet availability, parents’
educational and occupational levels), other changes in reading behavior
and attitudes (time spent reading, reading enjoyment, and frequency of
reading magazines, comic books, non-fiction books, and newspapers),
educational changes (regarding student-teacher ratios and grade levels),
and changes in sample characteristics (coverage of all 15-year-olds,
gender distribution, and student age). Still, it is important to note that
this study only considered variables included in the PISA datasets.
Possibly confounding factors that were not taken into account include
objective country characteristics such as changes in population growth,
expenditure on education, and income inequality. Moreover, countryspecific changes could explain part of the variation in trend changes,
such as teacher shortages or major curriculum changes.
When dealing with repeated measures (cross-sectional or otherwise),
the statistical phenomenon of regression to the mean always lurks in the
background (Barnett et al., 2005; Ostermann et al., 2008). This implies
that if a variable is measured at an extreme level on one occasion, it is
likely to be less extreme on a subsequent measurement. Such observed
fluctuations may be misinterpreted as genuine changes. The preferred

5.2. Theoretical considerations
Despite claims in the popular media, scientific research as yet has
hardly produced convincing evidence for negative effects of textese on
reading and writing skills. Youth who engage heavily in textese exchanges do not seem to be poor readers or writers. However, the present
study shows an unmistakable correlation between per-country trend
changes in online chatting and reading literacy. In this paper, the link
between chatting and reading literacy is conceived as an effect of
changing environments. This paper’s focus on long-term trends is more
in line with concerns raised by teachers and opinion makers. Their
worries focus on the disappointing reading and writing skills of today’s
youth in comparison to the past. The bulk of prior academic research
findings on textese effects relate to cross-sectional findings.
Prior research showed that countries with low chatting prevalence in
2009 (i.e., less than 35% of 15-year-olds chatting daily) showed strong
increases in online chatting during 2009–2018 (Luyten, 2023). These
countries usually showed improvement in reading literacy during
2000–2009, but these trends typically reversed when after 2009 the
online chatting growth accelerated. Countries with high online chatting
prevalence in 2009 showed no improvement in reading literacy on
average during 2000–2009. These countries did show improvement in
the subsequent period, when online chatting growth decelerated.
However, this same study also indicated that reading literacy levels do
not drop with moderate increases in online chatting, that is, if the annual

increase of 15-year-olds chatting daily does not exceed 5%.
Countries in Asia and Latin America typically exhibited accelerating
trends in online chatting, in contrast with the deceleration observed in
most Western countries after 2009 (see Table B3). Generally, this
divergence coincided with their respective levels of economic prosperity. Additionally, Western countries generally exhibited higher
reading literacy averages. Nevertheless, Japan and South Korea stood
out as exceptions, with both impressive reading literacy levels and GDPs
on par with the most affluent Western nations. Despite their economic
affluence and high reading literacy levels, these countries displayed
online chatting and reading literacy trends consistent with their Asian
and Latin American counterparts.
Research based on tightly controlled experiments has provided
ample evidence of adverse effects of reading from screens on comprehension. However, reproducing evidence of this relationship “outside
the laboratory” appears remarkably difficult. Large-scale survey
research on the relationship between reading literacy and use of digital
media yields inconclusive results (Gubbels et al., 2020). Still, this does
not imply that the “laboratory findings” are irrelevant in everyday life.
The current study shows a clear correlation between reading literacy
and a most pervasive aspect of digital media use. Changes in reading
fiction and awareness of useful reading strategies partly mediate the
relationship.
The correlation between online chatting and reading literacy is a
novel finding, whereas effects of reading fiction and reading strategies
have been reported in prior research (Artelt et al., 2001; Brown et al.,
1984; Jerrim & Moss, 2019; OECD, 2010, 2021). However, the correlation of reading literacy with reading strategies and the frequency of
reading fiction is also observed at the individual level. Students who
engage more often in reading fiction and apply useful reading strategies
score higher on reading tests. The correlation between changes in online
chatting and reading literacy relates exclusively to changes in
country-level aggregates.
This correlation between per-country changes in online chatting and
reading literacy may be explained by changes in general language use.
The underlying assumption is that with increased online chatting, language use has changed significantly (especially among 15-year-olds).
This in turn may have resulted in increased exposure to syntactically
simplified language, which can explain declining reading literacy levels.
Although this may seem a plausible assumption, as language is acquired and adapted in interaction with one’s social environment,
changes in language use were not included as an explanatory variable in
the current study. This would require an operationalization for
measuring changes in language use at the country level, which would
have then been included as an explanatory variable. That was considered to be beyond the scope of this study, but it would certainly be a
valuable topic for future research.
In this study, the effect of online chatting is conceived as an effect of
changing environments. The possibility that the effect of online chatting
is also reflected in differences between individuals has not been
considered. Research results on the effects of textese have provided little
evidence that frequent communication via digital media is associated
with poor reading or writing skills. It should be noted that this line of
research is almost exclusively based on cross-sectional comparisons. The
rigorous intervention study by Wood et al. (2011) showed no effect on
language skills of providing mobile phones to 9- to 10-year-olds after 10
weeks. However, survey research in which children were followed for
several years has indeed shown negative correlations between access to
home computers and mobile phones and their reading skills (Dempsey
et al., 2019; Vigdor et al., 2014).
With regard to the individual level, it may therefore be premature to
definitely rule out long-term effects of digital media use. However, the
PISA surveys do not allow for monitoring longitudinal development of
individual reading literacy over time. Across the seven triennial surveys
conducted from 2000 to 2018, the reading proficiency of 15-year-olds in
various countries was evaluated for each specific year. Consequently, it

is feasible to assess changes in reading literacy on a per-country basis.
However, in terms of individual students, the surveys only provide crosssectional data. Examining the correlation between online chatting and
individual reading literacy growth requires individuals to be followed
over time. Undoubtedly, there is a critical need for research on the
enduring effects of digital media on reading literacy (Wolf, 2018).
5.3. Policy implications
This study suggests that a major exogenous development may have
shaped changes in national reading literacy averages. The diffusion of
digital media poses new challenges for the education sector. From a
policy perspective, an important message is that blaming education
professionals (teachers, school principals, and policymakers) for
declining reading scores is hardly warranted. The issue requires close
attention, but deteriorating reading levels should not be interpreted as
conclusive evidence of failing education. One could only infer that education professionals have not reacted adequately to changing circumstances. However, such conclusions are easy to draw in hindsight. At the
turn of the century, few experts would have foreseen exactly how far and
at what pace various ICT applications would spread and what their effects would be.
Still, it is important to acknowledge that the rise of online chatting
may have had an adverse impact on the reading literacy of 15-year-olds
across the world. It seems that under current conditions, young people
acquire fewer reading skills in out-of-school contexts than before. As a
result, education is faced with new challenges in order to maintain
reading literacy standards. Schools and teachers will have to devote
even more effort than before to prevent further decline. Even in the
digital age, traditional reading skills remain important. Reading skills
remain instrumental in searching for and interpreting information online (Hahnel et al., 2016, 2018; Kanniainen et al., 2019; Salmerón et al.,
2018). The education sector is expected to develop solutions to challenges that result from the highly successful initiatives of ICT companies.
The findings in this paper indicate two approaches for enhancing
reading literacy: promotion of reading fiction and a determined focus on
the acquisition of reading strategies. These are hardly novel insights.
Still, the present study stands out from the bulk of educational research.
It shows that per-country growth on these variables coincides with
improvement in reading literacy. Mainstream educational research
primarily focuses on differences between individuals.
Frequent reading is probably the best way to preserve the reading
literacy of upcoming generations. However, this should not be limited to
short informal texts in headline language from smartphone displays. In
the pre-internet era, young individuals spent more non-school hours
reading books, newspapers, and magazines. Nowadays, social media
takes up much more of their free time. The development of reading
literacy will therefore depend more than before on the efforts of formal
education. It is imperative to recognize that this goes beyond the domain
of language education alone. Texts play a pivotal role across diverse
school subjects, such as history, geography, biology, physics, and
chemistry. Thus, it becomes essential to address and enhance reading
literacy also within these subject areas. An additional advantage may be
that practicing reading skills in various contexts can illuminate its significance to students.
In many respects, ICT has made people’s lives easier and more
enjoyable. Nevertheless, it would be a severe loss if today’s youth no
longer learn to master traditional reading skills. Approaches such as
skimming and scanning may become the only methods that young
readers master. In an ideal world, readers would be able to adapt their
approaches to changing circumstances. Sometimes superficial methods
such as scanning and skimming might be appropriate. However, more
thorough and time-consuming approaches are called for when it comes
to fully understanding complex texts. It is to be hoped that future youth
will learn to tailor their reading approaches to varying circumstances.

Appendix A. Datasets and Country Weights
A basic description is provided of both datasets used in this study. This includes details on standardized and non-standardized country weights.
Two datasets
The main findings reported in this study relate to a dataset in which each of the 39 records represents one country, namely, the SPSS dataset
PISA2000_18Wide39Countries.sav. A different dataset (PISA2000_18Long39Countries.sav) was used for a number of preparatory analyses. In this
dataset, each record represents the measurements of a specific year for each country. For most countries, the number of measurements equals seven.
The total number of records in this dataset is 262 (see Table B3). The main purpose of the preparatory analyses was estimating the per-country trend
changes (i.e., shifts in changes) for reading literacy and a number of control variables. In addition, this dataset was used to assess to what extent
reading literacy per country varied between years. A large amount of variance between years indicates strong fluctuations over time. This would imply
that the reading literacy averages lack statistical reliability.
Five variables are present in both datasets:
1. CNT: country identifier (e.g., Brazil)
2. Continent: country’s continent (e.g., South America)
3. ValidMeasure: number of valid measures per country (seven in most cases)
4. FirstMeasure: first year of measurement (either 2000 or 2002)
5. Students2000-18: average number of students represented per year (non-standardized country weight)
The dataset PISA2000_18Long39Countries.sav includes three additional basic indicators:
1. Year: year of measurement (2000, 2002, 2003, 2006, 2009, 2012, 2015, 2018)
2. Year Cntrd: year centered at 2009 (− 9, − 7, − 6, − 3, 0, 3, 6, 9)
3. Stage: Indicator denoting whether the measurement relates to 2000–2009 or 2012–2018
Next, this dataset includes 10 variables for which measurements are available every year. First is the main variable of interest, namely, each
country’s average reading literacy score per year (READING). Next is a range of nine variables that served as control variables. These include (among
others) percentages of students with internet at home (InternetPrc) and student-teacher ratios (STratio). For these variables, trend changes were
estimated by means of multilevel analysis (in SPSS). These trend changes indicate to what extent the trends across years accelerated or decelerated
after 2009. The results per country are stored in the dataset PISA2000_18Wide39Countries.sav. The trend change for reading literacy is stored under
the variable name TrendchangeReading, and the trend change for internet at home is stored as TrendchangeInternetPrc, and so forth.
Moreover, the dataset PISA2000_18Wide39Countries.sav contains six additional trend change variables that were calculated differently. These all
relate to reading-related behavior, for example, the percentage of students reading fiction at least once a month (TrendchangePrcFiction). For these
variables, measurements are only available in 2000/2002, 2009 and 2018. For online chatting (TrendchangePrcChat), only data from 2009 to 2018
are available. For reading strategies, the variable UNDREM2009_18 denotes the change during 2009–2018.
The PISA2000_18Long39Countries.sav includes 10 additional variables indicating the predicted scores per year for reading literacy and the nine
aforementioned control variables. These variables were stored as PRED_Reading, PRED_InternetPrc, PRED_Stratio, etc. The predictions were obtained
via multilevel analysis.
The PISA2000_18Wide39Countries dataset includes standardized country weights in addition to the variable Students2000_18, which represents
the non-standardized country weights. The standardized weights make up the variable WEIGHT. Furthermore, this dataset includes information on the

Appendix B. Trend Changes and Stability of Reading Literacy across Years
The stability of per-country reading literacy averages across years was very high. A detailed account is presented of how the trend changes in
reading literacy were estimated. Results per country are reported.
Stability of reading literacy across years
To assess the stability of the per-country reading literacy averages across years an intercept-only multilevel model was fitted to the data in
PISA2000_18Long39Countries.sav using SPSS software. In this dataset, each record represents the measurements per year. Consequently, most
countries comprise seven records.

significance is less relevant for this purpose.1 The intercept-only model is specified in the equation below:

Online chatting
In PISA 2009 and 2018, students were asked to indicate how often they engaged in a number of online reading activities. In addition to online
chatting, this included reading emails, reading online news, searching information online to learn about a particular topic, taking part in online group
discussions or forums, and searching for practical information online (e.g., schedules, events, tips, recipes). The students were asked to respond to the
following question:
How often are you involved in the following reading activities?
(Please select one response in each row. If you don’t know what the activity is, please select “I don’t know what it is.”)
A slight difference between the questionnaire items in both years relates to the examples mentioned (in the international version of the questionnaire). In 2018, WhatsApp and Messenger were suggested as examples of online chat services. In 2009, it was MSN. The variables in the PISA
datasets denoting the prevalence of online chatting are ST26Q02 for 2009 and ST17Q02IA for 2018.
In both years, the answer categories were exactly the same, namely:
1. I don’t know what it is
2. Never or almost never
3. Several times a month
4. Several times a week
5. Several times a day
Changes over time in online chatting are based on the percentages of students per year and country that selected the fifth category. In the 2000/
2002 questionnaires, online reading activities were not covered in much detail. Only one item addressed the frequency of reading emails and web
pages as a component of general reading activities (e.g., reading newspapers, magazines, etc.). It is assumed that the percentages of 15-year-olds
involved in online chatting several times a day in 2000 barely exceeded zero in each and every country.
The dataset PISA2000_18Wide39Countries.sav includes the following four variables with regard to (changes) in chatting prevalence for 15-yearolds:
1. Chatperc2009: percentage chatting several times a day in 2009
2. Chatperc2018: percentage chatting several times a day in 2018
3. ChatChange2009_18: change in percentage chatting several times a day during 2009–2018
4. TrendchangePrcChat: trend change in percentage chatting several times a day
5. Z TrendchangePrcChat: Z-score transformation of TrendchangePrcChat
Reading fiction
In 2000/2002, 2009, and 2018, students were asked to indicate how often they read certain materials. With regard to magazines, comic books,
non-fiction books and newspapers, students were asked to answer the following question:
How often do you read these materials because you want to?
The wording of the question was identical in 2000/2002, 2009, and 2018. In 2018, the following instruction for the respondents was added:
“Please take into account reading on paper and on digital devices.” The variables in the PISA datasets denoting the prevalence of reading fiction are
ST36Q03 for 2000/2002, ST25Q03 for 2009 and ST167Q03IA for 2018.
The response categories remained (virtually) constant:
1. Never or almost never (In 2000/2002: Never or hardly ever)
2. A few times a year
3. About once a month
4. Several times a month
5. Several times a week
The prevalence of reading fiction per country and year is measured as the percentage of students who reported reading fiction at least once a
month, that is, the percentage of respondents who selected answer categories 3, 4 or 5.
Changes are computed for the period up to 2009 and for the period 2009–2018. The trend changes are computed by subtracting changes in the
period up to 2009 from the 2009–2018 changes. If the first available measurement related to 2002, the difference between 2002 and 2009 was
increased by 9/7 in order to estimate change over 2000–2009.

Reading strategies
Information on student awareness of reading strategies was addressed in the PISA 2009; 2018 questionnaires. Students were asked to rate the
usefulness of six strategies for understanding and remembering a text. The responses served as input for constructing the UNDREM index. The usefulness of six strategies was rated on a scale from 1 to 6 (not useful at all – very useful).
Reading task: You have to understand and remember the information in a text.
How do you rate the usefulness of the following strategies for understanding and memorizing the text?
a. I concentrate on the parts of the text that are easy to understand.
b. I quickly read through the text twice.
c. After reading the text, I discuss its content with other people.
d. I underline important parts of the text.
e. I summarise the text in my own words.
f. I read the text aloud to another person.
The optimal ordering of the above set of strategies was established by a panel of reading experts (c-d-e-a-b-f). Scores were computed that indicate
how often each student rated a more useful strategy more favorably than a less useful one. These scores were standardized to a zero mean and a
standard deviation that equaled 1 in OECD countries in 2009. National averages computed for 2009 and 2018 served as the input for the per-country
change scores in awareness of reading strategies. Detailed information on per-country awareness of reading strategies can be found in the SPSS data
file PISA2000_18Wide39Countries.sav. This involves the following three variables:
1. UNDREM_mean2009: country average on the reading strategies awareness score in 2009
2. UNDREM_mean2018: country average on the reading strategies awareness score in 2018
3. UNDREM2009_18: change in reading strategies awareness during 2009–2018
4. ZUNDREM2009_18: Z-score transformation of UNDREM2009_18
Appendix D. Operationalization of Control Variables
Fifteen variables were considered as possibly confounding factors. Their operationalization is described in detail.
Overview of control variables
The variables that were considered as control variables to assess the robustness of the relationships reported in Fig. 4 are listed in Table D1,
grouped in four categories. The first category relates to general societal changes. This includes per-country trend changes in family background and in
percentages of students with internet access at home. The second category involves educational changes. The third category includes changes in
sample characteristics. Gender balance and average age of the students are included to rule out that changes in these variables could account for
correlations between the variables in the conceptual model. Discovering clear trends or trend changes in gender distributions and mean ages seems
unlikely. In contrast, this does not apply to the coverage of all 15-year-olds. In a number of Asian and Latin American countries the percentages of 15year-olds attending schools increased substantially over 2000–2018.
The final category involves other changes in reading behavior than reading fiction. It includes variables that address the amount of time students

Operationalization
Trend changes were computed per country. The input for these trend changes are averages and percentages per country and year. When computing
these aggregates, the student scores were weighted. The weights used in the calculation (W_FSTUWT) indicate how many individuals in the population
are represented by each sampled respondent. This ensures that computation of national aggregates yields unbiased population estimates. In many
cases, the sample designs deliberately overrepresent certain groups of students (e.g., by region). The weights add up to the population sizes of each
country (see Table B3).

Percentage immigrants
The PISA datasets from 2003 and later contain an index called IMMIG. This is based on questionnaire items that address the country of birth of the
students, their mothers and their fathers. The index can take on three values:
1. Native students (at least one parent was born in the country of assessment)
2. Second-generation students (the student was born in the country of assessment, but their parents were born in another country)
3. First-generation students (both student and their parents were born in another country)
The PISA 2000/2002 dataset does not include an IMMIG index. In that case, the variable was constructed using the original questionnaire items
(ST16Q01, ST16Q02, and ST16Q03).
For the analysis of country aggregates, the total percentage of both first- and second-generation students was used (categories 2 and 3). Per year,
this percentage was computed for every country (stored as ImmigPrc in PISA2000_18Long39Countries.sav). Next, trend changes in these percentages
were estimated (stored as TrendchangeImmigPrc and ZTrendchangeImmigPrc in PISA2000_18Wide39Countries.sav).
Parents’ education
The PISA questionnaires asked students to indicate the highest educational level completed by both their mothers and fathers. The responses were
classified according to the UNESCO International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED). In 2000/2002, national qualification levels were
recoded to six categories (coded 0–5):
1. No education (ISCED 0)
2. Primary education (ISCED 1)
3. Lower secondary (ISCED 2)
4. Upper secondary, work-oriented (ISCED 3B, 3C)
5. Upper secondary academic, post-secondary (ISCED 3A, 4)
6. Tertiary (ISCED 5A, 5B, 6)
In later years, ISCED 5B (vocational tertiary) was distinguished as a separate level between level 4 (ISCED 3A, 4) and level 6 (ISCED 5A, 6). In order
to maintain comparability with the 2000/2002 data, ISCED 5A, 5B, and 6 were combined into a single category in the later datasets.
The student responses yield two indices: one denoting the highest level completed by the student’s father (FISCED) and the other for the student’s
mother (MISCED). These were combined into a new index (HISCED) that represents the highest education level completed by either parent. In 2000/
2002, only FISCED and MISCED were included in the PISA dataset. These could easily be combined to construct a HISCED index. Per year, national
HISCED averages were computed (stored as Hisced in PISA2000_18Long39Countries.sav). Trend changes in HISCED served as a control variable
(stored as TrendchangeHisced and ZTrendchangeHisced in PISA2000_18Wide39Countries.sav).
Parents’ occupation
By means of open-ended questions, students provided information on their fathers’ and mothers’ occupations. The responses were mapped to the
International Socio-Economic Index (ISEI) of occupational status (Ganzeboom et al., 1992). This index ranges from 10 to 90. The scores for both
parents were used to construct a new index (HISEI) that denotes the highest occupational status of either parent. National averages per year (stored as
Hisei in PISA2000_18Long39Countries.sav) were used to estimate trend changes. Trend changes in HISEI served as a control variable (stored as
TrendchangeHisei and ZTrendchangeHisei in PISA2000_18Wide39Countries.sav).
Internet availability
In every year, students were asked to report the availability at home of about a dozen items. In this study, the availability of a link to the internet is
particularly relevant. The list changed in some respects over time. However, availability of a link to the internet was addressed every year. In 2000/
2002 the exact question was: “In your home, do you have:”. Students were asked to indicate yes or no for a list of items. In 2003 the wording of the
question was changed to: “Which of the following do you have in your home?”. The question remained the same in the following years, although the
item list was adapted over time. The exact variable names in the PISA datasets per year that record the student responses are listed in Table D2. The
percentages of positive responses per year (stored as InternetPrc in PISA2000_18Long39Countries.sav) were used to estimate trend changes (stored as
TrendchangeInternetPrc and ZTrendchangeInternetPrc in PISA2000_18Wide39Countries.sav).
Student-teacher ratio
The questionnaires administered to school principals yielded information on both the number of students enrolled and the number of teachers at
work in each school (part-time and full-time). Dividing the number of students by the number of teachers yields the student-teacher ratio (STRATIO).
Part-time teachers are counted as 0.5 and full-time teachers as 1.0. This information is stored in the STratio variable in the PISA2000_18Long39Countries.sav dataset. Information on trend changes is stored in the TrendchangeSTratio and ZTrendchangeSTratio variables in
PISA2000_18Wide39Countries.sav.

Although gender differences in test scores are frequently reported, it seems unlikely that changes in gender distribution can account for per-country
trend changes in reading literacy. Fluctuations over time in the national percentages of female students are most likely due to sampling error.
Student age
The age of every student participating in PISA was determined by subtracting the birth year and month from the testing year and month. Information regarding birth year and month was gathered from both the questionnaire and the student tracking forms. In the original PISA datasets from
2000 up to 2018, this information is stored in the variable AGE. The average student ages per country and year (stored as Age in PISA2000_18Long39Countries.sav) were used to estimate trend changes (stored as TrendchangeAge and ZTrendchangeAge in PISA2000_18Wide39Countries.sav).
Coverage of all 15-year-olds
PISA targets national populations of 15-year-olds. Students are sampled via a two-stage sampling procedure. Schools are the primary sampling
units. Consequently, 15-year-olds can only be selected if they attend school. The extent to which the PISA samples cover the entire national populations of 15-year-olds varies both across countries and years. It seems conceivable that such variations correlate with changes in reading literacy.
For the 2003–2018 period, data on population sizes are recorded in Annex A2 of the report on the main results from PISA 2018; OECD, 2019b).
Data on the population sizes represented by the samples and the total numbers of 15-year-olds are reported per country and year. Thus, coverage of
15-year-olds per year and country was computed. For coverage in PISA 2000/2002, data are reported in the PISA 2000 technical report (OECD, 2002,
pp. 135–136) and the PISA report on findings for the countries participating in 2002 (OECD/UNESCO-UIS, 2003, pp. 251–253). Information per
country and year is stored in the Coverage variable in the SPSS file PISA2000_18Long39Countries.sav. Trend changes regarding coverage are stored in
the variables TrendchangeCoverage and ZTrendchangeCoverage in PISA2000_18Wide39Countries.sav.
Reading behavior and reading attitudes
For the control variables regarding societal changes, educational changes and changes in sample characteristics, measurements are available for
every year that a country participated in PISA. This does not apply to the variables that relate to reading behavior and attitudes. In this case, measurements are only available for the years 2000/2002, 2009, and 2018. Still, this suffices for an assessment of trend changes. In this case, changes are
computed for the period up to 2009 and for the period 2009–2018. Trend changes are computed by subtracting the change in the period up to 2009
from the change over the period 2009–2018. If the first available measurement relates to 2002, the difference between 2002 and 2009 was increased
by 9/7 in order to estimate 9-year growth.
Reading time
In the PISA surveys that focused on reading literacy, students were asked to indicate how much time they spent reading for enjoyment. The
response categories remained identical over the years:
1. I do not read for enjoyment
2. 30 min or less each day
3. More than 30 min to less than 60 min each day
4. 1–2 h each day
5. More than 2 h each day
Country average scores were calculated per year. The following variables represent the student responses on reading time: ST34Q01 (2000/2002);
ST23Q01 (2009); ST175Q01IA (2018). In 2000/2002, reading time was addressed with the following questionnaire item:
Each day, about how much time do you usually spend reading for enjoyment?

Appendix E. Controlling for Confounding Variables
Including the control variables changes the path coefficients in Fig. 4 only to a limited extent.
Additional models were fitted in several stages to assess the correlation of potentially confounding variables with one of the focal variables in this
study. The conceptual model was extended with one control variable each time. All variables involved were transformed into z-scores before the
analyses were conducted using the lavaan package in R. This ensured equal variance for all variables and prevented the output from repeatedly issuing
variance heterogeneity warnings.
Trend changes in percentage of immigrant students was the first control variable for which effects on any of the focal variables was assessed. The
original model (see Fig. 4, main text) was extended with paths from this variable to each of the four focal variables. In this case, one of the four path

Figure E1 shows a graphic display of the final version. The elaborated model can hardly be considered an improvement over the model in the main
text (Fig. 4). The fit indices fail to show acceptable values. Given the limited number of 39 countries, including 10 variables in a path analysis would be
considered excessive. The six control variables were added via a purely data-driven approach. The only consideration for including certain paths was
that they yielded statistically significant coefficients. Several coefficients suggest relationships in the opposite direction of what one would intuitively
expect. This applies to the negative path coefficients from trend change in parents’ occupation to online chatting, from parents’ education to reading
literacy, and from grade level to awareness of reading strategies.
One may legitimately question the reliability of the elaborated model, given its complexity in combination with the limited size of the dataset.
However, the sole reason for constructing the elaborated model was to test the robustness of the main model presented in Fig. 4. In that respect, the
findings are reassuring. Even if every path suggesting a significant relationship with any of the focal variables is included, the coefficients are still
similar to the ones in the initial version (see Table 4 in the main text). The reliability of the elaborated model is of minor importance.
