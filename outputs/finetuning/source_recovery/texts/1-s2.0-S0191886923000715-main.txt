To motivate students, intrinsic interest is generally preferred over the instrumental values (useful for future
study/work) of the tasks. Such general principles could be complicated by the differential work-related values
and attitudes across cultures. Understanding and applying the appropriate motivational systems in congruence
with students’ cultural beliefs is important. Using self-determination theory, we examined the possible differential importance (and other complicated interactions) of motivational factors in representative student samples
from 19 countries and economies in two cohorts of large-scale international surveys (in total 188,481 students in
the Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study [TIMSS], 2015 and 2019). Results showed that
emphasizing task instrumental values helped academic performance for students with a low intrinsic interest in
collectivistic cultures. In contrast, stressing instrumental values did not help low-interest students in individualistic cultures. Notably, intrinsic motivation facilitates academic performance universally in all cultures.

1. Introduction

1.1. Motivation and human agency

Motivation affects human performance, emotion, cognition,
behavior, and well-being. However, its effects would vary with the
specific achievement and cultural context (Minkov et al., 2017; Ryan &
Deci, 2020; Vansteenkiste et al., 2020). While intrinsic interest is universally considered helpful and desirable, the use of instrumental value
is often more debatable. Notably, parents in the East emphasize filial
piety, being hard-working, and task utility (for future study and work),
among others (Markus, 2016; Minkov et al., 2017). Emphasizing task
utility (for future study and work) rather than intrinsic interest in
learning, in particular, can be seen as less desirable in some cultures. The
present study examined how these motivational (intrinsic and instrumental) factors affected academic achievement in a large-scale international survey of economies with diversified cultural values.
Specifically, the main and interactive effects of these motivational factors were compared across representative individualistic and collectivistic economies participating in two waves of the Trends in International
Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS; 2015 and 2019, Foy et al.,
2013; Mullis et al., 2020).

Motivational theories from the human agency perspective have been
proposed for over twenty years. One of the more prominent theories,
self-determination theory (SDT), has important implications for applications in psychology, education, and organizational management.
Emphasis is put on people’s inherent motivational propensities for
learning and growing and on how they can be supported and internalized by environmental facilitators (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci,
2017, 2020).
Served as an encompassing theory on motivation, SDT accommodates and connects a continuum of different related theories, such as
interest, goal, expectancy-value, self-worth, reinforcement, and social
cognitive theories (Jian & Liu, 2022; Sheldon & Prentice, 2019). Each of
these theories helps explain one or more types of motivation on the
continuum, from the intrinsic end to the extrinsic end. Intrinsic motivation, on the one end, is regarded as a kind of motivation focusing on
the task itself or individuals’ inherent interest and enjoyment (Deci &
Ryan, 2000; Ryan & Deci, 2020). Joy, interest, liking, exploration, and
curiosity-spawning activities are synonymous with intrinsic motivation.
On the other end, extrinsic motivation is linked with motives outside of

the task. It is sometimes called instrumental motivation (or value)
because it involves external components such as instrumental utility,
attainment, cost, pressure, and future goals (OECD, 2013; Ryan & Deci,
2020; Simons et al., 2000). It is characterized by an instrumental reason

important, even when the task is not enjoyable. This is more relevant to
achieving motivation, attainment (importance), and utility value, which
are related to the expectancy-value theory (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020;
Lauermann et al., 2017).

1.3. Cultural differences in the multiplicative model
The multiplicative model rests on the premise that instrumental
motivation works differently for low and high intrinsically-motivated
students. Thus, we may speculate that the multiplicative model will
work differently for cultures with varying emphasis on instrumental
value. Each culture has its specific value on various motivations
reflecting its cultural beliefs (Markus, 2016; Markus & Kitayama, 1991).
These beliefs are deeply rooted in the cultural system and will not be
easily changed. Logically classroom teaching and the related motivational system should be designed according to students’ epistemological
beliefs about what is “desirable” and “appropriate” in school settings.
The individualism-collectivism dimension is an important differentiation of the motivational beliefs of different cultures (Hofstede et al.,
2010; Minkov et al., 2017). In brief, an individual in an individualistic
culture “is expected to look after him- or herself and his or her immediate family,” whereas people in collectivistic cultures “are integrated
into strong, cohesive in-groups, which throughout people’s lifetime
continue to protect them in exchange for unquestioning loyalty” (Hofstede et al., 2010, p. 92). In the individualist West, the motivation for
“good” actions, such as persistent performance, is supposed to come
from inside the person. Such motivation is an expression of intrinsic
forces, which initiate the regulatory processes of self-expression, selfaffirmation, self-validation, and self-verification. In contrast, regulatory
forces that emanate from others or incentives such as grades or money
are commonly believed to be extrinsic to the self, undermining individual volition and reducing the agent’s performance.
In contrast, the situation is quite different in collectivistic cultures,
where individuals often imitate the “right” of others and fulfill rolerelated duties and obligations to maintain face, honor, and status. Individuals’ behaviors or actions can be explained as “I did that because I
should, because it is what people do, or because it was right or proper
expected of me” (Markus, 2016, p. 162). Doing what is expected or
required is not at odds with individual autonomy or choice but often
supports and co-occurs with it (Janoff-Bulman & Leggatt, 2002; Miller
et al., 2011). Thus, collectivistic individuals will more readily accept and
endorse extrinsic or instrumental values.
Studies have examined the relationship between motivation and
individualism/collectivism values. In the individualistic West, children
are more motivated when they have a choice and become less motivated
when authorities or peers choose for them. In contrast, children in a
collectivistic culture are less motivated when given a personal choice
but are highly motivated when having choices made for them by trusted
authority figures and peers (Baumeister et al., 2007; Hagger et al., 2014;
Iyengar & Lepper, 1999; Markus & Kitayama, 2010; Triandis & Suh,
2002). In addition, Chinese students feel less “controlled” than American students about teachers’ “controlling” behavior in the classroom,
and they do not reject external control (Cheng et al., 2016). Chinese
students do not emphasize internal motivation; instead, they will
gradually cultivate their interest once they understand the importance
of learning or are guided by their parents (Hau & Ho, 2010; Li, 2002; Li
& Fischer, 2004).

1.2. Is instrumental value helpful to all students?
While SDT and most other theories favorably evaluate intrinsic
motivation, we are less sure whether extrinsic motivation is beneficial.
Though extrinsic motivation can facilitate students’ academic performance, everyday life, and school activities in specific contexts of
learning, extrinsic reasons can possibly and easily “crowd out” (i.e.,
overjustify) intrinsic motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2020; Simons et al.,
2004). Historically, intrinsic and extrinsic motivations have been
considered antagonistic or additive. The antagonistic perspective assumes that intrinsic and extrinsic motivations are two ends of the
“motivation” dimension; intrinsic or extrinsic motivation is considered
educationally desirable. In contrast, the additive perspective considers
both intrinsic and extrinsic motivations help and work independently;
they add up to become the total “motivation” and their contributions
vary with the situational contexts.
In contrast to the above antagonistic and additive perspectives, a
multiplicative model has been proposed to examine whether extrinsic
motivation helps students under certain conditions (Cerasoli et al.,
2014; Sheldon & Prentice, 2019). One possible moderator is the level of
intrinsic interest. As suggested by SDT, “if an activity stimulates little or
no initial intrinsic motivation, one would not expect to find external
events undermining intrinsic motivation” (Deci et al., 1999, p. 635).
This indicates that extrinsic motivation will only be detrimental to individuals with well-developed individual interests. That is, likely only
for students initially with high intrinsic interest, the extrinsic value will
possibly push the intrinsic value aside (crowd out), diminish the positive
effects of intrinsic motivation, reduce the total motivational effects, and
lead to a net lower academic performance (Forest & Gagne, 2008).
However, for students with little interest initially, extrinsic value based
on an external drive may trigger the motivation to learn. This may be
referred to as the “situational interest” in the interest theory or the
cultivation of self-regulated learning in the social learning theory (Hidi,
2000; Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2020). Thus, extrinsic reasons can
compensate for students with low interest, but they can diminish the
motivation of students with a strong interest initially.
Initial empirical support for the multiplicative effect (Liu, Hau, &
Zheng, 2020) was obtained in analyses of the large-scale Program for
International Student Assessment (PISA). Results showed that instrumental motivation offered additional help on mathematics performance
for individuals with low enjoyment. In contrast, instrumental motivation would reduce or negatively affect academic performance for students already with high enjoyment. That is, extrinsic motivation is likely
detrimental to highly intrinsically motivated students but still facilitates
the academic performance of low-interest ones (Liu, Hau, Liu, et al.,
2020). In a similar line of research, Cerasoli et al. (2014) pointed out
that the effect of internal motivation on behavior was moderated by the
salience levels of the external stimuli. Thus, in job seeking, it was found
that rewards would facilitate individuals’ task performance on tasks
with little interest. However, rewards did not work for highly engaged
individuals (Kosfeld et al., 2017). Intervention research also showed that
emphasizing task utility and value was more beneficial for low-interest
participants (Ann & Hidi, 2019).

1.4. The present study
The present study was based on two related research questions from
the above literature review. First, extrinsic motivation, particularly
instrumental (e.g., helps future study and work, attainment importance,
and utility value), is often emphasized in educational settings. We
speculated that intrinsic and instrumental motivations might increase or
reduce the effects of each other to produce a “multiplicative effect”.
Second, we would further compare such multiplicative effects across
cultures with a possible differential emphasis on the two types of motivations. From a macrosystem level of analyses, this distinction would
be necessary because researchers and educators had to use motivational
strategies appropriate to the cultural emphases. Thus, we might use

2. Study 1: evidence from TIMSS 2015
2.1. Methods
2.1.1. Data and participants
In Study 1, data from TIMSS 2015, orchestrated by the Evaluation of
Educational Achievement (IEA), were used. The study monitored Grade
(G.) 4 and 8 students’ mathematics and science achievement trends in a
four-year cycle (Foy et al., 2013). Mathematics included algebra, data
and chance, number, and geometry subskills, while science included
biology, chemistry, earth science, and physics (Mullis et al., 2020).
As the full range of participating economies would have learning
conditions too different to be compared systematically, we focused on
the top-performing, developed, and large systems whose learning contexts were more comparable. As the main interest is to compare individualistic versus collectivistic economies, operationally, first, we
selected the representative top ten performing economies. Specifically,
the individualistic western economies were Australia, Canada, the
United Kingdom (UK), Ireland, and the United States (USA), while the
collectivistic eastern ones were Chinese Taipei, Hong Kong-China,
Singapore, Japan, and Korea (Huntington, 1996; Li, 2010). In total,
63,331 students from 1883 schools were included (37,422 individualistic and 25,909 collectivistic), with an average of 33.63 students from
each school. All students were from G.8, with the average age being
14.21 years (SD = 0.47) and 50.0 % of them were girls.
To test the robustness and generalizability of our results, we repeated
our analyses with economies above the median performance in both
science and mathematics domains. This involved 16 economies with
3107 schools, a total of 100,944 students (49.8 % being girls, with the
average age being 14.27 years, SD = 0.52; see Appendix 1 for details).
Since there was missing data due to the test design and administration,
the final sample included in the model involved 98,738 students taking
mathematics (3103 schools from 18 regions1) and 75,464 students
taking science (2285 schools from 13 regions).

2.1.3. Analyses
In the TIMSS sampling design, students were nested in different
schools, and collected in different economies. The appropriate multilevel model (MLM) was used in our analyses to control for the clustering
of students within schools (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). In the null model
analyses with the representative ten economies, school- and country
(economy)-level ICCs for mathematics and science achievements were
0.259 and 0.206 for mathematics, and 0.265 and 0.078 for science,
respectively. These results suggested a multilevel approach in analyses.
For the top 16 economies in the replication analyses, the ICCs were
0.294 and 0.155 for mathematics, and 0.288 and 0.072 for science. As
we were particularly interested in the individual motivational dispositions in each economy and that there were also small school/economies
differences (ICCs <0.070 for school- and <0.112 for country-level), the
within-level standardized scores of intrinsic and instrumental motivations and their multiplicative products were used in the following analyses (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). Two series of models were used in the
analyses.
Model 1: The Multiple Individualistic-Collectivistic Group
Model. The multiple group multilevel model (MG-MLM) was used based
on the representative ten economies, with individualistic-collectivistic
culture being the grouping variable. The motivational variables
(instrumental value, intrinsic motivation, and their multiplication
product) and the background variables (gender, SES) were individuallevel fixed effects. The three levels of residuals were modeled as
random effects. These effects were allowed to differ in different groups
(cultures). The model for mathematics achievement can be denoted as

2.2.2. Model 2: The Combined Individualism Interactive Model
In Model 2 mathematics, results showed that the motivational effect
changed for different cultures having different degrees of individualism;
for the interaction, beta = 0.30, p = .005, d = 0.01 (see also Fig. 2). The
motivational multiplicative effect was significant in collectivistic economies (sim.slp. = − 1.82, p < .001) and larger than that in individualistic
economies (diff = 0.85, p = .005). Specifically, for students from highly
individualistic cultures, intrinsic motivation and instrumental value
independently promoted mathematics scores. However, for students in
highly collectivistic economies, intrinsic motivation and instrumental
value interacted in that instrumental value helped achievement for
students with low intrinsic motivation (Fig. 2, solid lines with squares,
sim.slp. = 3.75, p < .001). This group of students also showed statistically stronger relationships between instrumental value and performance compared to individualistic students (dotted lines) either with
low (diff = 3.31, p < .001) or high interest in mathematics (diff = 2.70, p
< .001). It was also noted that intrinsic motivation strongly improved
mathematical performance in all economies (beta = 20.21, p < .001, d =
0.67).
Results for science were similar to those in mathematics, though the
effects were larger; beta = 0.63, p < .001, d = 0.02. Collectivistic students had stable multiplicative effects on motivation (sim.slp. = − 2.47, p
< .001). Specifically, for collectivistic students with low scientific interest, those with higher instrumental value would have higher science
achievement (solid lines with squares, sim.slp. = 7.66, p < .001); such an
effect was significantly larger than any other groups (yet the other
groups also had significantly small effects). The two cultures were
similar in that both intrinsic motivation and instrumental value helped
science achievement.

achievement, with the former being relatively stronger (Table 1, ranging
from 0.055 to 0.293). Intrinsic motivation and instrumental values were

lower magnitude, with motivation and achievement correlated from

4. Discussion and conclusion
4.1. Differential cultural effects of motivation
The present research explored the differential cultural effects of
motivations on achievement. Both TIMSS 2015 and 2019 results supported a consistent pattern that instrumental value helped enhance academic achievement for collectivistic students with low intrinsic
motivation. In contrast, low intrinsic motivation students in the individualistic western culture would not benefit from high instrumental
value. We addressed the above cultural differences using two mathematical models; the results were consistent and mutually supportive.
The above effects can be considered and interpreted within the
individualism-collectivism framework (Markus, 2016; Minkov et al.,
2017). Researchers have suggested that in the sociocultural learning
process, children are socialized by their caregivers and other agents (e.
g., teachers). It fulfilled children’s needs and preferences while transmitting the cultural beliefs and norms to construct children’s own
learning beliefs (Li, 2010; Li & Yamamoto, 2020). The process starts
even before early childhood schooling.
In the collectivistic culture, these social norms emphasize “social
utility,” “social value,” or “social importance,” and are embedded in
students’ value systems. To collectivistic children, instrumental values
are often seen as self-learning morals (Li, 2002). The “other regulation”
is indeed internalized as “self-regulation” rooted in the cultural epistemology. Thus, as observed in our results, such epistemology could
facilitate learning for students with low intrinsic motivation (interest) in
their learning tasks.

3.2. Results
Intrinsic motivation and instrumental value were moderately
correlated (0.541/0.549 and 0.619/0.630, respectively for mathematics
and science for 10 economies; see also Table 1). They were both positively related to their corresponding academic performance (0.090 to
0.296) while the former showed a larger correlation with its corresponding domain achievement.
3.2.1. Model 1: The Multiple Individualistic-Collectivistic Group Model
The results of TIMSS 2019 were similar to those of TIMSS 2015
(Tables 2 and 3). For mathematics, academic performance was related to
instrumental value negatively, but to intrinsic motivation positively. An
interactive effect was found with the collectivistic culture rather than
with the individualistic one. Specifically, similar to results in TIMSS
2015, those for TIMSS 2019 also showed instrumental value could help
mathematics performance for low intrinsic motivation students; interaction beta = − 5.76, p < .001, d = − 0.15, which showed a significant

2.66, p = .013. Similarly, instrumental motivation brought better science achievement for low intrinsic collectivistic students (sim.slp. =
11.86, p < .001), but not for individualistic ones.

As a major component of SDT, intrinsic motivation, triggered by basic
psychological needs, is the source of energy and basic needs for human
beings as agents in performing various tasks (Ryan & Deci, 2017). Even
for collectivistic students who may highly regard social values, the needs
for the psychological self are non-ignorable.
Compared with intrinsic interest, the impact of instrumental value is
somewhat controversial. Our results showed that it was positively
related to science achievement but not to mathematics (or larger in
science than that in mathematics). In the present study, science
achievement was mainly measured by students’ problem-solving ability
in everyday life. Perhaps instrumental value can help students evaluate
the various daily scientific phenomena and observations (Amabile,
1993; Amabile & Pillemer, 2012). This may help, therefore, the building
of the positive relations between instrumental value and the application
of everyday scientific knowledge.
Though instrumental value may be helpful for some students, it may
not help students who are already highly intrinsically motivated. Our
results showed that instrumental value might have potential drawbacks
for some students, particularly those with strong intrinsic interest.
Specifically, instrumental values may “crowd out” the benefits of interest and enjoyment, subsequently diminishing the net effects of motivations on academic achievement. Thus, our results support the
“crowding-out” explanation in line with SDT and alert us the danger of
over-justifying interesting tasks by imposing instrumental values. For
example, we should not emphasize the utility value of interesting
learning tasks (Cerasoli et al., 2014; Forest & Gagne, 2008).

4.4. Limitations and future directions
The present research suggests some conditions in which instrumental
value might be helpful. Likely, there are other contextual, cultural, and
dispositional (characteristics of participants) conditions such that
instrumental value may still be useful. Instrumental values and extrinsic
motivation may be undesirable to tasks with tangible outcomes, individual well-being, and other psychologically healthy students. However,
instrumental values (e.g., useful for future study and career) provide
easy enhancement to school study (Ryan & Deci, 2017) and may probably coexist with intrinsic motivation for creative-involving tasks
(Amabile, 1993; Amabile & Pillemer, 2012). Future studies in a wider
range of achievement outcomes and learning activities are definitely
useful.
There are intriguing non-linear and complicated interactive impacts
of instrumental values that have to be explored in future studies. For
example, for creative and problem-solving tasks that are inherently
interesting to start with, it would be interesting to test the effects of
additional instrumental utility. For tasks far beyond students’ ability to
learn, probably adding instrumental values will not help much. Even if
instrumental values are useful in some situations for a particular group
of people, researchers may still have to find out how we can effectively
internalize these values among our students.
5. Conclusion

4.3. Importance of controlling students’ background

In sum, our research showed: (a) in collectivist economies, students
with low intrinsic motivation would benefit from instrumental value; (b)
in individualistic economies, intrinsic motivation was consistently useful while instrumental value was not; (c) in highly collectivistic cultures,
the enhancement effects of instrumental value became more salient; (d)
intrinsic motivation was universally useful for all cultures. Frontline
practitioners and researchers are reminded of the importance of using
motivational strategies appropriate and congruent to the culture, students’ characteristics, task nature, and context of learning.

In our analyses, one of the main factors being statistically controlled
was students’ SES. The effect sizes of SES on achievement ranged from
0.33 to 0.47 (Table 2), which showed SES’s medium to large impact on
achievement scores in different economies and in both science and
mathematics. Despite SES equity being improving in many economies
(e.g., OECD, 2013, 2016), our results are in line with the literature
showing that SES’s effects are often substantial and non-ignorable. Social equity is still an important purpose of education to be improved and
closely monitored by educators and politicians. Additional resources for
the disadvantaged are always welcome and never too much.
Other than statistically controlling the effects of SES, our use of
appropriate analytical models in handling the clustered and multilevel
data is also important. The ICCs of academic achievement are large
enough, particularly for between-school differences, that the use of the
MLM analysis is necessary, as have been usually done in large-scale
international projects (e.g., Liu, Hau, & Zheng, 2020; Marsh et al.,
