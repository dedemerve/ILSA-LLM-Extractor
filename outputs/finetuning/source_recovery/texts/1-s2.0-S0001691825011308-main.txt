In the 21st century, dominated by artificial intelligence and technological innovation, scientific literacy has
become a core competency for global citizens. As a crucial environment for student development, the family not
only provides ascribed capital (e.g., economic capital, cultural capital) but also offers emotional support,
influencing students' self-directed learning efficacy, thereby playing a pivotal role in cultivating scientific literacy
that transcends traditional perceptions. Grounded in social cognitive theory, this study utilizes PISA 2022 data
from six East Asian regions (China's Hong Kong SAR, China's Macao SAR, China's Taiwan Province, Singapore,
South Korea, Japan), involving 34,968 students to investigate how family background influences scientific literacy. The results indicate: (1) Family background significantly and positively predicts students' scientific literacy. (2) Students' self-directed learning efficacy partially mediates the relationship between family background
and scientific literacy. (3) Family support moderates the first half of this mediating pathway. Based on these
findings, we recommend: prioritizing support for disadvantaged family backgrounds to promote equity in scientific literacy; enhancing self-directed learning efficacy to foster higher-order scientific thinking; and emphasizing family emotional support to cultivate a nurturing home environment.

1. Introduction
Scientific literacy, defined as the capacity to engage with sciencerelated issues and apply scientific ideas (OECD, 2019), has emerged as
a critical competency for addressing complex societal challenges in the
21st century. Despite concerted global efforts to promote equitable
science education, persistent disparities in scientific achievement
remain strongly correlated with socioeconomic status (Reiss et al.,
2020). Empirical evidence has consistently shown that students from
higher-income families exhibit superior scientific performance, due to
broader access to educational resources, enriched extracurricular opportunities, and enhanced parental academic involvement (Zhang & Liu,
2021). Notably, PISA 2022 data reveal that while disadvantaged students in OECD countries are seven times more likely to lack basic scientific proficiency, approximately 11 % of these students nevertheless
achieve performance levels within the top quartile of their national
distributions (OECD, 2023a, 2023b). This seemingly paradoxical finding
suggests that factors beyond traditional socioeconomic indicators may

significantly influence scientific literacy development, warranting systematic investigation.
To mitigate socioeconomic disparities in scientific literacy, educational systems have predominantly employed school-centric interventions emphasizing curriculum standardization, teacher
professional development, and resource redistribution (Bybee &
McCrae, 2021). Nevertheless, emerging evidence reveals the inherent
limitations of such institutional approaches in addressing fundamental
inequities rooted in family background, a multidimensional construct
encompassing economic capital, cultural capital, and social capital.
Empirical studies consistently demonstrate that students from disadvantaged backgrounds exhibit significantly lower scientific literacy
outcomes, partly due to constrained opportunities for informal science
learning and limited exposure to scientific discourse at home (Zhang &
Liu, 2021). While initiatives like Germany's MINT Action Plan 2.0
(German Federal Ministry of Education and Research, 2022) effectively
enhance school infrastructure, they frequently overlook the dynamic
interplay between familial environments and individual learning

beliefs (SDL efficacy), which in turn influence learning outcomes (scientific literacy). Family support is introduced as a contextual factor that

Grounded in SCT, this study incorporates family background and

factor, and scientific literacy as the behavioral outcome. To specify these
components and their relationships, we incorporate two supplementary
theories. First, Bourdieu and Passeron (1990) capital theory conceptualizes family background as a constellation of economic, cultural, and
social resources. These resources constitute foundational environmental
conditions that shape students' opportunities and verbal persuasions,
thereby influencing the development of SDL efficacy. Second, the social
support buffering model (Aneshensel & Stone, 1982) holds that supportive resources can mitigate the effects of adverse conditions. We posit
that family support serves as a proximal environmental resource that
moderates the effect of family background on SDL efficacy, buffering the
disadvantage of low capital or enhancing the advantage of high capital.
2.2. Family background and scientific literacy
Family background is defined as the various forms of economic, social, and cultural resources that a family acquires, such as family income, parents' occupations and parents' educational levels (Peverill
et al., 2021). A large number of studies have shown that family background can predict students' academic performance in different contexts
(Bae & Wickrama, 2015; Lee et al., 2023; Syariful et al., 2022; Wang
et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020). Li et al. (2020) revealed that family
background was significantly associated with children's Chinese and
mathematics performance. A meta-analysis (Liu et al., 2020) found that
family background had a stronger correlation with language achievement (i.e., Chinese and English) than science/math achievement and
general achievement. Focus on the science education, Morgan et al.
(2016) found that students from different family backgrounds have
already shown differences in scientific performance at an earlier stage of
their development, and this difference persists. Students who came from
better family backgrounds were more likely to study science subjects,
and more ready to major in science-related fields (Cooper & Berry, 2020;
Kurban & Cabrera, 2020). Demir et al. (2021) manifested that family
background can positively predict Canadian immigrant students' scientific performance. This leads to the first hypothesis.

RQ1. To what extent does family background positively predict students'
scientific literacy?
RQ2. Does SDL efficacy mediate the relationship between family background and scientific literacy?
RQ3. How does family support moderate the relationship between family
background and SDL efficacy?
By answering these questions, this study makes three key contributions. First, it uncovers the mediating role of SDL efficacy as a psychological mechanism through which socioeconomic status influences
science learning. Second, it identifies family support as a potential
moderator, revealing conditions under which disadvantaged students
can overcome structural limitations. Thirdly, examining the disparities
in students' scientific literacy from the perspective of differences in
family backgrounds offers a more integrative viewpoint for understanding educational inequity and provides empirical evidence for
developing targeted intervention measures in science education.

to effectively manage and perform functions toward certain goals

confidence in independently completing learning tasks, which is one of

3.1. Research design

H2a. Family background is significantly and positively related to
students' SDL efficacy.

This study employs a quantitative, observational, cross-sectional
design utilizing PISA 2022 data, an approach well-suited to the
research aims for several reasons. First, it allows for the simultaneous
analysis of direct effects, mediation via SDL efficacy, and moderation by
family support through latent moderated structural equation modeling.
Second, it leverages rigorously validated, large-scale data (N = 34,968)
capturing East Asian educational contexts. Third, addresses the ethical
and practical infeasibility of experimentally manipulating family background or support. Moreover, the PISA database, with its global scope,
has been widely utilized in numerous studies to investigate students'
scientific literacy (e.g., Alivernini & Manganelli, 2015; Tan et al., 2023).
Guided by a two-stage probabilistic sampling design for 15-year-olds in
regular/vocational education, PISA data collection adheres to standardized protocols, with subsequent preprocessing unified to ensure
consistency. Together, these features of this design provide a rigorous
framework for exploring how family background influences students'
scientific literacy in East Asia context.

H2b. SDL efficacy is significantly and positively related to students'
scientific literacy.
2.4. Moderating effect of family support
Many students from disadvantaged family backgrounds can still
demonstrate a high SDL efficacy. This strength in a risky environment
may be due to the moderating role of family support (Cao et al., 2021).
Family support refers to parental emotional acceptance, care, and understanding. Under the same family background conditions, family
support would intensify verbal persuasion and physiological arousal
among students, which are essential elements for modifying and
strengthening their SDL self-efficacy (Bandura, 2005). On the contrary,
due to the lack of emotional companionship, understanding and
encouragement from parents, students with low family support may lack
confidence in autonomous learning. Previous studies have noted that the
interaction between family background and social support can influence
self-efficacy. For instance, Chen et al. (2025) found that the interaction
between family cultural capital and social support can positively promote college students' academic self-efficacy. Liu et al. (2023) manifested that teacher support moderated the relationship between family
background and students' academic self-efficacy. For teenagers, parents
are the most important source of social support (Gariépy et al., 2016),
even in the context of disadvantaged background, a higher level of
family support can promote the generation of intrinsic motivation in
students during the learning process (Román et al., 2008), and help them
develop a higher level of SDL efficacy. Qian et al. (2024) proved that the
interaction between parental emotional support and family background
can promote the improvement of students' self-efficacy. This leads to our
third hypothesis.

3.3. Measures
3.3.1. Scientific literacy
According to PISA 2022, scientific literacy manifests through three
competencies: (1) Explaining phenomena scientifically: identifying, offering, and evaluating explanations for natural and technological phenomena; (2) Evaluating and designing scientific enquiry: identifying,
describing, and assessing the design, practice, and implementation of
scientific investigations; (3) Interpreting data and evidence scientifically: analyzing and evaluating diverse forms of scientific information,
arguments, and claims to draw appropriate conclusions. Using Item
Response Theory (IRT), PISA 2022 assigns each student 10 plausible
values based on their responses to test items. These values represent the
scientific performance distribution of all PISA participants, scaled to a
mean of 500 points with a standard deviation of 100 points. This study
employs the first plausible value for scientific literacy (PVSCIE1).

3.3.4. Family support
Family support was measured by the Family Support Scale (Item
ST300). Students evaluated ten behavioral items (e.g., “discussing your
school performance,” “spending time talking with you”) on a five-point
Likert scale, ranging from“1=Never or almost never” to “5=Everyday or
almost every day”. Higher values reflect stronger family support.
Following within-block matrix sampling methodology, this index was
similarly scaled to an OECD mean of 0 and SD of 1. In this study, the
Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.701, and the validation factor analysis
showed that the scale had good construct validity(χ 2/df = 35.546,
RMSEA = 0.033, CFI = 0.955, TLI = 0.943, SRMR = 0.023).
3.3.5. Control variables
Referring to related studies, this study included gender and grade as
control variables to more accurately examine the impact of family
background on students' scientific literacy.

3.3.2. Family background
PISA 2022 measures students' family background through the Index
of Economic, Social, and Cultural Status (ESCS). This composite variable
integrates three sub-indicators: (1) Highest parental occupational status
(HISEI), representing social capital, derived by coding parental occupations reported by students into International Socio-Economic Index
(ISEI) scores and selecting the highest value between both parents; (2)
Highest parental education (PARED), reflecting cultural capital, converted into years of education standardized across national systems with
the higher value of either parent retained; and (3) Home possessions
(HOMEPOS), indicating economic capital, calculated through Item
Response Theory (IRT) and Warm Likelihood Estimation (WLE) based
on household item ownership responses. The final ESCS index is
generated via weighted likelihood estimation, standardized to an OECD
average of 0 with a standard deviation of 1.

3.4. Data analysis
Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0. Firstly, a common
method bias test was conducted using SPSS 27.0 to ensure the absence of
significant common method bias issues in this study, which may affect
subsequent research. Secondly, descriptive statistics and correlation
analysis were performed in SPSS 27.0 to provide preliminary support for
hypothesis testing. Thirdly, Model 4 of the PROCESS macro in SPSS 27.0
was employed to examine the mediating role of SDL efficacy in the
relationship between family background and students' scientific literacy,
thereby evaluating hypotheses H1 and H2. Additionally, the significance
of the mediating effect was tested using the bias-corrected percentile
bootstrap method (5000 replications). A mediating effect was considered significant if the 95 % confidence interval (CI) did not include zero.
Lastly, Model 7 of the PROCESS macro in SPSS 27.0 was employed to
examine the moderating role of family support in the relationship between family background and students' SDL efficacy, evaluating hypothesis H3. The moderating effect of family support was considered
established when the interaction term involving family support and
family background was statistically significant.

Table 1 shows that family background was significantly and positively correlated with scientific literacy and SDL efficacy (r = 0.312; p <

Existing studies have not yet thoroughly explored the mechanisms
and boundary conditions through which family background influences
students' scientific literacy. This study empirically examined the relationship between family background and students' scientific literacy,
revealing the mediating role of SDL efficacy between them and the
moderating role of family support in the relationship between family
background and students' SDL efficacy. The results of this study require
further in-depth discussion.
First, the results showed a significant positive correlation between
family background and students' scientific literacy, indicating that
greater economic, social, and cultural capital possessed by families
correlates with better development of students' scientific literacy. This
supports prior findings on the positive influence of family background
on scientific literacy (Puzić et al., 2016) and validates cultural reproduction theory. That is, different social classes transmit uneven cultural
capital to their children, and the elite cultural evaluation mechanisms of
educational systems further reinforce this imbalance (Lamont & Lareau,
1988). Although the goal of science has shifted from cultivating elite
scientists to improving public literacy, science inherently entails systemic social exclusion (Yang & Jin, 2022), creating an invisible
“threshold” for public engagement in science. Archer et al. (2014)
concept of scientific capital reflects this reality: unequal access to
science-related resources and their unequal conversion within scientific
fields lead to disparities in students' scientific literacy. For example,
Economic capital can purchase and utilize science-related resources.
Social capital facilitates access to scientific opportunities through social
networks, deepening understanding of scientific knowledge and careers.
Cultural capital endows individuals with scientific dispositions,
enhancing scientific literacy through the acquisition of knowledge and
skills (Archer et al., 2014). Additionally, PISA 2025 incorporates a
“scientific capital” sub-dimension within its assessment framework to
support “scientific identity formation” (OECD, 2023a, 2023b), signaling
heightened attention to inequalities in scientific literacy. As science
education now targets all citizens rather than elites alone, students from
disadvantaged family backgrounds require focused support.
Second, the results indicated that SDL efficacy mediates the relationship between family background and scientific literacy, suggesting
that family capital can influence scientific literacy by shaping students'
SDL efficacy. This extends prior research across disciplines and cultural
contexts, which posits that family socioeconomic status affects academic
achievement through the mediating role of academic self-efficacy
(Caprara et al., 2008), by specifically identifying SDL efficacy as a
mediator. The impact of family background on SDL efficacy can be
explained by the Family Stress Model and Family Investment Model: The
Family Stress Model posits that family background affects family functioning and individual adaptation, putting disadvantaged students at
higher risk of developmental issues (Masarik & Conger, 2017), such as

6.1. Implications
This study offers new perspectives on the formation and development of students' scientific literacy, with significant theoretical and
practical implications.
Theoretically, this study makes a significant contribution by integrating SCT with Bourdieu's reproduction theory and the social support
buffering model, thereby advancing a more nuanced theoretical
framework for understanding educational equity. We achieve this by
operationalizing the “environment” in SCT's triadic reciprocal determinism through the lens of Bourdieu's capital theory, thereby concretizing “family background” as a constellation of economic, cultural, and
social capital. Furthermore, we specify the moderating mechanism
within the environment-person relationship by invoking the social
support buffering model, positing that family support serves as a proximal environmental resource that can buffer the constraining effects of
disadvantaged capital. This integrated model not only decomposes and
elaborates the pathways of triadic reciprocal determinism but also expands the boundaries of SCT by introducing capital-based stratification
and psychosocial buffering into the study of scientific literacy.

6.2. Limitations and future research directions
Despite its contributions, this study is subject to several limitations
that also point to actionable avenues for future research. First, while the
use of PISA 2022 data provides a robust and internationally comparable
dataset, its cross-sectional design inherently constrains causal inference.
Future research should employ longitudinal or experimental designs to
robustly establish causality. For instance, tracking students' development over time could elucidate how changes in family support dynamically influence the development of SDL efficacy.
Second, the measures in this study relied solely on students' selfreported data. Future research could incorporate parental perspectives. By collecting and matching data from both parents and students,
researchers can not only achieve data triangulation and enhance the
overall validity of the findings but also compare the discrepancies between parent and child reports on the same constructs (e.g., family
support). Analyzing such perceptual gaps can reveal potential potential
ideological divergences in family interactions, thereby deepening
research implications.
Third, and most critically, our substantial sample size (N = 34,968)
across six East Asian regions is a strength in terms of statistical power,
but it also presents a specific limitation regarding generalization. The
findings are robust within the shared cultural context of highperforming East Asian education systems. Future work should test this
moderated mediation model in contrasting cultural settings (e.g., individualistic Western systems vs. collectivistic Eastern systems) to determine which pathways are universal and which are culture-specific. This
would significantly advance the theoretical generalizability of the social
cognitive framework applied to educational equity.
