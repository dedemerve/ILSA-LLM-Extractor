Evaluating uncertainty: the impact 
of the sampling and assessment design 
on statistical inference in the context of ILSA
Diego Cortes1*   , Dirk Hastedt1 and Sabine Meinck1 
Abstract 
This paper informs users of data collected in international large-scale assessments 
(ILSA), by presenting argumentsunderlining the importance of considering two design 
features employed in these studies. We examine a commonmisconception stat-
ing that the uncertainty arising from the assessment design is negligible compared 
with that arisingfrom the sampling design. This misconception can lead to the erro-
neous conclusion that there is always a relatively lowrisk of ignoring the uncertainty 
arising from the assessment design when reporting estimates of population param-
eters. We use the design effect framework to assess the impact that the sampling 
and the assessment design have on theestimation. We first evaluate the loss in effi-
ciency in the estimation of a population parameter attributable to each ofthe designs. 
We then examine whether knowledge about the effect of one design feature can 
justify any belief about theeffect of the other design feature. We repeat this examina-
tion across different parameters characterizing theachievement distribution in a popu-
lation. We provide empirical results using data collected for PIRLS 2016. Our empiri-
cal results can be summarized in two general findings. First, when estimating mean 
achievement, the effectof the sampling design is often substantially larger than that of 
the assessment design. This finding might explain themisconception we try to address. 
However, we show that this is not true in all instances, and the magnitude of thediffer-
ence between both design effects is context dependent and hence not generalizable. 
Second, differences in designeffects become less predictable when estimating other 
parameters, e.g. the proportion of students reaching a certainthreshold in the achieve-
ment scale (i.e., benchmarks), or an association estimated using linear regression. This 
contribution underlines that accounting for all sources of uncertainty in the estimation 
is of paramount importanceto obtain credible inferences. We conclude that it is dif-
ficult to justify a priori the belief that the effect of the samplingdesign in the estimation 
is always greater than that of the assessment design.

Introduction
Data derived from international large-scale assessments in the field of education (ILSA) 
provide a broad spectrum of opportunities to learn about educational outcomes within 
and across populations. For example, data derived from these studies describe the 

Page 2 of 21
Cortes et al. Large-scale Assessments in Education  (2025) 13:10
probability distribution of student achievement in a given population at some point 
in time. Parameters characterizing this distribution are then reported as key results 
of these studies. The mean of this distribution and the probability of a student reach-
ing some threshold in this distribution are examples of such parameters. Further, an 
important attribute of this data is its comparability over time and across populations. 
Populations that participate in consecutive data collections can examine how these key 
parameters change over time. Moreover, the internationally standardization of survey 
procedures and instruments allows to compare parameters across populations. Finally, 
a central outcome of the collective efforts of ILSA are the publicly available databases, 
which researchers can use to perform more in-depth analyses. For example, they can 
examine the relationship between student achievement and school- or student-level 
characteristics. The empirical research in the field of internationally comparative educa-
tion uses a rich variety of quantitative methods for descriptive and predictive analysis. 
The empirical evidence derived from these studies is increasingly used to inform educa-
tional policies (see, for example, Baek et al., 2018).
The data collection process in ILSA are built upon two designs, both of which are 
based on the principles of randomization. First, members of the study population are 
included into the survey following a complex sampling design, typically in the form of a 
two-stage stratified random sample (see Meinck & Vandenplas, 2021 for an overview). 
This random mechanism generating data allows analysts to make inferences about 
the entire study population from its sampled members. Second, the set of students 
participating in ILSAs is assessed through a matrix sampling of items, in which test 
booklets are randomly rotated across students (von Davier et al., 2020). This is because 
the framework used to assess a subject domain is typically extensive; therefore, the 
pool of items needed to measure it is typically large. Hence, to prevent overburdening, 
students participating in these assessments are exposed to only a fraction, or a sample, 
of the overall item pool. For example, the assessment design created for the Progress 
in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS) in 2016 contained about eight hours 
of testing for grade four students, however the actual testing time for each student was 
limited to 80 min (Mullis & Martin, 2015). This random mechanism generating student 
item responses allows analysts to make inferences about the distribution of proficiency 
in a subject domain within a group of students.
In this paper, we focus attention solely on the statistical inference problem that arises 
from the uncertainty in the estimation generated by these two designs, which we refer to 
as design-based uncertainty. We therefore abstract from inferential problems arising from 
model-specification, non-response, or any other problem in which uncertainty is gener-
ated outside the design of the study and therefore cannot be quantified with any degree of 
precision. Randomization in each design component allows us to quantify the amount of 
uncertainty attributable to each design. The sampling variance and the measurement var-
iance are ancillary measures of estimated parameters that reflect the magnitude of such 
uncertainties. The standard error of any estimated population parameter is derived as the 
sum of both measures. The larger is the standard error, the larger is the uncertainty in the 
estimation. Thus, variances are informative about the sample distribution of an estimated 
parameter. However, the variance of an estimated parameter alone is not useful to assess 
the efficiency of a complex design, as it is an absolute measure of uncertainty.

In this paper we use the design effect framework pioneered by Kish (1965), to assess 
the impact that the complex sampling or assessment design has in the estimation. We 
elaborate two different arguments. First, we evaluate the loss of efficiency in the estima-
tion of population parameters attributable to each of the designs. That is, we estimate 
sampling and assessment design effects in different populations and across different 
parameters. Our empirical results show that, in the context of PIRLS 2016, the effect of 
the complex sampling design tends to be larger than the one induced by the assessment 
design. This is particularly true when the parameter of interest is the mean of the pop-
ulation-wide achievement distribution. However, when we estimate other parameters, 
such as the probability of a student reaching some international benchmark or an ordi-
nary least squares (OLS) coefficient, there is a clear attenuation of such tendency. This 
first set of results calls for caution when generalizing design effects in different popula-
tion and across different parameters.
The second argument relates to the importance of accounting for the uncertainty 
arising from the assessment design. We want to address the misconception that we 
sometimes encounter in research papers and conference contributions, which suggests 
that because the measurement variance tends to be small, it is ‘safe’ to ignore the 
uncertainty arising from the assessment design when making statistical inferences about 
population parameters. For example, according to the editors of the journal ‘Large-scale 
Assessments in Education’—a highly specialized journal in the field of ILSA—seven out 
76 of the papers submitted to the journal in 2021 were desk-rejected because they failed 
to account for the uncertainty arising from the assessment design in the estimation 
(Hastedt, personal communication, February 9, 2023). In this paper, we show that the 
impact of ignoring either source of uncertainty is hard to predict, as it depends on 
many factors. Hence, our results emphasize the relevance of accounting for both—the 
assessment and sampling designs—during the estimation.
To examine these two arguments, we use the context of PIRLS in its data collection of 
2016 (Mullis & Martin, 2015; TIMSS PIRLS International Study Center, 2017). PIRLS is 
a study conducted by the International Association for ethe Evaluation of Educational 
Achievement (IEA), with a 5-year cycle. A central objective of this study is to generate 
knowledge about the distribution of student achievement in a reading assessment among 
grade four students. We use the construct of ‘reading achievement’—as defined and 
reported by the study—as our main outcome of interest. As many other ILSA, PIRLS also 
collects contextual information regarding the learning environment of students. That is, 
school principals, teachers, and parents of students participating in the study respond to 
background questionnaires. We also refer to, and use some of, this contextual information 
along this paper. Finally, we emphasize that we use data derived from PIRLS to contex-
tualize the two arguments we want to discuss. However, the discussion we bring in this 
paper could also be contextualized in other well-established ILSA (for example, the Trends 
in International Mathematics and Science Study TIMSS, and the Programme for Interna-
tional Student Assessment PISA). This is because most well-established ILSA use a sam-
pling and an assessment design to inform their data collection, both of which are based on 
the principles of randomization and therefore each design generates a measurable degree 
of uncertainty. That is, only the estimation that incorporates both sources of uncertainty 
can lead to correct statistical inferences when using data derived from these studies.

the assessment design in the estimation. We also derive measures to assess the impact 
that ignoring either design has on the process of making statistical inference. In Sec-

PIRLS 2016. Finally, in Section “Conclusion” we conclude.

A fundamental objective of most ILSA is to estimate population features relevant for 
characterizing education systems. For example, PIRLS attempts to generate knowledge 
about the probability distribution of achievement in the reading assessment among

To learn about θ , PIRLS informs its data collection process with a sampling and an 
assessment design, both of which are based on the principles of randomization. The 
sampling design governs who in the population is included into the study, while the 
assessment design governs which test items are administered to each participating 
student. Once information has been gathered, processed, and quantified, a conclusion 
about θ is justified with statistical inference. We denote this conclusion θ  , which is the 
estimate of θ . Since central to our paper is the appraisal of the uncertainty induced by the 
complex sampling design and assessment design on θ  , it is appropriate to first describe 
these sources of uncertainty. We then discuss how these are typically quantified.
Uncertainty induced by the complex sampling design
We begin by outlining and discussing three aspects of the sampling design typically 
implemented in PIRLS, which are known to have an impact on the uncertainty arising 
in the estimation of population parameters. In the interest of space, and of focusing 
on the theme of uncertainty, we do not summarize design features of PIRLS but refer 
the reader for information about this to the Methods and Procedures in PIRLS 2016 
(Martin et al., 2017). Chapter 3 describes how schools and students are included into the 
survey, as well as how estimation weights are computed (LaRoche et al., 2017). Chapter 4 
introduces the reader to the Jackknife Repeated Replications (JRR) technique, which is 
used to approximate the sampling distribution of an estimated population parameter 
(Foy & LaRoche, 2017).

The third aspect is the effect of stratification. In PIRLS, sampling stratification is usu-
ally performed either to control for sample sizes across strata or to enhance efficiency 
in the estimation of population parameters. Here we focus on the latter, as sample sizes 
are not considered an attribute of a complex sampling design, but rather an attribute 
of any probabilistic sampling plan, either complex or unrestricted (i.e., see Section “A 
design effect standpoint”). Efficiency in the estimation is achieved through stratification 
to the extent that the observable characteristics used to group sampling units can pre-
dict survey outcomes. This is because a consequence of stratification is that sampling 
units sharing similar characteristics are placed together in variance strata when using 
the JRR methodology for variance estimation. The more homogeneous are sampling 
units in terms of survey outcomes within variance strata, the less variable are the pseudo 
estimates arising from JRR methodology. Everything else constant, a stratified sampling 
design is at least as efficient as an unstratified sampling design.
At this point, we find it relevant to emphasize that each ILSA is unique in their 
target population, leading to necessary distinctions in their sampling design. However, 
in principle, they all follow a stratified multi-stage random process to select students 
into the assessment. Therefore, inferences derived from these studies are subject to the 
sources of uncertainty outlined above. For example, TIMSS and PIRLS have a long-
standing standard of selecting intact classes to participate in the study as a second 
sampling stage, while PISA selects a subset of 15-year-olds directly. This distinction 
is a result of the target population definition, yet both designs aim at a self-weighting 
sampling process, are subject to clustering effects, and use stratification to enhance 
efficiency or control sample sizes.

A design effect standpoint
The sampling and assessment design effect
Leslie Kish once voiced: `… it took me about 40 years to wake up to the fact that design 
effect is a steadying factor, very important, compared to variances and to coefficients of 
variation’ (Kish, 2000). The term design effect was coined by him and defined as the ratio 
of the variance of an estimate under a complex sampling plan and the variance of an esti-
mate under a simple random sample without replacement (Kish, 1965) or with replacement

Conclusion
To conclude, the main purpose in this paper is to highlight the importance of accounting 
for all sources of design-based uncertainty in the estimation in order to properly 
justify the conclusions drawn about a population parameter. To do so, we have carried 
out our analysis by presenting some important theoretical results that draw on the 
design effect framework and provided some practical implications when constructing 
confidence intervals as measures that reflect uncertainty. We, however, did not examine 
the implications that omitting one or the other source of uncertainty would have for 

Page 19 of 21
Cortes et al. Large-scale Assessments in Education  (2025) 13:10
	
hypothesis testing. For example, one could present statistical inference in which ignoring 
the assessment design would lead to falsely rejecting the null hypothesis. We believe 
such an exercise would be redundant, and to some extent misleading, for the purpose 
of this article. This is because, as any classical theory of knowledge would emphasize, 
a conclusion can only be known to be (probably) true if it is justified. Therefore, any 
conclusion reached by means of statistical analysis in the context described in this paper 
must take account of both sources of uncertainty in order to be justified; otherwise, the 
conclusion cannot be considered knowledge, even if the conclusion happens to be true.
To summarize, in this paper, we use the design effect framework initiated by Kish 
(1965) to assess the impact that the complex sampling design and the assessment design 
have in the estimation of population parameters in ILSA. We first discuss measures that 
quantify the loss in efficiency in the estimation induced by these two designs. We then 
highlight the importance of accounting for the uncertainty arising from both designs 
when doing statistical inference. We make particular emphasis on the assessment design, 
as it is frequently ignored, potentially leading to incorrect inference about population 
parameters.
As noted by Sarndal (1992), design effect measures incorporate not only the design-
based uncertainty but also the efficiency of the estimator. In the context of this paper, 
this becomes clear when we compare the effects of the sampling and assessment design 
on different parameter. When looking at mean achievement estimates, across most of 
the study populations, the sampling design drives the uncertainty in the estimation. The 
relative weight of the sampling design effect reduces substantially once we look at per-
formance at specific benchmarks, for example the probability of a student scoring below 
475 points, and when we compare different students of different gender.
This paper discusses—and provides evidence about—sampling and assessment 
design effects in the context of PIRLS. However, the results provided here could easily 
be incorporated in other ILSA studies. We find it important to underline that even 
when considering all information about the sampling and assessment design for a 
given ILSA and country, it is hard to disentangle the impact of all design features. This 
is because they all interact with another. For example, studies without class sampling 
(e.g., PISA, ICILS 2013 and 2018) may have smaller sampling design effects. Yet, a 
priori, it is hard to predict what is the relative contribution of the sampling design to 
the overall survey design.
Overall, this contribution underlines the importance of accounting for both the 
assessment and sampling design whenever estimating population parameters with 
ILSA data. That is, using correct methods for estimating sampling variance, using all 
available plausible values for estimating measurement variance, and then combining 
both variance components to achieve good approximation of the uncertainty of an 
estimated parameter. Neglecting any of these features will ultimately lead to incor-
rect standard errors and confidence intervals, and therefore wrong inferences. This 
might lead to ungrounded recommendations in education policy, something that, we 
believe, must be avoided by serious scholars and analysts.
Finally, all ILSA studies come with extensive technical documentation that can 
be used to determine correct analysis procedures. Further, handbooks and software 
manuals describe the proper handling of ILSA features for specific analyses. Decades 

Page 20 of 21
Cortes et al. Large-scale Assessments in Education  (2025) 13:10
ago, the use of so-called shortcut-methods (for example, using just one plausible value 
or ignoring the complex sampling design) was defended with the considerably longer 
runtimes of the correct analysis methods. Given the power of computers nowadays 
and the availability of information, we find it hard to justify the incorrect handling of 
data derived from ILSA.
