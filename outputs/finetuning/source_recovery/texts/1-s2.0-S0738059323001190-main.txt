The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), the world’s most extensive assessment of student
scholastic abilities, included in its 2015 cycle a human-agent measure of collaborative problem-solving skills
(CPS). To better understand the development of collaboration proficiency in youth, we assess the effects on CPS
of several individual, mezzo, and national level variables, focusing on intra-school and inter-school wealth
inequality. The results of the three-level hierarchical linear models on 46 countries show that both types of
inequality have negative effects on CPS individual scores. In the case of intra-school inequality, the negative
effect holds after controlling for individual differences in science performance. Generalized trust and political
equality, measured at the national level, counteract these effects independently, reaffirming the importance of
education to democratic citizenship and institutions.

1. Introduction
The Covid-19 pandemic led to massive educational disruptions
worldwide. Besides significant academic losses, prosocial skill development has also been negatively impacted, disproportionately affecting
students with lower socioeconomic status (SES) (Hack and Zhang,
2021). One of these skills is collaborative problem solving (CPS), which
refers to a process whereby two or more individuals come to a shared
understanding of a problem, and then pool knowledge, skills, and effort
in pursuit of a resolution (OECD, 2017a). The ability to solve common
problems through collaboration is an essential skill for the 21st century
(Graesser et al., 2018). Since 2000, the Programme for International
Student Assessment (PISA) has measured the ability of 15-year-olds to
apply reading, mathematics, and science proficiency. In 2015, PISA
launched its sixth iteration of the instrument and included CPS as a
fourth competency area. Using digital technology, CPS test-takers
interact with an algorithm to solve problems. This human-agent process draws students to decision-making points that indicate a willingness
and ability to collaborate. The technology provides an innovative way to
evaluate characteristics associated with CPS performance. We ask how
the social context, specifically SES inequality, impacts CPS as an element
of academic achievement and social acumen.

CPS proficiency requires cognitive ability to diagnose problems and
process solutions. However, it also depends on interpersonal skills
including effective communication, confidence, and diplomacy
(Andrews-Todd and Forsyth, 2020). We posit that the social context of
learning will influence the development of this fundamental skill and be
reflected through performance on the CPS exam. Where inequalities
prevail, the interpersonal skills needed for CPS may be in short supply
because of diverging values among individuals from different social
stations (Penn, 2017).
Children begin to collaborate at a formative age and benefit from
instruction in such behavior. Schools have long been recognized as an
appropriate setting for such learning to take place. “It would be
impossible to find a deeper sense of the function of education in
discovering and developing personal capacities, and training them so
that they would connect with the activities of others” (Dewey, 1916, p.
89). Collaboration inspires “a philosophy of interaction and personal
lifestyle” that encourages mutual respect, shared responsibility, and
consensus-building, making it an important student learning goal (Laal
et al., 2012, p. 1698). CPS expands educational experiences from individual to group achievement. As such, the social context where learning
takes place may further our understanding of CPS performance.

Thus, collaboration involves participants working in a relationship with
“a large degree of mutual engagement, joint decision making, and discussion” (Rochelle and Teasley, 1995, p. 70). Collaboration rests on
reciprocity through joint efforts that move toward a common goal
(Bedwell et al., 2012, p. 134). With continued interaction, group
decision-making and behavior take on synergetic qualities so that the
outcome rises to something greater than the sum of the individual
contributions, highlighting the importance of process over result (Longoria, 2005, p. 126).
Collaborators share common values and “goals that are mutually
agreed upon based on an established value base to which all stakeholders have a commitment” (Graham and Barter, 1999, p. 7). The
collective outcome cannot be realized without the contribution of each
party. Although each participant may envision and value the outcome
differently, all appreciate the collective effort, in part, because they
possess a broad, but shared, conception of the problem and are
committed to finding a resolution (Rochelle and Teasley, 1995).
Collaboration is not immune to conflict. Discord might be directly
related to the task, which might extend to diagnosing the nature of the
problem. Actors may also disagree on aspects of the collaborative process, including the division of labor, mutual expectations, and the
importance of different contributions. Clashes of personalities and
competing perspectives can also generate friction. Collaborators, however, use their social skills to harmonize efforts toward the desired
outcome (Jehn, 1997; Care et al., 2016).
Conceptually distinguishing between collaboration and cooperation
is instructive. Cooperation describes “when people act pro-socially in
situations in which there is not only a personal disincentive to be groupregarding but also an incentive to take advantage of the helpful actions
of others” (Parks, 2015, p. 525). Thus, cooperation predicts freeriding
inclinations, so that an individual enjoys an outcome without incurring
the costs (or risks) of contributing. Singing in a choir represents an
example of cooperation, where a member might not have memorized all
lyrics and allows the group to carry the song. Compare that to the
collaborative process in which the executive committee of that same
choir selects the songs to be part of the program. Expanding the scale, we
see collaboration among public health officials tasked with addressing
the outbreak of the coronavirus, including developing an effective
vaccination. People eligible to be vaccinated represent those with an
opportunity to cooperate or freeride.
Self-interest and social pressure are important, but the impulse to
cooperate is based on experience, which extends to evolved tendencies,
learned associations, and an ability to react to social cues (Parks, 2021).
In this way, cooperation relies on a similar array of interpersonal skills
that support CPS. Yet, collaboration is a more intentional process, which
begins with the realization of a shared goal to resolve a problem. Despite
the presence of this shared goal, at the onset, participants do not
necessarily agree upon an outcome. The dynamism of a collaborative
process brings indeterminacy in decision-making resulting in innovative
outcomes (Heath, 2007, p. 147). When joint efforts disperse costs, and
benefits transcend individual enjoyment, collaboration and cooperation
become overlapping without losing meaning. Collaboration brings
stakeholders into a collective process “designed to produce solutions
that none of them working independently could achieve” (Gray, 1989, p.
11). Since collaboration can create new solutions that no individual
envisioned or could enact, the unique outcome comports with satisfying
individual self-interest, which is not predetermined.
The close relationship between collaboration and cooperation is not
merely academic. Trust relationships are the foundation for social
cooperation, and by extension CPS (Warren, 2018). People are willing to
work together when others show a willingness to invest their trust. As
social beings, we care what other people think about us and often find
satisfaction through their affirmation (Zak, 2007). The building of such
relationships further adds to the quality of democratic governance,

Modeling the relationship between SES and academic achievement
faces serious endogeneity problems, however. SES inequality interacts
with other social characteristics, such as race, which also corresponds to
student learning outcomes (Caldas and Bankston, 1997). SES inequality
may also reflect disproportionate stocks of social capital projecting
negatively into the learning environment (Coleman, l990, p. 69–70). An
important element of social capital is generalized trust, the extent to
which people have faith in fellow citizens, which, among other things,
facilitates collaborative behavior (Alesina and La Ferrara, 2002; Uslaner
and Brown, 2005). Generalized trust, by extension, is associated with
successful democratic governance and economic development (Putnam,
2000; Uslaner, 2002; Warren, 2018; Zak and Knack, 2001).
We contribute to the literature that addresses the effects of SES
inequality within secondary schools on CPS performance, a prosocial
skill. Models that treat inequality purely as a national phenomenon,
using indicators such as the Gini coefficient, measure only one remote
manifestation of income inequality (Park et al., 2021). However, youths
are more likely to perceive SES inequality that prevails within their
school, and perhaps inequality that exists among schools within their
educational system. Including intra- and inter-school inequality within
our model adds to an understanding of the relationship between
collaborative behavior and exposure to inequality at the school and
school system levels (Akram et al., 2021; Bourguignon, 2015).
CPS includes cognitive and social dimensions. Included within the
CPS composite are skills related to sharing ideas and accommodating
one’s own knowledge base to new perspectives (Andrews-Todd and
Forsyth, 2020). While these skills rely in part on social abilities, a
cognitive component is present as well. CPS performance correlates with
other measures of collaboration including student self-ratings, teacher
ratings, and co-player ratings, even when controlling for cognitive assessments of reading (Stadler et al., 2020). A validation study showed
that human-human interactions produced similar outcomes to the
human-agent design (Herborn et al., 2020), but questions remain
regarding the ecological validity of human-agent instruments
(Pásztor-Kovács et al., 2021). Thus, CPS captures a social dimension that
reflects one’s willingness to engage with others, the ability to work with
alternative viewpoints, and the patience to navigate around a host of
social differences to resolve a problem (Care et al., 2016).
We begin with a discussion of collaboration, establishing a theoretical link between CPS and other positive social outcomes. We then review the relationship between socioeconomic inequality and academic
achievement with an eye toward the development of CPS skills. We next
develop a model to evaluate the assertion that inequality negatively
impacts CPS performance. The model considers several levels of exposure to SES inequality including intra-school disparities, inter-school
SES differences, and national-level indicators of inequality among
households. The model also considers national levels of generalized trust
and political equality, as well as several control variables, including
home education resources. We conclude with a discussion of the limitations of our study and implications of the findings.

engagement among participants (Lewis, 2006). The process “creates a

Intra-school SES dynamics influence student learning outcomes as
well (Marks, 2006). As average SES levels increase within a school, academic achievement levels increase for most, if not all, students
including those from lower SES households (Caldas and Bankston,
1997). However, the SES distribution within the school may be equally
important. A smaller standard deviation of intra-school family income,
regardless of the average household income for a school, is positively
associated with academic achievement (Marks et al., 2007). However,
we do not know if this outcome extends to CPS.
Exposure to SES inequality in the school setting, regardless of the
average SES level, tends to decrease the inclination for student cooperation (Ten Dam and Volman, 2003). Further, the opportunity structure
and student motivation influence the effect (Levi et al., 2004). Students
who are motivated by academic achievement tend to be more willing to
engage in collaborative behavior to advance their academic goals.
However, if social performance is the chief motivator for a student, they
will likely shy away from collaboration with colleagues who they
perceive to be from a different social station, albeit for different reasons
depending on their own social status (Levi et al., 2004).
In sum, evidence suggests that SES inequality, on individual, mezzo,
and macro levels, negatively affects student learning outcomes, many of
which relate to academic achievement. Since CPS has a cognitive
component, we should expect SES to contribute to explaining CPS performance. However, CPS also relies on social skills. Traditional disciplines, like science, also have a social element, which advances
academic achievement, but CPS is more highly dependent on it. Indeed,
exposure to inequality may have a greater effect on CPS performance
since it requires effective communication, group decision-making, and
teamwork to complete tasks. Due to SES differences, students may
possess distinct social orientations, leading to differences in framing
problems, and consequently, divergent approaches to finding solutions.
Although national levels of inequality likely play a role, such measures capture only one aspect of inequality (Park et al., 2021). We focus
on SES equality on the intra-school and inter-school levels for CPS
performance while controlling for national-level wealth inequality. We
also control for student performance on the PISA science exam to gain
perspective on how academic achievement in science converges with
CPS skills.

3. Inequality as a predictor of collaborative problem solving
3.1. Inequality as a multilevel concept
Socioeconomic status (SES) can be conceptualized in different ways,
but commonly, researchers use some combination of family income,
parental education, and parents’ occupation coded for status (McLoyd,
1998; Sirin, 2005). To our knowledge, no study has estimated the
relationship between SES inequality and CPS. Given that CPS encompasses academic and social components (Andrews-Todd and Forsyth,
2020), we refer to existing literature that examines both academic
achievement and social consequences to gain a deeper understanding.
On an individual level, a positive relationship between SES and academic achievement is well-established (Coleman, l990; Sirin, 2005).
Academically, students who have greater access to educational resources at home, come from a supportive family background, and enjoy
favorable economic circumstances tend to outperform their peers who
are less privileged. The effect of SES on cognitive development varies
with age, the critical range being from 12 to 15 years old when the
achievement gap doubles between students with higher and lower SES
characteristics (Caro et al., 2009).
A student’s SES status, thus, has an impact on academic achievement. However, less clear is the effect that exposure to SES inequality
might have. In a highly SES diverse educational setting, do we see lower
achievement outcomes from all students, regardless of their personal
SES characteristics? The utilization of multilevel models demonstrates
consistent findings where SES distribution is linked to academic
achievement, and higher levels of as the inequality are inversely related
to academic performance (Sirin, 2005, pp.432–433). Despite this strong
influence however, cross-nationally we see wide variance in the strength
of the effect suggesting that macro level elements moderate the relationship (Else-Quest et al., 2010; OECD, 2017a, p. 175).
Some evidence shows that economic development moderates the
relationship. As an economy develops, initial income gaps stratify the
educational system, and this creates wider inequality until a higher level
of development can be sustained (Baker et al., 2002). Indeed, parents’
education levels predict student achievement in countries with high
levels of inequality compared to societies where inequalities are relatively low (Martins and Veiga, 2010). Inherent intelligence, as a predictor of status, is suppressed for those in lower SES categories, an effect
not seen among the privileged of society, indicating that national
inequality produces an indirect, negative effect on academic outcomes
(Tucker-Drob and Bates, 2016). Inequality at the societal level can
become institutionalized within an educational system so that opportunities to learn are not equal among students. However, attempting to
draw inferences about academic performance from national inequality
measures is fraught with error. Students experience education within
their schools and within a broader educational system, both of which
filter the effect of national indicators of inequality.
Multilevel approaches, exploring mezzo level inequality, promise to
add to the explanation of variance. Sastry and Pebley (2010) show that
median neighborhood wealth, along with degree of concentration of
wealth within that neighborhood, can be used to estimate the negative
effect of inequality on academic achievement. However, other factors
map with income diversity so that, in some settings, the size of a community may better capture how SES differences impact academic outcomes (Lounkaew, 2013). For these reasons, modeling inter-school

Generalized trust refers to having faith in strangers, especially those
who you consider different from yourself (Uslaner, 2002). Having trust
in others facilitates complex decision-making where actors do not know
each other and yet must collaborate to accomplish a task (Shapiro, 1987;
Yamagishi, 2001). As a heuristic, generalized trust allows one to quickly
assess the level of faith they should place in others. People who have
higher levels of generalized trust are more likely to engage in collective
endeavors than those who are suspicious when operating with little
additional information (Balliet and Van Lange, 2013; Holm and Danielson, 2005; Petersen, 2009; Yamagishi, 2001). Most individuals are
conditional collaborators, meaning that when faced with social dilemmas they are guided by the social exchange heuristic, which motivates them to act in concert with others and expect reciprocity
(Sønderskov, 2011). People who are trusting tend to process information
more effectively, enhancing their ability to problem solve, especially in
situations that involve social interaction (Yamagishi, 2001).
When treated as a national average, generalized trust serves as a
moderating factor in the relationship between perceived social support
and their overall life satisfaction among adolescents (Bi et al., 2021).
Trust is a sociocultural phenomenon that creates a climate of mutual
confidence and faith among individuals. Trusting others goes hand in
hand with collaboration and positively impacts the perceptions participants have of each other (Bi et al., 2021; Van Lange, 2015). Trust elicits

Although we treat inequality as operating on different levels, the

data to the individual level. With only 46 countries, all macro level

3.3. How political equality moderates the effect of inequality on
collaboration
Political equality entails the principle of treating all citizens as equals
within the political process, ensuring that everyone possesses equal
rights to influence decision-making and equitable access to political
power (Dahl, 1989). Political equality serves as a foundation for the
legitimacy of a democratic system. Bringing stability and predictability
to the political arena, political equality also advances notions of good
governance (Rothstein and Teorell, 2008).
Where political equality prevails, transparency characterizes the
political process so that information related to policy options and
decision-making is readily available to all citizens. Political equality
implies few to no barriers for citizen engagement. Furthermore, public
officials are responsive to citizen demands, including their willingness to
step down on behest of the people. Thus, transparency, participation,
and representation are three necessary ingredients for political equality
(Rothstein and Teorell, 2008).
Such an environment empowers individuals to take an active role in
civic problem solving, opening the door for citizens and officials to
collaborate (Kosack and Fung, 2014). As democracy deepens, the process reinforces collaborative impulses among perceived equals. For
involved individuals, collective endeavors yield a different kind of
satisfaction than individual pursuits. CPS is woven into the idea of broad
democratic decision-making as citizens come together to diagnose and
address social problems. The actors do not know the outcome, but all
share a common interest in the collaborative process. CPS achievement
combines participant cognitive abilities with social emotions, ranging
from anger to enjoyment, all of which contribute to successful outcomes
(Camacho-Morles et al., 2019). As such, political equality is expected to
moderate the negative influence of SES inequalities on CPS.

To measure student collaborative skills, we use the PISA measurement of CPS performance. CPS is the capacity of an individual to
effectively engage in a process whereby two or more participants
attempt to resolve a problem through shared understanding and effort.
The human-agent approach has students engage in a simulated group
project on a computer where an algorithm brings students to decision
points. At these critical junctures, participants choose to collaborate or
not, and the program pools student knowledge, skills, and effort through
the responses. (OECD, 2017b, p. 26). Three interrelated domains characterize the pooling process: 1) questioning: establishing and maintaining a shared understanding of the problem, 2) asserting: taking
appropriate action to solve the problem, and 3) requesting: establishing
and maintaining team organization. Each of the three break down into a
four-step process: a) exploring and understanding, b) representing and
formulating, c) planning and executing, and d) monitoring and reflecting. (Herborn et al., 2017).

(Dal Bó et al., 2010). The principle of political equality coincides with
citizen engagement and confidence in the political system through
representation, while holding public officials accountable. Such a
context is compatible with citizens who develop a propensity to
collaborate. Group decision-making, collectively assigning roles, and
coordinating the execution of tasks are more easily attained among
people who already value shared engagement and responsibilities. We
generate two more hypotheses based on the moderating influence of
political equality.

impact on CPS performance in societies with greater levels of political
equality.

H1a. Students who attend schools with higher levels of intra-school
SES inequality develop weaker collaborative skills compared to students attending schools with lower levels of intra-school inequality.
H1b. Students who attend a school within a national system of schools
with higher levels of inter-school SES inequality develop weaker
collaborative skills compared to students whose education system has a
lower level of inter-school inequality.

H3b. Political equality will moderate the negative impact of interschool SES inequality on CPS scores, so that inequality has less of an

As part of the application of the 2015 PISA exam, students complete a
survey which asks for demographic information, including family income. To calculate intra-school SES inequality, we take the standard
deviation of the mean household income from each student within each
school. Thus, we measure SES differences to which students are exposed
at school regardless of the mean family income level.
To estimate inter-school inequality, we take the standard deviation
of the school means for each country, which we treat as a national school
system, and thus a macro level variable. The resulting measure points to
a different type of exposure to inequality compared to that within a
student’s school. Inequality across schools refers only to the population
of families with 15-year-old students, and thus deviates from alternative
national measures. Our inequality measures show that 75 % of the
countries in the 2015 sample have experienced rising inequalities on one
or both measures compared to 2006 (OECD, 2017b).
We also evaluate generalized trust and political equality, two national level factors, which may moderate the relationship between our
measures of inequality and CPS outcomes. We posit that the quality of
being a high-trusting country will mitigate the negative effect of SES
inequality on students’ CPS performance. Trusting societies encourage
norms of reciprocity, an expected component of successful CPS
engagement. Thus, students raised in a high-trusting country are less
susceptible to the divisive effect that inequality might have on CPS. We
advance two hypotheses regarding the moderating impact of generalized trust.

To estimate political equality, we employ a measure developed by
the Democracy Barometer Project. Their index of political equality includes three elements: transparency, participation, and representation.
The three components advance the notion that all citizens are treated as
equals in the political process, have equal rights to influence decisionmaking, and have equal access to political power.4

5.1. Results from modeling inequality and CPS
Table 2 shows the outcomes of applying hierarchical random effects
models to PISA data with randomized intercepts and slopes. Results are
grouped based on the level of analysis for each independent variable:
micro (1), mezzo (2), and macro (3). Model 1 (M1) includes only intraschool inequality at the mezzo level. Model 2 (M2) adds interaction
terms to the mezzo level, considering how generalized trust and political
equality might interact with intra-school inequality. Model 3 (M3) is the
same as M1 but also includes SCIENCE as a control factor. Model 4 (M4)
replicates M2 with the addition of SCIENCE.
Our first hypothesis asserts that exposure to higher standard deviations of intra-school inequality results in poorer CPS performance.
Across the four models, the expectation holds. The first iteration (M1)
reveals the statistically significant coefficient of − 10.61. When interaction terms are added (M2), the strength of the intra-school inequality
coefficient increases (b=− 15.02).7 M3 adds student science exam performance to the model as a control. SCIENCE has a powerful effect on
CPS (b=53.67), which lowers the magnitude of the intra-school
inequality effect, but the negative coefficient remains strong and statistically significant (b=− 4.93). Adding the interaction terms (M4) increases the strength of the intra-school inequality coefficient as we saw
comparing M1 to M3 (b=− 0.716). In the end, we feel confident in
confirming H1a.
The second hypothesis (H1b) predicts that exposure to inter-school
inequality will be associated with lower CPS scores. M1 and M2 produce negative coefficients supporting the hypothesis with or without the
interaction terms. However, that support is lost when SCIENCE is added
to the equations (M3 and M4). The result suggests that inter-school
inequality may negatively influence student academic achievement
more generally but not CPS specifically. Thus, we cannot confirm H1b.
Turning to our control variables, at the individual level, a student’s
economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS), produces a consistently
positive and statistically significant effect on CPS performance across
the four models. The influence diminishes in M3 and M4 pointing again
to the strong impact of SCIENCE being included in the model. Students
who do well on the PISA science exam also do well on the CPS evaluation. However, ESCS independently exerts influence on CPS performance. Family wealth does not produce a statistically significant
coefficient when SCIENCE is added, suggesting that family wealth
contributes more generally to academic outcomes but not specifically to
CPS.

5.2. Results showing how the relationship between inequality and CPS are
moderated
We also advance hypotheses regarding the moderating effects of
generalized trust (H2a and H2b) and political equality (H3a and H3b). A
culture of trust and the practice of political equality may soften the
negative impact that intra-school and inter-school inequality has on CPS
performance. We visually present the moderating effects of both types of
inequality on CPS outcomes in Figs. 2–5. We treat the moderating effects
of generalized trust and political equality as dichotomies, where the top
quartile refers to the “high” value, while the lower three quartiles
represent “low” trust or political equality.
Fig. 2 shows that inequality within the school setting negatively
impacts CPS. However, all else equal, students perform better on the CPS
exam in countries where generalized trust is stronger. The trust dividend
expands as intra-school inequality widens represented by the gentler
slope in countries with higher trust.
Fig. 3 shows the moderating effect of political equality on the relationship between intra-school inequality and CPS. In schools with high
inequality, CPS scores decrease. Political equality is a complex measure
that estimates political transparency, political participation, and government responsiveness. Where political equality is higher, the effect of
rising inequality on CPS is not as dramatic demonstrated by the different
slopes in Fig. 3. However, the intercept shows that where political
equality is low, CPS performance is stronger where intra-school
inequality is lower. In other words, students have higher CPS scores
when they live in countries with low political equality but also go to
schools where intra-school inequality is low, compared to students in
countries with higher measures of political equality. As intra-school
inequality increases within the less politically equal contexts, intraschool inequality exerts a more intense negative impact on CPS performance. Where political equality is high, the effect of rising intra-school
inequality has less of an impact on CPS scores.
Fig. 4 shows that generalized trust mediates the relationship between
inter-school inequality and CPS performance. However, trust does not
act as a moderator since the slopes for high and low trusting countries
are nearly identical. Therefore, we cannot confirm H3b.

6. Discussion
Collaborative problem solving is an essential skill for this millennium
having applications in the economic, political, and social realms. We ask
if SES inequality inhibits CPS performance. The 2015 PISA application
included an assessment of CPS using a human-agent approach that
controls for environmental and social factors. Thus, digital technology
through the CPS exam offers new insight into the effects of inequality on
academic achievement. The PISA data also provide an innovative way to
assess effects of intra-school and inter-school inequality.
National level measures of inequality as predictors of academic
success are fraught with endogeneity problems. While 15-year-old students surely feel the effects of national income inequality, for most, their
day-to-day reality revolves around school. If exposure to inequality
impacts academic achievement, it is more likely to produce an observable effect when that inequity characterizes their school or educational
system. Our findings confirm this, to a point. The national measure of

Our analysis also highlights how important the national context is to
understanding student CPS performance. Generalized trust is a societal
asset that contributes to collaborative tendencies. Indeed, we find that
student CPS scores are higher in more trusting societies, although the
direct effect is rather small. Trust has a larger impact as a moderator on
the relationship between intra-school inequality and CPS. Thus, the
negative impact of intra-school inequalities is not reversed within
trusting societies, but it is softened. Trust is a fickle commodity, however, and one that can erode through persistent inequity, which would
have a cascading negative effect on collaborative skills in the future.
We also assess the influence that political equality has on CPS. Like
generalized trust, we measure political equality on the national level.
Political equality moderates the relationship between inequality and
CPS, mitigating the negative impact of intra-school inequality. In
countries with high political equality, we also see CPS rates higher
where inequality across schools is higher. We know that in most OECD
countries, income gaps are rising, with fewer occupying a place in the
middle. “Income inequality is less of a concern when children in lowincome families have a good chance of climbing up the income ladder
when they grow up” (OECD, 2017a, p. 174). From this perspective,
countries with higher levels of political equality are likely those where
greater opportunities for social mobility prevail. However, further
analysis is needed before we can be sure of this interpretation.
Taken together, we see that generalized trust and political equality
contribute positively to students acquiring CPS skills, and this is positive
for democracy. However, many countries have deficits in both trust and
political equality. Our findings serve as a warning sign. As this generation of students matures, fewer citizens with CPS skills may make it more
difficult to sustain democracy. Indeed, signs of this are already visible.
Many OECD countries are experiencing rising income inequalities, and
often in these cases, trust and political equality are in short supply.
Within such a context we may see more support for illiberal candidates
and parties among these youth, straining democratic institutions
further.

6.1. Limitations of the study
Our research design has several limitations that future inquiries
should consider. First, the measurement of SES and CPS limit the inferences we can draw from the findings. Intra- and inter-school SES are
based on respondent perceptions of their status, which is contextually
relative. CPS is derived from a human-agent instrument, which facilitates a standardized test environment. However, ecological validity
would be better approximated with human-human instruments

(Pásztor-Kovács et al., 2021). Additionally, the CPS measure simulates a
problem-solving task. Problem solving in real-life situations needs
further assessment for construct validity since non-simulated collaborative opportunities may yield different results. Furthermore, the CPS
exam applied in a school setting may not translate to the same behavior
within civic life.
Secondly, when employing national-level variables, we recognize the
limitations of using a relatively small number of countries. The sample
itself is subject to selection bias including only countries willing or able
to participate in the PISA project. Thus, we must exercise caution
regarding any inferences about aggregate citizen values and political
systems. HLM tests control for some imbalance among the number of
units in a level, but distortions persist. We also acknowledge that our
measures of trust and political equality are derived from data sets independent from PISA, and thus do not offer individual level insight. Our
purpose is to illuminate contextual effects on CPS acquisition. However,
we recognize that it is a leap to assume these measures adequately
represent national social norms, such as reciprocity, and moderate the
effects of inequality on CPS among 15-year-old students in the predicted
way.
Lastly, the analyses would benefit from having additional and more
refined measures. Try as we may, we cannot eliminate endogeneity issues. For example, the model would benefit from an individual level

measure of generalized trust. Other mezzo level measures would
strengthen the analysis, whether that be characteristics of the school or
the community in which the school operates.
6.2. Conclusion
Collaborative problem-solving (CPS) skills are prerequisites for
economic performance and the functioning of democratic institutions.
The collective nature of CPS draws attention to the importance that
social context may play in developing this competency. As the gap in
wealth widens, CPS performance is impeded. To better understand the
development of collaboration proficiency in youth, our study assessed
the effects on CPS of two types of inequality: intra-school and interschool wealth inequality. It relies on the PISA 2015 data, which includes measures of CPS of 15 years old in 46 countries. Although the
design and analysis are subject to limitations, we find that exposure to
inequality has a negative effect on CPS individual scores. Intra-school
inequality has the stronger effect of the two and remains significant
when controlling academic aptitude, measured through performance on
the PISA science exam. Moreover, generalized trust and political
equality alleviate these effects independently.
In an increasingly globalized world, a premium is placed on intercultural competence. This skill is vital for collaborative problem solving.

as equip them with communication skills to bridge the gap, may advance
CPS skills in the process. Future research should consider how policies
can be crafted to best manage exposure to SES diversity and process it
