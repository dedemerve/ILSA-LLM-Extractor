A B S T R A C T  
The significance of teacher collaboration in driving school changes has been widely acknowl­
edged. However, previous studies have rarely delved into the relative contributions of different 
forms of teacher collaboration. In this study, we aimed to address this gap by identifying two 
forms of collaboration and examining their effects on school innovativeness and innovative 
teaching practice using structural equation modeling. Our sample comprised 3,835 teachers of 
lower secondary schools in Taiwan, drawn from the TALIS 2018 dataset. The findings of our study 
suggested the differential effects of the two forms of collaboration. "Professional collaboration," 
characterized by deeper-level engagement, was found to have a more substantial association with 
both school innovativeness and innovative teaching compared to "exchange and coordination for 
teaching." Furthermore, our analysis revealed mediation effects in the pathways examined. 
Specifically, school innovativeness partially mediated the effect of "professional collaboration" 
and fully mediated the effect of "exchange and coordination for teaching" on innovative teaching.   
1. Introduction 
Teacher collaboration is an effective strategy for capacity building, instructional improvement, and enhancing student achieve­
ment (Hargreaves, 2019; Ronfeldt et al., 2015). Its significance in today’s educational reform context is even more manifest as we 
confront global changes in the environment, society, and technology, and stand on the brink of transforming school education. To 
equip young people with the competencies needed for the future, a shift toward learner-centered pedagogies is imperative. Innovative 
approaches like inquiry-based and collaborative learning play a crucial role in fostering students’ key skills and attitudes. The call for 
learner-centered pedagogy is evident in curriculum reforms worldwide. While each country follows a unique trajectory of change, 
there has been a recent emphasis on innovative teaching centered around the learner. For instance, in Taiwan, the Curriculum 
Guidelines of 12-year Basic Education, launched in 2019, advocate for learners’ active participation (Ministry of Education the Re­
public of China Taiwan, 2014). To implement the new competency-oriented curriculum, Taiwanese teachers must move away from 
traditional methods and embrace innovative pedagogies that engage students in self-questioning, metacognition, problem-solving, and 
collaborative learning (Fullan, 2011; Hattie, 2009). However, innovative practices require supportive conditions to flourish, and 
teacher collaboration emerges as a critical catalyst.

learning (Darling-Hammond & Richardson, 2009; Dufour & Eaker, 1998; Hargreaves, 2019; Louis et al., 1998). Teacher collaboration

Furthermore, innovation is essential for school improvement and sustainable development. In the organizational research litera­

that embrace openness to change are assumed to have more courage to take risks and experiment with new pedagogies. Therefore, we

also posited that teacher collaboration contributes to a school’s propensity for innovation (Vangrieken et al., 2015). Empirical evi­

collaboration and innovative teaching. As teachers exchange knowledge, engage in professional reflection, and cultivate a collective 
sense of autonomy, our hypothesis suggests that this would result in heightened innovation within schools. Subsequently, this 
increased school innovation would influence teachers’ adoption of innovative pedagogical practices. 
As mentioned earlier, Taiwan embarked on competency-oriented curriculum reform in 2019, emphasizing the importance of 
teachers adopting innovative pedagogies that align with the new curriculum. This sense of urgency has piqued our interest in 
investigating the factors that drive innovative teaching, with a particular focus on teacher collaboration and school innovativeness. 
Leveraging the OECD’s Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018 dataset, we have conducted our study by selecting a 
sample of lower secondary school teachers from Taiwan. It should be noted that the data we analyzed was collected prior to Taiwan’s 
curriculum reform in 2019. Therefore, the results can offer insights into how well-prepared teachers were in terms of their collabo­
rative behaviors and innovativeness in anticipation of the new curriculum. Additionally, our exploration into the catalysts for inno­
vative teaching can be valuable for policymakers and school leaders, as it can assist them in designing effective policy tools to stimulate 
innovative teaching practices. 
2. Literature review 
2.1. Teacher collaboration and its effects 
Teacher collaboration holds a significant role in the professional lives of educators, providing them with a platform for continuous 
reflection and improvement in their teaching practices. Extensive research has examined teacher collaboration, often considering it 
within the context of professional communities or teacher teams, treating it as a collective concept to investigate its effects (Vangrieken 
et al., 2015). Numerous studies have highlighted the positive outcomes associated with teacher collaboration (Vangrieken et al., 
2015). At the individual teacher level, the benefits include increased motivation, improved interpersonal communication, enhanced 
efficacy, and the adoption of innovative behaviors and pedagogy (Coburn et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2022; Methlagl, 2022; Nemerˇzitski 
et al., 2013; Vangrieken et al., 2015; Vescio et al., 2008). At the organizational level, teacher collaboration contributes to a school 
climate that fosters innovation, a more equitable culture, responsiveness to students’ needs, and a collective disposition toward 
inquiry-based practices (Moolenaar et al., 2011; Vangrieken et al., 2015). 
To gain a nuanced understanding of how teacher collaboration promotes improvement and innovation among teachers and schools, 
researchers have categorized teacher collaboration into different typologies. Ronfeldt et al. (2015) focused on collaboration in the 
domains of instruction, students, and assessment, revealing that collaboration related to assessment was the most significant predictor 
of student achievement gains in math and reading. Another typology proposed by Little (1990) distinguished collaboration based on 
interdependency and collegiality, resulting in four typologies: storytelling and scanning, aid and assistance, sharing, and joint work. 
These collaborative activities exhibit varying degrees of interdependency (Kelchtermans, 2006), ranging from individuals coming 
together to form an aggregate to a cohesive community. In assessing teacher collaboration, OECD (2020) utilized two forms of 
collaborative practices for the TALIS 2018: professional collaboration and exchange and coordination for teaching. These forms 
indicate varying degrees of interdependency. Professional collaboration encompasses practices such as teaching jointly, observing 
colleagues’ classes and providing feedback, engaging in joint activities, and participating in collaborative professional learning. On the 
other hand, exchange and coordination for teaching involve activities like exchanging teaching materials, discussing student learning, 
and working with peers to ensure common standards for student assessment. Although activities involving discussion or idea exchange 
are more common in teachers’ daily interactions compared to collective work (Cheng & Ko, 2009; Kwakman, 2003), researchers 
contend that higher levels of interdependency among collaborating teachers facilitate greater learning among teachers (Hammerness 
et al., 2005; Imants, 2003; Little, 1990). 
In addition to the argument that various collaboration types imply hierarchical relationships, recent studies have challenged this 
assertion, emphasizing that each type of collaboration serves distinct functions in facilitating teacher professional learning (De Jong 
et al., 2019; Doppenberg et al., 2012; Van Gasse et al., 2017; Van Waes et al., 2016). While these studies offer evidence of how different 
types of teacher collaboration are linked to opportunities for professional growth, they have yet to provide a clear understanding of 
whether these various collaboration types contribute differentially to innovation. Moreover, Hargreaves (2019) contended that while

2.2. School innovativeness as a mediator 
The concept of school innovativeness draws upon social capital theory, which explores the dynamic interactions between teachers 
and the school climate (Geijsel, 2001). School innovativeness is viewed as both a process and a product of education, with the process 
referring to schools’ capacity for self-renewal as measured by the organizational climate for change. In this study, school innova­
tiveness is defined by teachers’ perception of the organizational openness towards innovations and new ideas. 
Studies have investigated the factors leading to school innovativeness and its outcomes. Kirsten and Du Preez (2010) proposed that 
an innovative climate can be cultivated through the development of teamwork. Moolenaar et al. (2011) further suggested that teachers 
who are part of a social network are more inclined to take risks, thereby promoting productivity and fostering an innovative climate. 
Collaboration has also been identified as a prerequisite for school innovativeness, facilitating school reforms and enabling the 
experimentation of new ideas (Bryk & Schneider, 2002; Westheimer, 2008). These findings underscore the significance of building 
collaborative relationships in the educational context to facilitate organizational change. Regarding the outcomes, previous studies 
have indicated that an innovative climate contributes to creative behavior (Antoni, 2005; Jaiswal & Dhar, 2015) and the adoption of 
innovative teaching practices (Bl¨omeke et al., 2021; Chou et al., 2019; Thurlings et al., 2015). In an environment that supports 
innovation, teachers are more willing to be vulnerable and engage in risk-taking with colleagues within the organization (Moolenaar 
et al., 2011). Conversely, a lack of a culture of learning and support hinders teachers from embracing innovative behaviors (Thurlings 
et al., 2015). The promotion of collegiality has been identified as a significant driver in creating school environments that welcome 
new ideas and possibilities. In line with the global trend of fostering teacher autonomy and collaboration, the concept of "collaborative 
professionalism" has gained traction (Hargreaves, 2019). In Taiwan, the Ministry of Education has also encouraged and supported 
various forms of professional learning communities in K-12 schools (Pan, 2014; Pan et al., 2017). 
Based on the review of previous studies, it can be concluded that school innovativeness can be nurtured through teacher collab­
oration and networks, resulting in positive effects on creative behaviors and the adoption of innovative instructional approaches. 
Therefore, this study treated school innovativeness as a mediator to assess its role in mediating the association between teacher 
collaboration and the implementation of innovative teaching practices. 
2.3. Innovative teaching 
Innovative teaching refers to teachers designing their classes and teaching styles with creativity and new ideas, incorporating 
technology and new methods to enhance students’ learning achievement (Kalyani & Rajasekaran, 2018). In today’s rapidly changing 
world, teachers must continuously learn innovative teaching methods to keep up with the pace of education reforms. Many teaching 
strategies are considered innovative pedagogy, such as utilizing audio and video tools, fostering creativity through brainstorming, and 
providing opportunities for exploration and problem-solving (Baskaran & Rajarathinam, 2018; Kalyani & Rajasekaran, 2018). A report 
on Innovative Teaching and Learning Research has identified three critical elements of innovative teaching practices (Bernard & 
Langworthy, 2011): a student-centered pedagogy serving as the foundation for curriculum design, extending learning beyond the 
classroom by providing real-world problems for students to discuss and utilizing information and communication technology in 
teaching. 
Le Donn´e et al. (2016)) conducted a study using the TALIS-PISA linking database to investigate the connection between teaching 
strategies and students’ mathematics performance. Their findings identified three teaching strategies that have a particularly positive 
impact on learning outcomes: active learning, cognitive activation, and teacher-directed instruction. Both active learning and 
cognitive activation are innovative teaching strategies that prioritize student-centered approaches. Active learning strategies 
emphasize students’ active participation and engagement during classroom learning activities, such as group work, integration of 
technology, and student self-evaluation. Cognitive activation involves fostering critical thinking, problem-solving, and 
decision-making skills among students. These two teaching strategies align with the innovative pedagogies proposed in the OECD’s 
TALIS 2018 (Ainley & Carstens, 2018). 
Regarding the factors that drive innovative teaching, teacher collaboration stands out as a significant catalyst. Through a quan­
titative approach, Methlagl (2022) identified that educators who abstain from collaborative activities are less inclined to utilize 
effective teaching techniques, such as clear instruction and cognitive activation. This observation was drawn from a study involving 
lower secondary teachers in participating OECD countries. Similarly, Sargent (2015) observed a greater prevalence of innovative 
teaching methods among Chinese elementary school teachers who are actively involved in collaboration. In a U.S. context, Ronfeldt 
et al. (2015) also suggested that teachers exhibit greater improvement rates when they operate within schools characterized by higher 
collaboration quality. Shifting the focus toward teacher innovativeness yielded comparable findings, highlighting the effect of 
collaboration on teachers’ openness to new ideas and strategies in adapting their teaching (Çoban & Atasoy, 2020; Lin, 2022). 
Nevertheless, these studies have yet to delve deeply into the specific effect of different forms of collaboration on teaching practices. 
Hence, there remains a clear need for further research in this area.

2.4. Conceptual framework 
In Hargreaves’ (2019) review article, he suggested that an evolutionary collaborative culture, rather than administratively 
contrived collegiality, facilitates effective teacher collaboration. Joint work among teachers allows them to accumulate professional 
capital, contributing to the shared responsibility of education professionals at all levels in advancing student learning through the 
exchange of knowledge, experiences, and collective work (Ontario Ministry of Education, 2016). Hargreaves referred to this elevated 
form of collaboration as "collaborative professionalism." Therefore, it is necessary to expand teacher collaboration by progressing from 
lower degrees to higher degrees of interdependency and collegiality. 
While collaboration activities such as storytelling, scanning, providing aid and assistance, sharing, and joint work (Little, 1990) 
have been argued by some scholars to be non-hierarchical (De Jong et al., 2019; Doppenberg et al., 2012; Van Gasse et al., 2017; Van 
Waes et al., 2016), they do indeed exhibit varying degrees of interdependency and collegiality. Supovitz (2002) discovered that 
teachers who function as a cohesive team, engaging in activities like preparing instruction, co-teaching, and observing one another, 
acquire better student outcomes. Joint work in instructional planning and exercises can contribute to teacher improvement (Methlagl, 
2022; Ronfeldt et al., 2015; Sargent, 2015). Building upon these empirical findings, this study postulated that professional collabo­
ration, characterized by deeper-level engagement such as joint teaching, observing teaching, and engaging in joint activities, would 
serve as a more potent predictor of innovative teaching practices compared to activities primarily focused on surface-level collabo­
ration, such as exchanging and coordination for teaching. Through active involvement in collective activities, teachers have the chance 
to observe their peers’ teaching methods, prompting reflection on their pedagogical perspectives and practices, thus fostering 
collaborative learning. This dynamic interplay among professionals encourages experimentation with novel instructional approaches. 
A similar rationale applies to the relationship between teacher collaboration and school innovativeness. Through deeper levels of 
collaboration, teachers become more receptive to new ideas and practices, fostering an environment conducive to innovation. Prior 
research supports this contention (Kirsten & Du Preez, 2010; Moolenaar et al., 2011; Westheimer, 2008), suggesting that teamwork, 
networks, and learning communities all contribute to a climate that promotes innovation. Furthermore, an innovative climate en­
courages teachers to embrace innovative teaching behaviors (Bl¨omeke et al., 2021; Chou et al., 2019; Thurlings et al., 2015). In schools 
with an innovative climate, teachers are granted greater autonomy in their pedagogical practices. Hence, this study not only explored 
the association between school innovativeness and innovative teaching but also regarded school innovativeness as a mediator, 
examining its role in mediating the relationship between teacher collaboration and innovative teaching. 
The conceptual framework of the study is presented in Fig. 1, and the research questions addressed are as follows:  
1. To what extent do teachers perceive teacher collaboration, school innovativeness, and innovative teaching?  
2. How are different forms of teacher collaboration related to innovative teaching?  
3. How does school innovativeness mediate the associations between teacher collaboration and innovative teaching? 
3. Method 
3.1. Data source and sample 
The data for this study were obtained from TALIS 2018 (Ainley & Carstens, 2018). The TALIS 2018 research team employed a 
stratified random sampling approach, which entailed the inclusion of a minimum of 200 schools and at least 20 teachers from each 
selected school. The sample size considered the population size of each respective country, with a target response rate of over 75 % for 
both schools and teachers. The overall response rate for the survey was 56.25 % (Jerrim & Sims, 2019). For this study, we focused

specifically on data obtained from lower secondary school teachers in Taiwan, one of the 48 countries and economies covered by the 
survey. The analysis was based on 3835 valid responses, with the sample consisting of 68 % female teachers and 32 % male teachers. 
On average, the teachers in our sample had 15.23 years of teaching experience (SD = 7.61), with 11.32 years at their current school (SD

(0.8 %). 
3.2. Measures 
In this study, the items under investigation were chosen from the teacher questionnaire of the TALIS 2018 (OECD, 2018). Our 
independent variable, teacher collaboration, was assessed using two subscales: "professional collaboration" (TT3G33A, B, C, and H) 
and "exchange and coordination for teaching" (TT3G33D, E, F, and G), each comprising four items. In the structural equation modeling 
analysis, items TT3G33H and G were excluded due to their lower factor loadings. To gage the frequency of teacher collaboration, a 
six-point scale was employed, wherein respondents could indicate their level of engagement as follows: 1 = never, 2 = once a year or 
less, 3 = 2–4 times a year, 4 = 5–10 times a year, 5 = 1–3 times a month, and 6 = once a week or more. These two subscales were 
designed to assess the extent of teacher involvement in collaborative activities, encompassing activities such as exchanging teaching 
materials or collaborating in co-teaching scenarios. Illustrative sample items for "professional collaboration" and "exchange and co­
ordination for teaching" include statements like "Teach jointly as a team in the same class" and "Exchange teaching materials with 
colleagues," respectively. 
The mediating variable, school innovativeness, was assessed using four items (TT3G32A, B, C, and D). Participants indicated their 
level of agreement on a four-point Likert-type scale, ranging from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree." This measure captured 
teachers’ perceptions of their school’s openness to applying new ideas. A sample item is: "Most teachers in this school are open to 
change." 
The dependent variable, innovative teaching, consisted of six items related to "teaching practices." Participants rated their 
agreement on a four-point Likert-type scale, ranging from "never or almost never" to "always." "Innovative teaching" comprised two 
subscales: "cognitive activation" (TT3G42 E, F, G and H), referring to teaching practices that promote critical thinking and problem- 
solving abilities in students, and "enhanced activities" (TT3G42O and P), which involve collaborative student work and the use of 
technology for projects or assignments, requiring more time from both teachers and students compared to traditional instruction 
(O’Shea, 2021). Sample items for "cognitive activation" and "enhanced activities" include "I give tasks that require students to think 
critically" and "I give students projects that require at least one week to complete," respectively. 
3.3. Analytical approach 
Descriptive statistics were computed using SPSS 23. To examine the mediation effects, we employed AMOS 24 software. For re­
spondents with missing data, the expectation-maximization (EM) technique was utilized to estimate missing values. The study per­
formed confirmatory factor analyses for measurement models and structural models to assess mediation models examining the 
connections among teacher collaboration, school innovativeness, and innovative teaching. Model fit was evaluated using the indices 
suggested by Bowen and Guo (2011), including the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), 
the goodness of fit index (GFI) and the comparative fit index (CFI). We also incorporated three additional indices, namely the stan­
dardized root mean squared residual (SRMR), the normed fit index (NFI), and the adjusted goodness of fit index (AGFI). Parameter 
estimation and inference utilized the bootstrapping method, drawing 5000 samples repeatedly (Hair Jr et al., 2017). This approach 
allowed for the assessment of the significance of the estimated model parameters.

4.2. Measurement model 
Table 2 demonstrates that the standardized regression weights were between 0.68 and 0.89, indicating all the factor loadings were

4.3. Structural model 
An investigation into the relationships between teacher collaboration, school innovativeness, and innovative teaching practices 
was conducted using a structural equation model. School innovativeness was analyzed as a mediator in this study. Fig. 2 illustrates the 
standardized estimation of the structure model. Considering that the sample size can significantly affect the chi-square value, espe­
cially in larger samples where achieving a non-significant chi-square value is more challenging, our study chose to utilize alternative 
measures for assessing the model fit. The data indicated a good fit for the model, as evidenced by the following indices: RMSEA = 0.04, 
TLI = 0.97, GFI = 0.98, CFI = 0.98, SRMR = 0.03, NFI = 0.97, AGFI = 0.97 (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988; Bentler & Bonett, 1980; Scott et al., 
1994). 
Fig. 2 presents several key findings from the study. Regarding the effects of teacher collaboration, the results indicate that both 
forms of "professional collaboration" (β = 0.19, p < 0.001) and "exchange and coordination for teaching" (β = 0.15, p < 0.001) had 
direct positive influences on school innovativeness. However, only "professional collaboration" had a direct positive association with 
innovative teaching (β = 0.44, p < 0.001). Additionally, although the relationship between school innovativeness and innovative 
teaching was weak, it was statistically significant (β = 0.05, p < 0.05). Noteworthily, school innovativeness was a significant mediator 
for both the relationships between "professional collaboration" and innovative teaching, as well as "exchange and coordination for 
teaching" and innovative teaching. 
To validate these mediation effects, a bootstrapping method with a 95 % percentile interval was conducted. The results in Table 4 
indicate that both "professional collaboration" and "exchange and coordination for teaching" enhanced teachers’ innovative teaching 
practices by strengthening school innovativeness. The path of "professional collaboration → innovative teaching" showed a partially 
mediated effect, while the path of "exchange and coordination for teaching → innovative teaching" exhibited a fully mediated effect.

5. Discussion 
5.1. Teachers’ perceptions of teacher collaboration, school innovativeness, and innovative teaching practice 
Teacher collaboration has garnered significant research attention due to its positive effects on teachers’ work and student 
achievement (Hargreaves, 2019; Ronfeldt et al., 2015). As a result, promoting teacher collaboration has become a global educational 
policy. Since 2010, the Ministry of Education in Taiwan has initiated Professional Learning Communities (PLCs) to enhance collab­
oration among teachers (Pan, 2014; Pan et al., 2017). Evaluating the progress of the PLCs initiative requires empirical evidence, and 
the analysis of TALIS data in this study provides insights. 
Our respondents perceived their engagement in teacher collaboration at a moderately low level, indicating that despite the pro­
motion of PLCs since 2010, the effort has not been fully realized on school campuses. This highlights the fact that teacher collaboration 
does not occur spontaneously; it requires time to develop and must be nurtured within a culture of trust. The policy alone does not 
mandate changes. Expected outcomes of mutual learning and benefit among teachers cannot be induced by contrived collegiality 
(Hargreaves, 2019). Creating conducive conditions to foster teachers’ collaborative work involves cultivating a culture of trust and 
implementing structured designs by setting clear goals, facilitating open communication, and encouraging active participation from all 
parties.

study. This form of collaboration represents a deeper level, where teachers usually connect as a team, network, or learning community 
to engage in experiential learning. They collaborate within authentic situations to enhance their teaching practices, akin to what Little 
(1990) termed "joint work." Joint work involves interactions among teachers based on shared accountability for teaching, collective 
autonomy, support and leadership in professional practice, and group associations rooted in their professional roles. This deeper-level 
collaboration, although crucial, was found to be less common. Lower secondary school teachers in the study showed a stronger 
inclination towards activities such as exchanging teaching materials, discussing students’ learning development, and assessing stu­
dents’ progress. These activities demonstrate lower interdependency compared to joint work. The findings reflect the typical daily 
experiences of teachers, as also noted by Cheng and Ko (2009) and Kwakman (2003), who observed that teachers often have limited 
involvement in professional learning activities beyond mere conversations or discussions. Regarding the innovation measurements, 
school innovativeness was rated only at a moderate level, and innovative teaching practices were perceived to be at a moderately low 
level by teachers in the study. While innovation is crucial for school development (Bernard & Langworthy, 2011; Thurlings et al., 
2015), teachers and administrators often exhibit a low tolerance for uncertainty within the school system. Innovation involves dis­
rupting established routines and norms, which can be daunting for implementers who prefer to stay within their comfort zones 
(Serdyukov, 2017). Consequently, spreading innovation across schools becomes challenging, as reflected in our study. 
5.2. Different forms of teacher collaboration related to innovation and mediated effects 
Previous research has underlined the role of teacher collaboration in nurturing pedagogical innovations (Methlagl, 2022; Ronfeldt 
et al., 2015; Sargent, 2015; Vangrieken et al., 2015). Our study further elucidates the nuanced results concerning the differential effects 
of two forms of collaboration among teachers. We found that teachers’ adoption of innovative teaching approaches was associated 
with deeper-level "professional collaboration." 
In Confucian heritage societies, teachers typically employ the direct instruction method. While effective in imparting content 
knowledge, it may fall short in cultivating essential competencies such as critical thinking, creativity, and collaboration vital for to­
morrow’s society. With the advent of the new curriculum reform in 2019, there has been a paradigm shift towards student-centered 
pedagogy. Innovative teaching approaches, including posing thought-provoking questions, facilitating inquiry, and guiding problem- 
solving, are now expected to be integrated into classrooms. However, this shift presents challenges for Taiwanese teachers who must 
adapt and acquire new skills. Learning in authentic situations is an effective way for teachers to master innovative teaching methods. 
Unlike contrived collegiality lacking voluntariness and a developmental focus, "professional collaboration" epitomizes collabora­
tive professionalism (Hargreaves, 2019). It provides a platform for teachers to exchange expertise and work together to enhance 
student learning outcomes. Teachers acquire the skills to implement innovative teaching strategies through classroom observations 
and joint teaching, where they reflect critically on their practices with the help of data and feedback (Levine & Marcus, 2010). This 
situated learning approach was found in this study to be more effective in supporting innovative teaching, as it offers insights that 
cannot be gleaned solely through information exchange among colleagues. Such deeper-level collaborative endeavors also foster 
receptiveness to innovation. The frequency of "professional collaboration" positively correlated with teachers’ propensity to embrace 
change and adopt innovative instructional methods, consistent with prior literature (Methlagl, 2022; Ronfeldt et al., 2015; Sargent, 
2015; Vangrieken et al., 2015). In contrast, "exchanging and coordination for teaching" did not demonstrate a significant direct 
connection to innovative teaching practices, but it did contribute to fostering school innovativeness. Collegiality reduces teacher 
conservatism and cultivates a school climate conducive to innovation (Bryk & Schneider, 2002; Moolenaar et al., 2011; Westheimer, 
2008). Both forms of collaboration among teachers demonstrated effects on school innovativeness, with "professional collaboration" 
showing a slightly stronger relationship compared to the surface-level collaboration of "exchanging and coordinating for teaching. 
In addition to the direct effects of the two forms of teacher collaboration on innovation, the mediation model offers further insights 
into the relationships among the variables under investigation. School innovativeness served as a mediator between both forms of 
teacher collaboration and the adoption of innovative teaching practices. “Professional collaboration” revealed its effect within a partial 
mediation model, while “exchanging and coordination for teaching” exhibited its effect within a full mediation model. These findings 
suggest that school innovativeness is a significant mechanism through which different forms of teacher collaboration influence the 
enactment of innovative teaching. 
In summary, innovative teaching practices were closely linked with deeper-level rather than surface-level collaboration. The 
distinction in the effects of these collaboration forms on innovation can be attributed to the depth and nature of collaboration. 
“Professional collaboration,” involving collective goal achievement, thorough discussions on identifying student learning challenges, 
lesson planning, and selecting instructional approaches, provokes teachers to reflect on the "why," "what," and "how" of instruction. The 
strong sense of collegiality fostered by “professional collaboration” catalyzed teachers to embrace new ideas and practices and 
implement them effectively. On the other hand, mere "exchange and coordination for teaching" could not directly foster innovative 
teaching; instead, its effect was mediated by school innovativeness. This suggests that an organizational climate that embraces change 
is crucial in arousing the effect of collaboration on innovative teaching practices. Moreover, compared to innovative teaching prac­
tices, school innovativeness was more easily elicited by collaborative behaviors. Both deeper-level and surface-level collaboration 
displayed some connection to school innovativeness, albeit to varying degrees, yet only deeper-level collaboration was associated with 
innovative teaching practices. It may be because pedagogical changes often demand a greater degree of interdependent collegiality to 
develop new skills.

6. Conclusion and implications 
Our study underscores the role of teacher collaboration in innovative school climate and teachers’ adoption of new instructional 
approaches. Innovative teaching tends to flourish in environments where teachers collaborate (Bernard & Langworthy, 2011). 
However, the existing literature lacks a clear understanding of how different forms of collaboration relate to innovation. Thus, our 
study examined two forms of teacher collaboration and investigated their connections with school innovativeness and innovative 
teaching. The findings suggested that "professional collaboration," characterized by a higher degree of interdependence, had a more 
significant effect. It demonstrated a stronger association with school innovativeness and innovative teaching in comparison to "ex­
change and coordination for teaching." Additionally, we found mediation effects within the pathways, indicating that school inno­
vativeness partially mediated the effect of "professional collaboration" and fully mediated the effect of "exchange and coordination for 
teaching" on innovative teaching. 
Although our study has yielded meaningful findings, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. We utilized the pre-existing 
TALIS dataset to gauge teacher collaboration, relying solely on the available survey items for assessment. This approach limited us 
to the existing parameters. Furthermore, self-reported data inherently presents limitations in accurately depicting the true state of 
affairs. To address these constraints, it is crucial to broaden the range of measurement items for the variables under scrutiny and to 
incorporate alternative methods, such as observational techniques. Second, our study primarily focused on examining associations 
between various constructs. Future research could incorporate cultural and micropolitical perspectives to analyze the shared values 
and norms within a collaborative culture, as well as the intentional, calculated, and strategic actions taken by groups (Kutsyuruba, 
2011). Moreover, our study centered on the initiation stage of the curriculum policy before the launch of the 2019 Curriculum 
Guidelines in Taiwan. It deserves to explore how deeper-level collaboration contributes to teachers’ paradigm shift toward 
student-centered pedagogy during the policy implementation stage. It can provide insights into the long-term effects of collaboration 
on instructional practices and student outcomes. 
Based on the findings, we further propose several implications for practice. Our study highlights the vital role of collaboration as a 
catalyst for school improvement, with deeper-level collaboration having a more substantial effect than mere "talking and exchanging" 
among teachers. Collaboration that delves deeper into principles and processes allows teachers to reframe their pedagogical con­
ceptions. To ensure effective collaboration, school leaders should deliberately design and promote the depth and quality of collabo­
rative efforts, moving beyond informal activities (Hargreaves, 2019). 
Additionally, when leading school change, it is important to consider the cultural characteristic of avoiding uncertainty, which is 
prevalent in Asian societies (Hallinger & Kantamara, 2000). In the context of Taiwan, where teachers feel pressured to prepare students 
for good grades in entrance examinations for higher-level schools, this credentialism reinforces their hesitation for change. In this 
regard, designing a tightened and deepened type of collaboration within schools can be effective in assisting teachers to collaborate 
with a clear focus and necessary depth. Through this dynamic process, teachers may gain greater courage and willingness to practice 
pedagogical approaches that foster higher-order skills in students. 
Furthermore, our findings suggest that even surface-level collaboration can influence teachers’ exercise of innovative teaching 
through a climate that supports change. Therefore, school leaders can foster trust among faculty members and create an environment 
that encourages risk-taking, thereby increasing teacher receptivity to educational reforms. In essence, promoting effective collabo­
ration while considering cultural characteristics and nurturing an atmosphere of innovation is crucial for the improvement of teaching 
practices. It is the responsibility of school leaders to design and develop collaborative structures that facilitate teacher growth and 
encourage innovation.
