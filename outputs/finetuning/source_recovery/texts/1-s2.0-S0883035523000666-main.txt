Chinese students’ reading performance in the Programme for International Student Assessment
(PISA) has attracted worldwide attention. Using 2018 PISA data from Beijing-Shanghai-JiangsuZhejiang, Hong Kong, and Chinese Taipei (n = 25,338), we explored whether the relationship
between reading instruction, engagement, and performance differs across the three Chinese regions. Results revealed that disciplinary climate, teacher support, and cognitive engagement had
a significant positive relationship, while teacher-directed instruction and student-perceived
reading difficulty had a significant negative relationship with reading performance in all three
regions. Distinct patterns were identified in the relationships between student-perceived teacher
stimulation, teacher feedback, self-concepts of reading competence and reading performance
across the three regions. The findings highlight the importance of considering intra-cultural diversity when studying Chinese educational practices.

1. Introduction
The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) is a large-scale triennial student assessment that measures 15-yearolds’ reading, mathematics, and science ability (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2019a). Each
round of PISA focuses on a major domain that comprises the majority of the assessment time, and questionnaires are distributed to
gather information on teaching and learning approaches. Reading was the major domain in the 2000, 2009, and 2018 PISA. With
rigorous standardized statistical procedures, the PISA’s results on reading ability are comparable across different countries (Ho & Lau,
2018; Li, Lei, & Pace, 2013; OECD, 2020; Sellar & Lingard, 2013). As Chinese students’ consistently outstanding reading performance
in the PISA gradually gained worldwide attention (Lau & Ho, 2016; Li et al., 2013; Ma, Luo, & Xiao, 2021; Sellar & Lingard, 2013),
several studies were conducted by international researchers to understand the underlying reasons (e.g., Ho & Lau, 2018; Khine, Fraser,
Afari, & Liu, 2023; Koyuncu & Fırat, 2020; Sellar & Lingard, 2013; Tan, 2017a). However, the reading instruction and engagement
factors associated with Chinese students’ outstanding reading performance in the PISA across different Chinese societies have not yet
been fully examined.
Previous studies on the relationship between reading instruction and reading performance have demonstrated conflicting results.

While some previous studies have revealed a significant positive relationship between different types of teaching practices (cognitive
stimulation, supportive climate, classroom management and structure) and learning or reading performance (e.g., Guthrie & Klauda,
2014; Klieme, Pauli, & Reusser, 2009; OECD, 2019a), others have found no significant relationship between these variables (Ho & Lau,
2018; Khine, Fraser, Afari, & Liu, 2023; Yu, 2019). There are also similar ambiguities regarding reading instruction in the Chinese
context (Ho & Lau, 2018; Khine, Fraser, Afari, & Liu, 2023; Ma et al., 2021; Qian & Lau, 2022; Sellar & Lingard, 2013). In terms of
reading engagement, although previous studies have basically agreed that cognitive engagement and motivational engagement are
closely related to students’ reading performance in both the Western and Chinese contexts (Artelt & Schneider, 2015; Bagci, 2020; Ho
& Lau, 2018; Klauda & Guthrie, 2015; Ma et al., 2021; Qi, 2021), they have rarely considered the potential instructional or
teacher-related factors (e.g., teachers’ qualifications, instructional practices) that might influence these associations. Research on
Chinese students’ performance in the PISA has also neglected the regional differences within Chinese societies (Koyuncu & Fırat, 2020;
Tse et al., 2016; Zhang & McBride-Chang, 2011). Additional well-designed studies involving different Chinese societies are, thus,
needed to further clarify the associations between these factors.
As McBridge-Chang (2004) noted, to reach a more comprehensive understanding of Chinese students’ reading performance in the
PISA, it is important to examine various regions because their political, social, and educational systems vary widely (Lau & Ho, 2016;
Tse et al., 2016). However, owing to a lack of empirical studies, it remains largely unknown how reading instruction, reading
engagement, and their association with reading performance vary across different Chinese regions. In the 2018 PISA, more than 250,
000 students from Beijing-Shanghai-Jiangsu-Zhejiang (B-S-J-Z), Hong Kong, and Taipei—three developed Chinese societies with
relatively rich educational resources—completed the computer-based assessment questionnaires on teaching and learning approaches
simultaneously, providing researchers with abundant data through which to explore the issue. Thus, using the PISA 2018 dataset, the
present study examined the characteristics of reading instruction, reading engagement, and their associations with students’ reading
performance across B-S-J-Z, Hong Kong, and Taipei after controlling for the key confounding variables.
2. Literature review
2.1. PISA 2018 and Chinese students’ reading performance
PISA 2018 defined reading literacy as “understanding, using, evaluating, reflecting on and engaging with texts in order to achieve
one’s goals, to develop one’s knowledge and potential, and to participate in society” (OECD 2019a). With reading as its major
assessment domain, PISA 2018 not only used computer-based reading assessments to evaluate students’ composite reading performance but adopted background questionnaires to survey students’ perceptions of reading instruction and their reading engagement
based on the tridimensional model of quality of teaching (TMQT) (Klieme et al., 2009) and reading engagement theory (Guthrie &
Klauda, 2014). These data could enhance our understanding of the relationships between students’ reading performance and various
factors pertaining to reading instruction and engagement.
Chinese students have consistently achieved outstanding reading scores in multiple cycles of the PISA, which has attracted
increasing global attention from researchers and policymakers (Ho & Lau, 2018; Li et al., 2013; Ma et al., 2021; Phillipson & Lam,
2011). Among all the participating regions, students from Shanghai and Hong Kong ranked 1st and 4th, respectively, in the PISA 2009
paper-based reading assessment (OECD, 2010). In PISA 2018, students from B-S-J-Z and Hong Kong ranked 1st and 4th, respectively, in
the computer-based reading assessment (OECD, 2019b). Taipei students’ performance remained above the OECD average in multiple
cycles (23rd in PISA 2009, 17th in PISA 2018). According to some researchers, Chinese students’ leading performance, as well as the
teaching and learning practices in the country, have become an important reference for many developed countries, including the USA,
England, and Australia (Sellar & Lingard, 2013; Tan, 2017a). However, the current characteristics of Chinese reading instruction and
reading engagement, as well as their associations with students’ reading performance across different Chinese regions, remain
underexplored.
2.2. Reading instruction and reading performance
Instructional practices play a significant role in reading performance and educational effectiveness (Guthrie & Klauda, 2014;
Guthrie, Wigfield, & You, 2012; Hu & Wang, 2022; OECD, 2019a). Among the different theoretical frameworks of instruction, Klieme
et al. (2009) TMQT has been widely applied (e.g., Lazarides & Buchholz, 2019; Tan & Dimmock, 2022). The TMQT categorizes
classroom instruction into three dimensions: cognitive activation, classroom management and structure, and supportive climate
(Klieme et al., 2009; Praetorius, Klieme, Herbert, & Pinger, 2018). Cognitive activation refers to the teaching practices that engage
students in higher-level thinking processes through stimulation strategies and challenging questions; classroom management and
structure involve establishing clear and consistent rules, expectations, and procedures; and supportive climate—which draws on
self-determination theory—refers to supportive teacher–student interaction, constructive teacher feedback, and other caring teaching
practices (Klieme et al., 2009; Praetorius et al., 2018). Aside from the three abovementioned dimensions, some studies suggest that
perceptual learning function sets the foundation for complex reading cognition; hence, to foster fluent readers, teachers should provide
extensive practice for children to speed up the visual perceptual process until they can automatically match visual symbols with
various sounds and meanings (Abadzi, 2017; Chung, 2011). However, for most middle-school students, who have already become
fluent readers, instructional practices involving cognitive activation and supportive climate play an important role (Guthrie & Klauda,
2014; Lau, 2020; OECD, 2019a). Thus, PISA 2018 used the TMQT model to investigate 15-year-old students’ perceptions of teachers’
reading instruction. Specifically, the index of teacher stimulation is derived from the dimension of cognitive activation; the indices of

teacher support, teacher feedback, adaptation of instruction, and teacher enthusiasm are derived from the dimension of supportive
climate; and the indices of disciplinary climate and teacher-directed instruction are derived from the dimension of classroom management and structure (OECD, 2019a).
All three TMQT dimensions of instructional practices have a positive relationship with students’ learning achievement (Klieme
et al., 2009; OECD, 2019a). Teachers’ stimulation of reading engagement promotes students’ reading comprehension (Guthrie &
Klauda, 2014; Lau, 2020), and disciplinary climate has been shown to influence learning performance among Chinese and Western
students (Ho & Lau, 2018; Hu & Wang, 2022; Huang, Ruan, & Capps, 2012). Further, teachers’ support has been found to significantly
improve learning motivation and, thus, promote learning achievement in China and most OECD countries (Fu & Chen, 2015; Hu &
Wang, 2022; OECD, 2019a), while teachers’ feedback could facilitate learning improvement (Wong, Tao, & Konishi, 2018). However,
Fauth, Decristan, Rieser, Klieme, and Büttner (2014) found that none of the three dimensions of instructional practices had significant
effects on German students’ learning outcomes. Moreover, Jensen, Solheim, and Idsøe (2019) observed no relationship between
teachers’ emotional support and students’ reading achievement in Norway. Teacher-directed instruction and teacher feedback have
also been found to be negatively associated with students’ learning achievement in some circumstances (Hu & Wang, 2022; Yu, 2019).
Thus, more studies are needed to clarify the contradictory results among students from different cultural backgrounds.
2.3. Reading engagement and reading performance
Reading engagement has been broadly recognized as a core element in students’ reading development (Guthrie & Klauda, 2014;
Klauda & Guthrie, 2015; Lau & Ho, 2016; OECD, 2019a). According to reading engagement theory, reading engagement comprises
reading motivation, the use of cognitive and metacognitive strategies, prior knowledge, and social interaction (Guthrie et al., 2012).
The different dimensions of reading engagement can generally be grouped into cognitive or non-cognitive reading engagement
(OECD, 2019a). Cognitive reading engagement refers to a reader’s metacognitive and cognitive willingness and effort to use
self-regulated strategies to improve reading comprehension; non-cognitive reading engagement, meanwhile, refers to emotional responses to reading, such as feelings, motivations, beliefs, and self-concepts about reading activities (Guthrie et al., 2012; Klauda &
Guthrie, 2015; OECD, 2019a). PISA 2018 used this model to investigate students’ reading engagement (OECD, 2019a).
Students’ reading engagement has been a consistently robust predictor of reading performance (Ho & Lau, 2018; Klauda & Guthrie,
2015). Cognitive reading engagement has significant positive effects on students’ reading comprehension, particularly the metacognition of remembering, understanding, and summarizing strategies (Artelt & Schneider, 2015; Lau & Ho, 2016). The metacognition
of assessing the credibility of reading materials is becoming increasingly crucial for achieving reading comprehension goals in digital
reading (Hu & Wang, 2022; Koyuncu & Fırat, 2020). Regarding non-cognitive reading engagement, the joy of reading and students’
self-conceptions of their reading competence are robust positive predictors of reading achievement (Bagci & Unveren, 2020; Ho & Lau,
2018), whereas student-perceived reading difficulty is a negative predictor because it often causes reading avoidance (Klauda &
Guthrie, 2015). However, previous studies on reading engagement have rarely controlled for teacher-, school-, or instruction-related
factors that may potentially confound these associations (Koyuncu, & Fırat, 2020; Ma et al., 2021; Sellar & Lingard, 2013); hence,
additional well-designed studies are needed.
2.4. Reading instruction and reading engagement and their relationship with reading achievement in three Chinese societies
Chinese language differs from languages with alphabetic scripts in perceptual visual complexity because Chinese language has a
morphosyllabic writing system (Abadzi, 2017; Zhang, Xie, Xu, & Meng, 2018; Zhu et al., 2019). The visual information conveyed by
each Chinese character, a square-shaped symbol with 1–64 strokes extending from top to bottom and left to right, is much more than
that conveyed by each English letter (McBride, 2016; Tse, Marton, Ki, & Loh, 2010; Zhu et al., 2019). Therefore, to fluently read
Chinese texts, students rely heavily on memorization and practice to be automatized in mastering numerous sounds, strokes, components, configurations, and meanings related to Chinese characters (Abadzi, 2017; McBride, 2016; Tse et al., 2010).
In this context, Chinese reading instruction has long been criticized as highly teacher-centered, exam-oriented, and rote-based
(Chen, 2014; Li et al., 2013). Chinese teachers typically teach large classes through a didactic approach and emphasize discipline
over stimulating students’ reading engagement and supporting individual needs (Chen, 2014; Huang et al., 2012; Phillipson & Lam,
2011). However, since Shanghai’s excellent performance shocked the field in 2009 (Sellar & Lingard, 2013), some researchers have
challenged this negative stereotype, revealing that the pedagogies in Chinese societies have gradually moved toward more
student-centered approaches (Lingshuai & Minxuan, 2012; Tan, 2017a). Nevertheless, these studies were primarily observation-based.
To further clarify the characteristics of Chinese reading instruction and its influence on students’ reading performance, some
studies have used PISA datasets and revealed mixed results. In terms of the cognitive activation dimension, Ho and Lau (2018) found
that student-perceived teacher stimulation in Hong Kong was lower than the OECD average and had no significant relationship with
students’ reading performance. Khine, Fraser, Afari, & Liu (2023) study with B-S-J-Z students supported these findings. However, in
other recent studies, the mean score of student-perceived teacher stimulation in B-S-J-Z was higher than the OECD average and was a
significant positive predictor of students’ reading performance (Guo, Li, & Zhang, 2018; Ma et al., 2021; Qian & Lau, 2022). Regarding
the dimension of classroom management and structure, student perceptions of the disciplinary climate in China have been observed to
be more positive than the OECD average and to be a strong predictor of Chinese students’ reading performance (Guo et al., 2018; Ho &
Lau, 2018; Qian & Lau, 2022; Sellar & Lingard, 2013). However, teacher-directed instruction was negatively associated with Chinese
students’ reading performance in another study (Hu & Wang, 2022; Qian & Lau, 2022). Regarding the supportive climate dimension,
teacher support has been found to be an essential factor in Chinese students’ reading achievement (Ma et al., 2021; Sellar & Lingard,

is the large variation in confounding variables controlled for in previous studies and second is the use of students from a single Chinese
region as samples. Thus, rigidly designed studies that involve multiple Chinese regions, examining their intra-cultural differences, are
needed.
In terms of reading engagement, earlier studies tended to view Chinese students as passive rote learners and mainly attributed their
good performance to hard work (Biggs, 1996; Byun & Park 2012; Morrison, 2006; Niu & Sternberg, 2001). Recent empirical studies,
however, correspond with the mixed findings of prior research. On one hand, findings that Chinese students have substantially higher
levels of reading enjoyment than their Western counterparts refute the passive learner stereotype (Ho & Lau, 2018; Lau & Ho, 2016;
Ma et al., 2021; Qi, 2021). On the other hand, in line with earlier studies, Chinese students have been shown to have lower metacognitive awareness of effective reading strategies, lower self-concepts of their reading competence, and higher levels of perceived
reading difficulty than their Western counterparts (Gao et al., 2022; Lau, 2019; Lau & Chen, 2013), even though meta-cognition and
self-concepts of reading competence positively contributed to their reading achievement (Ho & Lau, 2018; Hu & Wang, 2022; Koyuncu
& Fırat, 2020; Lau & Ho, 2016; Ma et al., 2021; Qi, 2021). In addition, when analyzing Chinese students’ reading engagement, previous
studies have rarely considered the potential influence of instruction, teacher quality, and other school-related factors and have largely
neglected intra-cultural differences.
As there are substantial intra-cultural differences in the social, political, and education systems of the Chinese societies participating in PISA 2018 (B-S-J-Z, Hong Kong, and Taipei), studies involving the three regions are urgently needed to promote a better
understanding of the outstanding PISA reading performance of Chinese students in relatively well-developed regions from the
perspective of reading instruction and engagement (Liu, Zhou, Liu, Zhou, & McBride-Chang, 2010; McBridge-Chang, 2004; Zhang &
McBride-Chang, 2011). Owing to its specific colonial history, Hong Kong identifies with Western values more than do B-S-J-Z and
Taipei (Lau & Ho, 2016; Tse et al., 2016). Taipei has a less competitive education system than Hong Kong and B-S-J-Z owing to the
decrease in fertility rate and the rapid expansion of higher education (Chien, Lin, & Chen, 2013). In terms of educational policies,
reforms to the Chinese language curriculum in Hong Kong and Taipei emphasized the development of students’ abilities and
encouraged diversified instructional approaches (Hong Kong Curriculum Development Council [HKCDC], 2001; Ministry of Education
of Taiwan [MOETW], 2014; Tse et al., 2016), while curriculum reforms in mainland China emphasized student-centered and dialogic
instruction (Ministry of Education of People’s Republic of China [MOEPRC], 2011; Tam, 2015; Tan, 2017b). With respect to the
writing system, Hong Kong and Taiwan use traditional Chinese characters, which carry more complex visual information and require
more reading practice than the simpler Chinese characters used in mainland China (McBride, 2016; Tse et al., 2010). Some researchers
have argued that these macro-level differences might lead to distinct patterns in reading instruction and reading engagement and their
relationships with students’ reading achievement (Tse et al., 2016; Zhang & McBride-Chang, 2011). However, to the best of our
knowledge, no studies have explored how these relationships vary across B-S-J-Z, Hong Kong, and Taipei. Thus, it remains largely
unknown whether these three Chinese regions share similar or different patterns.
2.5. The present study
Against this background, the present study examined reading instruction and reading engagement and their relationships with
students’ reading performances across B-S-J-Z, Hong Kong, and Chinese Taipei, based on the PISA 2018 dataset. The study’s primary
purpose was to help international researchers and policymakers understand the universals and specifics of reading instruction and
reading engagement and their associations with students’ PISA reading performance across different relatively developed Chinese
regions. The research questions are as follows:
Research Question 1: What are the levels of reading performance, reading instruction, and reading engagement among students in
B-J-S-Z, Hong Kong, and Chinese Taipei?
Research Question 2: What are the relationships between reading instruction, reading engagement, and reading achievement in BJ-S-Z, Hong Kong, and Chinese Taipei?
3. Methods
3.1. Database and sample
We used PISA 2018 data from three Chinese regions—B-S-J-Z, Hong Kong, and Chinese Taipei—three major Chinese regions that
share the Confucian cultural heritage but have different social and educational systems. These three regions were chosen by OECD
because they are relatively developed, with good educational resources, and they can match the level of development of OECD
countries (OECD, 2019a). The PISA participants from these Chinese regions included 25,338 15-year-old 9th and 10th graders from
720 schools; 48.7% were girls (n = 12,349), and 51.3% were boys (n = 12,989). To be specific, in B-S-J-Z, the total sample included 12,
058 15-year-old students from 362 schools, with 47.9% girls and 52.1% boys. In Hong Kong, the total sample included 6037 15-year-old students from 166 schools, with 48.9% girls and 51.1% boys. In Taipei, the total sample included 7243 15-year-old students from
192 schools, with 50%girls and 50% boys.

3.2. Measures
We used data on students’ reading achievement measured by the computer-based standardized reading assessment of PISA 2018;
the indices derived from the PISA 2018 student questionnaire, including demographic details, student-perceived instructional practices, and students’ reading engagement; and the indices on school background derived from the PISA 2018 school questionnaire.
3.2.1. Reading achievement as the dependent variable
Reading achievement was represented by the reading literacy scores from the PISA 2018 computer-based reading assessment,
which included 56 texts of two types (single- and multiple-source), 245 items in varied formats (e.g., multiple-choice, closed-constructed response, open-constructed response), and 65 reading fluency sentences (OECD, 2019a). All texts and questions were
translated into Chinese (OECD, 2019a). These texts and items were primarily designed to measure three cognitive reading processes
(locating information, understanding, and evaluating and reflecting). A combined literacy scale with five aspects calculated using the
Rasch method was used to represent overall reading achievement, with a mean score of 500 points and a standard deviation of 100 for
the participating regions (OECD, 2019b).
3.2.2. Student-perceived instruction indices as predictors of reading achievement
In large-scale international assessments such as the PISA, teachers’ instructional practices are often measured by students’ perceptions of those practices (e.g., Fauth et al., 2014; Hu & Wang, 2022). Measures of students’ perceptions outperform observational
data because they represent many students’ experiences and evaluations over multiple classes instead of relying on observations made
by researchers in just a few classes (Jensen et al., 2019). Based on the aforementioned dimensions of the TMQT—cognitive activation,
classroom management and structure, and supportive climate—we selected seven independent variables on reading instruction from
the instructional indices constructed from responses to the PISA 2018 student questionnaire (OECD, 2019a).
(1) For the cognitive activation dimension, teacher stimulation (STIMREAD) was constructed based on four items on students’
perceptions of the frequency of teachers’ practices for stimulating reading engagement in Chinese classes. (2) For the supportive
climate dimension, teacher support (TEACHSUP) was constructed based on four items on students’ perceptions of the frequency of
teachers’ supportive behaviors in Chinese classes. (3) Teacher feedback (PERFEED) was based on three items on students’ perceptions
of the frequency of teachers’ feedback in Chinese classes. (4) Teachers’ adaptation of instruction (ADAPTIVITY) was based on three
items on students’ perceptions of the frequency of teachers’ adaptation of instruction in Chinese classes. (5) Teacher enthusiasm
(TEACHINT) was based on responses to four items that assessed students’ perceptions of their Chinese teachers’ enthusiasm while
teaching. (6) Lastly, for the class management and structure dimension, disciplinary climate (DISCLIMA) was constructed based on five
items asking students about the frequency of disturbing behaviors that occurred in their Chinese classes. All the DISCLIMA items were
reverse-coded before scaling. (7) Teacher-directed instruction (DIRINS) was based on four items that asked students about the frequency of teacher-directed instructional practices in their Chinese classes. Participants responded to the items of all instruction indices
on a four-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating “Never or hardly ever” and 4 indicating “Every lesson or almost every lesson.” For sample
items, please refer to Appendix 1. The Cronbach’s alpha for all indices was between 0.81 and 0.93 across the three Chinese regions (see
Table 1).
All seven indices were constructed using item response theory modeling (IRTM), with values corresponding to Warm Likelihood
estimates (WLE). The Warm Likelihood estimates were also standardized so that the mean of the index values was 0 and the standard
deviation was 1 for OECD participants (OECD, 2019c). A positive value indicated that participants of that region had a more positive
perception of a given type of instruction than the OECD average. A negative value indicated the opposite. For detailed information on

3.2.3. Reading engagement indices as predictors of reading achievement
Based on reading engagement theory and previous studies, we also selected six independent variables related to students’ cognitive
and non-cognitive reading engagement from the reading engagement indices in the PISA 2018 student questionnaire (OECD, 2019a).
For the cognitive engagement dimension, (1) the understanding and remembering (UNDREM) variable was based on six items about
students’ evaluation of the usefulness of understanding and remembering strategies for solving reading problems. (2) Metacognition of
summarizing (METASUM) was based on five items about students’ evaluation of the usefulness of summarizing strategies for solving
reading problems. (3) Metacognition of assessing credibility (METASPAM) was based on five items that asked students about the
appropriateness of methods for checking the credibility of an email. Participants responded to each item in the three indices on a sixpoint Likert scale, with 1 indicating “Not useful at all” and 6 indicating “Very useful.”
For non-cognitive engagement, (4) the joy of reading (JOYREAD) variable was based on five items on students’ attitudes toward
reading. Two negative JOYREAD items were reverse-coded before scaling. (5) Self-concept of reading competence (SCREADCOMP) was
based on three items that asked students about their reading competence. (6) Perceived reading difficulty (SCREADDIFF) was based on
three items asking students whether they encountered difficulties in reading. For sample items, please refer to Appendix 1. Participants
responded to each item of non-cognitive engagement on a four-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating “Strongly disagree,” and 4 indicating “Strongly agree.” The Cronbach’s alphas of all the reading engagement indices were between 0.77 and 0.89. The construction
methods and standardization procedures are the same as for the instruction indices. For detailed information, please see the PISA
technical report (OECD, 2020). A positive value indicated that participants of that region had a higher level of reading engagement
than the OECD average. A negative value indicated the opposite. Details of the internal reliability and mean scores for the selected
reading instruction and engagement variables are shown in Table 1.
3.2.4. Other variables as control variables
According to previous PISA studies, factors like students’ gender, socioeconomic status, school background, and teachers’ quality
are potential confounding variables that may influence students’ reading performance (e.g., Ho & Lau, 2018; Koyuncu & Fırat, 2020;
Ma et al., 2021; OECD, 2019a; Wang & Hu, 2022). Therefore, the present study included the following student- and school-level
control variables in the analysis: proportion of girls (PCGIRLS) school’s mean economic, social, and cultural status (SCHESCS), students’
individual ESCS (INDESCS) student-teacher ratio (STRATIO), proportion of certified teachers (PRATCE), proportion of teachers with a
master’s degree (PROATCE), shortage of educational materials (EDUSHORT), shortage of educational staff (STAFFSHORT).
3.3. Statistical analysis
Descriptive analysis was used to examine the characteristics of current instructional practices and reading engagement. A two-level
hierarchical linear model (HLM) using the statistical software Hierarchical Linear Modeling 6.0 was adopted to explore the relationship between instructional factors, reading engagement, and reading achievement as students were nested in schools and HLM is
effective for dealing with nested data (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). HLM analysis includes three steps. In step one, we ran a null model
with no variables to demonstrate the variation of reading achievement within and between schools. In step two, background and the

4. Results
4.1. Levels of reading achievement
The mean overall reading score for OECD countries was 487 points (OECD, 2019b). As presented in Table 2, Chinese students across
the three regions had higher reading scores on all three reading processes and the two text types than the OECD average. B-S-J-Z
students outperformed students in the other two regions in all reading processes and text types. Students in all three regions scored
slightly higher on evaluating and reflecting than on locating information. B-S-J-Z and Chinese Taipei students scored higher in reading
multiple-source texts than single-source texts. Hong Kong students scored the same on both text types.
4.2. Levels of instructional practice
As shown in Table 3, students in all three Chinese regions perceived the disciplinary climate more positively than the OECD
average. In addition, in all three regions, students’ perceptions of teachers’ stimulation of reading engagement were higher than the
OECD average and slightly higher than for teacher-directed instruction, teacher support, and teacher adaptation. B-S-J-Z students had
more positive perceptions of almost all the instructional practices than those from other regions.
4.3. Levels of reading engagement
As shown in Table 4, students across the three regions had lower-level metacognition of effective summarizing strategies than the
OECD average. In particular, students from Hong Kong and Chinese Taipei had lower levels of metacognition of understanding,
remembering, and assessing credibility than the OECD average. However, students in all three regions had substantially higher levels
of joy of reading than the OECD average. Hong Kong and Taipei students had lower self-concepts of their reading competence than the
OECD average, and B-S-J-Z and Hong Kong students perceived a slightly higher level of reading difficulty than the OECD average.
4.4. Relationships of reading instruction and reading engagement with reading achievement
As shown in Table 5, the results of the HLM analyses of Model 1 indicated that students’ perceptions of disciplinary climate and
teacher support were significant positive predictors of their reading achievement, whereas teacher-directed instruction was a significant negative predictor of reading achievement in all three Chinese regions after controlling for background variables. Teacher

5. Discussion
5.1. Chinese students’ reading performance in PISA 2018
The outstanding reading performance of students in B-S-J-Z and Hong Kong observed in the present study is consistent with the
strong performance of East Asian students identified in previous studies (Ho & Lau, 2018; Lau & Ho, 2016; Li et al., 2013; Ma et al.,
2021; OECD, 2019b; Qian & Lau, 2022). Chinese students also scored higher on reading multiple-source texts, which involves more
complex processes such as integrating ideas and handling conflicts between different pieces of information, and on evaluating and
reflecting, which are regarded as higher-level reading processes (Barzilai, Zohar, & Mor-Hagani, 2018; OECD, 2019b). Thus, our
results support some previous studies (Lau & Ho, 2016; Li et al., 2013) refuting the notion that Chinese students are better at rote-based
learning than at higher-level reading skills such as integration and evaluation (Biggs, 1996; Phillipson & Lam, 2011; Wang, 2013).
However, B-S-J-Z students’ particularly outstanding performance should be interpreted cautiously. First, B-S-J-Z are well developed regions with more resources than other parts of China, and hence these students’ outstanding achievement does not represent the
performance of all Chinese students. Second, as pointed out by some researchers, in developed municipal cities like Beijing and
Shanghai, many migrant students may be excluded from the PISA sample because of the Hukou system. Initiated in the 1950s, Hukou is
a household registration system restricting personal access to social services, such as healthcare, housing, and educational resources
(Loveless, 2014; Ma, 2020). As a population control measure, some super municipalities like Beijing and Shanghai still have strict
Hukou systems, which ensure that only students who have local Hukou, usually the ones who are born in Beijing or Shanghai, can be
admitted to local public high schools (Loveless, 2014; Ma, 2020; Xu & Dronkers, 2016). This means that most migrant students aged
14–15 need to return to their place of Hukou registration to attend academic high schools. As the learning performance and educational resources of most migrant students are often inferior compared with native students in Beijing and Shanghai (Luo & Zhang,
2017; Ma, 2020), B-S-J-Z students’ particularly high scores might be because of the selectivity of the sample.
Another factor that might account for the variance in students’ reading performance stems from the differences in traditional and
simplified Chinese characters used by the three regions. Traditional Chinese characters used by Taipei and Hong Kong students contain
approximately 22.5% more visual features than simplified Chinese characters, and thus reading in traditional Chinese is more timeconsuming and laborious (McBride, 2016), which may influence students’ reading speed (Wang, He, & Legge, 2014). However,
some researchers suggest that this decoding-level influence on reading speed diminishes as children become more proficient readers in
middle school (Cirino et al., 2013; Foorman, Wu, & Quinn, 2020). An alternative explanation for Taipei students’ relatively moderate
reading performance might be related to their relative inexperience with constructed-response items in the PISA reading assessment
because in Taiwan, Chinese reading tests and practices mainly consist of closed multiple-choice questions (Lin, Tzou, Lu, & Hung,
2021).
5.2. Chinese reading instruction and its relationship with reading performance
Reading instruction in three Chinese regions shares some characteristics. First, in the present study, Chinese students’ positive
perceptions of the disciplinary climate were consistent with the findings of numerous previous studies (Guo et al., 2018; Ho & Lau,
2018; Hu & Wang, 2022; Huang et al., 2012; Lau & Ho, 2016; Qian & Lau, 2022). Differing from the Western view that quiet learners
are passive, Chinese teachers believe a good disciplinary climate fosters effective learning because students require a silent environment to process high-level thinking and deep reflection (Huang & Asghar, 2016). Second, the present study revealed that
student-perceived teacher stimulation was more positive than the OECD average and the perceptions of teacher-directed instruction
across all three Chinese regions. These findings contradict some previous studies that have found that Chinese reading instruction
overemphasizes teacher-centered knowledge transmission and ignores stimulation of students’ reading engagement (Chen, 2014; Ho &
Lau, 2018; Phillipson & Lam, 2011; Watkins & Biggs, 2001). The reason for this inconsistency might be a recent shift in teachers’
pedagogies toward a student-centered approach across the three Chinese regions (Ding & Ye, 2021; Lingshuai & Minxuan, 2012; Qian
& Lau, 2022; Tan, 2017a; Tse et al., 2016). Among the three regions, B-S-J-Z students had more positive perceptions of almost all
instructional practices, which supported the recent findings of Qian and Lau (2022) and might be related to the influence of both
conventional double basal instruction that emphasizes clear class structuring (Shao & Jiang, 2018) and dialogic instruction highlighted
by the recent Chinese language curriculum reforms. Dialogic instruction defines reading as a process whereby students, teachers, texts,
and authors dialog with each other (Lau & Chen, 2013; MOEPRC, 2011; Phillipson & Lam, 2011) and requires teachers to stimulate
engaged reading with questioning strategies and high-quality teacher–student dialogues (Jiang, 2005).
Regarding the commonalities in the relationships between reading instruction and reading performance, first, disciplinary climate
and teacher support were robust positive predictors of students’ reading achievement in all three Chinese regions, which was
consistent with the results of many previous studies (Guo et al., 2018; Ho & Lau, 2018; Hu & Wang, 2022; Huang et al., 2012; Lau &
Ho, 2016; Qian & Lau, 2022). Second, teacher-directed instruction was found to be a negative predictor of students’ reading
achievement in all three regions. Though inconsistent with the TMQT hypothesis (Klieme et al., 2009) and a few previous studies
(McKnight et al., 2016; OECD 2019a), the results do align with those of some more recent studies (Hu & Wang, 2022; Qian & Lau
2022). PISA 2018 adopted a computer-based reading assessment, which involves dynamic features and sets high demands for students’
self-regulated learning abilities (Chen, Wang, & Chen, 2014; OEDC, 2019a). With teacher-directed instruction, however, teachers often
tend to assume a high level of control to ensure learning outcomes (Yu, 2019), which may restrict students’ need for autonomy and the
self-regulated ability to monitor multiple tasks in digital reading (Deci & Ryan, 2008); this is particularly true of secondary school
students, who desire a greater sense of ownership (Hu & Wang, 2022).

Despite these commonalities, the present study revealed some differing patterns in the associations between the variables across the
three regions. First, while the significant positive relationships between teachers’ stimulation, adaptation of instruction, and students’
reading achievement in B-S-J-Z and Hong Kong were consistent with those of numerous previous studies (e.g., Fu & Chen, 2015;
Guthrie & Klauda, 2014; Lau & Ho, 2016; McKnight et al., 2016; Qian & Lau, 2022), the relationship we observed between the three
variables for Chinese Taipei students were non-significant. One possible explanation might be that Taipei students were more influenced by teachers’ support than teachers’ stimulation or adaptation. The HLM results of the present study indicated that the influence
of teachers’ support on reading achievement was substantially stronger than that of teachers’ stimulation and adaptation for Taipei
students. Some studies also suggested that teachers’ support is one of the most important instructional approaches affecting Taipei
students’ learning motivation and achievement (Lee, Lee, & Chen Hsieh, 2022; Lei, Cui, & Chiu, 2018).
Second, opposite patterns were identified in the association between teacher feedback and reading performance across the three
regions. One possible explanation might be related to the specific feedback strategies adopted by teachers in the three regions (Hu &
Wang, 2022). In B-S-J-Z and Hong Kong, where the education systems are highly competitive and class sizes are usually large, teachers
are under intense pressure to help their students outperform others in college entrance exams; thus, many teachers’ feedback is
achievement-based and delivered in a simple and controlling way (e.g., “Right answer,” “Well done”) (Han, 2019; Liu, 2013; Salili,
Zhou, & Hoosain, 2003). According to previous studies, such feedback can be detrimental to students’ learning outcomes because it
may impair their sense of autonomy, taking away their responsibility to motivate and regulate themselves (Fisher & Frey, 2021; Wong
et al., 2018). By comparison, Taipei teachers’ feedback may not be as highly achievement-based and controlling as that of teachers in
B-S-J-Z and Hong Kong owing to Taipei’s less competitive education system (Chien et al., 2013). The curriculum reforms in Taipei
particularly emphasized shifting teacher feedback from focusing on achievement to self-regulation processes and established a series of
professional training programs to reach this goal (Ding & Ye, 2021; Ministry of Education in Taiwan [MOETW] (2014); Zhang &
Zhang, 2010). Since PISA 2018 did not collect any qualitative data, more studies are needed to further verify these potential
explanations.
5.3. Chinese students’ reading engagement and its relationship with reading achievement
Consistent with several previous studies (Ho & Lau, 2018; Lau & Chen, 2013; Lau & Ho, 2016; Qi, 2021), Chinese students in all
three regions showed common trends of having substantially higher levels of reading enjoyment and lower levels of metacognitive
awareness of the effectiveness of cognitive strategies than the OECD average. As many Chinese teachers are unfamiliar with instruction
in reading strategies, Chinese students are rarely given opportunities to control their own learning processes autonomously or practice
cognitive strategies independently (Huang et al., 2012; Lau & Ho, 2016). Hong Kong and Taipei students’ lower-level self-concepts of
their reading competence than the OECD average, as well as Hong Kong and B-S-J-Z students’ higher-level perceptions of reading
difficulty than the OECD average, supported prior findings about Chinese students (Lau, 2019; Wang, Schwab, Fenn, & Chang, 2013).
The results might be related to the lack of praise that is common in Asian classrooms (Salili et al., 2003; Watkins & Biggs, 2001) and the
emphasis on challenging reading and interpretation tasks in Hong Kong and B-S-J-Z due to curriculum reforms (HKCDC, 2001;
MOEPRC, 2011). However, B-S-J-Z students generally reported more positive reading engagement on almost all the indices than
students in Hong Kong and Taipei, which might be related to curriculum reforms in mainland China that emphasized teaching students
effective reading and critical thinking strategies (MOEPRC, 2011) and particularly required teachers to protect students’ reading
confidence by giving them sufficient praise and encouragement (MOEPRC, 2011).
The positive associations between metacognitive strategies, joy of reading, self-concept of reading competence, and reading
achievement, which were commonly demonstrated across the three Chinese regions, are consistent with previous findings (Artelt &
Schneider, 2015; Ho & Lau, 2018; Klauda & Guthrie, 2015). In the present study, among all the metacognitive strategies, assessing
credibility was the strongest positive predictor of students’ reading performance in all three regions, which might be associated with
the computer-based reading assessment adopted by PISA 2018. As digitalized information is typically more diverse, unreliable, and
conflicting than offline reading material, the ability to assess the credibility of information sources can help readers monitor their
reading process more effectively (Goldman, Braasch, Wiley, Graesser, & Brodowinska, 2012). The negative relationship that was
commonly observed between perceived reading difficulty and reading performance in the three Chinese regions also confirms some
prior findings (Artelt & Schneider, 2015; Ho & Lau, 2018; Klauda & Guthrie, 2015). Perceived reading difficulty might increase
reading avoidance and hinder students’ intrinsic motivation to read; thus, it may impair students’ reading performance in the long
term (Ho & Lau, 2018; Klauda & Guthrie, 2015).
Though there were commonalities, different patterns in the relationship between students’ self-concepts of their reading competence and their reading achievement were also found across the three regions. The finding that self-concepts of reading competence
were significant for students in Hong Kong and Taipei supports numerous previous studies that have highlighted the important
relationship between students’ perceived reading competence and their actual reading performance (Gao et al., 2022; Hu & Wang,
2022; Koyuncu & Fırat, 2020; Ma et al., 2021). The finding that self-concepts of reading competence were insignificant for B-S-J-Z
students, however, might be because of teachers’ achievement-based feedback delivered in a simple and controlling way in a highly
competitive context (Han, 2019; Liu, 2013), which might impede the development of students’ self-concepts of reading confidence and
restrain its potential effects on reading performance (Fisher & Frey, 2021; Wong et al., 2018). In future, more studies are needed to
clarify the reasons for the different patterns in the relationship between self-concepts of reading confidence and reading achievement.

6. Conclusion, limitations, and suggestions for future research
In conclusion, the present study is valuable in enhancing our understanding of which reading instruction and reading engagement
factors are significantly associated with Chinese students’ reading performance. It makes a unique contribution to the fields of reading
and PISA research by revealing how the patterns of the relationship between reading instruction and engagement factors and reading
performance vary across different Chinese regions that are relatively developed. While previous studies have investigated these relationships in other countries or a single region in China, the present study, to our knowledge, is the first to compare their patterns
across three major Chinese societies (B-S-J-Z, Hong Kong, and Taipei) based on a large-scale dataset.
This study has several important implications for educators and policymakers in these regions with regard to the formulation of
effective reading curriculum and instruction strategies. As Chinese students’ reading performance has become an important reference
for several countries like the USA, England, and Australia (Sellar & Lingard, 2013; Tan, 2017a), this study might also be informative for
policymakers and educators in these regions to determine what factors really work in promoting students’ reading performance.
Furthermore, the different patterns revealed across the three Chinese regions indicated that the relationship between teaching,
learning, and students’ learning performance is deeply intertwined with concrete cultural, social, and educational contexts. Thus,
researchers and policymakers should consider both cross- and within-cultural differences and concrete cultural and educational
contexts when examining relevant issues or developing education strategies. Finally, the results provide some practical teaching
implications on the strength of good disciplinary climate, teachers’ stimulation, appropriate feedback strategies, joy of reading, and
metacognition of assessing credibility in facilitating students’ reading performance. A less competitive education system might also be
helpful for teachers to develop appropriate feedback strategies.
Several limitations of the study and suggestions for future research are noted below. First, HLM analysis only reveals correlations,
not clear causalities; thus, future studies should adopt a longitudinal reciprocal design to examine the causal relationships between the
examined variables. Second, several factors that can influence reading achievement were not included in this study, for example,
cultural beliefs, exam systems, family support, and extramural tutoring. A qualitative longitudinal approach to investigating the
fundamental factors influencing students’ reading achievement over time would be informative. Third, although B-S-J-Z, Hong Kong,
and Chinese Taipei are three typical Chinese regions, they do not represent the whole country. Many 15-year-old migrant students with
low performance might have been excluded from the samples in B-S-J-Z owing to the Hukou system. Although many important
background factors were controlled for, we have to admit this limitation in the PISA sample.
