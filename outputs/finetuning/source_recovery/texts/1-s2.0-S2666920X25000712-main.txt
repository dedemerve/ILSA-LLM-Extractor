Given the risks and ethical concerns of integrating generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) into education,
scholars have argued for a critical-reflective education approach that addresses the long-term implications of
GenAI. However, empirical research on GenAI education approaches is scarce. This study investigates the
prevalence of protective-preventive, critical-reflective, and creative-productive GenAI education approaches in
highly digitized Swiss upper secondary schools and how students’ experiences of these approaches relate to two
aspects of their digital agency: GenAI confidence and GenAI responsibility. Using data from 2357 students, the
results showed that the critical-reflective approach was the most commonly experienced and significantly predicted students’ GenAI confidence and responsibility. The creative-productive approach positively and significantly predicted GenAI confidence but not responsibility, while the protective-preventive approach, although the
second most common approach, was not significantly related to either outcome. However, these approaches
explained little variance in the dependent variables, suggesting that they may not yet be effectively implemented
or that digital agency is primarily developed outside schools. Analysis of the control variables showed that
identifying as a female had a negative significant effect on GenAI confidence and a positive significant effect on
GenAI responsibility. The findings highlight the importance of adopting a critical-reflective GenAI education
approach with attention to gender issues in fostering responsible and confident digital citizens.

1. Introduction
The release of ChatGPT to the general public in 2022 sparked a flurry
of commentary discussing how generative artificial intelligence (GenAI)
will change classroom practice. Alongside high hopes of seeing GenAI as
a tool to better support students and teachers in their everyday teaching
and learning activities, many authors have raised concerns about issues
such as academic dishonesty, exposure to false or dangerous information, environmental impact, increasing datafication, detrimental effects
on learning, reproduction of biases, and the tendency to over-rely on this
technology (Kasneci et al., 2023; Williamson et al., 2023). Critical
scholars from different disciplines have even suggested calling large
language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT “stochastic parrots” (Bender
et al., 2021) and argued that these technologies should be considered to
produce “bullshit” (Hicks et al., 2024) to highlight their limitations and
contrast the hype and mystification around Artificial Intelligence (AI) in
various fields, including education (Nemorin et al., 2023).

Faced with the challenges and concerns surrounding the use of
ChatGPT in education, some schools and countries have initially banned
this new technology (Johnson, 2023; McCallum, 2023). To address the
risks around GenAI, experts and educational researchers have highlighted the need for a critical-reflective GenAI education approach that
considers the potential long-term impact of this technology on education
and society rather than banning its use (Damberger, 2021; Macgilchrist,
2024; Mishra et al., 2024; UNESCO, 2024; White et al., 2023). However,
research on GenAI in education so far has focused predominantly on
attitudes and perceptions toward the use of GenAI in school (Albayati,
2024; Cai et al., 2023; Chan & Hu, 2023; Farhi et al., 2023), on GenAI
actual usage (Hays et al., 2023; Süße & Kobert, 2023), on GenAI challenges and opportunities (Kasneci et al., 2023; Zhang & Tur, 2023), and
on GenAI impact on teaching and learning (Deng & Yu, 2023; Gerlich,
2025; Krstić et al., 2022; Lo et al., 2024; Rizvi et al., 2023; Vargas-Murillo et al., 2023). Furthermore, most studies focus on teachers’

2. Theory
2.1. Media literacy education approaches and GenAI
Media literacy education can be broadly defined as “the study of the
media with the aim of cultivating people’s media literacy” (Lee, 2010).
Whilst this definition may appear straightforward, it is by no means
conclusive. Indeed, there are numerous divergent definitions of media
literacy in the field of media literacy education (see Potter, 2022). One
of the most prominent definitions is that of the National Association for
Media Literacy Education (NAMLE), which states that media literacy is
“the ability to access, analyze, evaluate, create and act using all forms of
communication” (NAMLE, n.d.). In addition to this, the NAMLE has also
established 10 Core Principles of media literacy education, which could
be regarded as an extension of their definition (see NAMLE, 2023).
Media literacy education scholars distinguish between different approaches that are commonly implemented in real-world settings to
address the diverse challenges and opportunities presented by (new)
technologies (Botturi, 2019; Hobbs, 1999; Süss et al., 2018). Hobbs
(1999) for example, emphasized that media literacy education can take
many different forms by showing how different approaches diverge in
answering seven core questions, such as “Does media literacy [education] protect kids?” and “Does media literacy [education] require student production activities?”. These questions highlight the different
objectives that shape media literacy education initiatives, from preventing inappropriate use of technology to encouraging creative
expression. There are many different classifications of media literacy
education approaches. For example, Potter (2010, 2013) distinguished
three natural interventions typically used by parents with their children:
restrictive, co-viewing, and instructional. Concerning formal education,
Tulodziecki and Grafe (2012) distinguished a “protecting-supporting
concept,” an “aesthetic culture-oriented concept,” a “functional
system-oriented concept,” a “critical-materialist approach,” and lately
an “action-oriented concept” of media education that tries to empower
learners. Similarly, Botturi (2019) identified five approaches to media
literacy education: protective-preventive, critical, creative-expressive,
functional, and civic. One problem with these distinctions is that the

c) Creative-productive Approach
The creative-productive approach focuses on developing individual
practical skills and competencies to effectively use media and technologies self-directedly (Baacke, 1996; Burn, 2009; Halverson & Sheridan,
2014). Here, GenAI is mainly viewed as a tool that empowers students to
be active, constructive, and creative learners and practitioners.
These approaches—protective-preventive, critical-reflective and creative-productive—are not mutually exclusive and can be combined according to the educational context and needs (Botturi, 2019; Süss et al.,
2018). For example, depending on the learning goals teachers desire to
achieve, they may use one approach rather than another or even a combination of approaches. Other approaches to media literacy education may
also exist and may overlap, in part, with the approaches presented here.
2.2. Digital agency and GenAI

b) Critical-reflective Approach

Scholars and experts advocating for a critical-reflective approach to
GenAI (Mishra et al., 2023; Mishra et al., 2024; UNESCO, 2024; White
et al., 2023) argue that one of the goals of this approach is to promote
students’ individual and collective agency over their learning and their
digital future. For example, in the UNESCO AI competency framework
(UNESCO, 2024), the term “agency” is used 80 times. The framework
delineates four dimensions of AI competency: (a) a human-centred
mindset, (b) ethics of AI, (c) AI techniques and applications, and (d)
AI system design. The first dimension, in particular, underscores the
significance of empowering students to comprehend and assert their
agency over AI. Within this framework, affirming human agency over AI
signifies acknowledging AI as a human creation for which humans bear
responsibility and that humans – including students – can co-create and
co-control to foster a more sustainable future. This perspective is
increasingly important in a world where technologies are becoming
more pervasive, their mode of operation more opaque, and they are
developed and controlled by fewer individuals and used by the masses.
According to a literature review by Siddiq et al. (2024), the term’
agency’ is commonly used in relation to digital technologies but
under-theorized in research. The review reveals a lack of a consistent
definition, with only a few studies explicitly defining the term (Brevik
et al., 2019; Passey et al., 2018). These studies position digital agency
differently, creating challenges in linking theoretical foundations to
empirical research and distinguishing it from related terms such as
digital competence or digital literacy. For example, Brevik et al. (2019)
consider digital agency as part of digital competence, while Passey et al.
(2018) consider digital competence as part of digital agency. This
different understanding of the term may also depend on the authors’
understanding of digital competence. The literature review also highlights the importance of specifying whether digital agency is a concept
or a construct to ensure consistent empirical work.
In this exploratory study, we rely on the conceptualization of Passey
et al. (2018), who defined digital agency as “the individual’s ability to
control and adapt to a digital world,” encompassing three key
components.

The critical-reflective approach emphasizes the development of the
knowledge and competencies needed to understand how GenAI works,
to critically evaluate GenAI outputs, and GenAI’s potential long-term
impacts on society (e.g., societal, political, ethical, and environmental
implications). For example, this approach aims to expose the power
imbalances and inequalities perpetuated by these technologies, not only
in the immediate outputs they produce (e.g., reproduction of biases and
stereotypes present in the dataset on which they were trained) but also

a) Protective-Preventive Approach
The primary aim of the protective-preventive approach is to protect
students from the potential risks and harms associated with GenAI. This
includes, first and foremost, concerns about the negative impact of
GenAI on students’ agency and learning, such as fears that students will
become overly reliant on AI and thereby learn less (Kasneci et al., 2023;
Ng et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). This concern may reflect a real
challenge as a recent correlational study by Gerlich (2025) found a
negative significant correlation between the frequency of GenAI use and
critical thinking skills, mediated by increased cognitive offloading.
Other common concerns include academic dishonesty, copyright and
intellectual property issues, homogenization, the spread of misinformation, manipulation, reproduction of bias, loss of human touch, the
widening of the digital divide, and privacy and security issues (Kasneci
et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2025; UNESCO, 2023; Wang et al., 2024). In
response, this approach typically advocates restricting or prohibiting the
use of GenAI in specific educational contexts or for certain pedagogical
purposes to mitigate these risks. It is grounded in principles drawn from
child protection, consumer protection, and broader media
risk-prevention frameworks (see Livingstone & Third, 2017). UNESCO’s
recommendation to ban the use of GenAI for children under the age of 13
is an example of this approach (UNESCO, 2023). Another example is the
call by UNESCO’s Assistant Director-General for Education to slow
down, pause or even stop the integration of GenAI in education until the
necessary controls, rules or regulations are in place and we have a better
understanding of the complex impact of GenAI on learning and society
(Giannini, 2023).

Similarly as the understanding of agency encountered in the UNESCO AI

In the context of GenAI, digital agency is essential. As GenAI technologies become more integrated into different aspects of everyday life
and society, they raise questions about human autonomy and ethical
responsibility. Without digital agency, individuals risk becoming passive
recipients of technology and losing control over how it shapes their lives
and decisions, including in the political sphere (Bajohr, 2024; Damberger, 2021; Zuber & Gogoll, 2024). Therefore, fostering the development of GenAI agency in schools is also relevant to democracy and the
promotion of active citizenship.
In the context of everyday learning processes, GenAI presents challenges and opportunities for developing digital agency (i.e., the ability
to control and adapt one’s learning process to the opportunities and
challenges posed by GenAI). On the one hand, the integration of GenAI
into learning environments offers the opportunity for enhanced
personalization, enabling students to access information easily, reducing
language hurdles, and providing tailored feedback and assistance in
problem-solving tasks, thereby potentially boosting cognitive engagement, autonomy, motivation, and self-regulation (Kasneci et al., 2023;
Lo et al., 2024; Zhang & Tur, 2023). GenAI can also promote critical
thinking if used appropriately, and students are encouraged to assess
and verify GenAI responses (Zhang & Tur, 2023). However, as already
mentioned, there are significant concerns regarding overreliance on
GenAI. Scholars have highlighted that using GenAI may stifle creativity,
critical thinking, and independent learning (Gerlich, 2025; Kasneci
et al., 2023). As students increasingly depend on GenAI to generate
solutions or assist in creative tasks, their ability to develop original
ideas, connect with their imagination, and exercise control over their
learning and decision-making process might diminish, leading to passive
consumption of information rather than active engagement and autonomy (Chan & Hu, 2023; Deng & Yu, 2023; Vargas-Murillo et al., 2023;
Zhang & Tur, 2023). In addition to the opportunities and challenges for
learning, scholars have also raised concerns about issues such as privacy
and security, the spread of inaccurate information, an increase in academic dishonesty and plagiarism, the perpetuation—and reinforcement—of biases and stereotypes present in the training data, and threats
to sustainable development through increased energy consumption
(Abramski et al., 2023; Bender et al., 2021; Chan & Hu, 2023; Kasneci
et al., 2023). These considerations raise the need to adopt appropriate
GenAI education approaches and develop guidelines for integrating
GenAI in educational contexts.

3.1. Context
Switzerland’s upper secondary school landscape mainly comprises
vocational schools (attended by around two-thirds of young people) and
general or specialized baccalaureate schools (attended by around onethird of young people; Bundesamt für Statistik, 2024). Vocational education typically combines apprenticeships with school-based learning,
while general and specialized baccalaureate education focuses solely on
school-based education. All upper secondary school curricula emphasize
developing digital competencies as a transdisciplinary key goal, with
some programs including computer science as a subject. Despite the
inclusion of digital competencies in the curricula, PISA 2022 results
show that the use of digital technologies for learning purposes in Swiss
secondary schools is below the OECD average, with students using
digital technologies for less than 2 h per day at school (OECD, 2023).
Outside of school, digital technology use is prevalent among young
people, with 99 % of those aged 12 to 19 using cell phones, 96 % using
the internet, and 91 % using social networks daily or several times a
week (Külling et al., 2022). The OECD Truth Quest survey (OECD, 2024)
found that the skills of the overall Swiss population in identifying AI and
human-generated misinformation are well below the average of OECD
countries.

2.4. Research questions
Given the lack of research on GenAI media literacy education approaches and their relationship with students’ GenAI-related digital
agency, this exploratory study aims to answer the following research
questions.
a) What is the prevalence of different GenAI media literacy education
approaches in high-tech upper secondary schools?
b) How do different GenAI media literacy education approaches predict
students’ beliefs regarding GenAI responsibility and confidence?
Due to the lack of empirical studies on this topic, it is challenging to
formulate hypotheses (especially regarding the prediction of GenAI responsibility). However, considering the studies on the relationship between the general frequency of technology use and students’ digital selfefficacy, we expect that approaches involving the active use of GenAI in
the classroom (i.e., the creative-productive and the critical-reflective
approaches) may have a significant and positive impact on the prediction of GenAI confidence.

Data were anonymously collected through an online survey on the
Unipark platform between the end of November 2023 and March 2024
in 15 upper secondary schools (five vocational schools, five general
baccalaureate schools, and five specialized baccalaureate schools) in the
German-speaking part of Switzerland. The schools were selected from a

German-speaking part of Switzerland. School leaders were contacted via
email and asked to forward a survey hosted on the Unipark platform to
all students enrolled in the penultimate year of their educational programs. The participation of the students was voluntary.

3.3. Measures
3.3.1. GenAI confidence
We developed a short four-item scale to measure self-assessed GenAI
confidence (see Table 1). The items addressed students’ confidence
regarding two essential areas of digital literacy (Johnson, 2015):
knowing how digital technologies work and being able to use them
appropriately and effectively across different contexts, with a stronger
focus on the latter. The response format was a five-point Likert scale
ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). An exploratory
factor analysis (EFA) with oblimin rotation provided evidence for the
unidimensionality of the scale, and a CFA with a single factor showed a
good fit to the data (except for the upper limit of the RMSEA, which
suggested some caution): χ2 (1) = 7.44, p < .001, CFI = 0.997; TLI =
0.98, RMSEA = 0.06, 90 % CI [0.02, 0.1], SRMR = 0.01. All standardized factor loadings were significant and ranged from 0.72 to 0.97,
indicating that the items were a good indicator of the latent construct. In
the reliability analysis, the scale showed respectable reliability (see
Table 1). Furthermore, the analysis indicated that dropping any item
would decrease the reliability of the scale (additional details are provided in Table A1 in the Appendix).

3.3.4. Students background variables
In this study, several background variables were included to account
for their potential influence on the results. We assessed gender by asking
students to identify themselves as male, female, or other. This variable
was transformed into two dummy variables, with “male” as the reference category. The language spoken at home was measured by asking
the students to indicate the primary language spoken in their household.
This variable was dichotomized, with 1 indicating German or Swiss
German and 0 indicating any other language. The type of schooling
distinguishes between general baccalaureate, specialized baccalaureate,
and vocational school. Two dummy variables were created for the
analysis, with general baccalaureate as the reference category. Finally,
parental education was taken into account for both parents. A higher
level of education indicated that the parent had attended a universitylevel education, a low level of education referred to parents who had
only received basic vocational training or compulsory schooling, and a
middle level of education referred to everything in between. Dummy
variables were created for the analysis, with low parental education as
the reference category.

3.3.2. Gen AI responsibility
A short three-item scale was developed to measure students’ beliefs
about GenAI responsibility (see Table 2). Students were asked to select a
response on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree)
to 5 (strongly agree). An EFA with oblimin rotation provided support for
our hypothesized unidimensionality of the scale. We also conducted a
CFA, which showed that the standardized factor loadings ranged from
0.48 to 0.85 and were all statistically significant. Other goodness of fit
indices cannot be reported, as the scale is just identified. The scale also
showed acceptable, although not optimal, reliability (see Table 2),
which would not increase if any items were dropped (additional details
are provided in Table A2 in the Appendix).

3.4. Multilevel multiple regression analysis
Due to the nested structure of the data (Schielzeth & Nakagawa,
2013), with students grouped within classes and schools, we adopted a
multilevel multiple regression approach. As only 15 schools were
considered, and a minimum of 50 groups is recommended for unbiased
estimates in multilevel regression analysis (Maas & Hox, 2005), we
chose to use school class as the only grouping variable. As a result, the
variance attributable to both the school and class levels is combined at
the class level. Given that we cannot separate these two sources of
variance and that the primary focus of this study is at the individual
student level, we applied a grand mean centering procedure. Finally, the
estimates were z-transformed to facilitate comparisons between the
different predictors. This approach allows us to examine contextual and
individual effects simultaneously (Enders & Tofighi, 2007; Paccagnella,
2006; Wu & Wooldridge, 2005) This method is very similar to a simple
multiple regression analysis, but it also allows the intercepts to vary by
class. We expect the variance to vary considerably, especially in vocational schools, where we find, for example, classes of hairdresser, electricians, and IT specialists.

4.1. Descriptive statistics

experience of different GenAI education approaches (see Table 5)
showed that the most popular approach was the critical-reflective (M =

SD = 0.95; mode = 1). However, these scores also indicated that all

the standard deviations indicated moderate variability in the responses.
Regarding GenAI confidence (see Table 5), the mean of the scale (M

positive confidence in knowing how GenAI worked and applying it to
different contexts. With regard to GenAI responsibility (see Table 5), the
results (M = 3.32; SD = 0.97) showed that students also had slightly
positive beliefs about their responsibility for using GenAI. In both cases,
the standard deviation indicated moderate variability in the responses.

For each dependent variable (GenAI confidence and GenAI responsibility), three models were tested.
a) Model 0: This baseline model, which included no predictors, was
utilized to calculate the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). The
ICC is indicative of the proportion of variance that can be attributed
to class membership. Moreover, it served as a benchmark for evaluating the additional explanatory power of subsequent models.
b) Model 1: This model incorporated only background variables, such
as gender identity, home language, type of schooling, and parental
education level, to assess their predictive power on the dependent
variables.
c) Model 2: The final model included the background variables from
Model 1, along with GenAI education variables, to evaluate the
combined influence of these predictors on GenAI responsibility and
self-assessed GenAI confidence.
We calculated two R2 measures. Marginal R2 was calculated to estimate the variance explained by the fixed factors, while conditional R2
captured the total variance explained by both fixed and random factors
(Nakagawa & Schielzeth, 2013). The comparison of R2 values between
Models 1 and 2 highlighted the additional variance in the dependent
variables explained solely by the media school-based activity variables.
We also calculated likelihood ratio model testing to assess whether
Model 2 fit the data significantly better than the simpler Model 1 (Peugh,
2010).

should include a more varied range of schools, including those with

Additionally, some measures used in the study (e.g., GenAI confidence) were self-reported. Although self-reports are commonly used in
educational research, they introduce potential biases, such as social
desirability, response styles, or recall inaccuracies. Future studies should
consider combining self-reported measures with more objective data,
such as teacher evaluations, to reduce the risk of these biases. Furthermore, in the multilevel analysis conducted, the school and class levels
were collapsed into a single level due to sample size constraints. Due to
this problem, this study was unable to distinguish between the variance
explained by the class level and the variance explained by the school
level. Future studies should disentangle these levels by increasing the
number of participating schools and classes, allowing for a more detailed
multilevel analysis. Other limitations of this study are that the reliability
of some of the scales, although acceptable, was not optimal and that
asking students to estimate the proportion of teachers using each GenAI
education approach (rather than rating a specific teacher) may have
resulted in a tendency toward middle values. Future research should aim
to refine, extend, and adapt the scales developed in this study to improve
them.
Finally, the study is based on cross-sectional data, meaning we can
infer only associations rather than causality. To better understand the
causal mechanisms behind the observed relationships, future research
should adopt longitudinal designs, which can track changes over time,
or experimental designs, which can manipulate key variables to test
causal hypotheses.

4.2. Multilevel multiple regression analyses
Hierarchical multilevel regression results (see Tables 7 and 8)
showed that experiencing a critical-reflective approach to GenAI education positively and significantly influenced students’ GenAI confidence (β = 0.11, p < .001) and GenAI responsibility (β = 0.16, p < .001),
while experiencing a creative-productive approach significantly and
positively predicted GenAI confidence (β = 0.08, p < .01). By contrast,
adopting a protective-preventive approach did not significantly predict
the variance in either GenAI confidence or responsibility. The ICC for
GenAI confidence was 0.07, suggesting that about 7 % of the variance in
this variable could be explained by aspects related to the class or school
level (this may include student or teacher factors). In the case of GenAI
responsibility, the ICC was 0.05, meaning that about 5 % of the variance
could be explained by factors related to the school or class level. Analysis
of the control variables revealed that identifying as “female” significantly affected both dependent variables: GenAI confidence in a negative way (β = − 0.24, p < .001) and GenAI responsibility in a positive
way (β = 0.09, p < .01). Introducing the GenAI education variables into
the model with the background variables (Model 1) increased the R2
marginal from 0.06 to 0.09 in the case of GenAI confidence and from
0.02 to 0.05 in the case of GenAI responsibility. R2 conditional increased
from 0.11 to 0.13 in the case of GenAI confidence and from 0.10 to 0.11
in the case of GenAI responsibility. Despite the minimal increase in
explained variance between Models 1 and 2, Model 2 fit the data
significantly better in both cases than the simpler Model 1 (p > .001).
Examination of the quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots and residual histograms revealed no significant problems in any of the cases.

6. Discussion
The aim of this study was to investigate two key aspects: (a) the
prevalence of three different media literacy education approaches to
GenAI in high-tech schools in the German-speaking part of Switzerland
and (b) how the experience of these approaches predicts students’ GenAI
confidence and responsibility, controlling for relevant students’ background variables. In this concluding chapter, we discuss how the results
of our study contribute to answering our research questions.
a) What is the prevalence of different GenAI media literacy education
approaches in high-tech upper secondary schools?
The results of the descriptive statistics revealed that GenAI education
in school is relatively uncommon and that the critical-reflective
approach is the most common GenAI education approach adopted by
teachers (M = 2.74; SD = 1.08), followed by the protective-preventive
approach (M = 2.41; SD = 1) and, lastly, the creative-productive
approach (M = 2.06; SD = 0.95). The dominance of the criticalreflective approach aligns with the recommendations of scholars and
experts, who advocate for fostering students’ critical engagement with
GenAI, especially in light of its ethical and societal implications
(Damberger, 2021; Mishra et al., 2024; UNESCO, 2023; UNESCO, 2024;
White et al., 2023). This finding is encouraging, but further research is
needed to understand whether teachers adopt a critical-reflective
approach because they are highly aware of its limitations and potential long-term societal and ethical implications (see Mishra et al., 2024)
or for other reasons.
The widespread adoption of the protective-preventive approach
raises concerns as it did not predict any of the outcome variables in our
study. However, this is not surprising as the first reaction to the release
of a new technology is often to adopt a protective-preventive approach,

5. Limitations
While this explorative study provides valuable insights into the
prevalence of different GenAI media literacy education approaches in
everyday educational contexts and how these experienced approaches
relate to students’ GenAI confidence and responsibility, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the present research focuses on two

(2018). Future research should also consider GenAI competence. To
assess this construct, a direct measurement approach, such as
performance-based tests, should be adopted. Existing research indicates
that students, particularly boys, tend to overestimate or underestimate
their digital competencies (Porat et al., 2018; Siddiq & Scherer, 2019),
highlighting the need for objective measures beyond self-reports.

Giannini, 2023). Finally, the low prevalence of the creative-productive
approach could reflect that GenAI has not yet been fully integrated
into everyday school activities and remains an occasional activity for

Regarding the prevalence of different approaches, it is also interesting to note that the critical-reflective approach correlates moderately
with the other two approaches, while the protective-protective approach
correlates minimally with the creative-productive approach. It is
conceivable that this pattern of correlations will change over time and
with the progressive integration of GenAI into educational practices.
Future research could explore these changes. Furthermore, as the
different approaches can be combined (Botturi, 2019; Süss et al., 2018)
and students may experience various combinations of approaches

depending on their teachers and school contexts, future research could
also examine which different combinations of approaches students
experience using methods such as latent profile analysis.
b) How do different GenAI media literacy education approaches predict
students’ beliefs regarding GenAI responsibility and confidence?
The results of the multilevel hierarchical regression analysis indicate
that experiencing a critical-reflective approach to GenAI education is a
significant predictor of both GenAI confidence (β = 0.11, p < .001) and
GenAI responsibility (β = 0.16, p < .001). By contrast, the creativeproductive approach predicted only GenAI confidence (β = 0.08, p <
.01), while the protective-preventive approach did not significantly
predict any outcome. These findings suggest that the critical-reflective
approach is the most versatile and effective in fostering both GenAI
confidence and responsibility, providing empirical support for the recommendations of experts in the field (Damberger, 2021; UNESCO, 2024;
UNESCO, 2023; White et al., 2023; Mishra et al., 2024). These results are
consistent with other findings, highlighting how the use of GenAI contributes to developing both GenAI skills and the understanding of ethical
issues (Kajiwara & Kawabata, 2024; Farhi et al., 2023; Sullivan et al.,
2024). If these aspects are to be promoted, it is therefore essential to
ensure that students have the opportunity to actively use GenAI, at least
in certain contexts and for specific learning goals.

deeper understanding of how GenAI functions and fosters more
responsible, autonomous, critical, and competent GenAI use in realworld contexts, which inherently require awareness of the sociocultural implications of these technologies and thus demand competencies that go beyond purely technical skills (Chiu, 2024; Kajiwara &
Kawabata, 2024; Pinski & Benlian, 2024).
Notably, the GenAI education approaches predicted only a tiny—almost negligible—portion of the variance in the outcome variables.
Therefore, researchers should be cautious in drawing theoretical or
practical implications from this exploratory study. This lower amount of
explained variance could be due to schools’ limited influence on
fostering GenAI agency (which may be acquired outside of school) or the
possibility that teachers’ current GenAI educational practices are not yet
sufficiently consolidated and effective, possibly due to a lack of guidance
from schools and institutions and a lack of GenAI competence and
experience among teachers (Kohnke et al., 2025; Süße & Kobert, 2023;
Wu et al., 2024). Additionally, our operationalization of the variables
may have influenced this result. Specifically, the scale used to assess
students’ experiences of GenAI education approaches required them to
evaluate all of their teachers simultaneously, which may have led to a
tendency toward middle values and reduced explained variance in the
dependent variables. Nevertheless, despite the small amount of
explained variance, both hierarchical multilevel regression analyses
showed a significant increase in explained variance from Model 1 to
Model 2, underscoring the relevance of examining GenAI education
approaches in future studies (see also Consoli, 2024). Future research
should aim to try different operationalizations of the constructs included

in this study and include a wider range of variables to see if it has an
impact on the amount of explained variance.
The gender differences observed in this study, with gender exerting a
negative impact on GenAI confidence (β = − 0.24, p < .001) but positively influencing GenAI responsibility (β = 0.09, p < .001), suggest that
boys may be overconfident in their abilities, while girls may approach
GenAI with a heightened sense of responsibility. These findings are
consistent with those of Zhong and Liu (2024), who investigated AI
literacy in secondary school students and found that boys had significantly higher AI knowledge (measured on a self-reported scale) than
girls, while girls had significantly higher AI affectivity (a construct that
also includes aspects such as ethical awareness and responsibility) than
boys. Furthermore, they reflect patterns seen in the broader research on
the gender digital divide (Perifanou & Economides, 2020; Siddiq &
Scherer, 2019) and gender stereotypes in education (Kollmayer et al.,
2018). Although girls tend to outperform boys in performance-based
digital competence tests (Siddiq & Scherer, 2019), they underestimate
their digital literacy. Future research should explore these gender dynamics in more depth.
Finally, the fact that the combined class and school levels explained
7 % of the variance in GenAI confidence and 5 % in GenAI responsibility
points to the need for further investigation into factors at these levels.
These could include teacher characteristics, class characteristics, or
curriculum-related factors that shape students’ experiences and outcomes. Understanding the influence of these factors will be crucial for
developing effective GenAI education strategies in the future.

While this exploratory study provides initial insights into the prevalence of three GenAI media literacy education approaches in highdigitized Swiss upper secondary schools and their impact on two
distinct aspects of GenAI agency, further research is needed to draw
practical or theoretical implications and better understand which factors
may influence the success of GenAI education. By expanding on these
findings, future studies can contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of how to best prepare students for the challenges and opportunities GenAI presents.
