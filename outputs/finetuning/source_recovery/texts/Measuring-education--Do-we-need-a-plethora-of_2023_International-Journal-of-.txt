Measuring education: Do we need a plethora of assessment studies or just a 
single score?

A B S T R A C T  
The major international large-scale assessments in education prioritize the three core domains such as mathe­
matics, science, and reading, neglecting other important domains such as civic education or computer and in­
formation literacy. The present study aims at determining the added value of different international large-scale 
assessments to evaluating educational systems across the globe. Using country-level data from all recent inter­
national assessments by the IEA and the OECD, we conducted a series of correlational analyses and multidi­
mensional scaling to determine the similarities and differences among educational assessments. The findings of 
this study show that the various measures of achievement correlate to different degrees at country level. While 
mathematics, science, and reading are the most closely correlated, the correlations with civic education and 
computer and information literacy are considerably lower. We conclude that subject domains as civic education 
and computer and information literacy add a significant value to the assessment of education.   
Introduction 
The most common approach to assess the quality of education is by 
measuring the knowledge and skills that students gained at school 
through standardized testing. In this regard, international large-scale 
assessments (ILSAs) provide indicators of educational quality and effi­
ciently measure the educational systems in different countries. The first 
international assessments in education were administered in 1964 with 
then launch of the IEA’s First International Mathematics Study (OECD, 
2015), but the rise of ILSAs started since the mid-1990s (Addey et al., 
2017), and since then, the number of studies, their frequency, and the 
number of participating countries have considerably increased. 
Although the purpose of ILSAs which is to assess the scholastic per­
formance of students is certainly worthy, there are particular concerns 
associated with the existing ILSAs. Thus, Emler et al. (2019) pointed out 
that the high cost is among the significant side effects of educational 
large-scale assessments since they require considerable finances to 
design, implement, and analyze the test studies. Countries not only have 
to cover the participation fees but also the costs to administer the sur­
veys in the respective countries. Such high cost of ILSAs might be a 
burden for countries and their citizens as these expenses are covered by 
taxpayers. 
Non-monetary costs for participation in ILSAs refer to the technical 
administration of testing and might present certain challenges for 
schools. Participation in large-scale assessments require selected schools 
to dedicate efforts for preparation and actual testing, including 
communication with ILSA staff, arrangement of pre-assessment visits to 
schools, and so on. On one hand, such involvement of schools can be 
characterized as capacity building process where school representatives 
acquire new competencies to administer testing. On the other hand, this 
experience can be time-consuming for teachers and school leaders as 
well as might divert their attention from other important duties to be 
performed at school. Furthermore, ILSAs are criticized for putting un­
necessary pressure on students and teachers, distracting them from 
learning the curriculum and rather forcing them to prepare specifically 
for the international assessments. 
Even though ILSAs provide valuable information, they are also 
associated with considerable costs. This cost-benefit tension related to 
the implementation and use of ILSAs leads to the questions about the 
necessity of administering a plethora of assessment studies in education. 
Do different ISLAs provide similar or disparate inferences about the 
educational systems across the globe? Should we focus on measuring a 
few subject domains such as mathematics, science, and reading or 
should we consider including other domains in ILSAs in order to obtain a

comprehensive picture of the education system? To answer these ques­
tions, this study aims at examining the correlations between various 
ILSAs and analysing their convergence and divergence. 
Some may argue that it is sufficient to study only one subject domain 
(e.g., mathematics) to evaluate educational policy as several recent 
studies revealed that student performance tends to be similar across 
different domains (e.g., Wendt & Kasper, 2016; Bergold et al., 2017). 
Other scholars also argue that the country scores of different ILSAs are 
typically quite similar (Klieme, 2016; Rindermann, 2007) which chal­
lenges the idea that different ILSAs provide added value to inform policy 
and research. In the scientific literature, there is evidence that certain 
assessment studies, such as PISA in mathematics and TIMSS 8th Grade, 
show high similarity (Klieme, 2016); however, there is little evidence if 
the results of ILSAs in core domains (mathematics, science, and reading) 
and non-core domains (e.g., civic education, computer sciences, etc.) 
demonstrate substantial differences. If it turns out that there are 
considerable differences between test results in core and non-core do­
mains, this may mean that some large-scale assessments are stronger 
correlated than others. 
The overarching goal of assessment is to build a comprehensive 
picture of educational system which can be achieved by measuring 
multiple aspects of the system. Measuring the same domains or domains 
that are highly correlated is less likely to provide an added value. In this 
regard, a reasonable approach to address this issue could be to integrate 
the data from various assessments that measure different subjects, 
grades, and competences. This approach might provide valuable infor­
mation about the educational progress and better guide researchers and 
policymakers in developing educational systems. The question remains 
about what aspects of the educational system can be tested in a way that 
it contributes to the further knowledge about education. 
What and how do different ILSAs measure? 
There are various international assessments in education that are 
conducted in specific regions and worldwide. Among the regional large- 
scale assessments, the most prominent ones are Latin American Regional 
Comparative and Explanatory Study (ERCE, for its Spanish name) and 
Southern African Consortium for Monitoring Educational Quality 
(SACMEQ) that focus on designing the achievement assessments in 
accordance with the regional educational context. As for the worldwide 
assessments, these are the international large-scale assessments (ILSAs) 
coordinated by the International Association for the Evaluation of 
Educational Achievement (IEA) and organization for Economic Co- 
operation and Development (OECD) that gained momentum in educa­
tional communities. Therefore, for the sake of simplicity, the focus of the 
present study is on the ILSAs which have been coordinated by IEA and 
OECD. These international assessments measure academic achievements 
of students and thus reveal cross-country variations as well as unique 
features of educational systems pertaining to a specific country 
(Hanushek & Woessmann, 2011). Moreover, the IEA- and OECD-led 
assessments provide representative samples of students and schools 
and in this way can capture different factors impacting the inputs, 
processes, and outputs of educational systems (Gustafsson, 2008). 
Interestingly, the early IEA studies covered a wide range of domains 
to be tests in a single study which shows that education was viewed as an 
aggregation of multiple characteristics and measures which should be 
analysed in toto in order to evaluate the educational quality and prog­
ress. For example, the Six Subject Survey (1967–1971) comprised 
assessment in science, reading comprehension, literature, English as a 
foreign language, French as a foreign language, and civic education. 
At present, the IEA ongoing studies, including the flagship TIMSS 
(Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study) and PIRLS 
(Progress in International Reading Literacy Study), are more focused on 
a narrow scope of thematic assessment. CIVED (Civic Education Study) 
represented a novel domain to test–civic education–and was conducted 
by IEA in 1999–2000 among students to assess their knowledge and 
understanding of democracy, citizenship, diversity, and social cohesion. 
The recent innovations in ILSAs introduced by IEA are the International 
Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS) and the International 
Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS) that were first con­
ducted in 2009 and 2013 respectively. However, the number of coun­
tries participating in ICILS and ICCS is relatively small at least in 
comparison to the number of participating countries in TIMSS and 
PIRLS. 
OECD has been spearheading the renowned programme for Inter­
national Student Assessment (PISA) since 2000 to evaluate the scholastic 
performance of 15-year-old students worldwide. PISA primarily mea­
sures cognitive skills in three major subject domains: mathematics, 
science, and reading. Different cycles of PISA also included additional 
domains for assessment such as financial literacy, collaborative 
problem-solving, global competence. However, the additional domains 
in PISA are not obligatory for all participating countries, and typically 
the number of countries that have undertaken the assessment in addi­
tional subject realms is small compared to the full sample of countries in 
PISA. 
Table 1 presents an overview of the ILSAs conducted since 1995 till 
2019, featuring the coordinating organization, target population, num­
ber of participating countries, and subject domains that were tested. In 
certain cases, the number of participating countries varies across tested 
domains within the same study and cycle. As can be seen from Table 1, 
the majority of ILSAs have been conducted in the areas of mathematics, 
reading, and science, while other domains such as civic education or 
information literacy are tested less frequently and with fewer partici­
pating countries. 
A possible explanation for the strong emphasis on dominant subjects 
among assessment studies can refer to the idea that the three domains 
(mathematics, science, and reading) are the strong predictors of labor 
force quality (e.g., Chetty et al., 2011; Dougherty, 2003; Heckman et al., 
2006). This has prompted to use the academic achievement in these 
three domains as the primary measures in assessment studies (e.g., 
Watts, 2020; Chetty et al., 2011; Curto & Fryer, 2014; Deming, 2009; 
Loeb et al., 2007). However, Chen and Luoh (2010) challenged this idea 
based on their results of cross-country growth study which demon­
strated that test scores in mathematics and science from PISA and TIMSS 
do not exhibit strong link to cross-country income differences. 
Although the test items in PISA do not exclude curriculum-related 
aspects, they are rather based on the concept of competency and thus 
measure functional skills and the extent to which students are prepared 
to participate in the modern society (OECD, 2003). On the other hand, 
the distinct feature of many assessment studies administered by IEA is 
that they are designed in accordance with the official curriculum of 
participating countries (Wagner & Hastedt, 2022). The IEA have also 
conducted ILSAs that are not curriculum-based such as Pre-Primary 
Project, ICCS, and ICILS. Moreover, major ISLAs of both OECD and 
IEA encompass such domains as mathematics, science, and reading and 
largely focus on the population of approximately the same age group 
(for example, 15-year-olds in PISA and eighth-grade students in TIMSS, 
ICILS, and ICCS). Moreover, considering that OECD and IEA also intend 
to measure change in student achievement as well as country’s educa­
tional environment in the long term, their ILSAs contain constructs and 
frameworks that tend to remain comparable across cycles within the 
programmes. Such a design of ILSAs allows for a longitudinal analysis to 
evaluate the changes in the context factors at both individual and school 
levels (Jude & Kuger, 2018). 
The existing ILSAs test different subject areas, and the plurality of 
domains to be tested leads to the question of what learning outcomes are 
considered relevant and necessary for evaluating the effectiveness and 
efficiency of educational systems across countries over time. To under­
stand this issue, we suggest examining various perspectives on education 
and the factors that shape student performance in ILSAs.

Theoretical perspectives on education 
The existence of a variety of student tests leads to the questions about 
the necessity of administering a plethora of assessment studies in edu­
cation. Do different ISLAs provide similar or disparate inferences about 
the educational systems across the globe? One of the approaches to 
answer this question is to compare the country scores from different 
assessments. If countries perform similarly across the assessments, pre­
sumably there is little reason to participate in different assessments, and 
a single score in one domain could suffice to draw conclusions about the 
whole educational system of a country. Nevertheless, if countries 
perform differently on different assessments, then the more data we 
have from different assessments and subject domains, the more com­
plete information about the educational systems we have. In that case, 
the question would be about which assessments and subject domains 
add more value to understanding the progress of educational systems. 
There are several theoretical frameworks from economics, psychol­
ogy, and sociology that could interpret the existing differences in test 
performance among students participating in ILSAs. This issue can be 
examined from the psychological perspective acknowledging the 
dominant role of the general intelligence in student achievement. As 
some proponents of this theory claim, different ILSAs mostly measure 
general intelligence, and this measuring approach largely contributes to 
similarity among different assessment studies. Given the effects of 
educational tracking on students’ academic achievements, the social 
stratification viewpoint claims that the differences in test performance 
are due to the structure of school systems. Economic development 
argument sees the financial gap between countries as the major factor 
for unequal scores among participating countries. 
Student general ability framing the scores in ILSAs 
The general ability approach in ILSAs has traditionally been associ­
ated with cognitive psychology, specifically with the theory of general 
intelligence or ability (g-factor). It posits that students who perform well 
in one subject domain are more likely to achieve high scores in other 
subject domains. Rindermann (2007) claimed that ILSAs mostly mea­
sure the g-factor of students rather than their specific achievements in 
different domains. This explains the strong correlation between the 
performance of students in different subject domains in ILSAs. Based on 
these findings, Rindermann suggested that it is more appropriate to 
work with a sum values of different scales and studies in order to 
measure the quality and progress of education: 
Research done at the national level about the effectiveness of edu­
cation or characteristics of societies (economic growth, wealth, de­
mocracy, liberty, peace, health, etc.) should be done with sum values for 
a large sample of countries. The use of single scales or single studies is 
scientifically inappropriate. (p. 693) 
However, it is crucial to acknowledge the need for a nuanced un­
derstanding of the associations between general ability and performance 
at both the individual and group levels (e.g., H¨arnqvist et al., 1994). At 
the country level, the focus is on the relationship between the overall 
performance of a country in ILSAs and various factors, such as socio­
economic indicators, educational policies, or cultural influences. Such 
examination seeks to understand the broader educational landscape and 
identify factors that contribute to country-level performance 
differences. 
On the other hand, studies focusing on the student level within a 
country explore the relationship between individual students’ general 
ability and their performance in specific subject domains. These ana­
lyses aim to examine the extent to which general ability factors 
contribute to students’ achievement within a specific educational 
context. 
Therefore, it is imperative to differentiate between the associations 
observed at the macro level (country-level) and micro level (student- 
level) when discussing the influence of general ability on educational 
outcomes in ILSAs. By recognizing this distinction, researchers can 
provide a more nuanced understanding of the complex interplay be­
tween general ability, subject-specific achievements, and broader 
educational factors at different levels of analysis. 
Social stratification in education 
Sociological theory proposes to consider differences in school 
structures when analysing differences in test results in international 
educational assessments (LeTendre et al., 2003; Kerckhoff, 2001). In 
countries with early tracking system, different curricula exist for 
different school tracks: Vocationally oriented students focus on learning 
the core domains such as reading, mathematics, and science, whereas 
academically oriented tracks cover a broader range of subjects. In 
contrast, countries with late tracking system offer the same curricula for 
all students despite their school track. These differences in schooling are 
expected to affect the test scores of students and eventually the country 
rankings in ILSAs. 
Economic development and educational production function argument 
Economic perspective regards student competencies or achievement 
as outputs of investments into school system (Hanushek, 1987). By 
increasing the resources invested in the school system, it is expected the 
student competencies can be enhanced which would lead to a better test 
performance of students in ILSAs. Therefore, a thorough analysis of test 
performance can reveal the preparedness and quality of the school 
systems worldwide (OECD, 2013). Countries with higher gross domestic 
product (GDP) per capita tend to perform higher in ILSAs (e.g., OECD, 
2019) which supports the economic development argument claiming 
that the general level of resources allocated to support students have a 
direct impact on their scholar performance. Thus, countries with higher 
national incomes can allocate more funding to education sector, and this 
could explain their better performance in ILSAs in comparison with 
low-income countries. 
Nevertheless, limited resources argument suggests that even in a 
group of similarly developed countries, there may be a variation in how 
each country spends the budget on education, either prioritizing a spe­
cific subject domain (e.g., mathematics) or a group of subjects (e.g., hard 
sciences). This may mean that students from developed countries might 
differ in their test performance depending on the investment priorities of 
their governments in the education sector. 
Curricula around the world 
The curriculum argument incorporates the above-mentioned theo­
retical perspectives: the curriculum is a reflection of the country’s spe­
cific context, economic growth, and educational traditions which shape 
the student development and consequently their academic performance. 
National curricula may capture different things which would result in 
differences in the scores among countries in ILSAs. 
The sine qua non for any ILSA is the comparability of the subjects to 
be measured across the participating countries. In this regard, the study 
by Benavot et al. (1991) provides a solid rationale for studying com­
petencies internationally. Benavot et al. examined the educational 
curricula in mass school systems across countries and over an extensive 
period of time. The researchers analyzed the content of different na­
tional curricula and classified their subjects into general categories such 
as language, mathematics, natural sciences, social sciences, religious 
and moral education, esthetic education, physical education, health 
education, vocational education/practical subjects, and other. Benavot 
et al. (1991) asserted that, despite national variations in economic or 
political structures, the range of subjects that are taught in primary and 
secondary schools is virtually identical throughout the world. Based on 
the results of their study, Benavot et al. contented that the national 
school systems are shaped by the standardized models of mass

Reflecting on the balance between the subjects in the curricula, 
Meyer et al. (2017) stressed that the consistent trade-off can be seen not 
between language and mathematics but rather between language and 
science, arts, physical, and vocational education. This implies that 
certain countries give priority to language instruction, whereas other 
countries, on the contrary, put greater emphasis on a range of subjects 
other than language. Meyer et al. (2017) also reported that the modern 
European educational system has switched from the traditional 
language-laden curriculum to the curriculum oriented towards science, 
vocational training, and arts. However, Cha (2007) also made a 
comparative data analysis of the school content and suggested that 
foreign language instruction, namely English, has become an institu­
tionalized subject at schools across the globe and an important locus in 
many national curricula. 
Interconnectedness of different ILSAs 
Much of the research in the realm of educational assessment is 
focused on the inter- and intra-dimensionality of test studies. With the 
rise of international educational assessment, there have been also a 
number of studies that compared different ILSAs. 
Previous research has revealed high intercorrelations among 
different achievement tests. For instance, Bergold et al. (2017) noted 
that analyses of the PISA test scores in mathematics, reading, and sci­
ence showed a strong intercorrelation at country level from r = 0.75 to r 
= 0.81 (Reilly, 2012) and even higher from r = 0.95 to r = 0.99 (Rin­
dermann, 2007). TIMSS and PIRLS data from Grade 4 demonstrated 
lower but still significant correlations at student level in the range be­
tween r = 0.54 and r = 0.74 (Bos et al., 2012). It is important to note that 
correlations among various tests differ at student and country levels 
(Baumert et al., 2009). Some countries may perform high on all tests, 
while other countries may show low scores on all tests. Thus, if we look 
at the correlation at the country level, we might observe high correlation 
of all tests just because countries perform either high or low in all tests. 
However, at the student level within a country, tests may be not corre­
lated or even negatively correlated. A student may be good at mathe­
matics but poor at reading, and such individual scores would result in 
relative differences within countries. Rindermann (2007) compared the 
national test scores from PISA of 15-year-old student achievement and 
used adjustment formulas for students older or younger than the tar­
geted age to compare TIMSS data of Grades 4 and 8, as well as PIRLS 
data from Grade 4 and concluded that the results are rather homoge­
neous. It was asserted that even though diverse scales of student 
assessment and diverse test approaches are used, different ILSAs mea­
sure the same construct. Rindermann (2007) suggested that the high 
correlations are due to the similarities of tests within studies, including 
cognitive demands for solving test items, as well as to the common 
developmental factors at the individual and national levels. Neverthe­
less, a caution must be applied when interpreting Rindermann’s results 
as he used a set of highly heterogeneous countries for analysis. Even 
correlations of 0.8 and higher do not imply that all countries perform at 
the same level in various assessments. Anscombe’s quartet illustrates 
that it is important to study data carefully because nearly identical data 
sets might have quite distinct distributions when graphed. 
In contrast, Olsen and Grønmo (2006) compared TIMSS and PISA 
and noted that some Nordic and English-speaking countries performed 
relatively better in PISA than in TIMSS, while Eastern European coun­
tries performed relatively better in TIMSS. They explained these findings 
by the differences in methodologies that the two studies apply. Thus, the 
researchers pointed out that the assessment frameworks in these two 
ILSAs are constructed following different perspectives on mathematics. 
Moreover, the items in PISA test the competence of using the numerical 
information taken from real world context, while TIMSS focuses on pure 
mathematics and comprises items which require knowledge from the 
field of algebra and geometry (Olsen & Grønmo, 2006). 
Wu (2010) compared the similarities and differences between PISA 
2003 and TIMSS 2003. She utilized several parameters for an in-depth 
comparison, scrutinizing survey methodologies, mathematics frame­
works, and content of the tests. Echoing the findings by Olsen and 
Grønmo (2006), Wu claimed that Western countries performed rela­
tively better in PISA than in TIMSS; however, Asian and Eastern Euro­
pean countries demonstrated better scores in TIMSS as compared to 
their results in PISA. Wu linked these differences between countries’ 
performances in two ILSAs to the content balance of the tests and the 
sampling approaches, namely pointing out that TIMSS adopts 
grade-based criterion for sampling, whereas PISA is guided by the 
age-based selection of students. 
The present study 
The existing assessment studies in education address different 
learning outcomes in education as well as follow specific assessment 
frameworks and methodological procedures. However, researchers 
pointed out that the results of large-scale educational assessments are 
rather homogenous and thus provide similar inferences about the aca­
demic performance of test takers. Consequently, having such a variety of 
assessment studies raises the concern whether they duplicate each other 
in terms of their input, or they bring different insights into the quality of 
educational systems worldwide. 
Previous research on comparability of ILSAs primarily examined 
core subject-domains such as mathematics, science, and reading, leaving 
out other important domains such as civic knowledge and computer 
literacy. Moreover, most studies were limited to a subset of countries or 
regions to be included in the correlational analysis of ILSAs. These 
limitations of previous research make their findings incomplete, and it 
still appears to be problematic to answer the question whether different 
ILSAs provide added value to assess the quality of education. 
The present study investigates the necessity of having such a variety 
of assessment studies as well as educational indicators and aims at 
shedding light on the interconnectedness between them. In particular, 
the study focuses on various ILSAs, which have been administered by 
OECD and IEA worldwide, and examines the correlations between them 
from the perspectives of the content and sampling approach. 
The hypotheses that underpin this study are formulated below.  
• H1: The correlation is higher for different studies measuring the same 
subject domains than for different studies measuring different sub­
ject domains.  
• H2: The correlation is higher for studies measuring the same age 
cohort in comparison with studies of different age cohorts.  
• H3: The correlation is higher for studies closer in time than for studies 
that are more distant in time.  
• H4: The correlation is higher for studies administered by the same 
organization than for studies administered by different organizations 
(IEA vs. OECD). 
Methods 
Sample 
The study used the data from different cycles of PISA, TIMSS, ICILS, 
and ICCS. The ILSAs selected for the analysis covered such domains as 
mathematics, reading, science, problem-solving, collaborative problem- 
solving, financial literacy, civic education, computer and information 
literacy. The number of participating countries/regions might vary 
depending on the cycle of the study as well as on the tested domain. The 
analyses we conducted involved all countries that participated in the 
selected ILSAs. However, such analysis of the data of all participating

Measures 
Achievement scores 
The achievement variables are all the domains that were included in 
the different cycles of the ILSAs. All cycles of PISA tested mathematics, 
science, and reading, while additional domains were included in certain 
cycles, for example, global competence in PISA 2018. TIMSS consis­
tently evaluated the student performance in two domains: mathematics 
and science. The relatively new studies ICILS and ICCS focus specifically 
on computer and information literacy and civic knowledge respectively. 
The test scores of each International Large-Scale Assessment (ILSA) 
are comparable within the different study cycles but not across different 
studies. Within each ILSA, the achievement scales were designed to have 
an average score of 500 and a standard deviation of 100 among the 
participating countries in the first study cycle. In subsequent study cy­
cles, the same scales are used, but the means and standard deviations 
may differ due to changes in the sample of countries and progress in 
national performance distribution over time. In PISA, the scale for 
achievement scales were established in the year when a particular 
domain became the primary focus for the first time (e.g., reading in 
2000, math in 2003). It is important to note that the metric of the PISA 
scale is based only on OECD countries. Moreover, it should be noted that 
the achievement scores TIMSS have different scales for Grade 4 and 
Grade 8 assessments.”Analyses 
Correlational analysis 
The first methodological step was to conduct the correlational 
analysis of the country mean scores based on the data of the selected 
ILSAs. The analysis intended to compare the mean scores of different 
countries in two subject domains which were tested in ILSAs comparably 
at the same time. For this purpose, TIMSS 2019 Mathematics was 
compared with TIMSS 2019 Science for both Grades 4 and 8, demon­
strating the comparison between two different subject domains tested 
within the same assessment study, as well as TIMSS 2019 Mathematics 
was correlated with ICCS 2016 for Grade 8, showing the comparison 
between two different subject domains tested within different assess­
ment studies. These studies were selected for the correlational analysis 
because we aimed at demonstrating whether country scores in different 
subject domains equate and provide the same information about the 
educational achievement of a country. 
Furthermore, we compared the correlations between studies testing 
the performance of different age cohorts. We used the data from the six 
cycles (1995, 2003, 2007, 2011, 2015, 2019) of TIMSS Grade 4 and 
Grade 8 and compared how the performance in mathematics and science 
of fourth-grade students are correlated with the performance of eighth- 
grade students. 
To illustrate country test performance over time, we computed cor­
relations for countries participated in PISA reading literacy assessment 
among 15-year-olds starting from 2000 till 2018, using the latest PISA 
2018 as a reference point. This correlation was done to test our hy­
pothesis on whether studies that are closer in time show higher corre­
lation in comparison to studies that are more distant in time. 
Multidimensional scaling 
In the next step, the multidimensional scaling was applied. Multi­
dimensional scaling (MDS) represents a non-linear dimensionality 
reduction strategy to visualize the level of similarity between data by 
displaying the distance matrix (Cox & Cox, 2008). We conducted MDS 
by computing cross-country correlations in order to reduce the 
complexity of the data analysis. The multidimensional scaling was 
applied to analyze the latest cycles of PISA, TIMSS, ICCS, and ICILS and 
visualize how these studies are correlated to each other. 
Results and discussion 
Correlational comparison of different subject domains 
The correlational analysis of the country mean scores from ILSAs 
only partially support our assumption that assessment studies produce 
different indication of how well students perform on tests from a 
particular country. Fig. 1 demonstrates a high correlation of 0.94 be­
tween the performance of participating countries in TIMSS 2019 Science 
and Math for Grades 4 and 8. We can see here that countries perform 
relatively similarly in these two domains within the same assessment. 
Hence, based on this graph, we can assert that high performance in 
mathematics is a good predictor of high performance in science. In other 
words, there is little added value of a science score if we already have a 
math score – and vice versa. Fig. 1 shows that the performance of 
countries in two subject domains in TIMSS gives the same information 
about the progress of educational system as the correlation is rather high 
between two domains.

ILSAs was conducted also for 34 OECD countries as illustrated in (Fig. 2).

with the pattern observed for all participating countries, the findings for 
OECD countries reveal the following: 
1. TIMSS 2019 mathematics and science scores exhibit a high correla­

Comparing different age cohorts 
The comparison between different age cohorts gives additional 
findings about the added value that ILSAs bring to the educational 
community. To analyze how ILSAs measuring different age groups are 
related to each other, we took data from TIMSS Mathematics and Sci­
ence Grade 4 and Grade 8 in the timeframe between 1995 and 2019 (see 
Fig. 5). Interestingly, two cohorts perform relatively similarly in math, 
but their performance in science fluctuates over the time. A high cor­
relation in mathematics suggests that countries that do well in Grade 8 
were already good in Grade 4. By implication, the added value is low for 
TIMSS Math, and the inferences about the performance of one of the 
grades can be predicted based on the performance of another grade. On 
the other hand, the mediocre and low correlation in science implies that 
there is an added value to conduct assessment in this subject domain in 
two grades. Moreover, further analysis could demonstrate whether a 
country does well in science in primary school and poor in secondary 
school or vice versa. Such an analysis might help a country to focus on 
investing more in science field at the relevant educational level.

Changes over time in country performance 
Fig. 7 shows the country mean scores in PISA reading literacy over 
time, starting from 2000 and till the latest cycle in 2018. Initially, we 
assumed that the correlation would be higher for studies closer in time 
than for studies that are more distance from each other in time. How­
ever, we do not observe this pattern in our findings. Although the cor­
relation appears to be higher for studies closer in time for certain 
countries such as Chile, Estonia, the Netherlands, Poland; this is not a 
general pattern that can be identified here. Hence, our hypothesis con­
cerning the correlation among ILSAs over time is not supported. 
It is also important to acknowledge the impact of the weak test 
design in PISA until 2012, which introduced relatively large linking 
errors that affected the comparability of results across different assess­
ments (Robitzsch & Lüdtke, 2019). The presence of these linking errors 
can lead to substantial variability in country reading mean scores and 
may have influenced our findings. Thus, considering the limitations 
imposed by the test design during that period is necessary when inter­
preting our study’s results and understanding the potential sources of 
variability in country-level performance.

Conclusions 
The education as well as school curricula around the globe encom­
passes a wide range of subjects, yet the ILSAs mostly focus on measuring 
the scholastic performance in mathematics, science, and reading. The 
present study set out with the aim to analyze the interrelation of ILSAs 
and identify the value they add to the comprehensive assessment of 
educational system based on the similarity or divergence of conclusions 
one can draw relying on the test scores from different ILSAs. Our results 
showed that there are conspicuous differences between country mean 
performance in various ILSAs which leads to contrasting interpretations 
about the national educational achievement. Such disparity was evident 
through the analysis of ILSAs which included different subject domains 
and coordinating institutions. The gap between the country mean per­
formance in different ILSAs was particularly prominent when the anal­
ysis involved ICCS and ICILS. This finding indicates that the assessments 
on civic knowledge and computer and information literacy are more 
likely to alter the traditional perspective on the national achievement 
which is formulated based on the country performance in the core do­
mains such as mathematics, science, and reading.

Importance of participation in non-dominant studies 
Unfortunately, there is a small number of countries that participate 
in the tests in those subject domains that bring additional benefits to the 
educational assessment. It can be assumed that national authorities 
might have the preconceived idea that tests in mathematics, science, and 
reading can fully capture the progress and quality of their educational 
systems. The results of our study challenge this view on educational 
assessment and demonstrate the importance of participating in less 
popular but still highly relevant test domains. Thus, it can be advised to 
countries and educational policymakers to consider participating not 
only in the largest assessment studies such as PISA and TIMSS, but also 
in smaller-scale studies such as ICCS and ICILS that have the largest 
added value. 
Emphasis on a limited set of subjects 
Among existing ILSAs, there is a strong emphasis on mathematics, 
science, and reading in terms of the number of assessment studies as well 
as the number of participating countries. More countries tend to actively 
participate in studies that measure the dominant subjects such as 
mathematics, science, and reading, while other studies testing non- 
dominant subjects involve much fewer countries. 
Taking a more holistic perspective in measuring learning outcomes is 
possible. Benavot et al. (1991) has shown that countries not only have in 
common subjects such as mathematics, science, and reading, but sub­
jects such as physical education, music, fine arts, history and others are 
also well represented in the curricula around the world. Importantly, it 
is possible to test a wider range of subjects through assessment studies. 
For example, the Six Subject Survey has tested six subject domains in the 
1970s. 
The narrow focus on mathematics, science, and reading has impli­
cations not only for policy makers but also for researchers. ILSAs are 
increasingly used for secondary analyses (Hopfenbeck et al., 2018; 
Lenkeit et al., 2015), and for this reason ILSAs might set a misleading 
research agenda if they fail to consider other educational outcomes.

The present study examines the academic performance in ILSAs at 
the country level, which goes beyond the individual test performance 
and consequently shows the average national score in the specific

The focus of the present study was on academic achievement of 
students, although there are other important educational outcomes that 
can be measured and analyses, for instance, attitudes, motivation, and 
values of students. 
In this paper, we examined the comparisons of student scores in 
ISLAs at the country level. We neither argue nor think that our findings 
can be generalized on the student-level analysis. Both perspectives on 
academic achievement are relevant for analysis; however, we decided to 
focus on country-level analysis because multiple measures are available 
on country but not student level: We included in the analyses those 
countries that took part in various ILSAs, but there are different inde­
pendent samples of students that participated in different ILSAs. This 
makes it more challenging to draw comparisons of different ILSAs 
achievement at the student level.
