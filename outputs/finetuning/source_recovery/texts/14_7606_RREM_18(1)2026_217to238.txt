ABSTRACT
This article examines how public education spending and teacher-related factors shape student
performance across Austria, Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands, and Bulgaria. Using OECD and
PISA data (2000–2023), we analyze per-student expenditure, student–teacher ratios, teaching
hours, and efficiency measures. Results show a strong correlation between spending and
performance, though with diminishing returns. Teacher quality and deployment prove equally
decisive: Austria and Ireland benefit from training and curriculum coherence, the Netherlands
from teacher autonomy, while Bulgaria faces equity gaps despite efficiency. Principal Component
Analysis highlights two main dimensions—resource–performance and teacher efficiency.
Sustainable improvement requires aligning spending with teacher development, evaluation, and
accountability.

INTRODUCTION
Does spending more on education yield better student performance? Is this the only variable
that drives educational outcomes? While the debate is ongoing, international comparative studies
allow us to examine how education systems with varying financial commitments perform on
standardized assessments. This article expands the analysis by incorporating data on teacher
quality and instructional conditions. Since high-quality education depends not only on
infrastructure and salaries but also on the development of human capital, teacher
professionalism deserves particular attention.
This article examines five European countries—Bulgaria, Ireland, Germany, Austria, and the
Netherlands—with varied educational systems and financial profiles to determine how spending
and teaching-related metrics correlate with student performance (using average PISA scores
as the benchmark).
The choice of Bulgaria, Ireland, Germany, Austria, and the Netherlands reflects both diversity
and comparability across European educational systems. These countries differ significantly in
their levels of public investment in education, allowing for meaningful analysis of spendingperformance dynamics. They also provide geographic variation—spanning Western, Central, and
Eastern Europe—which helps capture structural differences shaped by history and policy
traditions. Furthermore, their distinct timelines of European Union accession (from founding
members such as Germany and the Netherlands to later entrants like Bulgaria) offer insight into
how varying stages of integration and economic development interact with educational
outcomes. The selected group also includes both higher- and lower-performing PISA and TALIS
countries, which enables us to explore efficiency and contextual factors beyond raw expenditure.
Together, these contrasts create a balanced comparative framework to understand how
spending, teacher quality, and systemic factors shape student performance.
Building on this comparative foundation, the study addresses the following main research
question: To what extent do financial investment (spending) and teacher-related factors—such
as student–teacher ratios, professional development, and institutional autonomy—explain
cross-country differences in PISA outcomes among Austria, Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands,
and Bulgaria?
The growing volume of research on the topic demonstrates that financial resources do
matter, but their impact is conditioned by teacher quality, institutional design, and policy
coherence. Simply increasing education budgets is unlikely to yield proportional improvements
unless resources are strategically allocated and embedded within effective governance
structures.

LITERATURE REVIEW
The relationship between education spending and student performance has been widely
debated in the academic literature. Hanushek (2003) argues that increased funding does not
necessarily lead to improved outcomes, highlighting inefficiencies in resource allocation. This
argument is consistent with the broader “education production function” literature, which models
achievement as the output of multiple inputs (e.g., spending, class size, teacher characteristics,
and institutional settings). Reviewing evidence from developed countries, Hanushek &
Woessmann (2008) show that estimated input effects vary widely across contexts, suggesting
that how resources are deployed often matters as much as their level.
In contrast, studies from the OECD (2020) and UNESCO (2021) emphasize the importance of
sustained investment, particularly in teacher quality and early childhood education, as key
drivers of academic achievement. Woessmann (2016) finds that institutional factors such as
accountability systems and school autonomy can mediate the effect of spending on outcomes.

At the cross-country level, Hanushek & Woessmann (2011) argue that international achievement
differences reflect both resource conditions and institutional arrangements, reinforcing the idea
that spending effects cannot be interpreted independently from governance and system design.
A substantial body of literature further underscores the central role of teacher quality. Rivkin
et al. (2005) demonstrated that teacher effectiveness has a stronger impact on student learning
than class size or per-pupil spending. Similarly, Rockoff (2004) and Chetty, Friedman, and
Rockoff (2014) provide robust evidence that high-quality teachers influence not only test scores
but also long-term life outcomes such as college attendance and future earnings. This
underscores the view that investments in professional development, training, and recruitment
of effective teachers are crucial for leveraging spending into measurable outcomes.
Cross-country research highlights the importance of systemic and institutional factors, on
top of individual teacher effectiveness. Woessmann (2016) points to the mediating role of school
governance, accountability systems, and standardized curricula. Ongoing teacher training and
professional development promote the development of high levels of corporate intelligence in
schools (Bolivar, 2013). A large cross-country review similarly concludes that school resources
can support achievement, but the size and consistency of these effects depend heavily on the
institutional context and how resources are translated into instructional quality (Hanushek &
Woessmann, 2017). This helps explain why comparable spending increases may yield different
learning gains across countries.
Beyond financial inputs, educational outcomes also depend on teacher motivation and
working conditions, which shape how resources are translated into classroom practice (Ryan &
Deci, 2000). Because motivation cannot be observed directly in the OECD/PISA dataset, this
paper interprets it indirectly through TALIS-based proxies (e.g., perceived autonomy,
collaboration, and participation in professional development). These indicators are discussed as
contextual mechanisms rather than modeled as causal drivers.
West and Woessmann (2010) also argue that school choice and competition can raise student
performance when paired with accountability and autonomy. Evidence from cross-national
analyses of school sectors further suggests that governance and institutional context condition
performance differences. Using comparative data, Dronkers & Robert (2007) show that
achievement patterns differ across public, government-dependent private, and independent
private schools, and that sector effects vary substantially by country—supporting the view that
institutional design shapes how resources and incentives translate into outcomes. These
findings suggest that financial resources interact with governance structures, making
institutional quality a key determinant of how spending translates into outcomes.
Research by Hernández-Ramos and Martínez-Abad (2023) emphasizes that intensive
professional teacher development within schools is strongly associated with a more positive
school climate, higher levels of innovation, greater cooperation, shared goals, and distributed
leadership across the educational community. In later studies, they reconfirmed this finding and
further highlighted substantial variability in teachers’ commitment to professional development,
noting that such differences were not explained by school-level factors. This underscores the
critical importance of ongoing teacher training as a driver of educational quality. Complementary
evidence from Spain based on PISA 2018 data reinforces these insights, showing that secondary
teachers’ professional development is closely linked to school-level organizational factors such
as collaboration, innovation, shared goals/responsibility, and distributed leadership (HernándezRamos & Martínez-Abad, 2023).
Ghazali and Fadzlee (2024) present an interesting approach to academic staff assessment
through the Restorative Quality Assessment (RES-Q) model. RES-Q is designed as a framework
for evaluating academic competencies and proposing countermeasures that can address
challenges in teaching and learning. The model highlights the spectrum of skills needed to create
favorable learning conditions and supports efforts to enhance overall educational quality.

Recent research by Jackson et al. (2015) using U.S. data suggests that increases in per-pupil
spending significantly improve long-term outcomes, particularly for low-income students. These
contrasting findings indicate that while money matters, how it is spent and within what
institutional framework remains crucial.
Another critical dimension is equity in education. According to OECD (2019) and Schleicher
(2018), countries with more equitable allocation of resources tend to achieve both higher average
performance and smaller socio-economic performance gaps. Empirical evidence from Baker et
al. (2018) and Jackson et al. (2015) further shows that increased spending has particularly strong
effects for disadvantaged students, highlighting the distributive role of education policy in
promoting fairness and mobility.
Efficiency in education has also become a central theme in research literature. Afonso and
St. Aubyn (2005) and Sutherland et al. (2007) use efficiency frontiers to show that some OECD
countries achieve high results with relatively modest budgets, while others underperform
despite higher levels of spending. Extending this line of research, De Witte and López-Torres
(2017) emphasize that governance quality and institutional reforms are essential in explaining
why certain countries achieve greater efficiency than others.
The debate extends beyond financial metrics to include how teachers and students perceive
quality of learning. Several studies highlight discrepancies between student self-assessments
and teacher evaluations, raising questions about subjective versus standardized indicators of
performance.
Kunter et al. (2013) found that while teachers’ judgments are generally predictive of longterm achievement, students’ self-reported engagement is equally important in capturing
classroom dynamics. Similarly, OECD’s TALIS reports (2019) reveal that teacher perceptions of
classroom climate and professional autonomy correlate strongly with student-reported
satisfaction and sense of belonging in school. Besides, the European Education and Culture
Executive Agency (2022) notes that teacher education should include both a general and a
professional component. Basic education platforms emphasize that teacher qualifications are
key to student outcomes, that the quality of teaching outweighs the quantity of resources.
Professional development must be continuous, and very successful systems invest in continuous
professional development (CPD).
As further evidence of the importance of continuous professional development, many
countries have established dedicated national platforms to support teachers in developing their
digital skills. For example, Austria has introduced Digi.folio, a comprehensive tool designed to
document and guide teachers’ digital competencies throughout their careers. In France, the
Réseau CANOPÉ provides extensive online resources, training modules, and collaborative
communities aimed at integrating digital technologies into classroom practice. Similarly, Italy’s
Scuola Futura platform offers structured professional development pathways, including
certification programs and innovative teaching resources that promote the effective use of digital
tools in education. These initiatives illustrate how governments are not only emphasizing teacher
training in general but also strategically prioritizing digital competencies as essential for modern
teaching. They demonstrate a shift from traditional professional development to dynamic,
technology-oriented systems that prepare teachers to meet evolving classroom challenges and
ensure students are equipped with 21st-century skills (OECD, 2019; UNESCO, 2018).
The OECD (2020, 2023) and UNESCO (2021) note that even with limited budgets, countries
with well-prepared teachers often achieve high levels of student performance. For instance,
Estonia and Finland have consistently demonstrated strong results in international assessments
despite operating with comparatively modest levels of expenditure. This evidence highlights the
critical role of teacher quality, showing that investment in professional preparation and ongoing
development can outweigh the sheer volume of financial resources in determining educational
outcomes.

Recent PISA 2022 findings further highlight the interplay between investment, equity, and
outcomes. According to OECD PISA results (2023), performance in reading, mathematics, and
science across OECD countries has declined since 2018, partly reflecting the impact of the
COVID-19 pandemic on schooling. However, countries with strong teacher support systems and
equitable resource distribution demonstrated greater resilience. For example, the Netherlands
and Ireland managed to maintain comparatively stable results despite the disruptions, benefiting
from robust continuous professional development programs and well-established teacher
autonomy frameworks. In contrast, Bulgaria continued to exhibit persistent equity gaps and
lower absolute performance, reflecting systemic challenges such as centralized exam-focused
accountability and limited teacher motivation, which constrained its ability to buffer the
pandemic’s negative effects. These cases illustrate how investment in teacher quality and
equitable policy environments can mitigate external shocks and sustain educational outcomes
during crises.

Educational systems structure
Educational systems across Austria, Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands, and Bulgaria vary
significantly in their degree of centralization, financing models, and institutional traditions. In the
European context, cross-national comparisons of secondary school systems often focus on how
institutional traditions explain the relationship between spending and outcomes. Austria
operates under a centralized quality assurance framework, though schools maintain
considerable autonomy in practice (European Commission, 2020). Germany is highly
decentralized, with educational policies and financing largely determined by the Länder, creating
significant regional variation (Horn, 2009; Dustmann et al., 2017). Ireland employs a more
centralized model, with national inspectorates overseeing schools and a strong reliance on state
funding (Coolahan et al., 2017).
The Netherlands represents a hybrid system, granting extensive autonomy to school boards
within a nationally regulated quality assurance framework (Dronkers & Avram, 2015). Bulgaria,
in contrast, is heavily centralized, with exam-focused accountability and financing directed
through national mechanisms (Boyadjieva & Ilieva-Trichkova, 2018). Together, these systems
illustrate a spectrum from decentralized autonomy (Netherlands, Germany) to centralized
oversight (Austria, Ireland, Bulgaria), shaping how financial resources and policies are
translated into outcomes. Yet, in this diversity there are some common elements and factors of
educational quality.

Students’ evaluation systems further illustrate cross-country diversity. Austria combines
national diagnostic tests (iKM PLUS) with the standardized Zentralmatura, balancing
centralization with teacher-assessed oral components (European Commission, 2020).
Germany employs centrally developed but low-stakes comparative tests (VERA) in lower
grades, with state-specific Abitur exams that integrate both central and teacher-based grading
(Dustmann et al., 2017). Ireland uses a dual approach: classroom-based assessments in the
Junior Cycle, combined with the fully centralized and externally graded Leaving Certificate
(Coolahan et al., 2017). The Netherlands relies on a dual model where school-based assessments
carry equal weight with national central exams, reflecting its tradition of school autonomy paired
with accountability (Dronkers & Avram, 2015). Bulgaria, by contrast, is among the most examdependent systems in Europe, relying on national external assessments at grades 7, 10, and the
fully centralized and externally graded State Matura at grade 12 (Boyadjieva & Ilieva-Trichkova,
2018).
These differences illustrate how evaluation systems shape not only learning outcomes but
also equity: systems with strong national exams ensure comparability but risk narrowing
curricula, while those with mixed models balance accountability with pedagogical flexibility.
Earlier comparative mapping of European school evaluation systems similarly emphasizes that
the balance between inspection, self-evaluation, and follow-up support affects whether
evaluation functions as developmental feedback or procedural compliance (European
Commission, 2015).

Teachers’ evaluation systems also reflect some institutional differences and play a critical
role in determining how resources influence quality. Austria embeds evaluation within its Quality
Management System, combining inspector oversight with structured professional development
(European Commission, 2020). Germany relies on land-specific inspections and appraisals,
producing a diverse landscape where career progression depends heavily on regional rules
(Horn, 2009). Ireland combines inspectorate evaluations with a self-evaluation cycle that
requires teacher reflection, supported by structured CPD linked to curriculum reforms
(Coolahan et al., 2017). In the Netherlands, teacher appraisal is decentralized and largely the
responsibility of school boards, yet embedded in a strong inspectorate framework that ensures
accountability (Dronkers & Avram, 2015).
Bulgaria applies a highly centralized approach, using formal career-stage attestations and
regional inspectorates, with credit-based CPD as a requirement for advancement (Boyadjieva &
Ilieva-Trichkova, 2018). Comparatively, systems like Ireland and Austria emphasize continuous
professional development, while Germany and the Netherlands highlight local autonomy, and
Bulgaria enforces progression through centralized examinations. These differences suggest that
evaluation structures can either reinforce or offset the impact of financial resources: where
appraisal is tied to professional development (Austria, Ireland), spending is more likely to
translate into improved outcomes, whereas in systems with rigid central oversight (Bulgaria),
evaluation risks becoming procedural rather than developmental.
Furthermore, comparative evidence indicates that teacher assessments and systemic
evaluation cultures can amplify or diminish the impact of resources. The European Commission
(2020) notes that in countries with strong external evaluation systems (e.g., the Netherlands and
Ireland), teacher judgments are often embedded within standardized frameworks, reducing bias

International assessments
All five countries participate actively in international assessments such as PISA, TALIS, and
Education at a Glance. PISA results reveal meaningful cross-country differences in both
efficiency (how effectively resources translate into learning outcomes) and equity (how evenly
those outcomes are distributed), showing that comparable levels of investment and similar
system inputs can be associated with notably different performance profiles across countries
(OECD, 2023). TALIS results provide insights into teacher professionalism, autonomy, and
collaboration, highlighting strengths in systems such as Ireland and Austria, where CPD is
embedded in teacher careers (OECD, 2019). Education at a Glance further contextualizes these
outcomes with indicators on teacher pay, teaching hours, and class sizes (OECD, 2024).
Economic literature suggests that educational outcomes in Austria, Germany, Ireland, the
Netherlands, and Bulgaria cannot be fully explained by spending levels alone (Hanushek &
Woessmann, 2011; OECD, 2020). Instead, the interaction between institutional structures, teacher
professionalism, and evaluation practices provides a more comprehensive explanation of crosscountry differences (Mourshed et al., 2010; OECD, 2020; Woessmann, 2016). Comparative
research thus sheds light on the necessity of considering both quantitative and qualitative
aspects of teaching and learning when assessing efficiency and performance (Hattie, 2009;
UNESCO, 2014).
Overall, international assessments confirm that while financial resources influence
performance, teacher professionalism, systemic evaluation, and governance structures strongly
mediate how investments translate into learning outcomes.

METHODOLOGY
This study employs a quantitative, cross-country comparative approach, combining OECD
Education at a Glance (UOE) indicators and OECD PISA data for Austria, Germany, Ireland, the
Netherlands, and Bulgaria over the period 2000–2022. The dataset integrates multiple
dimensions of educational inputs and outcomes, including:
• Public expenditure per secondary student (USD, PPP-adjusted) - OECD Education at
a Glance / UOE
• PISA scores in math, reading, and science (averaged) - OECD PISA
• Student–teacher ratios (upper secondary) - OECD Education at a Glance / UOE
• A derived efficiency ratio (PISA score per $1,000 spent) - own calculations based on
OECD inputs and PISA outcomes
• OECD data on actual teaching/instructional time per year - OECD Education at a
Glance / UOE
• Socioeconomic composition, measured using PISA ESCS (Index of Economic, Social
and Cultural Status), aggregated at the country level for each PISA cycle
• Early childhood education participation, proxied by OECD enrolment rates for
children aged 3–5 - OECD Education at a Glance / UOE
The analysis proceeds in three stages. First, descriptive statistics and regression analysis
are applied to explore the relationship between spending and student performance, establishing
a baseline association across countries and years. Both linear and quadratic models are
estimated to capture potential diminishing returns to investment. Second, dispersion analysis is
conducted through scatterplots, allowing visual inspection of how countries differ in translating
financial and teacher-related inputs into educational outcomes. Third, Principal Component
Analysis (PCA) is employed to reduce the dimensionality of the dataset and identify the main
axes of variation across systems.

LIMITATIONS
This study employs a correlational design, which inherently limits the ability to infer causal
relationships between education spending, teacher-related factors, and student performance.
While strong associations are observed, the directionality and underlying mechanisms cannot
be definitively established.
Another limitation is the omission of key confounding variables, particularly socioeconomic
status (SES), cultural attitudes toward education, and the quality and availability of early
childhood education systems. These factors are well-documented determinants of educational
outcomes and may influence both resource allocation and student achievement. Their exclusion
from the quantitative models restricts the comprehensiveness of the analysis and may bias the
estimated relationships.
Additionally, the study relies on proxy indicators such as student–teacher ratios to represent
teacher quality. However, as explicitly acknowledged, student–teacher ratio primarily measures
teacher deployment rather than instructional effectiveness or professionalism. Teacher quality
is a multifaceted construct influenced by policy context, professional development, motivation,
and institutional support, which are only partially captured through available quantitative
metrics. This proxy problem underscores the need for integrating qualitative insights and richer
data sources to fully understand the role of teacher quality in educational performance.
Future research should aim to incorporate these omitted variables and employ
methodologies capable of addressing endogeneity and causal inference to provide a more
nuanced understanding of the complex interplay among spending, teacher quality, and student
outcomes.

SPENDING AND PERFORMANCE
Across all five countries, education spending shows a strong positive correlation with
student performance. A simple linear regression model indicates that higher PPP-adjusted
spending per student is associated with higher PISA performance in the sample (R² = 0.702). This
finding confirms the expectation that financial resources matter for learning outcomes, but it
also highlights the need to examine how effectively those resources are translated into results.
The Netherlands and Germany consistently perform at or above OECD averages, while Bulgaria
lags in absolute performance but demonstrates proportional gains relative to its limited
resources.

Efficiency of education spending
Understanding efficiency is essential because equal spending does not necessarily yield
equal outcomes. This type of analysis provides useful insights into how effectively their education
systems convert financial input into measurable results. It shifts the debate from “how much is
spent” to “how well it is spent,” highlighting that improving efficiency can sometimes yield larger
benefits than merely increasing budgets.
The education spending efficiency ratio—measured as average PISA performance per $1,000
invested—reveals important cross-country differences. Bulgaria, despite lower average PISA
scores, demonstrates relatively high efficiency due to its modest spending levels. In contrast,
high-spending countries such as Austria and the Netherlands achieve excellent performance
but with slightly lower efficiency ratios, indicating diminishing marginal returns on investment.

Autonomy and continuous professional development (CPD)
Teacher professionalism, autonomy, and school-level collaboration constitute pivotal

outcomes, as evidenced by TALIS 2024 results (OECD, 2025). The survey highlights substantial
variation among the five countries studied, particularly in dimensions such as teacher autonomy

For instance, the Netherlands and Ireland report notably higher levels of teacher autonomy,
which correspond with their strong PISA performances despite contrasting student-teacher
ratios. This suggests that professional discretion enables teachers to tailor instruction
effectively, compensating for structural constraints such as larger class sizes.
Moreover, Austria and Ireland demonstrate robust cultures of professional collaboration and
sustained CPD engagement, aligning with their relatively high student outcomes and efficiency
ratios. TALIS 2024 results reveal that these countries embed CPD systematically within teacher
career pathways, fostering ongoing skill enhancement and instructional innovation (OECD, 2025).
In contrast, Bulgaria exhibits lower CPD participation and reports greater workload pressures,
which may constrain teachers’ capacity to translate resources into learning gains despite
relatively efficient spending patterns.
Thus, the observed quantitative patterns in student–teacher ratios and performance are best
understood through the qualitative lens of teacher policy environments. Institutional
arrangements that promote professional autonomy, continuous learning, and supportive
evaluation systems form the true foundation of teacher quality. These factors amplify or mitigate
the effects of deployment metrics, explaining why countries with similar staffing levels may
experience divergent educational outcomes.

DISCUSSION & ANALYSIS
This study provides evidence that higher per-student public spending is associated with
stronger PISA performance across the countries and time periods examined. Importantly, this
relationship is not driven solely by simple cross-country differences: when the analysis moves
to a multiple regression framework and controls for socioeconomic composition (PISA ESCS)
and early childhood education participation (OECD enrolment rates for ages 3–5), per-student
spending remains positively and statistically significantly associated with PISA outcomes.
Socioeconomic composition emerges as a particularly strong correlate, reinforcing that crosscountry performance comparisons are highly sensitive to differences in student background and
that these confounders must be accounted for to avoid overstating the role of funding alone.
The non-linear estimates further suggest diminishing returns beyond roughly $14,000 per
student, indicating that financial investment alone cannot indefinitely raise outcomes. This
plateauing effect is consistent with real-world examples: countries such as Luxembourg, which
spend heavily on education, do not necessarily outperform leaner but well-structured systems
like Estonia or Finland, where investment is lower but institutional design and teacher quality
are robust.
The efficiency ratio analysis underscored the critical importance of how resources are used
rather than simply how much is spent. The findings are consistent with the view that targeted
allocation (e.g., teacher training, curriculum modernization, equity-focused programs) may be
one pathway through which systems improve outcomes under fiscal constraints. Real-life
parallels are found in systems like Poland, which despite limited resources, introduced targeted
reforms in the early 2000s that translated into notable improvements in PISA outcomes. In

but lower efficiency (Germany in later years), point to critical policy lessons. They suggest that
improving outcomes is not solely about moving along the spending gradient but about finding the
optimal mix of autonomy, accountability, and teacher deployment.
The comparative evidence confirms that teacher-related factors are decisive in determining
whether financial investment translates into durable improvements. For instance, Austria’s
reliance on structured professional development highlights the role of systemic teacher training
in stabilizing outcomes. Germany’s dual system, integrating vocational and academic tracks,
shows how federal flexibility can coexist with strong standards to sustain performance. Ireland’s
reliance on centralized exams and national curricula, combined with embedded CPD,
demonstrates that coherence and teacher support can maintain results despite resource
constraints. The Netherlands provides perhaps the most striking example: its ability to sustain
high outcomes with large classes demonstrates that institutional trust, professional autonomy,
and accountability mechanisms can outweigh traditional inputs like class size or teaching hours.
Conversely, Bulgaria illustrates the dangers of underfunding: while efficiency is
comparatively high, absolute outcomes remain low, reflecting the corrosive impact of socioeconomic inequality, limited remuneration, and difficulty in retaining qualified teachers. Evidence

socioeconomic effects highlights how investment in teaching quality contributes not only to
student test scores but also to long-term national competitiveness, labor market adaptability,
and social cohesion. Systems that prioritize equity and teacher professionalism are better
positioned to withstand external shocks, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, and to ensure that all
students, regardless of background, benefit from public investment.

CONCLUSIONS:
The findings in this article indicate that educational performance and efficiency are shaped
less by any single input and more by how resources, professional conditions, and governance
features interact within each national system.
• The factors studied—spending levels, teacher-related indicators, and institutional
arrangements—intervene differently in each country. Their impact is shaped by national
traditions, governance models, targeted policy accompanied by specific measures and
socioeconomic conditions. As a result, cross-country variation in outcomes cannot be attributed
to one dominant variable alone.
• The resource–achievement gradient relationship is positive overall, but its strength
and efficiency vary across systems and over time. Commonly cited deployment factors—such as
smaller classes or higher teacher involvement—may support performance in some contexts, yet
the evidence does not suggest they function as universal drivers of achievement.
• Increasing teacher autonomy, curriculum coherence, continuous professional
development and well-established career paths are more consistently aligned with stronger
performance and more sustainable efficiency. This reinforces a central implication of the study:
how resources are organized and deployed can matter as much as how much is invested.
• Educational outcomes interact with labor markets, social cohesion, and innovation
capacity. This means that policies aimed at raising students’ performance, teachers’ motivation,
PISA or TALIS scores must be embedded within wider strategies for equity, economic
development, and social well-being, especially when systems face external shocks or persistent
socioeconomic gaps.
