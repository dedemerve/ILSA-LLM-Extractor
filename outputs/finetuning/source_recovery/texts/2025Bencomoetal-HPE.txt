Abstract
Inequality of Opportunity in Achievement (IOpE) measures the importance of factors beyond the student’s control in explaining differences in academic performance. Using PISA 2018, we estimate the
IOpE for Spanish regions using conditional inference tree (CIT) and forest (CIF). Using CIFs, IOpE is
twice as high as those obtained using traditional approaches (on average, 43% compared to 20%).
Murcia and Extremadura are among those with the highest IOpE, while Castilla-La Mancha and the
Pais Vasco have the lowest IOpE. The circumstances that contribute most to IOpE are the cultural environment at home (number of books) and parental occupation.
Keywords: Inequality of opportunity, Academic achievement, Machine learning, PISA, Spanish regions.
JEL Classification: D63, I24, I28, O52.

economic or cultural background (Coleman et al., 1966).
In this context, this paper analyses the performance of the Spanish education system in
terms of inequality of opportunity in educational achievement (IOpE) from a comparative
regional perspective. The study of IOpE at the regional level is particularly relevant in Spain
due to the devolution of educational competences to regional governments, which are responsible for the management of educational resources and for a large part of the design and
implementation of public policies. The devolution process, which began in the early 2000s,
has been long and complex, with very different starting points among the regions, resulting in
notable differences in terms of academic outcomes, efficiency and educational equity.
According to the 2018 report of the Programme for International Student Assessment
(PISA), the difference between the best (Galicia) and the worst (Canarias) performers in science was almost 40 points, which corresponds to more than one school year. This difference
is even more significant if we look at the gap between the percentage of students in Galicia
(13.5%) and Canarias (25%) who do not reach the minimum performance level in science.
Thus, these marked differences between regions motivate the relevance of comparing IOpE at
the Spanish regional level. However, due to the lack of external educational evaluations that
allow the comparison of performance between regions, the available evidence in this regard
is very limited (Martínez de Lafuente and Vega-Bayo, 2023; Sicilia and Simancas, 2023).
This paper is a contribution to this literature. We use data from the 2018 edition of PISA,
in which Spain participated with an expanded sample of students to obtain regionally representative data. These data provide an objective and comparable measure of students’ academic performance in three basic skills (mathematics, science and reading) and include extensive
information on students’ family and school socioeconomic background (Sicilia and Simancas, 2023; Marrero et al., 2024), which is a key element for measuring IOpE.
To estimate the effect of circumstances on educational achievement inequality, we use
machine learning (ML) techniques. Specifically, we use conditional inference trees (CIT)
and conditional inference forests (CIF), which have been shown to be superior to more traditional regression trees and random forests in a number of ways (Hothorn et al., 2006). While
these methods have recently been applied in the income inequality of opportunity literature
to overcome the limitations of traditional regression-based (linear or non-linear) approaches
that introduce downward or upward bias (Brunori et al., 2019, 2021, 2023; Brunori and Neid-

hofer, 2021; Plassot et al., 2022), to our knowledge they have not been applied to measure
IOpE. Most previous IOpE studies have used standard parametric or nonparametric methods
(Schütz et al., 2008; Martins and Veiga, 2010; Ferreria and Gignoux, 2014; Lasso de la Vega
et al., 2020; Sicilia et al., 2022; Marrero et al., 2024).1
If we apply these results from the income literature to the education setting, estimates
using traditional techniques are likely to be highly biased. Moreover, ranking and variable
importance analysis could be misleading. By using CIT and CIF, we attempt to overcome the
limitations of traditional regression techniques in the education literature. Thus, although this
is not a policy-oriented paper, correctly estimating IOpE and the factors that influence it is a
necessary first step in understanding the problem of educational inequality in a region and in
thinking about policy recommendations.
The research is original in several respects. From an applied perspective, it provides a
measure of comparative IOpE at the regional level, including all Spanish regions. This is
informative for the design of public policies, given the decentralised nature of educational
competences in Spain. From a methodological point of view, the application of ML techniques to the IOpE literature allows us to dimension the effect of the problem of upward and
downward bias in our context, where the data structure is different from that of income or
health, where ML approaches have already been used to estimate inequality of opportunity.
We estimate the entire IOpE distribution using bootstrapping, which provides not only point
estimates but also confidence intervals. In conclusion, this paper provides scientific evidence
to understand and address one of the most challenging issues in the Spanish education system: the formulation of educational policies and interventions aimed at reducing the existing
educational inequalities between regions.

2. Inequality of opportunities in educational achievement in Spain
Comparative evidence across education systems on the extent of inequality of opportunity
in terms of skills acquisition and learning (achievement) is recent and still scarce (compared,
for example, with evidence on inequality of opportunity in terms of income or earnings). Most
of it comes from information provided by international large-scale assessments (ILSAs),
as they allow comparisons between education systems. The evidence available for the case of
European education systems, including Spain in the comparison, is presented below.
Shultz et al. (2008) were the first to present comparative evidence for 54 countries on
the level of IOpE in primary and secondary education, using data from the Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) and the Progress in International Reading
Literacy Study (PIRLS) in 1995 and 1998, respectively. The authors measure the impact of

socioeconomic status (measured by the number of books at home) on student performance
and then examine the relationship between IOpE and certain institutional characteristics or
educational policies of education systems (early tracking, attendance at early childhood education, among others). According to this proxy of IOpE, Spain is in the group of countries
with the lowest effect of family background on educational achievement in the sample and
among European countries.
Martins and Veiga (2010) compare IOpE in 15 European countries using data from the
2003 edition of PISA. The authors find that inequality in students’ socioeconomic factors
accounts for between 15% and 35% of the total inequality in IOpE, with Spain also being
among the countries with the lowest inequality, but also among the countries where socioeconomic inequality is mainly explained by students’ background characteristics.
Ferreria and Gignoux (2014) measure IOpE in 57 countries that participated in the 2006
edition of PISA. The authors use a parametric linear regression model and include a wide
range of family circumstances. They find that IOpE can be as high as 35% and that it is not
correlated with countries’ average educational attainment or GDP per capita. The countries
with the highest IOpE are Central European countries (Belgium, France, and Germany) and
Eastern European countries (Bulgaria and Hungary), while the Nordic countries and Italy
have the lowest IOpE. Spain is in the middle.
Lasso de la Vega et al. (2020) compare IOpE in 20 European countries using information from PISA 2012 and applying the approach used by Jusot et al. (2013), which is based
on estimates of a linear regression that includes as explanatory factors variables related to
student circumstances, but also other factors related to student effort. As in previous studies,
the authors find that the Central European countries show the highest IOpE levels, while the
Nordic countries, Ireland and Spain have the lowest.
Sicilia et al. (2022) apply a parametric linear regression approach to the 2018 PISA data
to estimate IOpE for EU-27 countries and Spanish regions (see also Marrero et al., 2024, for
European countries). Spain and the Nordic countries have relatively moderate levels of IOpE
(around 20%), while the Central European countries –mainly Germany, Belgium, France
and the Netherlands– have the highest levels of IOpE (between 38% and 45%). Comparing
the Spanish regions, the authors find that the IOpE levels of the Spanish regions –and the
variation between them– are lower than those found for the European countries. The IOpE
for Spain is 19%, with the Baleares and Castilla-La Mancha at the lower end (16%), while
Asturias and Madrid exceed 21% and Murcia has the highest IOpE at 27%. These differences
do not seem to be linked to any geographical, economic, or socio-cultural pattern.
Sicilia and Simancas (2023) perform a comprehensive evaluation of the performance
of the Spanish education system in terms of efficiency and equity from a regional comparative perspective. To evaluate the educational equity, they compute multiple indicators
used in the literature using data from PISA 2018. To measure the influence of the students’
socioeconomic background on their performance they use the percentage of the variance
in achievement explained by the socioeconomic background (R squared in the regression),

which is close to our measure of IOpE. In line with Sicilia et al. (2022), the authors found

Finally, Martínez de Lafuente and Vega-Bayo (2023) uses a battery of ML techniques to

and Sicilia and Simancas (2023), there is no comparative work on IOpE in the Spanish regions. And, to the best of our knowledge, except for this second work, we are the first to use
ML techniques to estimate IOpE. In addition, we use conditional inference trees and forests,
which have been shown to have important advantages over traditional regression trees and
random forests (see section 3). We use a parameter tuning approach following Brunori et
al. (2023). To make IOpE estimates comparable across regions, we select a common set of
tuning parameters for all regions. As we will show in the results section, our IOpE estimates
are significantly higher than previous estimates, and in some regions they are almost double.

3. Methodology
The most common procedure for estimating IOpE is the one proposed by Ferreira and
Guignoux (2014). It is an adaptation of the proposal for estimating IO in income (Bourguignon et al., 2007; Ferreira and Guignoux, 2011; Marrero and Rodríguez, 2012). The
method is based on estimating a reduced form (usually linear or log-linear) that relates a
measure of educational attainment, E, to a set of circumstances
(unexplained part), ε: E = f(C1, ... Cj, ..., CJ) + ε.

This is the standard measure used in the literature. When measuring IO in terms of income or wealth, other inequality indices are often used, such as Gini, MLD or Theil 0. However, the measures of educational performance that is commonly used come from external
assessments (e. g. PISA) and is standardized by the average of the year of the assessment and
the competence. Indices such as the Gini or the MLD are affected by this standardization,
which would make it difficult to compare the IOpE results across years and competencies.
However, the variance is not affected by the standardization (neither the standard deviation
nor the coefficient of variation), which is why it is the measure commonly used in the education literature (Ferreira and Guignoux, 2014; Marrero et al., 2024).
By using this linear approach, we may be committing at least two types of bias in the fitted part, Ê, with opposite signs, which are translated into the estimate of the IOpE (Brunori et
al., 2019; 2023). The first is a downward bias, which is due to the fact that the circumstances
included in the model (those that we can measure and observe), as well as the functional form
considered, can only capture part of the total existing IOpE. The second type of bias, which
is upward, is due to possible problems of overfitting that we make by introducing irrelevant
circumstances into the model (e. g., by including quadratic or cross effects that are not relevant). This leads to an excess of uncertainty in the predictions of E and leads to an upward
bias in the estimate of IOpE.
This problem has recently been highlighted in the estimation of IO in income in several
papers, such as Brunori et al. (2019; 2023), Brunori and Neidhofer (2021), or Plassot et al.
(2022), among others. Dealing with these biases is not trivial, since conventional methods
tend to reduce one bias by increasing the other. For example, the usual way to reduce the
downward bias is to introduce interactions or quadratic effects of circumstance into the regression, but this increases the risk of overfitting. Brunori et al. (2019) propose to balance
the magnitude of these two biases using a regularisation procedure that minimizes the outof-sample mean squared error (MSE), to obtain IOpE estimates that are not biased in either
direction. Supervised machine learning (ML) methods using cross-validation (CV) or similar
approaches to assess the out-of-sample MSE is the procedure proposed by these authors.
More specifically, they propose to use Conditional Inference Trees (CIT) and Conditional
Inference Forests (CIF) (Hothorn et al., 2006) because of their flexibility to include nonlinearities and especially because of their advantages over other alternatives (latent variables,
lasso, etc.).
3.1. Conditional Inference Trees (CIT)
The goal of the algorithm behind a decision tree (Morgan and Sonquist, 1963; Breiman et al., 1984) is to make an out-of-sample prediction of the outcome of a target variable
(academic performance, in our case) based on a set of factors or characteristics (our set of
circumstances). In order to do this, the space of all possible combinations of circumstances
is roughly divided into non-overlapping groups. The name “tree” comes from the way the
algorithm generates meaningful sample splits. See Figure 1 for an illustrative example, which
will be discussed below.

We present the CIT results for their ease of interpretation and robustness. However, this
will not be our preferred method, as there are several estimation problems associated with the
CIT (Breiman, 1996, 2001; Friedman et al., 2009). Firstly, the results are highly dependent
on the sample used and on the presence of missing values: for example, small sample variations can cause the first partition to vary, which already conditions the entire resulting tree.
Secondly, they do not work well when highly correlated explanatory factors are included
(as is often the case in applications such as ours), since in this situation the tree tends to use
the information from a few variables and to omit the rest of the factors (even if they have
predictive capacity). In the second case, the trees tend not to use all the valid information
available (they use the information inefficiently). Both shortcomings can be addressed using
conditional inference forests (Breiman, 2001; Biau and Scornet, 2016), which will be our
preferred procedure.
3.2. Coditional Inference Forest (CIF)
A random forest model estimates a large number (N) of trees. Each estimated tree follows the procedure described above, but does not use the same sample or includes the same
explanatory factors. A CIF uses the Baggin (bootstrap aggregation) method to generate the
datasets used in each of the N estimated trees. Thus, each tree is estimated using a random
subsample (without replacement) of approximately 2/3 of the original sample (addressing the
first CIT problem commented above), and considers a subsample of M ≤ J randomly permuted
factors at each node (addressing the second CIT problem commented above). The selection
of variables and partitions given the M factors included follows the same procedure as in a
CIT, based on the p-values of the hypothesis tests discussed above. Once all the trees in the
forest have been estimated, the average of the predicted values obtained for each tree is taken
as the result of the prediction for the forest (Hothorn et al., 2004; Meinshausen, 2006): ÊCIF.
Finally, the variance of ÊCIF is calculated, and, as before, its ratio to the total variance is our
measure of IOpE:

.

In the case of the CIF, there are three parameters that are relevant to tune: the α (the same
as in the CIT), the number of trees to include (N) and the number of circumstances that are
randomly permuted in each case (M). The tuning of these parameters is explained in details
in Appendix 1, part C (following Brunori et al., 2023). The tuning parameters are referred as

3.3. Circumstances importance
For both CIT and CIF, there are several ways of calculating the importance of each factor
(circumstance) in predicting the target variable (academic achievement). In a very intuitive
way, the importance of each circumstance is basically calculated by measuring how much
predictive power (accuracy) is lost when each circumstance is removed. The greater the loss
of predictive power when the variable is removed, the greater the importance of that variable
in predicting the target variable (Strobl et al., 2008; Hapfelmeier et al., 2014). This measure
is conditional in the sense that the procedure takes into account the conditional correlation
between all variables. Furthermore, the procedure deals with the presence of missing values
(Hapfelmeier et al., 2014). The results are presented as weights to facilitate interpretation.2
Each weight associated with each variable ranges between 0 and 100 and adds up to 100.

4. Data and variables
4.1. PISA database
The IOpE is measured using data from the 2018 edition of the PISA assessment, conducted by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), in which
a total of 79 countries (39 from Europe) participated. In this edition, all Spanish regions participated in the assessment with an expanded sample to obtain regionally representative data
(MEFP, 2019), which is relevant for our study.
PISA was designed and implemented by the OECD in the late 1990s as a periodic international comparative study of certain student’s characteristics and skills. The target population for this assessment is students aged 15-16, who are completing compulsory education in
most participating countries. PISA assesses performance in three core competencies: science,
reading, and mathematics. It also provides additional information on students’ socioeconomic, family, and school backgrounds through questionnaires administered to students, parents,
teachers, and school principals. This assessment has been repeated every three years since
2000, and although each edition assesses all of the above competencies, each wave focuses
on a specific one.
The level of skill acquisition is measured on a continuous scale with a mean of 500 and a
standard deviation of 100 for the participating OECD countries. As explained earlier, for each
student we have ten scores for each skill, called plausible values. They are randomly drawn
from her score distribution, which is estimated from the responses to each test according to

— Household Wealth Index (Wealth): Is a composite index constructed by the OECD to
capture the level of household wealth based on the possession of material goods in
the household such as: a single room for the student, internet access, the number of
bedrooms, bathrooms, televisions, cars, mobile phones with internet access, computers, tablets and e-books. Household possessions are often used as a proxy for income
because the student often does not know the exact income of the parents (Hanushek
et al., 2020). The index is standardized, with a mean of zero and a standard deviation
of one for OECD countries.
— Number of books at home (Books): The following 4 categories were constructed:
0-25 books, 26-100 books, 100-200 books, and more than 200 books in the home.
— Household Cultural Possessions Index (Cultposs): This is a composite index constructed by the OECD on the basis of students’ responses on the possession in the
household of the following items: books of classical literature; books of poetry;
works of art (e. g. paintings); books of art, music or design; and musical instruments
(e. g. guitar, piano). This index reflects the cultural level of the household. The index is standardized, with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one for OECD
countries.

In addition to the individual characteristics and socioeconomic background of the students, we also consider the characteristics of the school. This is because school choice is generally a decision made by families and is beyond the control of students. However, the type
of school they attend, as well as their environment, can strongly influence their academic performance (Dills, 2005). Following the literature, we have included two school characteristics:
— School type (Pubschool): A dichotomous variable taking value 1 if the school is subsidized (private management and public financing) or private, and a value of 0 if the
institution is public (public management and public financing).
— Peer effect (Peer): To capture the effect of peers on students’ skills and learning, the
average socioeconomic status of school peers is used (Marrero et al., 2024). In this
case, we use the mean of the Economic, Social and Cultural Status (ESCS).4
Since families’ choices of schools for their children are related to the student’s circumstances, we have removed this effect from the school variables (Marrero et al., 2024). To do
this, we independently regress school type and peer effect on the set of circumstances and
take the residuals of each one (i. e., the part orthogonal to the circumstances). These residuals
would capture other factors related to school choice that are independent of socioeconomic
factors, such as the importance or value that families place on their children’s education.

5. Results
The IOpE estimates are obtained for all three competences (reading, mathematics and
science) and we find a great agreement in the IOpE levels and in the ranking of the regions,
regardless of the competence area. For the sake of simplicity, we present the results for science, since it is a hybrid domain and produces results that are generally intermediate between
those of the other two domains. The main results for the other competencies are available
upon request.
The IOpE estimates and the significance of the circumstances are both affected by the
type of sample used. Bootstrapping (random sampling with replacement) and the estimation
of confidence intervals would reduce this concern. Thus, for each estimated CIT and CIF, we
use the bootstrapping procedure to generate the confidence intervals. In our application, there
is an additional complexity because we have 10 plausible values for educational achievement
(PISA scores) for each individual and subject. Appendix 2 shows the detail to consider these
plausible values in the generation of the confidence intervals.

is illustrative to understand the results of our preferred scenario (the tuning parameter scenario).

First, the baseline scenario, which consists of setting a small value of α, α = 0.01, for
CIT and CIF, and a value of M equal to 4 for CIF, which is in line with the “rule of thumb”

given in Appendix 3 (for the CIT), Appendix 4 (for N* in the CIF) and Appendix 5 (α* and
M* in the CIF).

We interpret the large differences observed in the resulting α* between the CIT and CIF
approaches as follows. The CIF approach can make better use of the amount of information
available without running into serious overfitting problems. By tuning the key parameters in
the CIF, we can reduce the downward bias as much as possible, while at the same time being
confident of not incurring serious upward bias. Therefore, among the procedures considered,
we choose the CIF under the parameter tuning scenario as our preferred approach, and the
main results in the following sections are based on this choice.

5.3. The importance of circumstances on educational performance
We measure the importance of each circumstance based on its ability to predict educational performance. We have a measure of the degree of uncertainty in their estimates using
bootstrapping. A priori, the most influential factors have the greatest impact on the IOpE. The
importance of each circumstance is measured as a percentage of the predictive ability of all
circumstances, so the contributions add up to 100 for each region. In terms of which types of
circumstances are most important, we look for common patterns across regions. We also analyse the correlation between circumstance importance and IOpE size across regions. Finally,
we relate these results to the estimated trees under the baseline scenario.
We first emphasize that, contrary to what we obtain for the level of IOpE, the importance
of each circumstance is quite robust to the value of α in CIT and CIF. In general, the groups
of most and least important circumstances remain the same regardless of the value of α. The
results are very robust to the type of scenario and even to the approach (although some differences are observed when comparing the CIT with the CIF). We therefore focus on the results
for our preferred CIF model (parameter tuning).
Figure 4 summarizes, for each circumstance, the values of its importance, ordering the
regions in the same way as in Figure 2 (from highest to lowest IOpE level according to the

highlight the main findings.
First, a common pattern is that the most relevant factor is the number of books at home

and goods at home (HomCulPos and HomPos) and the parents’ occupation (MOcc, FOcc),

6. Conclusions
Inequality of opportunity in educational achievement (IOpE) refers to the part of inequality in learning that is generated by differences in factors (circumstances) given to students,
regardless of their talent and effort. Using the 2018 PISA data, we estimated the IOpE for
Spanish regions by applying ML methods, specifically the conditional inference tree (CIT)
and the forest (CIF). These methods have recently been used in the income inequality literature to overcome the problems of upward and downward bias of traditional methods, which
are based on linear regression. However, to the best of our knowledge, they have not yet been
applied to measure IOpE, and the education literature can benefit from this.
The main result is that our IOpE estimates under our preferred ML specification (CIF
using tuning parameters to minimize out-of-sample MSE) are more than twice the IOpE
estimates under linear regression methods in most regions. On average, the IOpE in science
for our preferred ML specification has a range of variability across regions from 40.5% to
48.7%, with Murcia, Extremadura, and Cataluña at the top, while Castilla-La Mancha and the
Pais Vasco have the lowest levels of IOpE. Using traditional linear regression approaches, estimates of IOpE range from around 16% to 27%. The differences do not seem to be related to
any geographical, economic or socio-cultural pattern. In this regard, the differences could be
attributed to differences in educational policies (e.g. access to early education, scholarships,
the school selection criteria, among others) and educational practices within schools (e.g.
grouping criteria, teaching practices, etc.). The analysis of the causal impact of these factors
on IOpE falls outside the scope of this work, but it is a future line of research.
The results show that the differences in IOpE between Spanish regions are smaller than
those observed between European countries, but also for Spanish regions when using traditional approaches. This result raises the question of whether the substantial differences
observed in IOpE between European countries stem from actual disparities or from the methodology employed. Although differences in IOpE across regions are smaller using ML methods, they are not insignificant. Differences in the cultural environment at home (proxy for
the number of books at home and other cultural possessions at home) is the aspect that contributes most to IOpE in all regions, followed by parental occupation. Other circumstances,
such as gender and migrant status, are relevant in only a few regions. There is no clear pattern
between the importance of circumstances and the level of IOpE.
Another notable result is the higher precision of the estimates of the CIF procedure compared to the CIT procedure. These results suggest that the use of CIF not only overcomes the
problems of downward and upward bias of linear regression techniques, but also provides
more accurate estimates of IOpE. That is, the procedure appears to be able to balance the
magnitude of the two biases and thus provide unbiased estimates of the IOpE given the set of
circumstances included. This result is extremely important, since the first step in designing
effective policies to reduce IOpE is to obtain a good measure of it.
It is important to note that our analysis is focused on providing a more efficient method
for measuring inequality of opportunity in education and is therefore primarily descriptive

Inequality of Opportunity in Education in Spanish Regions: A Machine Learning Approach

135

in nature. Consequently, the results of this research should be viewed as a first step towards
studying the IOpE using ML techniques. Therefore, the findings of this research provide
solid evidence for the use of novel ML in the field of educational inequality. Although it is
not policy-oriented, our work may be relevant in identifying which groups are most vulnerable and at risk of falling behind. If a particular circumstance, such as gender, is found to be
insignificant, this means that differences in educational achievement between its groups (e.g.
boys and girls) are minimal, and there is no need to be concerned about one group falling
behind the other. However, if a circumstance is highly important (e. g. having books at home),
schools and policymakers should implement targeted policies to provide additional support
to students from families with a poorer home cultural environment, preventing them from
falling behind and increasing inequality of opportunity.
Finally, since circumstances cannot be easily influenced by policymaker, it would be
worthwhile to study the role of the channels of transmission in the future (e.g. grade repetition, enjoyment of reading, educational or occupational expectations, technological skills,
etc.). The channels are factors through which differences in circumstances are ultimately
translated into differences in student performance (Marrero et al., 2024). In this regard, investigating the mechanisms behind the IOpE using ML methods is a natural extension of this
paper.

Appendix 1. Methodological details
Part A. The partition approach of the Conditional Inference Tree
The variable selection procedure in conventional decision trees (CART trees) has been
shown to be biased in favour of variables that are continuous or have multiple categories
(Breiman, 2001; Strobl et al., 2007). To overcome this problem, Hothorn et al. (2006) propose an alternative procedure based on so-called recursive binary partitioning, which results
in conditional inference trees (CIT) that do not exhibit these biases. Specifically, the partition
is chosen according to the strength of the association between the explanatory variable and
the target variable, using non-parametric tests (such as chi-squared) to test all possible associations. Briefly, the steps are as follows (see Hothorn et al., 2006, for more details):
1.	First, we test the null hypothesis, Ho, of overall independence between the target variable E and the entire set of circumstances, Φ(E|(C1,... Cj,..., CJ) = Φ(E), where Φ(·)
is the conditional and unconditional distribution function of E. Given a significance
level α, if Ho is not rejected, the procedure ends, and no splitting is made. Note that
this is a way of testing for overall IOpE.
2.	If the Ho is rejected, the p-values of all the independence tests between E and each
of the circumstances Cj,
, are computed, yielding a sequence of
p-values

, one for each circumstance Cj .9

3.	We choose the circumstance Cj* with the smallest p-value, pj*, which represents the
one with the highest association conditional on the node where the tree is located.
4.	For the chosen circumstance, we determine the partition point, , which makes the
groups in the generated split (i. e.,
versus

in terms of E.10
5.	The algorithm is repeated until, for each intermediate node and for each of the circumstances included in the model, we do not reject the Ho of independence. When this
happens, the tree stops growing and the resulting node is called the “terminal node”.
It is the node that allows us to summarize the final output (educational achievement)
of a group of individuals who share a set of common circumstances.
Part B. Regularization the size of the tree
We summarise this procedure as follows:
1.	Set up a grid of possible α values. In practice, we will use a grid ranging from 0.01 to
values below 0.99 (see Appendices 1 and 4 for more details).
2.	Divide the total sample (completely randomly) into K equal parts (5 in our application).

137

3.	Estimate the CIT using a subsample containing observations from K-1 partitions
(training sample).
4.	Predict the target variable E for the K-th omitted partition (the test sample), Êtest, compute the prediction errors, Etest – Êtest, and compute the corresponding (out-of-sample)
MSE.
5.	Repeat this procedure, each time excluding a different partition.
6.	Calculate the mean of the K out-of-sample MSE values obtained.
7.	Repeat steps 2-6 for each α.
8.	Select the α that minimises the mean of the MSEs in the CV, which we denote by α*.
Part C. The tuning approach of parameters in the conditional Inference Forest
The CIF, by considering random subsamples in each tree, already allows us to calculate
the out-of-sample forecast error with the observations it leaves out (out-of-bag, OOB), which
is about 1/3 of the total sample, so it is not necessary to perform a cross-validation exercise.
Thus, for each tree estimated in the CIF, we compute the OOB prediction errors, EOOB – ÊOOB,
and calculate the MSE based on these errors, which are also out-of-sample. The tuning of
these parameters is done in two steps, following Brunori et al. (2023):
1.	A value of N is obtained for which the out-of-sample MSE is practically unchanged,
which we denote as N*. In our case, this value is around 150, i. e., N* = 150.

2.	Given N*, we compute the predictions and associated MSE for a grid of α and M values.
3.	We choose the values of M and α that minimise the OOB MSE, which we denote as
M* and α*.
4.	For M* and α*, and given N*, we estimate and predict the CIF.

With all these parameters fixed, we can retrieve the prediction of all educational achievements for all students in the sample, ÊCIF, calculate its variance and obtain its ratio to the total
variance, var(ÊCIF)/var(E). Note that the fitted values and these variances are calculated for
the whole sample (i. e., they are not out-of-sample predictions). The out-of-sample predictions are only used to calculate the MSE and to refine the key parameters of the CIT and CIF.

Appendix 2. Bootstrapping, plausible values and confidence intervals
For each estimated CIT and CIF, we use the bootstrapping procedure to generate the
empirical distributions and obtain confidence intervals (i. e., the difference between the 5th
and 95th percentiles of the distribution obtained) of the estimated IOpE and the importance
of each circumstance. We perform 200 bootstraps in each case.
In our application, there is an additional complexity because we have 10 plausible values
for educational achievement (PISA scores) for each individual and subject. Report student
performance through plausible values is a standard practice in international surveys such
PISA. For each subject, each student answers a limited number of items from which a distribution is computed. Plausible values are randomly extracted from this distribution of results,
and they represent the entire range of each student’s skill or achievement. Hence, all values
must be considered to estimate any population statistic (OECD, 2019). In our setting, for
each region and subject, any estimator (e. g., mean, percentiles, variance) must be calculated
by computing it separately for each of the ten plausible values and then taking the average.
Thus, for each estimator (e. g., for the IOpE), we estimate the IOpE ten times (one for each
plausible value) and calculate the average. Taking this aspect into account significantly increases the computational time, so we have only considered this aspect in this last step.12 We
therefore proceed as follows:
1.	We start with the first iteration of bootstrapping, nboot = 1.

2.	We estimate the IOpE (and other estimators, such as the importance of each circumstance) for the ten plausible values.
3.	For each estimator, we take the average of the ten estimates, the resulting values being
the realizations for each nboot.
4.	We repeat steps 1-3 for each bootstrap (nboot = 1, ..., 200).

For all 200 realizations, we obtain the empirical distributions of each estimator and calculate the confidence interval as the difference between the 5th and 95th percentiles.

Resumen
La desigualdad de oportunidades en el rendimiento (DOE) mide la importancia de factores fuera del
control del estudiante para explicar las diferencias en el rendimiento académico. Utilizando PISA
2018, estimamos la IOpE para las regiones españolas utilizando árbol de inferencia condicional (CIT)

y bosque (CIF). Utilizando CIF, los DOE duplican a los obtenidos utilizando enfoques tradicionales
(en promedio, 43% frente a 20%). Murcia y Extremadura se encuentran entre las regiones con mayor
IOpE, mientras que Castilla-La Mancha y el País Vasco tienen el IOpE más bajo. Las circunstancias
que más contribuyen al IOpE son el entorno cultural en el hogar (número de libros) y la ocupación de
los padres.
