Background: This study examines the decline in average reading literacy among elementary school students in
European countries between 2016 and 2021, as documented by the Progress in International Reading Literacy
Study (PIRLS). This period was characterized by disruptions caused by COVID-19-related school closures and
changes in student composition, influenced by societal trends found in many European countries.
Aims: We estimated trends in reading literacy adjusted for changes in student composition to assess the relationship between the duration of school closures and school-related learning loss.
Sample: We utilized data from 2016 to 2021 with a sample of N = 187,386 students from 18 European countries/
regions participating in the PIRLS; this involved a representative sample of approximately 5000 fourth-graders
per year and country/region (Age M = 10.28, SD = 0.86; 49.50 % female).
Methods: Our analytical approach employed propensity score weighting to balance changes in student composition between the 2016 and 2021 student cohorts.
Results: Our findings revealed changes in out-of-school learning conditions across most of the 18 countries/regions. The average adjusted decline in reading literacy across all 18 European countries/regions was significantly
lower than the observed decline, but it remained substantial. The expected decline was associated with the
duration of COVID-related school closures, estimated at − 0.0015 SDs per day.
Conclusions: This study sheds new light on how student composition and school-related factors influence trends in
reading literacy. School closures and changes in the composition of student cohorts played a substantial role in
the decline in reading literacy observed between 2016 and 2021.

1. Introduction
Reading literacy is a crucial skill that students should master by the
end of elementary school; it is essential for transitioning from “learning
to read” to “reading to learn.” Studies have identified substantial
learning losses in reading at various grade levels and countries following
the COVID-19 pandemic (Betthäuser et al., 2023; Di Pietro, 2023;
Hammerstein et al., 2021; König & Frey, 2022; Storey & Zhang, 2024).
While the pandemic impacted students across the globe, policy responses varied significantly between countries. Despite numerous
studies on learning losses, there remains a relative scarcity of

comparative analyses into the extent of these losses between countries
and how differing policy responses are associated with the observed
learning losses.
The Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS) assesses fourth graders’ reading literacy worldwide every five years. Between 2016 and 2021, PIRLS found significant declines in reading
literacy in 14 of 18 European countries (Mullis et al., 2023). This period
is particularly noteworthy due to the disruptions caused by the
COVID-19 pandemic, with schools closed for an average of 75 days
across Europe. Researchers have highlighted the differences between
countries regarding the speed of remote education adoption, access to

solely attributed to school closures. Students in 2021 likely experienced
different out-of-school learning conditions compared to their peers in

in reading literacy across European countries, with a focus on COVID19-related school closures as a significant school-related factor. The

difficulty of acquiring reading literacy.
The following review introduces factors that explain why remote
learning during school closures may have been less effective for students’ literacy learning than in-school learning. We examine the potential magnitude of learning loss and findings on COVID-related

explore potential changes to out-of-school learning conditions for student cohorts from 2016 to 2021.
2. Reading literacy
Students’ reading literacy in fourth grade results from domainspecific knowledge and skills acquired over many years (Baumert
et al., 2009). Reading literacy, defined as the capacity to purposefully
extract meaning from written text (Britt et al., 2017), depends on
various foundational precursors. These include language comprehension, decoding fluency, knowledge, and reading strategies (Britt et al.,
2017).
Like other aspects of human development, the acquisition of reading
literacy results from the dynamic interplay between an individual and
their environment, encompassing both in-school and out-of-school influences (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006). As illustrated in Fig. 1, societal trends and events can affect students’ learning conditions in and
out of school.
2.1. Reading literacy acquisition in school
In-school learning plays a crucial role in developing reading literacy
through several interconnected mechanisms (Luyten et al., 2017). There
are at least three key factors that may have hampered the effectiveness
of remote learning during school closures in comparison to in-school
learning prior to the COVID-19 pandemic: (1) time allocated to
learning, (2) social structure, and (3) student-teacher interaction.
Time spent on academic activities in school (such as reading) is a
major predictor of reading literacy learning (Kraft & Novicoff, 2024).

2.2. Reading literacy acquisition outside of school
This study investigates changes in student learning environments
and reading literacy between 2016 and 2021, using Bronfenbrenner’s
ecological systems theory as its conceptual framework. Bronfenbrenner’s theory emphasizes the interplay between the systems that
shape individual development, ranging from immediate environments
(microsystems) to broader societal influences (macrosystems) and
temporal changes (chronosystems). Fig. 1 illustrates the key concepts
and relationships underpinning this study.
At the outermost layer, the chronosystem captures temporal changes
over time, with our focus on the period between 2016 and 2021. Significant societal shifts at the macrosystem level reshaped the educational landscape during this time. Central to these changes was the
COVID-19 pandemic, which caused widespread school closures and
disrupted traditional educational processes. Digitalization also
advanced rapidly, altering access to learning tools and resources (van
der Weel & Mangen, 2022). Immigration patterns further influenced
classroom diversity and student demographics (McAuliffe & Triandafyllidou, 2021), while the expansion of early childhood education
increased participation (European Commission, EACEA & Eurydice,
2019) and potentially affected school readiness for many children.
These macro-level changes directly affected students’ microsystems
and immediate learning environments in and out of school. School
closures during the pandemic reduced the days children spent in school,
while societal shifts outside school led to changes in other important
learning environments: increased participation in early childhood education, school readiness, changing home learning opportunities, and
parental engagement in reading.

2.1.1. Out-of-school days
Kennedy and Strietholt (2023) observed a statistically significant and
substantial negative association between school closures during the
COVID-19 pandemic and the decline in average reading literacy across
countries in PIRLS. Hill et al. (2008) reported an average reading literacy gain of d = 0.40 in fourth grade. This magnitude of progress is
consistent with the reading literacy differences observed between third
and fourth graders in Denmark and between fourth and fifth graders in
Norway, as assessed by PIRLS in 2016 and 2021, respectively
(Steinmann & Olsen, 2022).
For this study, we used a heuristic “gains per day of schooling”
approximation for benchmarking purposes, assuming that the rise in
reading literacy correlates proportionally with the number of school
days in a year. In European countries, students attend school for about
155 days per year on average (European Commission European Education and Culture Executive Agency, 2022). Therefore, the expected incremental learning loss of a day of missed school on reading literacy is
− 0.0026 (0.4/155) SDs.
Similarly, Kuhfeld et al. (2020) applied a heuristic “out-of-school
time” approximation, reviewing studies on learning losses associated
with out-of-school periods like summer breaks. Their review found that
fourth-grade students’ reading achievement declines by − 0.003 SDs per
day of out-of-school time.

2.2.1. Early childhood education and care (ECEC)
In some European countries, participation in non-compulsory ECEC
is linked to better reading literacy by the end of elementary school
(Hogrebe & Strietholt, 2016). ECEC attendance can boost cognitive and
reading precursor skills, such as word identification, at school entry
(Ansari et al., 2020). Preschool code-related and linguistic comprehension skills are associated with later reading comprehension (Hjetland
et al., 2020). Independent of ECEC, early language reading skills
consistently predict later reading abilities (Suggate et al., 2018).
Therefore, making ECEC more accessible and education-focused could
increase participation rates and enhance students’ reading skills at
school entry.
Since 2016, many countries, particularly in the Nordic and Balkan
regions, have broadened ECEC access, formalized educational content,
and improved staff qualifications (European Commission, EACEA &
Eurydice, 2019). Central and Southern European countries have also
transitioned from childcare-type settings to more education-focused
pre-elementary schools around age 3 (European Commission, EACEA
& Eurydice, 2019).
Participation in ECEC and obtaining school-entry skills positively
correlates with fourth-grade reading literacy. Due to efforts to enhance
ECEC accessibility and quality, we anticipate increased ECEC participation and reading-related skills at school entry in many European
countries. As a result, improved ECEC participation and enhanced
reading-related skills at school entry might have positively impacted the
average reading literacy of student cohorts in 2021.

2.1.2. COVID-related reading literacy loss in Europe
Several studies have investigated the learning losses observed after
the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic (Betthäuser et al., 2023; Di Pietro,
2023). A meta-analysis on COVID-related learning loss in reading found
a median decline of d = − 0.09 across various grades (1–11), comparison
periods, and countries (Betthäuser et al., 2023). Eight studies reported
results for students under 12 years old in European countries participating in PIRLS (Italy, Denmark, Belgium, Netherlands, Sweden, and
Germany) approximately one year into the pandemic; they found no
statistically significant learning loss on average. Di Pietro (2023) found
in their moderator-analysis that learning losses in European countries (i.
e., Belgium, Czech Republic, Germany, Italy, Netherlands, Norway,
Slovenia, and Spain) were 0.193 SD lower than in non-European countries (US, UK, China, Mexico), completely mitigating the overall learning

controlling for various family background factors (Rauch et al., 2017).
There are significant differences in immigration trends across European countries. Most southeastern and eastern European countries have
lower levels of immigration compared to other subregions of Europe. In
contrast, immigration trends in northern, western, and southern Europe
have been marked by greater net immigration, with a significant portion
being internal immigration within the EU (McAuliffe & Triandafyllidou,
2021). The arrival of relatively large numbers of immigrants and refugees to Europe in 2015 initiated a period lasting until about 2018 of
increased extra-European immigration, particularly affecting Austria,
Germany, Denmark, and Sweden (McAuliffe & Triandafyllidou, 2021).
Both intra-European and extra-European immigration is characterized
by families who speak a language different from the majority language

typically associated with lower reading literacy in the language of instruction. We anticipate that the proportion of students speaking a

2.2.3. Socioeconomic situation
The socioeconomic situation describes the resources available to a
family, and the socioeconomic situation of students’ families is associated with their academic success. According to the family investment
model (Conger & Donnellan, 2007), the resources (e.g., money, time,
knowledge) available to parents and their use (e.g., time spent on joint
activities with the child) are considered investments that have the potential to enhance children’s cognitive skills and language proficiency
(Conger & Donnellan, 2007). In line with these assumptions, families in
socioeconomically affluent situations provide their children with better
material resources at home, such as a dedicated place for learning or
books (Avvisati, 2020).
There has not been a general economic downturn in European
countries since 2016; the development of the average socioeconomic
situation has been favorable (Eurostat, 2022). However, despite the
overall improvement in the average socioeconomic situation, European
immigration has been characterized by the arrival of relatively young,
socioeconomically disadvantaged families. Consequently, the socioeconomic situation of young students differs from the overall socioeconomic development of the country, which may have seen changes to the
average socioeconomic situation of students between 2016 and 2021.
In summary, disadvantaged students tend to exhibit lower reading
literacy. Although the overall socioeconomic situation in Europe has
improved in recent years, it is uncertain to what extent families of fourth

2.2.4. Books at home
The number of books at home has been used to indicate the socioeconomic situation of students in a particular country in a student’s
cohort (Avvisati, 2020). The number of books at home shows substantial
and significant associations with students’ reading literacy (Heppt et al.,
2022). In most countries, the number of books shows a moderate decline
(Avvisati, 2020). Rather than indicating socioeconomic decline, this
development is more likely to be associated with the diminishing relevance of printed books.
2.2.5. Parents read behavior
Another factor concerns parents’ literacy behavior; empirical evidence exists for a relationship between parents and children’s reading
behavior. Avid readers tend to have parents who are avid readers
themselves (Pfost et al., 2016). Several mechanisms have been suggested
for the intergenerational transmission of reading activities
(Hemmerechts et al., 2017). At first, parents who read act as role models
that children may imitate later. Secondly, beyond acting as a role model,
parents who read might provide more and better support in reading by
providing opportunities for reading. Thirdly, parents may also affect
students’ reading activities through communication and interactions,
such as family book talk or book recommendations.
Digitization has introduced many alternatives to activities that previously required reading and/or access to printed materials (e.g.,
books). These alternatives include audio and visual formats for entertainment, learning, and information, such as podcasts, audiobooks, online workshops, and instructional videos. Consequently, it is reasonable
to assume that the parents of students in the 2021 cohort read less than
the parents of the 2016 cohort.
In summary, the presence of printed books at home and parents who
read are associated with higher reading literacy in students. However,
due to digitalization, the availability of books and the extent to which
parents read may have decreased. This decline may have contributed to
the lower average reading literacy of the 2021 student cohort.

The PIRLS is an international trend study that tracks students’
average reading literacy over time. To accurately assess the impact of
COVID-related learning loss, the composition of student cohorts must
remain comparable across different years. To address this, we analyzed
changes in students’ out-of-school learning conditions and adjusted the
trend to reflect a scenario where these conditions are consistent across
both cohorts (as illustrated in Fig. 2). We then correlated the observed
and adjusted trends with the duration of school closures, offering a
clearer understanding of how these disruptions influenced reading literacy trends.

3.2. RQ2: How large was the decline in reading literacy between 2016
and 2021 in European countries after adjusting for changes in out-ofschool learning conditions?
Changes in out-of-school learning conditions can be intertwined with
the effects of school closures. We expect that the presumed primary
negative impacts of immigration and digitalization on average reading
literacy will outweigh the potential positive effects of ECEC. Consequently, the decline in the adjusted trend should be smaller than the
observed decline.

4.2. Missing data
The proportion of missing responses varied depending on the country, year, and respondent group. The average missing rate per student
variable was 1.5 % in 2016 and 2.0 % in 2021. The rate of missing
student responses in all countries in both years was below 4 %, except in
Germany, where the rate was about 13 % in both 2016 and 2021. The
average missing rate of parent responses was about 10 % in 2016, and
increased slightly to 15 % in 2021. This increase was mainly driven by
higher missing rates in the Netherlands, Sweden, and Germany, which
rose from about 46 %, 14 %, and 28 % in 2016 to 53 %, 42 %, and 44 %
in 2021, respectively. For all other countries, the missing rate of parental
answers remained well below 20 % in both years.

3.3. RQ3: Is the adjusted reading literacy trend associated with the
duration of school closures?
The adjusted decline in average reading literacy should predominantly reflect school-related factors. Therefore, we expect that the
adjusted decline will be correlated with the number of days that schools
were closed. If school closure days are a complete loss for learning, we
anticipate a decline of approximately − 0.003 SDs per day of school
closure, based on the “out-of-school time” and “gains per day”
hypotheses.

4.3. Material

4.1. Participants

4.3.1. Reading literacy
The PIRLS test consists of text passages ranging from 425 to 908
words and test items based on these texts. Each student reads two texts
and works on approximately 30 test items within 90 min, with a 10-min
break (von Davier et al., 2023). The reliability of the reading literacy test
for European countries was Releap = .88 in both 2016 and 2021.

We utilized data from 2016 to 2021 from 18 European countries/
regions taking part in the PIRLS. The analysis used data from N =
187,386 students with an average age of M = 10.28 (SD = 0.86), of
which 49.50 % were female, as well as data from their parents and
school principals (von Davier et al., 2023). We used data that allowed for
an uncompromised trend estimation with a testing period between

between 2016 and 2021. The distribution of the “ECEC enrollment
under three” variable changed in Denmark and Belgium (fr.), and the

respective variables for the respective countries. Second, we calibrated
international item parameters using non-missing responses for each
construct separately with a multi-group generalized partial credit model
in TAM (Robitzsch et al., 2024). Third, we drew five plausible variables
for each country and year separately, including all other variables as
conditioning variables. This procedure resulted in five plausible values
that simultaneously function like multiple imputations, as the missing
responses are imputed based on the conditioning model. The expected
posterior (EAP) reliability ranged between Releap = .60 for essential
conditions for learning and Releap = .85 for school-entry skills.

5. Results
5.1. Changes in out-of-school learning conditions
In the first step of our analysis, we examined changes in out-of-school
learning environments between 2016 and 2021. Fig. 3 provides an
overview of the changes in the variables under consideration for each
country. Overall, ECEC participation and school-entry reading skills
significantly increased. However, there were notable declines in the
language of instruction, parental reading engagement, the availability of
printed books, and essential learning conditions. On average, in Europe,
we found a statistically significant increase in ECEC participation, d =
0.13, se = 0.01, p < .001, and school-entry reading skills, d = 0.03, se =
0.01, p < .001. This aligns with the expressed political effort to expand
ECEC in terms of accessibility and quality. However, there is a considerable variation between countries (See Fig. 3).
We found a statistically significant decrease in the language of instruction spoken, d = − 0.14, se = 0.01, p < .001, parents who enjoy
reading, d = − 0.09, se = 0.01, p < .001, presence of printed books at
home d = − 0.09, se = 0.01, p < .001, and essential learning conditions,
d = − 0.09, se = 0.01, p < .001. Among these, the language of instruction
spoken at home decreased stronger than the other out-of-school learning
conditions. The findings are in line with the presumed immigration and
digitalization trends.

4.3.3. Duration of school closures
The data on duration of school closures across countries were obtained from UNESCO’s UIS and are publicly available online (https://en.
unesco.org/covid19/educationresponse). The database categorizes days
between February 2020 and March 2022 for each country into “Fully
closed,” “Partially closed,” “Fully open,” or “Academic break.” Fully
closed was defined as government mandates requiring schools to close,
affecting most or all students. For our analysis, we counted the “Fully
closed” days between February 2020 and March 2021 (i.e., when the
PIRLS assessment started).

5.2. Adjusted reading literacy trend

4.4. Analysis

In the second step, we compared changes in reading literacy across
countries between 2016 and 2021. We report the observed differences in
the samples and the adjusted differences using propensity score
weighting to account for previously observed disparities in out-of-school
conditions. We found substantial differences between the observed and
adjusted trends, suggesting that the observed differences between 2016
and 2021 were partially due to changes in out-of-school learning conditions. After accounting for these differences by applying propensity
score weights, the average adjusted decline in reading literacy across all
European countries, d = − 0.11, se = 0.01, p < .001, was notably lower
than the observed decline, d = − 0.16, se = 0.01, p < .001, although it
remained statistically significant (Table 1). There are large differences in
how the adjustments affect changes in individual countries. In some
countries, the adjusted trends are more prominent; in others, they are
smaller; and in others, the adjustment has little effect (See Fig. 4).
Only 9 of the 18 countries/regions showed a statistically significant
decline with the adjusted trend. Denmark, Germany, Spain, and Sweden
observed a statistically significant decline before but not after the
adjusted out-of-school learning conditions in the 2016 and 2021 student
cohorts. In Sweden, however, the adjusted effect size, d = − 0.01, se =
0.04, p = .462, was statistically significantly lower than the observed
trend, d = − 0.16, se = 0.04, p < .001.

Pursuing the three main objectives (RQ1), we aimed to investigate
changes in out-of-school learning conditions between the 2016 and 2021
student cohorts using Cohen’s d effect size comparisons. The trends
between 2016 and 2021 were computed using the plausible values of the
out-of-school learning conditions, applying student sampling weights,
and utilizing the R package BIFIE.survey (BIFIE & Oberwimmer, 2022).
The standard errors were calculated using the jackknife replication
method (von Davier et al., 2023). The averages for European countries
and regions and their standard errors were calculated as simple composite averages (see Appendix D, Formula 1).
Subsequently, (RQ2) we employed propensity score weighting
(Fuentes et al., 2022) to balance out-of-school learning conditions between the 2016 and 2021 student cohorts and estimate the adjusted
trends with each plausible value. We used a logistic regression to
determine the likelihood of each student being a student in 2021. Students with out-of-school learning conditions that were more prevalent in
2021 received a higher probability and vice versa. We generated weights
using the inverse probability of being a student in 2021 for students who
were actually students in 2021 and the probability of being a student in
2021 for students in 2016. The weight was trimmed at the 99th
percentile and multiplied with the sampling weight of the PIRLS
(Ridgeway et al., 2015). The resulting weight was used to calculate the
adjusted trend. A detailed description of the process and robustness is
provided in Appendix C.

6.1. Changes in out-of-school learning conditions

6. Discussion

We investigated changes in out-of-school learning conditions that are
linked to broader societal changes, including (i) efforts to expand ECEC,
(ii) immigration, and (iii) digitalization. In line with the presumed
expansion of ECEC, participation rates and skills at school entry
increased statistically significantly on average across European countries. However, the average increase in ECEC participation and reading
skills at school entry was very small, presumably due to European ECEC
policies’ dispersed nature (Kascak & Koch, 2023). The effects of the
expansion of the ECEC on Europe’s average reading literacy might be
marginal at best.
In line with presumed immigration trends, we found an increase in
students who speak a language at home that differs from the language of
instruction and are socioeconomically disadvantaged. The trend of students speaking different languages at home than in school was relatively
strong and notably consistent: 14 countries reported an increase, while
only four showed no change or a decrease. More students may have had

6.4. Limitations
6.4.1. Unobserved variables
For this study, we attempted to acknowledge the most relevant aspects of student composition. However, we could not consider parental
education and occupation, for example. This is because the trend estimation for these factors was inconclusive, with countries showing unlikely changes in distribution between 2016 and 2021. Unlike the out-ofschool learning conditions, the questions on parental education and
occupation were structured differently in different countries and years.
Consequently, we opted to exclude these variables from this analysis.
We also could not fully consider the impact of digital learning resources in our empirical analyses. E-books and audiobooks can be
important learning resources and reflect an appreciation for reading and
literature in students’ families. However, research suggests that digital
reading habits for leisure are negatively associated with reading
comprehension in elementary students, while print reading habits for
leisure are positively associated (Altamura et al., 2025). Unlike printed
books, digital devices (i.e., tablets and smartphones) are not straightforward indicators of learning resources at home or socioeconomic situations due to their multi-functionality and presence in almost all
European households. Nonetheless, technology-delivered literacy instruction is effective, and access to it is crucial (Dahl-Leonard et al.,
2024). Our study, however, does not consider the possible positive or
negative effects of changes in access to and use of digital learning aids at
home from 2016 to 2021 on reading literacy.

6.2. Adjusted reading literacy trend
We investigated whether changes in out-of-school learning conditions in the 2021 student cohort compared to 2016 could explain the
observed declines in average reading literacy. We applied a propensity
score procedure to derive a decline in reading literacy under equal outof-school learning conditions in 2021 and 2016. After adjusting, the
average decline in reading literacy in European countries was about onethird smaller, indicating that the less favorable out-of-school learning
conditions explain a part of the decline in reading literacy.
Regarding effect sizes, the adjusted estimated decline was d = − 0.11.
In contrast to meta-analytic results, which suggested no learning losses
for reading in European elementary schools (Betthäuser et al., 2023; Di
Pietro et al., 2024), we found a substantial learning loss in elementary
education reading in European countries. The effect size of d = − 0.11 for
the overall decline is consistent with previous findings (median effect
size was d = − 0.09) on learning losses for reading, considering different
grades (1–11), comparison periods, and non-European countries
(Betthäuser et al., 2023). Many countries with large learning losses and
extended durations of school closures, such as Austria, Bulgaria,
Finland, and Poland, were not included in the meta-analysis, and
countries with slight learning loss, such as Sweden, Spain, and the Czech
Republic, were. This highlights the central role of the duration of school
closures, particularly when comparing learning loss between countries
and regions.
Sweden showed no decline after adjusting for changes in out-ofschool learning conditions, highlighting the unique policy of not closing schools during the pandemic, and also aligning with national studies
(Hallin et al., 2022). Consequently, Sweden’s observed decline in
reading literacy is likely due to the increased number of students with
difficult out-of-school learning conditions. Educational policymakers in
Sweden should focus on these students to mitigate learning loss and
social disparity.

6.4.2. Missing data
An essential challenge in analyzing international large-scale assessment data is dealing with missing values. Countries vary in their policies
regarding the mandatory completion of student, parent, and principal
questionnaires; Germany, the Netherlands, and Sweden had low
response rates in the parental questionnaires. This variability in
response rates can introduce biases and affect the reliability of the data,
making it difficult to draw accurate comparisons and conclusions across
different countries. However, by applying a plausible value procedure
that uses auxiliary variables, we were able to choose an appropriate
statistical method for handling the missing data (Hardt et al., 2012).
6.4.3. Partial school closures, quality of, and access to remote learning
There are many aspects of the COVID-related restrictions that were
potentially relevant to learning loss in the diverse educational systems of
European countries, including partial school closures and access to and
quality of remote learning. Some countries had substantial periods of
school closures in parts of the country, hybrid learning, or limited
opening hours and services. The UNESCO statistics recorded this as
“partial school closures.” Previous research has shown that the effects of
school closures on learning losses remain consistent regardless of how
school closures are measured (Kennedy & Strietholt, 2023). In our
analysis, partial school closures had no additional explanatory value
over complete school closures.

and access to digital learning played a role in learning loss. However, we

6.3. Association between the adjusted reading literacy trend and duration

We investigated the link between the number of school closure days
and the adjusted decline in reading literacy. The meta-regression results
indicated that after adjusting for changes in the composition of student
cohorts, the expected decline with no days of school closures was very
close to zero. This suggests that changes in student composition and
school closures explain the overall decline in reading literacy observed
across European countries and regions.
The overall rate of decline of − 0.0015 SDs per day was only about
half as large as the “out-of-school time” estimate of − 0.003 SD per day
(Kuhfeld et al., 2020) and the “gains per day of schooling” estimate of

Further, the out-of-school learning conditions of students in Europe

reading literacy learning. School systems in European countries need to
adapt to a student population with access to fewer resources in terms of
social, mental, and financial support and educational role models

work with but do not rely on parental support. These measures are
necessary for stabilizing reading literacy, mitigating disparities, and
better preparing for the diverse needs of today’s students and those to
come.
Future longitudinal studies should examine recovery trajectories, indepth analyses of differences between political responses between and
within European countries, and digital learning interventions that could
build on the study’s findings, particularly in the context of the specific
needs of different student groups.

7. Conclusion
Our study demonstrates that the decline in reading literacy among
fourth graders in European countries between 2016 and 2021 primarily
resulted from extended school closures. The shift to home-based remote
learning did not fully compensate for in-person in-school learning. More
extended closure periods resulted in more considerable learning losses.
Future events such as new pandemics, extreme weather, strikes, energy
shortages, or fiscal stress may force policymakers to reconsider school
closures. This study provides robust evidence of the expected learning
loss associated with school closures on students’ literacy on a national
scale. Policymakers can use these results regarding learning loss and its
associated long-run economic costs and impact on social disparity
(Hanushek and Woessmann, 2020) to plan remediation interventions
(Kaffenberger, 2021)—such as summer schools (Gambi & De Witte,
2024)—to address the consequences of school closures. Policymakers

Appendix C
1. Propensity score weighting
We chose propensity score weighting to adjust the reading literacy trend because (1) it can be easily combined with the statistical approaches used
in the PIRLS study, allowing us to directly compare published PIRLS results with the adjusted results from our study, and (2) it is intuitively clear that
reweighting the student population adjusts for compositional changes within the student population.
1.1. Specification of the propensity score model
The propensity score ei is the probability of being a student in 2021 Zi = 1 given the six out-of-school learning conditions (k) and their second-order
interactions (i.e., covariates) Xi.
F1 : ei = PR(Zi = 1 |Xi )
We estimate ei using logistic regression with Zi as a dependent variable, regressed on the six out-of-school learning conditions.
F2 : ei = β0 + β1…6 x1…6 + β7…22 (x1…6 × x1…6 )
Note. (a) School-entry reading skills, (b) ECEC Enrolment, (c) Speaking the test language at home, (d) Essential conditions for learning, (e) Printed
books at home, (b) Parents who enjoy reading.
The regression is weighted using sampling weights that weight the 2016 and 2021 cohorts equally (i.e., SENWGT; Ridgeway et al., 2015). This
means the logistics regression intercepts are defined as logit = 0 (i.e., probability 50 %) even if the population size changed between 2016 and 2021.
The C-statistic or Area Under the ROC Curve ranges between 0.59 in Italy and 0.73 in Sweden (See Fig. 1). A value of 0.5 means that students in 2016
and 2021 have equal learning conditions. A value of 1 means the out-of-school learning conditions are uniquely different in 2021 and 2016.
1.2. Distributional overlap between years of propensity score
We assessed the distributional overlap of the propensity score between the 2016 and 2021 cohorts and noted a substantial overlap between them
(See Fig. 1). A smaller overlap in the propensity score distribution indicates a reasonable degree of change in student composition. Propensities close to

1.3. Calculation of the propensity score weights
Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) uses the propensity score as a weight. The weights for subject i is defined as:
F3 :

IPTW takes the inverse probability of being a student in 2021 and being a student in 2021 (1/propensity score for students in 2021 and 1/
(1− propensity score) for students in 2016. We routinely applied a 99th percentile weight truncation, although there were few extreme weights.
Finally, we multiplied the IPTW truncated at the 99th percentile with the sampling weight (i.e., SENWGT; Ridgeway et al., 2015).
1.4. Evaluation of balance
Applying IPTW led to a balance among the out-of-school learning conditions (See Fig. 2). There is no statistically significant difference in out-ofschool learning conditions between the reweighted populations of 2016 and 2021. This balance of covariates suggests that the propensity score model
specification was adequate.

1.5. Interpretation of adjusted trend estimates
The observed trend is calculated as the difference between μ2021− μ2016, where the population is composed of students i with different out-of-school

learning conditions X. Students with a particular set of out-of-school learning conditions (i.e., students with average conditions across all six dimensions) have an expected reading literacy E(X). Reading literacy varies by σ to some degree, even if the out-of-school learning conditions are equal.
The expected values and the variance can differ between years. Reweighting the population does not change the expected values of students with a
particular set of out-of-school learning conditions or the variance from those expected values. However, it equalizes the proportion of representation in
the population. This adjustment can alter the population mean and the standard deviation. Fig. 3 illustrates the reweighting with three out-of-school
learning conditions (X1, X2, and X3). In the example, the observed trend is μ2021− μ2016 = − 26, and the adjusted μ′2021− μ′2016 = − 14.
