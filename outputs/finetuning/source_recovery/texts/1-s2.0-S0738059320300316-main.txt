Educational reform programs usually institute systemwide changes. However, this assumption of similar school
problems mask variations between schools and students. The 2018 Philippine PISA data (n = 7233) reveal
salient variations in academic achievement that happen between schools and among students. Between schools,
material resource and school climate problems have diﬀerent origins and inﬂuence depending on school type and
location, while among students, gender and socioeconomic status act as predictors but fail to fully explain the
variation. This has implications for school-speciﬁc investments for material resource and school climate improvement, and student-speciﬁc interventions that detect and instruct those who are lagging behind.

1. Introduction
Improvements and reforms in educational systems are often thought
of as institutional top-down changes that need to be consistent in order
to be eﬀective. System- and nationwide programs are often directed at
increasing school participation, providing easier access to education,
and promoting quality instruction (Kremer, 2003). In developing
countries, these systemwide reforms often increase likelihood of school
participation but do not always have clear positive eﬀects on student
achievement, measured through grades or test scores (Ganimian and
Murnane, 2016). Interventions that address education quality have
mixed outcomes, with some programs increasing students’ knowledge,
learning, and achievement while others being unable to attain this goal
(Mbiti, 2016).
Although studies show the eﬀectiveness of physical and human resources, better school climate, and improved pedagogy (Glewwe and
Muralidharan, 2016; Masino and Niño-Zarazúa, 2016), such interventions often have to be adjusted according to social and cultural contexts
presented by diﬀerent developing nations (Chimombo, 2005). Additionally, salient diﬀerences are discernible within national educational
systems that make certain allocations and interventions more urgent
and important with particular school types, locations, and populations
(Trinidad, 2019). In the Philippines, these contextual factors have not
been closely scrutinized due to the scarcity of open and representative
datasets that account for diﬀerences in students’ academic outcomes

(Baleos et al., 2017).
In 2018, however, the Philippines’ participation in the PISA (or the
Programme for International Student Assessment) presented an opportunity to investigate a representative sample of 15-year-old students,
and the various factors associated with their achievement, measured
through standardized reading and math test scores. I argue that
achievement variations between schools and between students help illustrate and identify where school and program investments can be
directed. In particular, the level of material resource constraints and
school climate/discipline concerns are varied for public vs. private,
high vs. low socioeconomic status, and rural vs. urban schools. In addition to school variations, students in the same school also vary in
terms of test achievement, which are only partially explained by gender
and socioeconomic status.
Thus, the research explores implications of these variations, and
how these insights can contribute to a more nuanced understanding of
Philippine education. Although this particular research does not so
much answer why student achievement in the Philippines is low, it
explores the sources of achievement variations and what can be done to
more fully address these inequalities.

2.1. Resources and school climate in developing countries
In the context of the Philippines and other low middle-income developing countries, education is an important concern because of the
relationship and impact of one’s educational outcomes on one’s economic success, and from a macro-perspective, a country’s educational
achievement on its economic health (Buchmann and Hannum, 2001).
For this reason, many developing countries focus on programs and
policies that target primary and secondary education (Glewwe, 2002).
In these developing countries, investments in secondary schools are
often in terms of economic incentives, health interventions, school infrastructure, and school programs (Petrosino et al., 2012). To help increase students’ education access, countries institute economic interventions, such as conditional cash transfers, scholarship programs and
school vouchers (Angrist et al., 2006; Catubig and Villano, 2017). Although it is one accomplishment to get students to enroll and attend
school, it is a whole other issue for them to learn basic and more advanced skills taught in school. In recent reviews, these programs that
target enrolment and aim to increase secondary school participation
have been documented to be eﬀective while greater variability exists
for programs that try to improve learning (Banerjee et al., 2013; Null
et al., 2017). They ﬁnd very few rigorous evaluations of school inputs or
characteristics—such as length of school day, class size, ability
grouping, and private education—and most of them are limited to a
single country or context (Null et al., 2017).
Ganimian and Murnane (2016) discuss factors that help with increasing students’ achievement and skills acquisition, inclusive of material resources and teacher incentives. Investing in functional school
infrastructure, quality learning materials, and resources that aﬀect the
daily experience of students and teachers have positive consequences
for student achievement (Della Sala et al., 2017; Duﬂo, 2001). In addition to physical resources, the education system’s human resources
also play a crucial role in students’ academic outcomes. In developing
countries where teachers may be absent or do not devote enough time
on instructional tasks, incentives and accountability structures are
considered very important (Duﬂo et al., 2012). In these examples,
physical and human resources play crucial roles in the presence of
better outcomes for students.
Another set of factors that need to be investigated in developing
countries is in terms of school climate, which approximates students’
experience of a school in terms of its academic rigor, social cohesion,
and perception of safety (Wang and Degol, 2016). In more developed
OECD (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development)
countries, most students report positive teacher-student relations, orderly classrooms, and cooperative learning environments. However, for
developing non-OECD countries, there is greater variability with some
countries like Kosovo and Vietnam having more supportive school and
disciplinary climates while others like Brazil and Tunisia having less
positive school and disciplinary climates (OECD, 2013, 2016). Interventions that address school climate often focus on factors that improve
the academic and social environment, such as promoting interpersonal
connectedness, positive discipline, student engagement, and teacher
professional development (Mitchell and Bradshaw, 2013; Libbey,
2004). Having a positive school climate is also associated with positive
educational outcomes, and can even mitigate the negative impact of
disadvantage on poor students (Thapa et al., 2013; Berkowitz et al.,
2017). In particular for middle-income developing countries, the
school’s disciplinary climate is signiﬁcantly associated with improved
learning outcomes (Lockheed et al., 2015). Thus, in addition to school
resources, school climate oﬀers a crucial lens for understanding differences in students’ achievement.
Although material, human, and school climate resources are important for student achievement, far less documented is how these resources are needed diﬀerently by diverse schools and students,

2.2. Philippine education system
The Philippine education system is a product of factors such as
colonialism, cultural exigencies, and demands for global competitiveness. Given the country’s history of being occupied by Spanish,
American, and Japanese forces, its current education organization reﬂects these inﬂuences through the prevalence of Catholic schools,
universal basic education, English language use in instruction, and the
awareness of the country’s position in Asia and Southeast Asia (de
Guzman, 2003d). In 2012, the Philippines launched the K-12 Program,
one of its most comprehensive education reforms that changed the
previous ten-year basic education (six years in primary school and four
years in high school) to the current twelve years in addition to kindergarten: six years of primary school, four years of junior high school,
and two years of senior high school (Okabe, 2013). Such reforms were
instituted to make students more prepared for higher education, more
employable straight out of senior high school, and more competitive
with students from other countries (Sergio, 2012).
Despite these reforms, however, the country’s system is still plagued
with perennial problems, such as the inadequacy of instructional materials, the lack or disrepair of learning facilities, and the insuﬃciency
of teacher training programs and professional advancement opportunities (Durban and Catalan, 2012). Reforms to improve student learning
and school quality have often been met with curriculum diﬃculties,
inadequate teacher preparation, and weak implementation (Bernardo
and Mendoza, 2009). The government’s under-investment in public
education has also contributed to the country’s dismal performance in
previous international tests like the 1999 and 2003 Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study, and in other standardized tests
that try to measure students’ academic performance (Adarlo and
Jackson, 2017; Okabe, 2013). Although quality in education is still an
important concern, one positive trend in recent years has been the increase in school participation and decline of dropout rates in primary
and secondary schools (Albert and Raymundo, 2016).
Aside from school and curricular concerns, certain segments of the
population are also more likely to be disadvantaged by the current
school system. Students in rural and public schools often have lower
achievement than those in urban and private schools, brought about by
both school and demographic factors (Lockheed and Zhao, 1993). On
average, girls also perform better than boys, in terms of academic
achievement and school completion (Albert, 2016). Parents and communities also play a role since parental participation in decision-making
is an important factor for the school’s improvement (World Bank and
Australian Aid, 2016). Although these diﬀerent factors inﬂuence the
Philippine education system, past research and assessment assume that
such problems are experienced similarly across diﬀerent schools and
also similarly impact students across diﬀerent backgrounds.
However, it is not just important to ask what factors contribute to
the country’s education problems but to ask how these factors are
variably inﬂuencing and aﬀecting schools of diﬀerent types, and students of diﬀerent backgrounds. With the Philippine data from PISA, or
the Programme for International Student Assessment, such an analysis
of variations in achievement outcomes can now be investigated more
thoroughly and systematically.
2.3. Programme for International Student Assessment
With the Philippines’ 2018 participation in PISA, the available data
can uncover relationships between diﬀerent factors contributing to
academic achievement. Although PISA is often thought of as a ranking
of countries according to reading, math, and science test scores of 152

teacher, and principal views on education, motivation, demographics,
and possible factors aﬀecting education. For 2018, more than 600,000

measured students’ ability to apply things learned from academic subjects onto real-life problems. Most were computer-based tests that allocated an hour for reading, and half an hour each for math and science.
The test’s language was determined by the oﬃcials in the country, with
most using the most common language in the country or the language
of instruction (Schleicher, 2019).
More than simply the test and ranking, this triennial test also asked
survey questions regarding various factors that relate to schooling in a
country. Students were asked about family demographics, and views on
reading, life, their school, learning time, and wellbeing. In addition,
teachers were asked about their background, education, professional
development, beliefs, and practices while principals were asked about
school background, teaching staﬀ, school management, and school
climate (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development,
2019). The availability of these measures has led to studies that compare diﬀerent countries, schools, and student experiences. Since this is
the Philippines’ ﬁrst time to join PISA, this availability also opens up
ways of understanding factors aﬀecting Filipino students’ academic
outcomes.

This study explored how resource and social factors within the
school inﬂuence students’ achievement, and thus, four variables were
computed to approximate the diﬀerences in resources and social climates between schools. Composite indices were created by performing
Principal Component Analysis to derive factor scores for each school
(Abdi and Williams, 2010). This process combined individual scores
that principals made in terms of how diﬀerent factors were hindering
instruction and student learning. Ranging from 1 to 4 (from “not at all”
hindering instruction to hindering it “a lot”), these scores were combined because creating an index was psychometrically more reliable
than just relying on single items.
After determining which individual scores can be combined, I
checked the Cronbach alpha and the percentage of variance explained
by the new composite variables, and these composite variables had
acceptable levels on both criteria. Four composite or index variables
were created from the individual scores through the Principal
Component Analysis, and the scores have been standardized with a
mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. Two were about common
resource constraints while another two were about school climate
deﬁcits. Table 1 shows the items included for each of the four variables
on resource constraints and school climate deﬁcit.
3.2.1. Resource constraints
One composite variable was about the material resource constraint
in a school, that includes the school’s lack and poor quality of educational materials and physical infrastructure, all rated in the four-point
Likert scale. The other composite variable was one that approximated
the problem of teaching staﬀ, particularly the quantitative lack of them
and the principal’s perception of their qualiﬁcations.

3. Data and methods
Data for this research came from the 2018 PISA, obtained through
the public use dataset available from the OECD website. The dataset for
this research was limited to the 7233 15-year old high school students
from the Philippines, 3365 males and 3868 females, who were part of
187 public and private schools chosen to represent the country’s school
system. However, a limitation exists as selection eﬀects may be present,
given that not all 15-year-olds were in formal schools. In 2017, the
Philippine Statistics Authority estimated that 9.1 percent of school-age
Filipino students are considered out-of-school (Pilar, 2018).
The dataset combined the student-level questionnaire and the
school-level questionnaire that was answered by the school’s principal.
The student-level data had standardized test scores, demographic details,
and Likert-scale questions on beliefs and behaviors while the schoollevel data had the demographic, resource, and climate proﬁle of the
schools.

3.2.2. School climate deﬁcit
To approximate the school’s social climate, I created two composite
variables that looked at problems in student attendance and student
discipline. The attendance variable was composed of principal’s assessment of the problem of absenteeism and students’ skipping classes.
The discipline variable was composed of the assessment for problems in
terms of respect, attention, alcohol/illegal drugs, and bullying. Such
composite variables for school climate deﬁcit were computed to be

3.1. Dependent variables
The two dependent variables were the standardized reading and
math test scores from the PISA test. For each test and student, PISA
reports ten plausible values but none of these values is the actual score
of the student. Rather, these represent ten random values drawn from
the posterior distribution of the students’ scores (Jerrim et al., 2017).
This means, that to obtain unbiased estimates, I had to run the same
regression model ten times, with each of the plausible value of the
scores, and compute for the unbiased estimates based on these sets of
estimates. However, the results generated from the regressions were
almost identical such that similar to what others have done and for
simplicity, I reported only the estimates from the ﬁrst plausible value
(Spiezia, 2010).
The standardized reading and math test score means for the sample
of Filipino students were 338.56 and 351.60, respectively. For comparison, the OECD average for these two tests were 487 and 489, respectively (Schleicher, 2019). Although one must note that the Philippines is not part of OECD, the mean test scores are concerning, given
that the Philippines was ranked the lowest country in reading and the
second lowest in math (Schleicher, 2019).

4.1. Correlations
Table 2 shows the correlations between the diﬀerent variables and
indices. Standardized reading and math test scores were highly positively correlated at 0.86. Aside from their correlation with each other,
three other variables were also signiﬁcantly and positively correlated
with both test score variables. First, socioeconomic status (SES) was
correlated to both test scores, at 0.40 and 0.36 respectively. Second, a
signiﬁcant positive correlation can be discerned between being in a
private school and performance in the tests. Third, being female also
had a statistically signiﬁcant association with the test scores, although
the correlation is relatively higher for reading (0.17) than math (0.08).
Certain variables were also negatively associated with test score
performance. First, individual student’s attendance problem was negatively associated with both test scores, −0.30 for reading and −0.28
for math. Material resource constraints and school attendance problem
were also negatively associated with both test score variables. However,
constraints with regard to teachers and discipline problems have very
small negative associations with the two test scores.
Other noteworthy associations include the negative association between private schools and material resource constraints at -0.52 and the
positive association between private schools and SES index at 0.29.

The recent availability of the Philippine PISA survey results and test
scores has opened the country’s education system to greater scrutiny
and possible reform. This present research seeks to add to the growing
knowledge of factors needed to be addressed to improve the country’s
quality of education. Although this research does not point to which
general factors contribute to the overall dismal performance of Filipino
students, it does highlight sources of variations and inequalities such that
they can be addressed through contextualized resource allocation.
Aside from this study’s speciﬁc contribution to knowledge about
Philippine education, it also provides a method for understanding developing countries’ education systems through PISA test scores and a
means for investigating where these countries may strategically invest.

One particular nuance in addressing student discipline is in terms of
the diﬀerent discipline concerns that depend on school situations.
Problems in terms of students’ lack of respect and attention are more
often present in urban schools rather than rural schools while problems
in terms of alcohol, illegal drugs, and bullying are more present in high
SES schools than low SES schools. Thus, schools may also be strategic
when identifying which student behaviors to address.
Third, the research conﬁrms what has been found in previous research regarding the role of gender and socioeconomic status on academic performance. In a report by the Philippine Institute for
Development Studies, Albert (2016) conﬁrms how disparities exist between the sexes in favor of girls, particularly in academic performance,
secondary school enrolment rates, cohort survival rates, and school
completion. Such situation has been attributed to the vulnerability of
boys to chronic absenteeism brought about by peer inﬂuences, vices,
and computer games (David et al., 2018). Similarly, economically-disadvantaged families can inﬂuence academic performance because of
connected problems and deﬁciencies in food access, health services,
supplementary education, and economic opportunities (Adelman et al.,
2008; Ramesh and Asher, 2000).
However, gender and socioeconomic status do not fully explain the
variability in terms of student test scores. They actually only explain
less than 15 percent of the variance, which points to how other cognitive and non-cognitive factors can be at play. Additionally, majority
of the test score diﬀerences are actually between students rather than
between schools. It suggests that inasmuch as there needs to be schoolspeciﬁc interventions that help with resource constraints and school
climate problems, schools must also address the individual diﬀerences
apparent between the students in the same school. In these cases,
schools must ﬁnd ways of identifying early those who are lagging behind in performance, and create structures for them to have more time
for learning and more directed and possibly one-on-one support. These
may come through programs like reading interventions, double dose
algebra, and activity-based learning and assessment (Christina and
Vinogradova, 2017; Nomi and Allensworth, 2013; Doherty, 2017).
This preliminary study into the Philippines’ ﬁrst year of participating in PISA has revealed important factors that inﬂuence academic
performance, and issues that may be addressed by diﬀerent school
programs. Although the availability of such a large dataset has provided
insights into Philippine education, certain limitations still exist with the
present research. First, the study only looks at associations between
school and individual factors and does not look into causal mechanisms.
For this to be done, randomized controlled and experimental designs
are necessary, which the present dataset does not provide. Second,
other student-level factors aside from gender and socioeconomic status
must be investigated to see what could be aﬀecting the greater variance
between students in the same school. Diﬀerent psycho-social factors,
like mindsets and aspirations, may be investigated in future studies.
Third, the present research does not explain the Philippines’ overall
dismal performance in relation to other countries. However, this preliminary study at least explains where the salient performance diﬀerences are at, and becomes a building block to understand why the
current state of Philippine education is in this shape.
This research contributes to the understanding of Philippine education in at least two fronts. On the one hand, this empirical investigation of a representative dataset conﬁrms educational factors
crucial for students’ performance: material resources, school climate,
and compensatory instruction—all related to creating conducive physical, social, and psychological environments for students and teachers.

in terms of what particular resources are necessary in a country’s school
system, what type of school climate is eﬀective given a particular culture, and which populations and schools need to be addressed ﬁrst. In
relation to the Philippine education system, I argue that nuances arise
with the need to address (1) material resource constraints in public
schools, (2) positive school climates that contextually incorporate discipline measures for diﬀerent types of schools, and (3) compensatory
education directed towards males, low socioeconomic status students,
and children lagging behind in academics. A number of empirical insights support this argument.
First, although it is fairly known how both material and human
resources support quality education (Ahmad et al., 2015; Stewart et al.,
1997; Belot and James, 2011; Afridi, 2011; Adukia, 2017), the Philippines’ more urgent concern is the lack of material resources and its
negative association with students’ performance. Hierarchical linear
models for reading and math test scores show the signiﬁcant negative
association for material resource constraints rather than human resource constraints. Thus, the more a school lacks resources, such as
learning materials and school buildings, the lower the school’s average
performance is. Although the mere provision of more material and
building resources will not necessarily lead to rise in test scores, the lack
of proper materials, classroom spaces, and physical equipment reduces
and impedes students’ learning because of how unconducive and distracting environments can adversely inﬂuence children’s intellectual
growth (Betts et al., 2000; Duﬂo, 2001).
However, not all schools have pressing concerns in terms of the lack
or the poor quality of educational materials and infrastructure.
Comparing diﬀerent school types and locations, public, low SES and
rural schools have the most pressing need for more resources. In particular, the largest disjuncture is between public and private schools,
with public schools mentioning how the concern is more likely for
them. Additionally, the concern is not only in terms of the lack of
materials and buildings but also in their quality. Although not as signiﬁcant a driver of students’ outcomes, teacher shortage is also a more
pressing concern for public, low SES, and rural schools. Such ﬁndings
point to the need for resources to be allocated to these types of schools,
and even, to understand the context for how material improvements
can aﬀect daily instruction. Resource allocation is neither merely putting in more money nor having a monolithic checklist of things schools
should have. When making decisions on which social investments to
fund, policy makers must be mindful of which programs, infrastructures, or materials have a direct impact on students’ daily experiences (Ganimian and Murnane, 2016).
Second, the study conﬁrms the importance of having a positive
school climate, where students feel engaged in their studies. However,
in the Philippine context, such positive climate may come in the form of
consistent and appropriate exercise of discipline measures. An obvious
ﬁnding is how a school’s attendance problem is negatively associated
with academic performance—a sign that people feeling disengaged are
less likely to excel. However, a counterintuitive ﬁnding comes when
principals’ perception of discipline problems positively associates with
students’ test scores. One hypothesis that unfortunately cannot be
tested is the possibility that these principals who view discipline as a
problem in school are also the ones who try to address these problems
more ﬁrmly. When administrators and teachers are cognizant of discipline problems, they are also more likely to address them through
authoritative means (Trinidad, 2018).
Based on the survey, more than 40 percent of Philippine schools
have problems with student absenteeism to some extent and this could
be more strategically addressed in a climate where students know what
are expected of them and discipline is applied consistently and appropriately. Rather than simply proposing that students need to feel engaged, the data suggests that such positive engagement must also be

resonate with other developing countries whose education systems are
inﬂuenced by material resource constraints, school climate deﬁcits, and
heterogeneity of student performance. In this case, they may consider
strategic investments in resource allocation, school climate, and compensatory education, with an eye toward the variations that happen in
