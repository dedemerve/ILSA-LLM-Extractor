Guided by the dual-factor model of mental health, the present study used a person-centered approach to explore
Chinese middle school teachers’ occupational well-being profiles and investigate how these profiles correspond
with teachers’ basic psychological needs satisfaction and teaching quality. Data from the Teaching and Learning
International Survey (TALIS) 2018 were used (N = 3,976). Four occupational well-being profiles were identified
through latent profile analysis, and they were associated differently with teachers’ basic psychological needs
satisfaction (i.e., autonomy, competence, and relatedness) and teaching quality (i.e., clarity of instruction,
cognitive activation, and classroom management). Findings provide practical implications to support teachers.

Promoting the occupational well-being of teachers is crucial for
ensuring high-quality teaching and, consequently, positive student
outcomes (Hascher & Waber, 2021). Research on teachers’ occupational
well-being has documented how teacher job satisfaction and stress are
linked to teaching quality and student outcomes (Capone & Petrillo,
2020; Klusmann et al., 2022). More specifically, the job satisfaction and
stress levels experienced by individual teachers play a crucial role in
their ability to provide effective instructional support, exhibit enthusiasm in teaching, and employ motivating teaching styles (Moè et al.,
2022; Wartenberg et al., 2023). These factors, in turn, have a significant
impact on students’ academic motivation, emotional well-being, and
overall achievement (Arens & Morin, 2016; Keller et al., 2018; Klusmann et al., 2022; Taxer & Frenzel, 2018; Zee & Koomen, 2016).
Moreover, at a broader level, schools with highly stressed and unsatisfied teachers may face challenges such as high attrition rates and a
shortage of qualified educators (Ryan et al., 2017). Therefore, placing a
high priority on the occupational well-being of teachers is crucial not
only for implementing effective teaching practices, but also for fostering
positive educational outcomes for students.
Teaching has been consistently ranked as one of the most stressful

professions (Herman et al., 2020). Consequently, many countries are
facing challenges, such as low teacher satisfaction, high burnout, and
increasing attrition rates (Holmqvist, 2019). These phenomena have
generated significant interest in studying teachers’ job satisfaction and
stress. However, most studies consider either teachers’ job satisfaction
or stress as the indicator of teachers’ well-being (e.g., De Cordova et al.,
2019; Ibrahim et al., 2021). For example, when exploring teachers’
occupational well-being, some studies focused exclusively on assessing
their job satisfaction (De Cordova et al., 2019), while others solely
examined their stress (Ibrahim et al., 2021). According to the dual-factor
model of mental health, satisfaction and stress coexist and jointly influence teachers’ occupational well-being and teaching quality (cognitive activation, clarity of instruction, classroom management;
Greenspoon & Saklofske, 2001; Kunter et al., 2013; OECD, 2020). Thus,
looking at job satisfaction or stress alone fails to obtain a comprehensive
picture of teachers’ occupational well-being. To address this limitation,
the present study was based on the dual-factor model of mental health
and examined teachers’ job satisfaction and stress concurrently.
Moreover, previous research has predominantly utilized variablecentered approaches to investigate the predictors and outcomes of
teachers’ job satisfaction and stress (e.g., Lopes & Oliveira, 2020; Nalipay, 2023). Although these approaches have been helpful for researchers to understand the relationships between constructs of interest
and in developing general intervention strategies, they are limited in

Notably, even within the same working environment, not all teachers

Collie & Mansfield, 2022). As such, variable-centered approaches are
less effective in identifying subgroups of teachers who share common
characteristics (e.g., low job satisfaction and high stress) and could

person-centered approach can be employed to identify subgroups of
teachers exhibiting similar patterns of job satisfaction and stress (Collie
et al., 2020; Howard & Hoffman, 2018). More specifically, the
person-centered approaches can be useful to identify distinct profiles of
individuals who are similar across a range of constructs and the extent
that these combinations are associated with certain outcomes (Morin
et al., 2016). By using the person-centered approach, researchers can
gain insights into the various indicators (e.g., basic psychological needs
satisfaction) and outcomes (e.g., teaching quality) that vary based on the
different profiles of teachers’ occupational well-being.
Guided by the dual-factor model of mental health, the current study
aims to contribute to the existing literature on teachers’ occupational
well-being by using a person-centered approach to identify subgroups of
teachers with different levels of job satisfaction and stress. Additionally,
the study explored how teachers’ occupational well-being profiles
correspond with their basic psychological needs satisfaction (i.e., autonomy, competence, and relatedness; Ryan & Deci, 2002) and teaching
quality (i.e., clarity of instruction, cognitive activation, and classroom
management; Kunter et al., 2013; OECD, 2020).

1.1. The positive aspect of teachers’ occupational well-being: job
satisfaction
Although there is no consensus on the definition of teachers’ job
satisfaction across studies (e.g., Capone & Petrillo, 2020; Lopes & Oliveira, 2020), it generally refers to teachers’ psychological experiences
derived from their subjective evaluation of their job (Weiss, 2002).
Teachers’ job satisfaction can be conceptualized either as a unidimensional construct, representing an overall evaluation of one’s job (e.g.,
Melesse & Belay, 2022), or as a multidimensional construct, comprising
satisfaction with the school and satisfaction with the profession (e.g.,
OECD, 2020).
Satisfaction with the school is influenced by factors related to the
school organization, such as teachers’ engagement in collaborative activities, their autonomy in decision making, and their relationship with
school leaders and colleagues (OECD, 2019). Satisfaction with the profession relates to teachers’ recognition of the benefits of being in the
teaching profession and their choice to pursue this career (OECD, 2019).
While satisfaction with the school and satisfaction with the profession
are expected to be correlated, these two dimensions of teachers’ job
satisfaction are distinct (Barbieri et al., 2019; Mostafa & Pál, 2018;
OECD, 2019). It is possible for teachers to be satisfied with the teaching
profession when it aligns with their professional goals, yet be dissatisfied
with the school due to unfavorable work environment and conditions
(Mostafa & Pál, 2018). Satisfaction with the profession is more strongly
influenced by teachers’ personal and professional backgrounds, whereas
satisfaction with the school is closely tied to organizational factors, such
as school practices, leadership styles, and school policies (Barbieri et al.,
2019).
In addition, teachers’ job satisfaction is widely acknowledged as an
influential factor for their teaching quality and student learning outcomes. More specifically, high job satisfaction in teachers is consistently
associated with great occupational commitment, increased emotional
and instructional support for students, and improved teaching quality
(Klassen & Chiu, 2011; Klusmann et al., 2022). This improved teacher
commitment and teaching quality are also linked to elevated student
self-efficacy, motivation, and academic performance (Arens & Morin,
2016; Klusmann et al., 2022). Therefore, teachers who experience job
satisfaction should be more likely to provide higher-quality instruction,
ultimately contributing to student achievement.

1. Dual-factor model of mental health
Traditionally, the field of psychology considers the absence of psychopathology as the indication of mental health (Jahoda, 1958). However, a paradigm shift in psychology challenges this notion and posits
that the absence of psychopathology does not necessarily imply mental
health, nor does the presence of psychopathology indicate the absence of
mental health (Keyes, 2005). This shift has led to the emergence of the
dual-factor model of mental health, which suggests that wellness and
illness are not opposing ends of a continuum but rather two distinct yet
interconnected constructs that together form the concept of psychological well-being (Greenspoon & Saklofske, 2001). Therefore, psychological well-being should be considered a holistic state where individuals
experience a high degree of wellness and a low degree of illness (Keyes,
2005).
In line with this model, Greenspoon and Saklofske (2001) further
proposed that since wellness and illness coexist, they should be
concurrently considered to obtain a comprehensive understanding of an
individual’s psychological well-being. They proposed that the presence
of wellness does not necessarily mean the absence of illness, and vice
versa. Although wellness and illness are closely related and have a
negative correlation, they should still be regarded as distinct concepts
and be independently linked to other variables. Empirical evidence
supports these theoretical assertions. For example, clinical studies have
demonstrated that individuals with mental disorders, such as affective
disorders, can still experience life satisfaction (Teismann et al., 2018),
while individuals without clinical mental illness may still experience
low satisfaction (Antaramian et al., 2010; Kelly et al., 2012). Additionally, a study conducted by Nalipay (2023) discovered that wellness (e.g.,
satisfaction) was uniquely associated with the positive aspect of teaching, whereas illness (e.g., occupational stress) was not. Therefore,
examining both wellness and illness indicators provide a comprehensive
portrait of an individual’s well-being.

1.2. The negative aspect of teachers’ occupational well-being: stress
Stress can be understood as a discrepancy between the demands
placed on teachers and their perceived ability to meet those demands
(Kyriacou, 2001). A global trend has emerged in recent decades, with
teachers reporting experiencing increased levels of stress (OECD, 2014).
Furthermore, studies have discovered that nearly all teachers, regardless
of their grade levels of teaching, experience moderate to high levels of
stress at some point in their careers (Herman et al., 2020; Pogere et al.,
2019).
In Collie and Mansfield’s (2022) investigation concerning the sources of teachers’ occupational stress, the researchers operationalized
stress as workload stress, expectation stress, and student behavior stress.
Workload stress assesses the extent to which teachers perceive an
excessive amount of lesson preparation, teaching, or grading (Collie &
Mansfield, 2022). Expectation stress evaluates the pressure teachers feel
to meet high expectations for them to meet requirements from authorities, address parental concerns, and ensure student achievement (Collie
& Mansfield, 2022). Student behavior stress pertains to teachers’
perception of the challenges associated with managing misbehaved or
disruptive student behaviors (Collie & Mansfield, 2022). Highly stressed
teachers tend to report high emotional distress, low motivation, and
high turnover intentions, which are associated with poor teaching
quality and subsequently, negative student outcomes (Harrison et al.,
2022; Li et al., 2022; Wu & Chen, 2018; Zhou et al., 2021).

1.3. On the merit of person-centered approach: previous findings
Compared to variable-centered studies, fewer studies use a personcentered approach to explore teachers’ occupational well-being.
Among those few studies, most focused on either teachers’ job satisfaction or stress as the indicator of teachers’ well-being (e.g., De Cordova
et al., 2019; Ibrahim et al., 2021). However, according to the dual-factor
model of mental health, satisfaction does not necessarily indicate the
absence of stress (Greenspoon & Saklofske, 2001). To gain a more
comprehensive understanding of teachers’ occupational well-being, it is
important to consider both satisfaction and stress.
One such attempt was made in Li and his colleagues’ (2021) study on
the occupational well-being of 117 beginning early childhood educators
in Hong Kong. The person-centered approach was utilized with eight
indicators (i.e., job satisfaction, vigor, dedication, absorption, occupational stress, emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced efficacy) and
two profiles were identified: medium well-being and relatively weak wellbeing- emotional exhaustion. Comparisons between these two profiles
suggested that medium well-being teachers reported fewer job demands
(e.g., shorter working time) and higher job resources (e.g., more support
at work).
Another example is the study conducted by Hwang (2022), which
used data from the Trends in International Mathematics and Science
Study (TIMSS) to investigate American mathematics teachers’ profiles of
job satisfaction and stress and their use of dialogic instruction (an
instructional strategy that requires teachers to assist students in finding
answers to problems, rather than directly providing them with answers).
The person-centered approach was used with both job satisfaction (e.g.,
“I am content with my profession as a teacher” and “I am satisfied with
being a teacher at this school”) and stress (e.g., “I have too many
administrative tasks” and “I have too many teaching hours”) as two
dichotomous indicators of teachers’ occupational well-being. The study
identified three teacher well-being profiles: high job satisfaction and very
low stress, very low job satisfaction and high stress, and moderately high job
satisfaction and slightly high stress. Results demonstrated that the profile
of teachers who experienced high job satisfaction and very low stress used
dialogic instruction the mostly frequently, followed by the profiles of
teachers who experienced moderately high job satisfaction and slightly high
stress, and teachers who experienced very low job satisfaction and high
stress. While this study supports the use of person-centered approaches
by identifying different combinations of teachers’ job satisfaction and
stress, the use of a single satisfaction indicator and a single stress indicator limits the exploration of more nuanced profiles that could be
identified using dimensional indicators (e.g., satisfaction with the profession, satisfaction with the school).

3. Demographic characteristics and teachers’ occupational wellbeing
Previous studies have revealed the associations between teachers’
demographic characteristics and their job satisfaction and stress. Studies
have consistently found that female teachers tend to report higher levels
of stress compared to their male counterparts (e.g., Wang et al., 2015).
However, the findings regarding the association between teacher gender
and job satisfaction have been mixed (e.g., Aydin et al., 2012; Klassen &
Chiu, 2011). Furthermore, teachers who have obtained higher levels of
education, chosen teaching as their primary career choice, secured
permanent positions, or accumulated more years of teaching experience
are more likely to report higher job satisfaction (e.g., Liu & Ramsey,
2008; OECD, 2019). Therefore, the present study aims to examine the
relationships between teachers’ demographic characteristics, including
gender, educational background, career choice, employment status, and
teaching experience, and the different profiles of teachers’ occupational
well-being.
4. Teachers’ occupational well-being and teaching quality
Broaden-and-build theory suggests that the experience of positive
emotions can broaden one’s thought-action repertoire (e.g., contentment generates elevated work performance), and that this could lead to
more sociopsychological, intellectual, and physical resources, and
hence, facilitate positive outcomes (Fredrickson, 2001). Therefore,
teachers who experience optimal occupational well-being should be able
to gain resources to enact more effective teaching practices. More specifically, satisfied teachers are often driven by greater motivation and
commitment, enabling them to establish positive relationships with
their students. This, in turn, enhances their interest in teaching and
improves their teaching quality (Harrison et al., 2023; Li & Wang, 2016;
Moè, 2016).
Teaching quality is typically characterized as the composite of
teacher-related characteristics that lead to positive educational outcomes (Cochran-Smith & Fries, 2005). It is linked to student learning
and academic achievement, therefore, serves as an essential indicator of
teacher performance (Hattie, 2009). As proposed by Kunter et al. (2013)
and OECD (2020), teaching quality encompasses a variety of instructional practices that can be grouped into three core dimensions cognitive activation, clarity of instruction, and classroom management.
Cognitive activation involves offering instructional assistance and
challenging assignments to engage students’ advanced cognitive processes. Clarity of instruction concerns the clear and coherent presentation of educational contents. Classroom management involves
efficiently supervising student discipline in the classroom, adeptly
managing disturbances, and establishing and upholding clear classroom
rules (OCED, 2020).

job satisfaction are associated with clearer instruction, more frequent
utilization of cognitively activated instruction, and improved classroom
management skills (Harrison et al., 2023; Viac & Fraser, 2020). On the

7. Method

5. Study contexts

The present study utilized the China dataset from the OECD Teaching
and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018. The TALIS study is
conducted every five years to gather information on various aspects of
teachers, students, and schools from nationally representative samples
of educational practitioners (see OECD, 2020 for more details). The
TALIS 2018 recruited approximately 260,000 teachers from 48 regions
(OECD, 2020). A total of 3,976 middle school teachers (Grades 7–9)
from Shanghai, China were included in the present study. The sample
comprised 74.0% female and 26.0% male teachers.1 More than
two-thirds of the participants were aged between 30 and 49 years
(68.8%) and nearly all of them had a bachelor’s degree or higher
(98.7%). On average, participants had 16.80 years of teaching experience (SD = 9.65, range 0–49). Most of the participants chose teaching as
their first choice of career (86.5%) and 30.1% of them secured a permanent position at school. The teachers taught a wide range of subjects,
with the most frequently reported subjects being Chinese (25.3%),
mathematics (18.9%), science (14.5%), and English (12.1%).

7.1. Participants

Deeply rooted in thousands of years of Confucian values, education
has long been regarded as the most crucial pathway to wealth and
success in the Chinese culture (Curdt-Christiansen, 2009). Teachers,
who play a pivotal role in this journey, are held to exceptionally high
standards and expectations (Gao, 2008). Driven by these ingrained
cultural beliefs, Chinese parents are investing more in their children’s
education and becoming more demanding when it comes to their children’s academic performance (Gao, 2008), which translates into
heightened expectations for teachers. In addition to parental demands,
Chinese teachers are also burdened with increasing responsibilities
assigned by school administrators (Ju et al., 2015).
Moreover, the Chinese education system is characterized by highstakes accountability, with schools often managed by hierarchical
leadership, and teachers having limited autonomy in decision-making.
Their evaluations primarily hinge on their students’ performance in
standardized examinations (Zhang & Sun, 2018). While Chinese teachers bear multiple responsibilities and work in a high-stakes accountability environment, most of them in government-funded schools enjoy
permanent positions, which provide them with a stable career, decent
salary, and various benefits (Gu, 2015). Moreover, given the paramount
importance of education in China, the teaching profession is highly
esteemed, and teachers hold a high social status (Gao, 2008).
Studies on Chinese teachers’ job satisfaction using variable-centered
approaches yielded inconsistent results. For instance, Chen (2017) reported that Chinese teachers typically experienced low job satisfaction.
In comparing dissatisfaction with the profession and the working environment, these teachers specifically reported higher dissatisfaction with
the teaching profession. In contrast, Yin’s (2021) study found that Chinese teachers generally reported moderate to high levels of job satisfaction. Therefore, more nuanced studies, adopting a person-centered
approach, are needed to systematically examine Chinese teachers’ job
satisfaction and occupational stress.

7.2. Measures
7.2.1. Teachers’ job satisfaction
Teachers’ job satisfaction was operationalized using two constructs:
satisfaction with the profession and satisfaction with the school (four items
each; satisfaction with the profession: e.g., “If I could decide again, I would
still choose to work as a teacher.” “I wonder whether it would have been
better to choose another profession” [reverse scored]; items TT3G53A,
TT3G53B, TT3G53D, and TT3G53F; satisfaction with the school: e.g., “I
enjoy working at this school.” “I would like to change to another school
if that were possible” [reverse scored]; items TT3G53C, TT3G53E,
TT3G53G, and TT3G53J; 1 = Strongly disagree, 4 = Strongly agree). For
data analyses, two total job satisfaction scores were calculated, with
higher values indicating higher levels of job satisfaction in the profession or at the current school. Reliability was estimated with McDonald’s
omega (ω) and good fit was observed for both the satisfaction with the
profession (ω = 0.75) and satisfaction with current school (ω = 0.76).

6. The present study

7.2.2. Teacher stress
Teacher stress was operationalized using three constructs: workload
stress (three items; e.g., “having too much lesson preparation”; items
TT3G52A-TT3G52C), expectation stress (three items; e.g., “being held
responsible for students’ achievement”; items TT3G52F, TT3G52I, and
TT3G52J), and student behavior stress (two items; e.g., “being intimidated or verbally abused by students; ” items TT3G52G and TT3G52H).
All items were measured using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = Not at all, 4 = A
lot) and led by the question: “Thinking about your job at this school, to
what extent are the following sources of stress in your work?” For data
analyses, three stress scores were calculated, with a higher score indicating a higher level of perceived occupational stress. Reliability was
adequate for all three dimensions (workload stress: ω = 0.83; expectation stress: ω = 0.72; student behavior stress: ω = 0.80).

The present study expands upon earlier research on teachers’ occupational well-being to explore profiles of teacher job satisfaction (i.e.,
satisfaction with the profession and satisfaction with the school) and
stress (i.e., workload stress, expectation stress, and student behavior

teachers’ occupational well-being profiles correspond with their basic
psychological needs satisfaction and teaching quality. Moreover, as
gender, educational background, whether teaching is the first choice of
career, employment status, and teaching experience have been found in
previous research to be closely related to teachers’ job satisfaction and
stress (e.g., Liu & Ramsey, 2008; OECD, 2019), we also examined their
associations with teachers’ occupational well-being profiles. Fig. 1

To answer RQ1, Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) was conducted to
identify profiles of teachers’ occupational well-being using MPlus 8.3
(Muthén & Muthén, 2017). In this analysis, two dimensions of satisfaction and three types of stress were entered as indicators. LPA solutions were generated, ranging from one to eight profiles, employing
maximum likelihood estimation. The analysis involved 5,000 random
sets of start values with 100 iterations and 200 final stage optimizations
(Morin et al., 2016). To determine the optimal profile solution, several
indices were consulted. These include (1) the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), (2) Bayesian information criterion (BIC), (3) the
sample-size adjusted Bayesian information criterion (SSA-BIC), (4)
Lo-Mendell-Rubins likelihood ratio test (LMR), (5) bootstrap likelihood
ratio test, (6) entropy, as well as (7) the size (i.e., >5% of participants)
and meaningfulness of the profiles (Morin et al., 2016; Tein et al., 2013).
Lower AIC, BIC, and SSA-BIC values indicate better profile solutions
(Nylund et al., 2007). The LMR and bootstrap likelihood ratio test
compare between a solution with k profiles and a solution with k-1
profiles. A significant p-value suggests that the k profile solution provides a better fit than the k-1 profile solution (Beyers & Seiffge-Krenke,
2007). Entropy values reflect the accuracy of classification, with higher
values indicating more precise classification (Celeux & Soromenho,
1996).
To address RQ2, the R3STEP command was employed to examine the
association between teachers’ basic psychological needs satisfaction and
profiles of occupational well-being. In addition, we also examined the
associations between teachers’ demographics (i.e., gender, educational
background, whether teaching was the first choice of career, employment status, and teaching experience) and their occupational well-being
profiles. (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2014a; 2014b). Lastly, to tackle RQ3,
the BCH command in MPlus was used to assess the associations between
each occupational well-being profile and teaching quality, including
clarity of instruction, cognitive activation, and classroom management
(Asparouhov & Muthén, 2014a, 2014b; Wang & Bi, 2018).

7.2.4. Teaching quality

of instruction, cognitive activation, and classroom management (four items
each; clarity of instruction: TT3G42A-TT3G42D, e.g., “I present a summary of recently learned content.” Cognitive activation: TT3G42ETT3G42H, e.g., “I present tasks for which there is no obvious solution.” Classroom management: TT3G42I- TT3G42L [all items were

never, 4 = Always). Reliability was adequate for all three practices

9.1. Teachers’ profiles of occupational well-being
Our study identified four distinct profiles concerning teacher’s
occupational well-being. Teachers who were highly satisfied were found
to be the most content with their profession and working environment,
experiencing the least amount of stress from the demanding workload,
high expectations, and student misbehaviors. These teachers have
attained a complete state of occupational well-being with high wellness
(i.e., satisfaction) accompanied with low illness (e.g., stress). Teachers
with a balanced profile exhibited moderate levels of satisfaction and
stress, representing the largest group, which constituted approximately
70% of the sample. These findings suggest that a large proportion of
teachers, although not the most satisfied, can at least strike a balance
between job satisfaction and stress. Moreover, teachers who were highly
unsatisfied reported the lowest levels of job satisfaction and comparably
high levels of stress. Lastly, teachers who were highly stressed reported
the highest levels of stress and relatively low satisfaction. These teachers
were stressed by their workload, the pressure to meet expectations from
authorities and parents, and the managing of student misbehaviors.
The findings from our study are consistent with previous research,
which suggests that teachers’ occupational well-being encompasses both
the positive and the negative aspects (e.g., Hwang et al., 2022; Li et al.,
2021). More specifically, the highly satisfied and the highly unsatisfied
teachers identified in our study with Chinese middle school teachers
replicate the high job satisfaction and very low stress and the very low job
satisfaction and high stress profiles of teachers identified in Hwang et al.’s
(2022) study with American mathematics teachers. More importantly,
our study extends existing literature by using multidimensional indicators to assess satisfaction and stress (e.g., satisfaction with the profession, satisfaction with school) and thus identifying more nuanced
profiles.

9. Discussion
The present study explored the profiles of Chinese teachers’ occupational well-being and examined how these profiles differ in their associations with basic psychological needs satisfaction and teaching
quality. Four profiles were identified: highly satisfied teachers, balanced
teachers, highly unsatisfied teachers, and highly stressed teachers.
Furthermore, results indicate that the four profiles correspond differently to teachers’ basic psychological needs satisfaction (i.e., autonomy,
competence, and relatedness) and teaching quality (i.e., clarity of instruction, cognitive activation, and classroom management). The results
of this study provide a valuable contribution to previous research that
focused on predictors and outcomes of teachers’ occupational wellbeing using variable-centered approaches (e.g., Lopes & Oliveira,
2020; Nalipay, 2023). By adopting a person-centered approach, the
present study specifically examined these relationships within different
subgroups of teachers, thus uncovering the differences that may otherwise overlooked when using variable-centered approaches (Hofmans
et al., 2020).

needs and well-being has been echoed in numerous previous studies (e.
g., Liu et al., 2021; Shim et al., 2022).
Interestingly, we found that teachers who reported high levels of
pedagogical autonomy were more likely to be highly unsatisfied teachers
than balanced teachers. Although pedagogical autonomy generally
contributes to teachers’ occupational well-being (Shim et al., 2022;
Uzman & Telef, 2015), evidence suggests that excessive autonomy may
not be beneficial, and could even be harmful in certain situations, such
as when teachers confront high cognitive demands (e.g., making critical
decisions at work; Wielenga-Meijer et al., 2012). It is possible that too
much autonomy can make teachers feel a lack of guidance and direction,
and hence, could be cognitively taxing for them. This is especially true in
the curricular and pedagogical aspects of teaching, which involves a lot
of critical decision-making (Ho, 2010). Studies have shown that autonomy should ideally be balanced with structure (e.g., guidance and
expectations) in order to attain optimal outcomes, such as improved
work engagement (Jang et al., 2010; Sierens et al., 2009)
Moreover, those who reported the lowest levels of relatedness were
more likely than others to be classified as highly unsatisfied teachers. A
lack of strong relationships with students and colleagues seems to
contribute significantly to teacher dissatisfaction, although it does not
necessarily lead to high stress levels. These findings suggest that while
both highly unsatisfied and highly stressed teachers experience poor
occupational well-being, the predictors for each may differ. These results echo Nalipay’s (2023) study, which identified teacher-student
relationship as a key determinant of teacher satisfaction rather than
teacher stress. Thus, fostering positive relationships with students and
colleagues may have significant implications for addressing high
dissatisfaction levels among teachers.
Moreover, our study’s findings indicate a clear association between
demographic factors and teachers’ occupational well-being profiles.
More specifically, gender disparities were evident in profile membership, with male teachers more likely to fall into the highly satisfied profile
than the balanced profile, whereas female teachers were more likely to
be classified as highly stressed rather than balanced or highly unsatisfied
teachers. This observation aligns with existing research that suggests
female teachers often experience higher stress levels than their male
counterparts, attributed to their heavier domestic duties, increased
caregiving responsibilities at home, and the struggle to balance work
and family life (Wang et al., 2015). Furthermore, teachers who identified teaching as their first career choice were more likely to be classified
as highly satisfied, as opposed to balanced, highly unsatisfied, or highly
stressed teachers. Those teachers who initially chose teaching as their
career might generally be more dedicated and motivated towards the
profession, leading to higher job satisfaction.

teachers revealed that new teachers were more likely to fall into the

to be categorized as highly unsatisfied. This observation aligns with

9.3. Teachers’ well-being profiles and teaching quality
As expected, highly satisfied teachers demonstrated superior teaching
quality, including clear instruction and cognitive activation, compared
to their counterparts in the other three profiles. In simpler terms, the
most content and least stressed teachers exhibit the best job performance. Interestingly, there were no significant differences in instructional clarity and cognitive activation among balanced, highly unsatisfied,
and highly stressed teachers. This implies that when teachers endure
relatively poor occupational well-being, irrespective of whether they
feel unsatisfied or stressed, their teaching quality is similarly compromised. Collectively, these insights corroborate previous research that
suggests satisfied teachers perform better at work (e.g., Harrison et al.,
2022) and align with the central principle of positive psychology, which
suggests increased well-being predicts enhanced work outcomes (Walsh
et al., 2018).
Finally, classroom management exhibited a different pattern,
though, with highly stressed teachers showing less effective control of the
class and spending more time dealing with student disruptions than
teachers from the other three profiles. In essence, only the most stressed
teachers reported the least effectiveness in classroom management,
while the other three profiles did not vary significantly.
9.4. Evidence supporting the dual-factor model of mental health
The findings of our study, which identified four distinct profiles of
teachers’ occupational well-being, supports the dual-factor model of
mental health. For instance, while both highly unsatisfied and highly
stressed teachers demonstrated poor occupational well-being, they
experienced unique combinations of job satisfaction and stress. Specifically, highly unsatisfied teachers reported experiencing very high level of
dissatisfaction but did not report very high stress. Similarly, highly
stressed teachers reported feeling very stressful, but they did not report
very low levels of job satisfaction.
Furthermore, our findings revealed that teachers with poor relationships with students and colleagues were more likely to be highly
unsatisfied, but not necessarily highly stressed. And when comparing between the highly unsatisfied and highly stressed teachers concerning their
classroom management, our results showed that the highly unsatisfied
teachers displayed significantly better classroom management than their
highly stressed counterparts. These results suggest that highly unsatisfied
and highly stressed teachers not only differ in their experiences of satisfaction and stress, but also in their predictors (i.e., relationships with
students and colleagues) and teaching quality (i.e., classroom management). Collectively, our findings provide empirical support for the dualfactor model of mental health, which posits that satisfaction and stress
coexist and are not mutually exclusive (Greenspoon & Saklofske, 2001).
This is also in accordance with Keyes’ (2005) proposition, which suggests that the absence of psychopathology does not necessarily indicate
mental health, and the presence of psychopathology does not necessarily
imply the absence of mental health. Similar findings have been observed
in previous literature examining the relationship between mental illness
and life satisfaction among various populations (e.g., youths: Antaramian et al., 2010; clinical patients: Teismann et al., 2018). Therefore,
our findings enrich the existing literature by demonstrating that satisfaction and stress are not simply opposing ends of a spectrum among the
Chinese teacher population; instead, they should be examined conjointly
to provide a comprehensive view of teachers’ occupational well-being.

approach also reinforce the concept that teachers are not a

9.6. Limitations of the present study
This study enhances our understanding of occupational well-being
profiles among Chinese teachers, but it does present some limitations.
First, the data utilized in this study was self-reported. While self-report
measures are a credible method to assess subjective experiences and
perceptions (e.g., job satisfaction and stress), an exclusive reliance on
self-reported measures could potentially lead to response bias, as participants may be inclined to provide socially favorable responses (e.g., to
intentionally report better teaching quality than they truly do; Greene,
2015). Therefore, it is recommended that future studies utilize multiple
methods (e.g., student ratings and observation; Kunter et al., 2013) to
corroborate the results, particularly regarding behavioral variables such
as teaching quality.
Second, the cross-sectional design of this study inhibits the ability to
draw causal inferences. This means that the direction of the relationship
between teachers’ occupational well-being and teaching quality remains
undetermined. Low job satisfaction and high stress could yield less
effective teaching practices, while the teaching practices employed
could also impact teacher job satisfaction and stress (Pozas et al., 2023).
Future research may benefit from a longitudinal design, where repeated
observations of the same variables and participants over time can enable
researchers to discern the temporal associations among teachers’ occupational well-being, basic psychological needs satisfaction, and teaching
quality.
Third, the use of quantitative approaches may not fully reveal how
teachers themselves experience and view occupational well-being.
Qualitative approaches can also prove valuable in garnering deep insights into teachers’ perspectives, thus enhancing the depth of our
findings. For instance, in-depth interviews can elucidate the connection
between teachers’ perceptions of poor relationships and their experiences of stress. Furthermore, interviews that explore how teachers
perceive their day-to-day experiences in relation to their occupational
well-being can uncover additional facets of job satisfaction and stress
not captured by standardized measures (e.g., Huynh et al., 2023).
Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that the items used in
this study were not derived from validated measurements, which may
pose challenges when making cross-cultural comparisons. To address
this limitation, future research could consider utilizing a validated crosscultural tool, such as the Basic Need Satisfaction and Frustration scale
developed by Chen et al. (2015), to assess teachers’ satisfaction and
frustration regarding their basic psychological needs.
Lastly, it is crucial to note that the teachers participating in our study
were recruited from schools in Shanghai, China, a highly developed
metropolis that offers more favorable financial, medical, and retirement
benefits to its teachers compared to many other regions in China (Liang
et al., 2016). Consequently, the findings and profiles identified in this
study may only be applicable to Chinese Shanghai teachers and may not
be generalizable to teachers in other regions, cultural groups, or
countries.

9.5. Practical implications
The dual-factor model of mental health suggests that individuals
attain high well-being when they exhibit high wellness and low illness.
In line with this idea, only 12.8% of the Chinese teachers surveyed in
this study achieved a complete state of occupational well-being,
demonstrating high satisfaction and low stress levels. In contrast, the
occupational well-being of most of these Chinese teachers was somewhat compromised. While a substantial proportion managed to maintain a balance between job satisfaction and stress (approximately 70%),
a noteworthy percentage of teachers experienced either extremely high
stress or very low job satisfaction (collectively accounting for around
18%). These teachers could potentially gain from targeted programs and
interventions tailored to meet their specific needs and enhance each
subgroup’s strengths, as opposed to generic solutions applicable to all.
Below, we offer a few specific recommendations informed by our
findings.
First, our results suggest that enhancing the sense of relatedness for
highly unsatisfied teachers might be beneficial. Therefore, strategies
promoting improved student-teacher relationships and fostering better
relationships among colleagues could be implemented. For instance,
Kincade and colleagues’ meta-analysis (2020) identified some best
practices for improving student-teacher relationships, while Pesonen
et al. (2021) suggested that planning, division of work, and shared responsibilities can nurture a sense of belonging among co-teachers.
Second, for teachers in the highly stressed profile, measures could be
undertaken to mitigate their experiences of three types of stress. Given
that sufficient support can alleviate occupational stress (Shernoff et al.,
2011), schools might consider providing teachers with enhanced support (e.g., emotional, social, and informational).
Third, our results reveal that high pedagogical autonomy makes
highly unsatisfied teachers rather than balanced teachers. Such finding
points out that pedagogical autonomy may not always enhance teachers’
occupational well-being. Since a balance between autonomy support
and structure (e.g., clear expectations) helps attain optimal work outcomes, autonomy should be granted with sufficient structure and
guidance, so that teachers do not feel lost (Jang et al., 2010; Sierens
et al., 2009).
Fourth, regarding gender differences, female teachers may require
more support than their male counterparts, as a higher number of them
tend to fall into the highly stressed profile. Female teachers may experience unique stressors, such as those related to their societal roles as
mothers and housewives, or the expectations for them to deal with
household chores by themselves (Abdulla & Mumthas, 2021). Additional support (e.g., organizing time management workshops) and resources (e.g., using technology to handle repetitive and taxing work) can
be made available to them to enhance their work productivity and
relieve some stress.

10. Conclusion
Utilizing a person-centered approach, the study identified four profiles of Chinese teachers’ occupational well-being (i.e., highly satisfied,
balanced, highly unsatisfied, and highly stressed) and explored the relationships of these four identified profiles with teachers’ basic psychological needs satisfaction and teaching quality. Our results support
the dual-factor model of mental health by showing that satisfaction and
stress are not opposite extremes of a spectrum and should be measured
simultaneously to obtain a comprehensive picture of teachers’

acknowledging the diverse subgroups of teachers, each with their

study also highlights the need for targeted support for teachers to

subgroups of teachers. Implementing such targeted initiatives could not
only foster more effective teaching practices, but also effectively
contribute to the retention of teachers in the profession, ultimately
leading to improved educational outcomes for students.
