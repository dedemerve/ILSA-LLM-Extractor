ABSTRACT

Mathematics performance is essential for educational and economic outcomes, and research emphasizes the role of noncognitive factors such as
self-efficacy, motivation, and socioeconomic background. The aim of the
study was to identify which noncognitive variables were the most effective
predictors of students’ mathematics achievement. PISA 2022 background
questionnaire data were analyzed using machine learning (ML) algorithms
on a cloud-based high-performance computing system. The secondary data
analysis covered 613,774 students across 81 countries and economies. The
analysis identified mathematics self-efficacy (MATHEFF) as the most significant predictor among 130 noncognitive variables. Two of the top three predictors were associated with socioeconomic status (SES). The decision tree
model achieved 71% accuracy in predicting mathematics achievement.
These findings underscore the global relevance of self-efficacy and socioeconomic predictors and demonstrate the methodological value of ML in
analyzing large-scale international educational data, providing evidence of
practical and policy significance.

Introduction
Mathematical skills are increasingly crucial in the twenty first century due to technological
advances (Martin, 2018). To effectively solve problems in daily life, including professional situations, mathematical knowledge is necessary. Mathematics is essential for individuals confronting
challenges in different areas of life (OECD, 2023c). For many individuals, understanding and
learning mathematics proves to be a difficult task. The challenges students encounter in math
highlight the need to analyze factors linked to academic achievement. When evaluating mathematical success, one of the factors considered is students’ cognitive ability. A student’s cognitive
ability is tied to their capacity for reasoning, memory, and language use (Goforth et al., 2014).
According to McCoach et al. (2017), childhood intelligence is a strong predictor of mathematical
success in later years. Taub et al. (2008) found that cognitive abilities like fluid reasoning, crystallized intelligence, and processing speed significantly impact math performance.
Along with cognitive characteristics, many noncognitive variables that can be grouped under
headings such as student, school, teacher, family also have the potential to affect mathematics
achievement. According to You et al. (2021), student-background variables such as gender, family
education level, socioeconomic status, student-level variables such as mathematics self-efficacy,
mathematics anxiety, mathematics self-concept and out-of-school study time, school-level variables
such as teacher-student communication, teacher support affect mathematics achievement. Again,

Among the non-cognitive variables, self-construct variables, such as mathematics self-efficacy,
mathematics anxiety, and mathematics self-concept, are widely used when examining mathematics
success. According to Bandura’s social-cognitive theory, people’s thoughts about themselves are
linked to how they behave, especially when faced with challenging conditions (Bandura, 1997).
In this respect, variables such as mathematics self-efficacy, mathematics self-concept, and mathematics anxiety become important when examining students’ mathematics success. A significant
number of adults and children experience mathematics anxiety, and this anxiety causes individuals
to avoid mathematical activities or seriously affects their mathematics learning and performance
(Dowker et al., 2016). According to Richardson and Suinn (1972), mathematics anxiety is a
feeling of tension and anxiety that prevents individuals from solving mathematical problems and
number-related operations in their daily lives or academic activities.
Another self-construct variable, mathematics self-concept, refers to an individual’s beliefs about
self-worth related to mathematical competence (Kaskens et al., 2020). Mathematics self-concept
is one of the variables that can be used when examining students’ mathematical achievement
(Lee, 2009; You et al., 2021). According to (Bong et al., 2003), as students’ achievement levels
in mathematics increase, their self-concepts related to mathematics also develop. The relationship
between mathematics self-concept and mathematics achievement is generally positive, according
to many studies (e.g. McWilliams et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2018).
The relationship between self-construct variables and academic achievement, together with
SES defined by Baker (2014) as a measure used to evaluate an individual’s economic and social
standing—has been a prominent focus of research in the literature (e.g. Olsen & Huang, 2021;
Rakesh et al., 2025; Schmidt et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). In addition, numerous other variables such as immigration status, age and grade level, gender, participation in early childhood
education, and grade repetition have been examined as factors associated with mathematics
achievement (Boman & Wiberg, 2024; Lee et al., 2019).
In summary, mathematics achievement is shaped not only by cognitive capacity but also by
students’ self-construct perceptions, their socioeconomic status, and numerous variables related
to the school and family context. This diversity reveals that mathematics achievement is a multidimensional construct and demonstrates the importance of examining these variables.
Math achievement and international large-scale assessments (ILSAs)
Today, countries are faced with new opportunities and challenges arising from the rapid proliferation of devices such as computers, robots, smartphones, and networked machines in all areas
of life. Therefore, the most commonly used argument for countries to prioritize mathematics
education for all students is the usefulness of mathematics in such various practical situations
(OECD, 2023c). At this point, the evaluation of mathematics achievement at the country level
becomes important. International Large-scale Assessments (ILSAs) play an important role in the
international evaluation of mathematics achievement on a country basis. ILSAs have become an
important element of global governance in education for measuring the educational performance
of countries and creating economic and educational policies based on the comparison of these
performances (Addey et al., 2017).
In evaluating mathematics achievement on an international scale, the PISA (Program for
International Student Assessment) conducted by the OECD and the TIMSS (Trends in International
Mathematics and Science Study) conducted by the IEA (International Association for the
Evaluation of Educational Achievement) stand out. TIMSS was first implemented in 45 countries
in 1995. The last one was conducted in 2023 and covers 72 countries/education systems. The
PISA test was developed by the OECD in the late 90s. The test was first implemented in 43
countries in 2000 and the last one in over eighty countries in 2022. In both PISA and TIMSS
applications, various data are collected through surveys in addition to tests. The aim of collecting

When examining non-cognitive variables in mathematics achievement research, secondary
survey data collected through ILSAs, such as PISA or TIMSS, are of interest to many researchers.
There are many studies examining mathematics self-efficacy, mathematics anxiety, mathematics
self-concept, or other non-cognitive variables using this data. For example, You et al. (2021)
used PISA 2012 survey data in their studies examining variables such as family education level,
socioeconomic status, mathematics self-efficacy, mathematics anxiety, mathematics self-concept.
Karakolidis et al. (2016) conducted their studies examining factors related to mathematics
achievement at individual and school levels with PISA 2012 data. Lee (2009) used PISA 2003
data in his study examining mathematics self-concept, mathematics self-efficacy, and mathematics
anxiety variables. ILSAS data were also used in the studies conducted by Beccuti (2024) and
Ersan and Rodriguez (2020). According to (Stankov et al., 2013), most of the ILSAs, such as
TIMMS and PISA, focus on assessing different aspects of academic performance. The background
questionnaires in these applications are not at the forefront when reporting rankings, but they
contain rich information about non-cognitive variables of potential importance for education,
and an increasing number of researchers are focusing more on non-cognitive variables.
Large-scale international datasets such as ILSA can possess highly complex and multidimensional data structures. As Romero and Ventura (2020) have noted, large amounts of data about
students are collected in various educational environments. All of this data produces a large
volume of information with high educational value; however, analyzing it manually or through
traditional methods poses a major challenge. Therefore, there is a growing need for tools that
enable the analysis of such data, which provide valuable insights into better understanding students’ learning processes. In this context, methods such as ML have the potential to offer an
important solution for examining large-scale educational data and generating meaningful results.
ML in educational research
In recent years, interest in the use of ML has increased in areas such as predicting student
outcomes, identifying at-risk students, personalizing learning experiences, and analyzing educational data (Ersozlu et al., 2024). ML is programming computers to optimize a performance
criterion by using sample data or past experiences (Alpaydin, 2020). According to Hilbert et al.
(2021), ML is a data-driven approach to computational learning and provides a powerful and
novel framework for data analysis, and it is likely to become an important factor in education.
ML encompasses a wide variety of algorithms, which are generally categorized as unsupervised
learning and supervised learning methods (Huang et al., 2025).
According to Järvelä et al. (2020), ML together with learning sciences, affective computing,
and interdisciplinary collaboration can help in the examination of complex phenomena. Unlike
traditional linear models, ML algorithms are highly suitable for capturing nonlinear relationships
and interactions among variables, which makes them ideal for investigating multifaceted constructs such as academic achievement, motivation, and family involvement (Khine et al., 2025).
In addition, they can be used in the analysis of internationally recognized assessments such as
PISA and TIMSS to improve the accuracy of predictions and to reduce biases that naturally
arise from the data (Ersozlu et al., 2024). According to a systematic review conducted by
Zambrano-Romero et al. (2025), there is great potential for future research on the use of ML
in education. The same researchers also state that future studies may focus on improving the
applications of these technologies and overcoming current challenges and limitations.
The prediction of students’ academic achievement is one of the topics in which ML methods
are frequently used. In the systematic review conducted by Ersozlu et al. (2024), the majority
of the examined studies (88%) employed ML methods to predict students’ achievement and to
identify learning patterns. However, Feng et al. (2025) developed an ML-based model that does

important variables affecting students’ mathematics achievement by applying various ML algorithms to PISA 2018 data.
With the increasing use of complex ML algorithms, various classifications have emerged based
on how these methods operate, such as black-box and white-box (also referred to as glass-box)
models. Some models, such as deep neural networks, offer high accuracy but are categorized as
black-box methods because their internal workings cannot be directly understood by users
(Rudin, 2019). In contrast, white-box ML methods, such as decision trees, explicitly reveal the
model’s decision logic, allowing users to interpret the reasoning process (Marcinkevičs & Vogt,
2023). Developing interpretable models is critically important in education, as they enhance
stakeholder trust, support human decision-making in the loop, and provide actionable and
comprehensible feedback (Zhang et al., 2025). Interpretable ML represents a promising approach
to addressing concerns about the opacity of ML models. This approach also enables ML systems
to express their processes in a human-understandable manner, thereby enhancing transparency,
trust, and accountability in data-driven decision-making processes (Wang & Luo, 2024).
ML methods are playing an increasingly important role in educational research and, in particular, provide a powerful tool for the analysis of large-scale international datasets such as ILSA.
This enables researchers to examine students’ learning processes, achievement, and contextual
factors in greater depth.
Current study
Mathematics achievement scores are commonly employed to gauge students’ academic performance in school. Nonetheless, there is ongoing discussion regarding the additional factors that
impact students’ math performance and how these factors differ between countries. Most studies
have examined only one or a few of the variables as predictors of mathematics achievement but
have not yet explored various student and teacher characteristics together (You et al., 2021).
Although PISA applications provide a comprehensive and large-scale dataset, a significant
portion of existing studies have conducted their analyses based not on the entire dataset but
only on specific subsets of the data. In the systematic review conducted by Wang et al. (2023),
it was found that one-third of the studies examining mathematics achievement using PISA data
relied solely on the data of a single country. From the perspective of variables, analyses in the
studies were again generally carried out with a limited set of variables. Wang et al. (2023) also
pointed out that variables such as gender and SES are more frequently emphasized in the studies,
although there may be missing variables that are related to mathematics achievement but were
excluded or not investigated. According to Zhu et al. (2025), although an increasing number of
PISA prediction studies have gradually incorporated ML techniques, most have selected potential
predictors based on literature reviews conducted by subject-matter experts. This approach can
sometimes be influenced by subjective perspectives, potentially overlooking important predictors
or overemphasizing less relevant ones.
However, analyzing a large number of variables and large-scale data also presents its own
challenges. For example, classical statistical approaches provide only a limited solution in complex

data structures, especially when there are many variables that are highly correlated with each

Therefore, analyzing as broad a set of variables as possible together with large-scale datasets

ILSAs offer insights into the educational effectiveness of various levels of education systems.
Trends can be measured over time due to the multiple cycles of the same ILSA. Nonetheless,
there are methodological challenges when comparing noncognitive and cognitive measures across
countries or measurement situations (Wurster, 2022). Faddar and Kjeldsen (2022) highlighted
the numerous challenges in handling and interpreting noncognitive measurements in the TIMSS
application. According to researchers, TIMSS’s international and intercultural nature presents
additional challenges, and ongoing efforts are being made to enhance the reliability and validity
of these studies.
Various data analysis techniques are employed in ILSA studies. Zheng et al. (2024) found
that the most popular data analysis technique in PISA-related research is regression analysis
(38.7%), followed by hierarchical linear models (10.6%) and structural equation model (8.7%).
The percentage of educational data mining analyzes is just 2.7%. Researchers have identified
educational data mining as an innovative tool for analyzing PISA data.
The collection of secondary data from ILSAs in dozens of countries over decades offers
researchers studying student mathematics achievement a valuable opportunity. Thus, considering
the noncognitive variables in question without regard to country or variable can be seen as an
alternative to the aforementioned limitations. Furthermore, taking a holistic approach can help
gain a better understanding of the overall picture of math achievement and the factors that
impact students’ success. The study by Aricak et al. (2023) emphasizes this issue, focusing on
noncognitive variables and their impact on reading performance in PISA 2018. In contrast to
other studies, the researchers sought to identify the most informative variables about student
performance, analyzing both country students and noncognitive factors together. The researchers
recommended applying this comprehensive approach to their studies in mathematics and science.
ML has recently been applied to PISA—one of the world’s most comprehensive ILSAs—to
explore and predict student performance. A growing number of studies have used these data to
identify factors that shape academic outcomes. For example, Zhu et al. (2025) analyzed the PISA
2022 dataset from the United States to predict mathematics achievement and pinpoint the variables with the strongest influence. Öz et al. (2025) examined model performance by comparing
stacking with several other ML techniques when predicting students’ results in mathematics,
science, and reading. Likewise, Huang et al. (2024) investigated the key determinants of mathematical literacy across six East Asian systems—Hong Kong, Macao, Taipei, Singapore, Japan,
and Korea—using ML methods. Cheung et al. (2024) took a broader perspective by analyzing
data from 79 countries and 103 variables to study academic resilience, employing Random Forest,
a typical black-box algorithm, to generate importance rankings. Gómez-Talal et al. (2025) focused
on predicting students’ mathematics performance using PISA 2022 data from Spain.
Although these studies have yielded valuable insights, most have concentrated either on
ranking variable importance or on optimizing predictive accuracy. The present study extends
this line of work by embedding interpretability into the core of the modeling process. Beyond
identifying the most predictive noncognitive variables and evaluating model performance, it
develops an interpretable model that exposes the hierarchical relationships among predictors. In
doing so, the study provides a model that not only performs accurately but can also be easily
interpreted by educators and policymakers, making its insights more actionable in real educational contexts.
In conclusion, using PISA 2022 data, this study seeks to address the following questions:
1.

The PISA 2022 application involved 613,774 students across 81 countries and economies.
PISA 2022 survey data was preferred in the analysis because the scope of the study was wider,
mathematics was the major domain in this cycle, and there were more survey items related to
mathematics.

Method
The study employed Information Gain, Decision Tree, and Random Forest ML approaches to
investigate the research questions. PISA 2022 test results and student questionnaires were utilized
as the primary data sources. The study is a secondary data analysis study that utilizes ML
methods. Glaser (1963) defines secondary data analysis research as the analysis of data collected
for another purpose, to address specific problems. Secondary data can be gathered from various
sources, such as systematic reviews, documentary analysis, government surveys, and student
performance tests like PISA (Smith, 2008).
Participants
PISA 2022 was conducted in 81 countries, which included 44 non-OECD economies and all
OECD countries except Luxembourg. This study consisted of 613,774 students from 81 countries
and economies, aged between 15 years, 3 months and 16 years, 2 months, who were enrolled in
school and had completed at least 6 years of formal education. Among the students, 50.2% are
male and 49.8% are female.
Variables
All variables used in this study consist of variables defined within the PISA 2022 Context
Questionnaire Framework, which is part of the PISA 2022 assessment (Oecd, 2023a). PISA 2022
includes seven questionnaires: Student, School, Financial Literacy, ICT, Well-being, Parent, and
Teacher. Among these, the Student and School questionnaires were considered core and were
administered in all participating countries/economies. The implementation of the other questionnaires was optional and left to the discretion of the participating countries/economies. As
mathematics was a major domain in PISA 2022, the questionnaires also assessed different
mathematical structures (Oecd, 2023a). To address the research questions outlined earlier, this
study incorporated data from the Student, Financial Literacy, Information Communication
Technology, Well-being, and Parent questionnaires.
PISA applications use questionnaire items to indirectly measure latent constructs, such as a
student’s self-efficacy in mathematics, sense of belonging, or socioeconomic background.
Meaningful indices are generated by applying transformations or scaling procedures to the items.
These indices are referred to as Derived Variables (DVs) by the OECD (2024). PISA 2022
includes three distinct types of DVs: simple questionnaire indices, scaled indices, and complex
composite indices. Simple questionnaire indices are created through arithmetic transformation
or recoding of one or more items. Scaled indices are derived from Item Response Theory (IRT)
(Rasch, 1960) models, including the Two-Parameter Logistic Model (2PLM) (Birnbaum, 1968)
and the Generalized Partial Credit Model (GPCM) (Muraki, 1992). Complex composite indices
are created by combining two or more items.
In PISA 2022, there were a total of 130 derived variables (DVs) across various questionnaires:
86 DVs (43 simple indices, 42 IRT indices, and 1 complex composite index) in the Student

The PISA 2022 test, as in 2015 and 2018, was mainly conducted via computer (Computer-Based
Assessment/CBA). In addition, paper-based assessment (PBA) tools were also provided for countries/
economies that did not have the opportunity to administer tests to their students via computer
(OECD, 2023b). The test aimed to measure an individual’s mathematical reasoning and their capacity
to formulate, use, and interpret mathematics to solve problems in real-world contexts for the major
domain of mathematics (OECD, 2023b). The test was designed and implemented as Multistage
adaptive testing (MSAT). MSAT, which was first used only for the main domain of reading in PISA
2018, was used for both the major domains of mathematics and reading in PISA 2022. The MSAT
design for mathematics in PISA 2022 consists of three stages, Core Stage, Stage 1 and Stage 2, with
a total of 99 units and 234 question items. The items were divided into three equivalent item sets,
each consisting of 78 items from 33 units. All students received the Core Stage, which contained
test booklets with questions of medium difficulty. During the next stage, students who completed
the MSAT were provided with test question booklets of either high or low difficulty in the first
stage, and in the second stage, they received test question booklets of high, medium, or low difficulty
depending on their performance. On the other hand, a subset of students (25%) received a linear
test instead of MSAT, regardless of their performance. The aim was to prevent students from facing
questions that might be either too simple or too complex for their level. The students received a
basic test question booklet from other item sets in the subsequent stages, after completing the one
in the Core Stage (OECD, 2024).
Based on the MSAT approach employed in PISA 2022, students who tackled high-difficulty
question booklets in Stage 2 displayed impressive math performance, whereas those who had
low-difficulty booklets showed weak performance. In the analysis conducted to verify this situation,
it was found that countries with a high proportion of students assigned to high-difficulty booklets
at Stage 2 were at the top of the PISA 2022 rankings, although countries with a low rate were at
the bottom of the rankings, and there was a correlation between these rates and the PISA 2022
mathematics test country rankings. The correlation coefficient was found to be 0.98. Details about
the low and high-level booklets assigned to Stage 1 and Stage 2 are provided in Appendix B.
In addition to survey data, the PISA 2022 webpage offers publicly accessible SPSS and SAS files
labeled “Cognitive Item Data File.” These files contain detailed information on the assignment of
adaptive and linear tests to students, including the specific stages and difficulty levels of test booklets
administered during adaptive testing. In ML analyses conducted based on the research questions of
the study, the data of students who were administered adaptive tests and assigned high-difficulty test
booklets in Stage 2 were used to represent students with high mathematics performance. Conversely,
the data of students assigned low-difficulty test booklets in Stage 2 were used to represent students
with low mathematics performance. This data was coded as “MS2_LEV” in SPSS and SAS files and
was used as the dependent variable in the analyses.
PISA is a large-scale international assessment that focuses on populations rather than on
individual student performance. In this assessment, scores called plausible values (PVs) are used
for student proficiency levels. In PISA 2022, students’ mathematics achievement levels were
reported with 10 PVs. In some studies based on PISA data, the average of these PVs, the highest
PV, or a specific PV has been used to represent student achievement. However, although PVs
are useful in large-scale assessments with large populations, they are not recommended for
individual-level scores (Jewsbury et al., 2024; OECD, 2009; Wu, 2005). Therefore, in this study,
instead of PV scores, the MS2_LEV variable, the details of which were explained above, was
chosen to represent students’ mathematics achievement at the individual level.

Data analysis procedure
ML analyses utilized information gain, decision tree, and random forest methods. In accordance with the previous headings, the 130 DVs in the questionnaire data were utilized as
the independent variable, whereas the difficulty level data of the test booklet assigned to
the students in Stage 2 (MS2_LEV) were employed as the dependent variable. The questionnaire and cognitive data files on the PISA website were merged into one file before
applying ML algorithms. The merging process utilized the CNTSTUID (Country Student
ID) 1 data as the key field, which was a unique code for each participating student and can
be found in both files.
Depending on the research questions of the study, whereas the focus was on students with
high mathematics performance and students with low mathematics performance, students whose
mathematics performance was at a medium level and therefore were assigned a medium difficulty
level booklet (MS2_LEVMedium) were excluded from the data. Following this process, ML analysis
was conducted on N = 293995 student data from 76 countries (See Appendix C). The dataset
consisted of derived variables represented by numerical values, and cases with missing data were
excluded from the analyses. In ILSA datasets, which are very large in size, deleting cases with
missing data is frequently applied. As also noted by Huang and Keller (2025), listwise deletion
is still commonly used for handling missing data in such contexts. OECD’s official PISA documentation (OECD, 2009) also describes procedures that involve the removal of cases with
missing values as part of data preparation in large-scale assessments.
Information gain
The first step in the ML analyses was to calculate the information gain values of the variables. Quinlan (1986) introduced information gain, a numerical measure based on Shannon’s
(1948) entropy formula, which is employed to evaluate the importance of a variable in
classification analyses. Variables with higher information gain values in the dataset play a
bigger role in improving classification performance by reducing entropy compared to other
variables. Some decision tree algorithms (Quinlan, 1986) utilize information gain to build
the tree structure and evaluate feature significance. In various fields, information gain is
frequently utilized to measure the significance of independent variables. For example, Al-Alawi
et al. (2023) used information gain analysis in their study, where they examined the factors
that negatively affect performance among low-performing university students. Similarly,
Bertram et al. (2021) investigated the extent to which computer-assisted assessment methods
in history education were suitable for evaluating student answers and examined which linguistic features had the highest information gain value.
In the information gain analysis in the study, 23 variables with no data were removed from
the 130 variables presented in the data file. The information gain values of the remaining 107
variables were calculated according to the dependent variable and ranked from largest to smallest
(Appendix D). Prasetiyowati et al. (2021) stated that different techniques can be used to determine the cutoff point (COP) in feature selection according to information gain values and
suggested that the standard deviation values of the features can also be used in determining the
value. The COP was determined using the +3SD value and formula (1), which utilized the mean
(M = 0.0254) and standard deviations (SD = 0.02276) of the information gain values for variable
filtering. Subsequent analyzes were continued with the Mathematics self-efficacy: Formal and
applied mathematics (MATHEFF), Home possessions (HOMEPOS), Index of economic, social
and cultural status (ESCS) variables, whose information gain value was higher than the COP
value (0.0937).

Decision tree
In the next step of ML analysis, a decision tree model was created using the variables selected
in the information gain analysis. The purpose of creating the model was to examine the hierarchical position of the MATHEFF, HOMEPOS, ESCS variables selected as a result of the
information gain analysis in the decision tree model and to analyze the predictive performance
of the model created with these variables. Unlike many modern black-box techniques—such as
deep learning, ensemble methods, and gradient boosting—that mainly strive for higher predictive
accuracy, this study, as discussed earlier, places equal emphasis on making the model interpretable. To achieve this, a decision tree model was employed because it offers a clear and transparent structure, which is crucial for uncovering the hierarchical relationships among noncognitive
variables. In doing so, decision trees help overcome one of the main drawbacks of black-box
models by allowing users to visually follow and understand how the model reaches its decisions.
Decision trees are one of the most commonly used models in ML for classification and
regression problems. In the process of constructing a decision tree, starting from the feature
that best splits the dataset, the “root”, “node” and “leaf ” components containing decisions or
predictions are created iteratively. Different metrics, such as information gain or the Gini Index,
can be used in the partitioning process of the tree construction. Information gain is used as
the splitting criterion in some decision tree algorithms, such as ID3 (Quinlan, 1986), C4.5
(Quinlan, 1993), CART (Breiman et al., 1984). Decision tree models are also advantageous in
terms of being clear and interpretable due to their hierarchical structure. Decision trees are
frequently used in many disciplines, especially in classification and regression problems. In their
study, Muñoz-Rodríguez et al. (2023) used decision tree analysis to explore how young individuals perceive the risks of internet use. In their study, Hussain et al. (2022) utilized a decision
tree to examine student performance and the factors that influence it. Wang et al. (2023)
employed decision trees and multiple techniques to identify bullying victimization risk in
adolescents.
The decision tree model in this study used the CART algorithm. This algorithm is based on
recursive binary splitting; each node is split into two child nodes, and this process is applied
recursively (Breiman et al., 2017). On the other hand, in algorithms such as ID3 and C4.5, some
nodes may be binary although others may be multi-branching, showing variation (Quinlan, 1986;
1993). This multi-branching capability can lead to overly complex and unwieldy trees that are
difficult to interpret. In addition, the CART algorithm can effectively handle both categorical
and continuous data types (Breiman et al., 1984), which makes it particularly suitable for the
present study, as the independent variables (DVs) consist of continuous (numerical) data. For
these reasons, the CART algorithm was selected as the decision tree approach used in this study.
As an additional benefit, the CART algorithm is among the algorithms widely preferred in
analyses (Martínez-Abad et al., 2020) and is directly supported within open-source ML libraries
and tools such as Scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011) and KNIME (Berthold et al., 2009). The
analysis did not include students who were missing data in MATHEFF, HOMEPOS, ESCS variables. The decision tree analysis continued with N = 216458 samples from 71 countries (See
Appendix C). Additionally, the class label distribution within the MS2_LEV variable was MS2_
LEVLow = 141515 (65.38%), MS2_LEVHigh = 74943 (34.62%).
Imbalanced data refers to datasets with unequal class distribution ratios in ML analyses.
Imbalanced data negatively affects model performance in classification analyzes (He & Garcia,
2009). To address this issue, under-sampling techniques like NearMiss (Zhang & Mani, 2003)
can be used to decrease the majority class samples, aligning them with the minority class ratio.
Alternatively, over-sampling techniques like SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002) can be employed to
increase the minority class samples and align them with the majority class ratio. After removing
students with missing data, the dataset was balanced using the NearMiss under sampling method.
This was necessary because of the unbalanced class distribution rates of MS2_LEVLow (65.38%)
and MS2_LEVHigh (34.62%). After NearMiss was applied, the class distribution ratio of the dataset

was equalized (MS2_LEVLow=74943, MS2_LEVHigh=74943) and the decision tree model was created with this dataset, consisting of 71 countries and 149886 students. although creating the
model, 80% of the dataset (119908 samples) was used for training and 20% (29978 samples) for

iteration, one part is reserved for testing although the remaining k-1 parts are used for training.
This process is repeated k times, and the average of the obtained results is taken to estimate
the generalization performance of the model.
In decision tree analysis, Accuracy (ACC), Recall/True Positive Rate (TPR), Precision/Positive
Predictive Value (PPV), F1-Score and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) scores were used
to evaluate the success of the model. In studies such as Batool et al. (2023) and Pan et al.
(2025), these metrics are reported as the most commonly used evaluation methods. These scores
are obtained from the True Positives (TPs), True Negatives (TNs), False Positives (FPs) and False
Negatives (FNs) values in the table called the confusion matrix used in model evaluation in
classification analyses. TPs represent samples in the dataset that are actually positive in class
but predicted as positive by the model, FNs represent samples in the dataset that are actually
positive in class but predicted as negative by the model, TNs represent samples in the dataset
that are actually negative in class but predicted as negative by the model, and FPs represent
samples in the dataset that are actually negative in class but predicted as positive by the model
(Kotu & Deshpande, 2019). ACC expresses the overall performance of the model, Recall expresses
how many of the actually positive samples were correctly predicted by the model, Precision
expresses how many of the samples predicted by the model as positive were actually positive,
and MCC expresses the correlation of the true classes with the predicted class labels. F1-score
is the harmonic average of the Precision and Recall values (Chicco et al., 2021).
In addition, the ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curve and the area under this curve
(AUC—Area Under the Curve) were also used to evaluate the model’s discriminative power
between classes. The ROC curve visualizes the classification performance of the model at different threshold values, although the AUC value summarizes the model’s success in distinguishing
between positive and negative classes into a single scalar metric. An AUC value close to 0.5
indicates that the model is making random predictions, whereas a value close to 1 indicates
that it has high discriminative power (Fawcett, 2006).

the importance levels of MATHEFF, HOMEPOS, ESCS variables selected as a result of information gain analysis. This approach served as a robust validation step to confirm the consistency
of the findings obtained earlier. Random forest is based on creating a stronger model by combining many decision trees. Each decision tree is trained on a randomly selected subset of data
and the final estimate is made using the results estimated by each tree. In addition, random
forest is one of the methods used to rank the importance of features in ML analysis (Breiman,
2001). In this respect, random forest has been used to determine feature/variable importance in
many studies in social sciences, such as education and psychology (Beaulac & Rosenthal, 2019;
Chung et al., 2022; Koch et al., 2022; Stieger & Wunderl, 2022). According to the systematic
review conducted by Ersozlu et al. (2024), the random forest algorithm is one of the most
commonly used methods in the analysis of educational data. For these reasons, the random
forest algorithm was also preferred in the present study.
In the random forest analysis, as in the decision tree analysis, students who did not have

Discussion
The objective of this study is to identify the noncognitive feature that best predicts students’
math performance. The analysis results indicate that the MATHEFF variable has the highest
scores and ranks first in importance among 130 noncognitive variables. This variable is situated
at the top hierarchical level in the decision tree analysis, known as the root level. Across all
analyses, mathematics self-efficacy consistently ranked as the most significant variable, irrespective
of country or noncognitive factors.
Bandura (1997) defines self-efficacy as an individual’s belief in their ability to achieve specific
gains in a subject through necessary behaviors. Self-efficacy in terms of mathematical achievement, as explained by (Hacket & Betz, 1989), is an individual’s confidence in their capability to
successfully accomplish a particular math task. According to Zakariya (2022), mathematics
self-efficacy involves students’ understanding of their past accomplishments, their assessment of
their own abilities, and their predictions of how they will perform in future math tasks. Therefore,
it is an important structure that determines students’ engagement in mathematical tasks. When
students do not believe in their abilities to accomplish certain tasks, they do not put in the
effort required to successfully complete the tasks. In addition, when they do not believe that
they can be successful at a given task, they need to have much higher levels of self-control and
motivation to be successful (OECD, 2013). In general, it is widely accepted that there is a positive relationship between students’ mathematical self-efficacy and mathematical achievement,
and there are many studies supporting this (e.g. Lee, 2009; Pajares & Miller, 1995; Stankov et al.,
2017; You et al., 2021).
When looking at the studies using PISA data, it is seen that mathematics self-efficacy is
emphasized as an important variable in many studies. Huang et al. (2024) conducted an analysis
using data from six East Asian countries/economies within the scope of PISA 2022 to predict
students’ mathematical literacy and to identify the determining factors affecting this performance.
The findings of the study reveal that mathematics self-efficacy is the strongest predictor variable.
In the study conducted by Wang et al. (2023), the researchers conducted a systematic literature

Journal of Research on Technology in Education

15

review on the factors that predict mathematics success in PISA. In their study, where they
synthesized the findings of 156 peer-reviewed articles, they found that fourteen factors, including
mathematics self-efficacy, had a positive or tended to have a positive effect on students’ mathematics achievement. You et al. (2021) aimed to investigate the factors that predict students’
mathematics achievement by evaluating student and school-level factors using PISA 2012 data.
In particular, they found that interest in mathematics and mathematics self-efficacy had a positive
effect on mathematics achievement among student variables. Karakolidis et al. (2016) showed
in their study using PISA 2012 Greece data that mathematics self-efficacy, along with variables
such as gender, mathematics self-concept, and attendance at preschool education, can statistically
significantly predict students’ mathematics achievement, and that students with high mathematics
self-efficacy and lower mathematics anxiety perform statistically significantly better in PISA
mathematics tests. In a study conducted by Zhao and Ding (2019), researchers also revealed
that mathematics self-efficacy is the only common factor that positively affects students’ mathematics achievement in both countries using data obtained from the United States and China
in the PISA 2012 application. As a result, the findings obtained in this study regarding mathematics self-efficacy are consistent with previous studies. The reason why mathematics self-efficacy
emerged as the most important variable may be that self-efficacy serves as a construct that
partially mediates the effects of many non-cognitive variables such as motivation, anxiety, and
strategy use. Therefore, it can be expected to occupy a central position in the model. On the
other hand, although some variables (e.g. teacher support, home environment) may differ across
countries, self-efficacy is consistently associated with achievement in many contexts.
In addition, Zhao and Ding (2019) identified self-efficacy as a common positive predictor in
both U.S. and Chinese samples. This indicates that self-efficacy is a fundamental variable with
global validity in the context of mathematics achievement. This study, through a comprehensive
analysis including a broad set of variables, demonstrated the high importance level of mathematics self-efficacy. Considering that previous studies have generally been conducted with limited
variables or limited country data (Karakolidis et al., 2016; You et al., 2021), the findings open
the discussion of generalizability within a broader framework. This contribution also supports
the models in the literature that explain mathematics achievement and reveals that it may be
beneficial for educational policies to take into account the dimension of self-efficacy.
Notably, two out of the top three variables in the analysis (HOMEPOS and ESCS) are linked
to socioeconomic status (SES). Baker (2014) defines SES as a metric for evaluating a person’s
economic and social status. According to a definition made by the U.S. Department of Education
(2012), a student’s SES consists of components such as parents’ education, occupational status,
household or family income, and these three components are called the “Big Three”. Similarly,
according to Zhao et al. (2023), family SES for students generally consists of three elements:
home ownership, parents’ occupational status, and education level. SES is considered as one of
the variables that affects students’ academic success. The academic success of students is significantly impacted by the socioeconomic status of their parents, as indicated by Sirin (2005).
ILSA applications, including PISA and TIMSS, incorporate background questionnaires that capture
noncognitive variables linked to students’ SES.
The “Economic, Social and Cultural Status” module (Module 2) in the PISA 2022 application
includes ESCS and HOMEPOS variables. The ESCS variable is calculated based on three indicators: highest parental occupation status (HISEI), highest parental education in years (PAREDINT),
and home ownership (HOMEPOS). The reason behind using these three components, which
align with previous PISA cycles, is that socioeconomic status is theoretically conceptualized
based on the aforementioned “Big Three” (OECD, 2024). The calculation of ESCS scores in
PISA 2022 followed the methodology used in PISA 2018. The methodology utilized an average
calculation of the three components to standardize the component scores and composite ESCS
score, resulting in a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 across OECD countries (OECD,
2024). In the HOMEPOS variable, students indicated whether and how they had certain items
in their homes, such as a room of their own, educational software, and applications. This variable

also includes country/economy-specific items, which are considered local measures of family
wealth in the context of the country/economy (OECD, 2024).
In terms of mathematics achievement, the existence of a relationship between students’ mathematics achievement and SES in general has been revealed by many studies. A similar situation
applies to studies conducted using PISA data. Wang et al. (2023) examined the studies using
PISA data and found that the student’s grade level and general family SES were positively correlated with mathematics achievement. In a study conducted by Lee et al. (2019), researchers
aimed to determine which SES variables had the best predictive validity for academic achievement
based on international data sets from PISA 2003, 2006, 2009, and 2012. Among 10 SES measures,
ESCS and HOMEPOS variables showed superior predictive power for student achievement, and
among the “Big Three” variables, HOMEPOS had strong predictive validity. Again, according to
the researchers, a composite measure consisting of occupation, education, and some family wealth
variables is likely to perform better than a single SES measure as a predictor of academic
achievement. Niehues et al. (2020) found in their study using PISA 2012 Turkey data that there
is a strong relationship between family SES and mathematics achievement. Researchers have
found that children from socioeconomically advantaged families have better math performance
and attend higher-performing schools. SES has the ability to impact math performance by linking
to various factors like health. In a study by Barr (2015) on 9th graders, it was found that students from higher family SES backgrounds have better health and academic outcomes in high
school. Based on research, it is commonly inferred that there is a significant relationship between
students’ math achievement and SES. The research findings also reinforce this inference in this
regard. Conversely, studies utilizing ILSA data typically focused on specific variables or economies and employed standard statistical methods. A remarkable finding of this study is that
regardless of the country, data on the relationship between SES and mathematics achievement
were analyzed using diverse methods like ML, and similar conclusions were reached.
Another objective of the study is to evaluate the effectiveness of prediction models based on
highly predictive noncognitive variables. To achieve this, a decision tree model was built using
noncognitive variables that showed higher ranking in information gain and random forest analyses. The MATHEFF variable is positioned at the highest level of the tree, as per the model.
At the second level, the HOMEPOS variable follows this variable. The variable positions in this
model, based on all PISA 2022 data, are most consistent with prior research. Gamazo and
Martínez-Abad (2020) conducted a study using ML methods to analyze factors related to students’ performance in reading, science, and mathematics on PISA 2018. In their secondary data
analysis, they built a decision tree model to predict school performance. The variable with the
highest predictive power in the root node of the model was the mean SES in schools. Prediction
models were developed by Kılıç Depren et al. (2017) using 11 student-based variables and three
different algorithms: decision tree, bayesian network, and logistic regression. The models were
built using TIMSS 2011 data. The research discovered that various factors, such as home education resources, parents’ education level, student engagement, and appreciation for learning,
significantly influenced math achievement.
The findings indicate that, similar to self-efficacy, SES has a strong effect on students’ mathematics achievement. Among the main reasons for this are that families with high SES can
provide their children with higher-quality educational opportunities and learning resources, that
these students generally attend better-equipped schools, and that parents’ educational level positively influences children’s academic motivation and perceptions of self-efficacy. These findings
are consistent with previous research; however, the original contribution of the study lies in
reaching similar results through the use of contemporary methods such as ML, applied to a
broad set of variables and a comprehensive dataset. Thus, the determining role of SES in mathematics achievement is reaffirmed through an alternative methodology, providing an additional
contribution to the existing understanding of this issue.
In addition, one of the key methodological findings that advances the field lies not only in
the ranking of variable importance but also in the hierarchical structure presented in Figure 1.

can be meaningfully predicted even with the use of only three key variables, and it particularly
reveals the strong predictive role of mathematics self-efficacy and socioeconomic indicators.
Therefore, we can conclude that the findings from the analyses conducted on the data of all
students, irrespective of country or culture, using all variables in the PISA application, a
well-known ILSA, align with the existing literature. The international study, utilizing big data
and ML techniques, solidifies the role of mathematics self-efficacy in determining students’
mathematics achievement. Additionally, variables linked to socioeconomic status can be seen as
potential influencers of math achievement.
This study was conducted using data obtained from hundreds of thousands of students without
making any distinction between countries or regions. Unlike analyses in the literature that are
generally carried out with limited variable sets or data from a small number of countries, this
research simultaneously considered 130 variables within a large dataset covering more than 80
countries. The findings reaffirm well-known strong predictors such as mathematics self-efficacy
and socioeconomic indicators; however, the fact that these results were obtained through information gain–based variable selection and ML algorithms provides a methodological contribution
that is original. Therefore, the study not only verifies established patterns but also offers valuable
contributions to the literature in terms of the validity of the methods used and the new evidence
it brings to learning research. In addition, approaching mathematics achievement from a broader
perspective may enable a better understanding of the multidimensional nature of the phenomenon and allow the “big picture” to be seen more clearly in the literature. In these respects, the
study highlights the advantages of ML methods in the analysis of large-scale educational data,
adding new depth and validity to existing findings in the literature. Moreover, the findings of
this study provide strong evidence for education policymakers and practitioners to prioritize
interventions targeting these factors. They highlight that national and school-level policies should
systematically address not only cognitive skills but also noncognitive dimensions, thereby contributing to more effectively enhancing students’ mathematics achievement and learning outcomes.

Limitations of the study
It is important to consider certain limitations when interpreting the results of the current study.
One goal of the study was to identify the most effective noncognitive variables for predicting
math achievement. Secondary data from the PISA 2022 application was employed for this study.
Therefore, the data and noncognitive variables analyzed in this study are constrained by the
data collected and variables used in the PISA 2022 application.
In addition, the importance ranking of non-cognitive variables in the study was obtained
using the Information Gain and Random Forest algorithms. However, if different algorithms
(e.g. SHAP—SHapley Additive exPlanations, LASSO—Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection
Operator) were employed, differences might emerge in the importance levels and rankings of
the variables. Finally, since only low- and high-achievement groups were considered in the
analyses, the findings may not be generalizable to students demonstrating moderate levels of
mathematics achievement. Another limitation concerns the handling of missing data. In this
study, cases with incomplete responses were excluded, which is a common practice in large-scale
educational datasets such as PISA. Nevertheless, different missing data strategies may lead to
variations in the ranking of predictor importance.

Suggestions for future studies
Based on the analysis results, it is recommended that future studies utilize ILSA data to investigate noncognitive variables. The best predictor in this study was MATHEFF (Mathematics
self-efficacy: Formal and applied mathematics). However, another variable related to self-efficacy,
MATHEF21 (Mathematics self-efficacy: Mathematical reasoning and twenty first century mathematics), is ranked lower. Studies that focus on mathematics self-efficacy variables can shed light
on the reasons for this.
Common statistical techniques are typically used in research that utilizes secondary data
obtained through ILSAs, as mentioned in earlier sections. According to Zheng et al. (2024), data
mining techniques are regarded as an innovative tool for analyzing these data. Researchers in
the field of mathematics achievement can explore new opportunities by incorporating data mining
techniques from artificial intelligence. Alternatively, when performing analyses in environments
with high computing capacity, such as GPU-supported cloud platforms, researchers can work
with much larger data sets and a greater number of variables, including common statistical
analyses. This alternative has the potential to deepen our knowledge of mathematics achievement
research or education research in a broader sense.

importance ranking of the variables, although the CART decision tree algorithm was employed

Therefore, decision tree models could be developed using different algorithms such as C4.5,
XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016), and Random Forest, and their performances could be compared. On the other hand, ML methods encompass a wide range; beyond decision trees, many
other methods such as Naive Bayes, k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), and artificial neural networks
can also be used for this purpose.
In ILSA applications, in addition to self-report data, the more widespread and systematic
collection of data related to students’ interactions with the system in computer-based applications
(e.g. process data, log data) and the analysis of these data particularly through ML methods
may provide significant contributions to research. In this way, basing analyses on multiple data
sources can offer valuable insights into areas such as students’ problem-solving strategies and
cognitive processes—domains that are difficult to capture with self-reports—thus enabling more
comprehensive results to be obtained. Future research could explore alternative approaches (e.g.
imputation techniques) to assess the robustness of the findings and examine how different
treatments of missing data may influence the results.
Finally, the source codes used in this study are open source and can be accessed by researchers
globally through GitHub. These codes can be readily modified and applied to different research
endeavors. In the analysis of PISA 2022 data, information gain, random forest, and decision
tree methods were employed. For future studies on math achievement, it might be suggested to
explore alternative ML methods or parameters using various ILSA datasets.
