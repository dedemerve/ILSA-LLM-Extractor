A B S T R A C T  
Conversational agents such as chatbots have recently gained a lot of traction in various industries, including 
education, marketing, and healthcare. In K–12 and higher education, conversational agents have been used to 
boost student learning and motivation. Recently, new applications of conversational agents, such as 
conversation-based assessments, have emerged in the context of educational assessments. Such applications aim 
to enhance students’ assessment experiences through the use of conversational agents in administering items (or 
tasks) and providing feedback to students. To discuss the utility of conversational agents in assessments, this 
paper puts a special emphasis on test-taking effort in low-stakes assessments. It provides a brief introduction to 
educational assessments and student test-taking motivation, presents the concept of conversational agents for 
learning and assessment along with several examples, examines the benefits and challenges of conversational 
agents used for learning and assessment purposes, and discusses the concept of conversation-based assessment. 
This position paper contributes to the literature by discussing the utility of conversation-based assessments as a 
novel tool to enhance test-taking effort among students and thereby obtain a more accurate representation of 
students’ academic performance. Specifically, we describe the advantages and disadvantages of using 
conversation-based assessments for addressing the lack of test-taking effort in digital formative assessments and 
discuss how conversation-based assessments can foster a more engaging testing experience for students.   
1. Introduction 
Conversational agents are interactive dialogue systems that can 
process a query (e.g., a question) and produce a human-like conversa­
tion using natural language processing (Chien & Yao, 2020). During the 
last two decades, researchers have been able to design and deploy suc­
cessful conversational agents, thanks to technological breakthroughs 
and advances in computational linguistics, artificial intelligence, and 
natural language processing (e.g., D’Mello & Graesser, 2013; Graesser 
et al., 2003). Recently, large language models such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT 
have demonstrated impressive performances on various tasks, such as 
generating human-like text, summarizing text, and retrieving answers to 
a set of questions within a conversation format. These models are trained 
on massive amounts of data, allowing them to learn the rules, patterns, 
and structure of language. These conversational agents are constantly 
improving as more data is used to train them and new techniques are 
developed to enhance their performance. They have the potential to 
revolutionize a wide range of fields, from healthcare to education (e.g., 
AutoTutor; Graesser et al., 2003). 
To date, researchers have developed and used different conversa­
tional agents in education and investigated their effects on learning 
outcomes. Conversational agents can improve motivation, feedback, 
personalization, and student learning (Craig et al., 2004; Howard et al., 
2017; VanLehn et al., 2007). These agents are mainly designed and used 
for instructional purposes –– such as virtual teaching assistants (e.g., 
Goel & Polepeddi, 2016), tutors (e.g., Dzikovska et al., 2014), and peers 
(or learning companions) (e.g., Howard et al., 2017) –– to support the 
student learning process. As large language models such as chatGPT 
continue to improve, they also have the potential to revolutionize how 
educational assessments are conducted. In recent years, conversational 
agents have been designed for assessment purposes to measure student 
learning outcomes in an interactive assessment environment –– namely, 
conversation-based assessments (CBA) (e.g., Ruan et al., 2019). CBA has 
recently been developed and discussed in the context of formative

This paper aims to discuss the problem of non-effortful test-taking

1.1. Assessment mediums 
Educational assessment plays a critical role in the process of 
learning, such as enabling teachers to measure and monitor student 
learning and holding students accountable for their learning (e.g., 
January et al., 2018; Tolar et al., 2014). Technology has impacted the 
mediums of educational assessment by advancing the design, adminis­
tration, and scoring of assessments. Computers have revolutionized the 
traditional assessment format (i.e., paper-and-pencil testing, PPT, where 
students provide their responses in writing) through their advanced and 
accelerated use at different levels of education (Wang & Shin, 2010). 
The digitization of educational assessments can be summarized under 
two categories: linear testing (i.e., computer-based testing; CBT) and 
adaptive testing (i.e., computerized adaptive testing; CAT) (Wang & 
Shin, 2010). 
CBT offers several advantages over PPT, such as flexibility of design, 
easy administration, and objective scoring, while, similar to PPT, it 
delivers the test items with a fixed-length form in a non-adaptive testing 
environment (Brüggemann et al., 2023). For example, test items on both 
PPT and CBT can be identical for all test-takers, and the scoring pro­
cedure 
(e.g., 
identifying 
correct 
or 
incorrect 
responses 
to 
selected-response items) can also be the same. Thus, the major change 
from PPT to CBT is the delivery mode of educational assessment. 
However, with advancements in technology and measurement theories, 
the adoption of CAT has changed both the delivery mode and adminis­
tration of educational assessments (Stone & Davey, 2011). In PPT and 
CBT, all test-takers typically respond to the same test items, whereas in 
CAT, each test-taker responds to a set of items adaptively selected from a 
large item pool based on the test-taker’s ability level (Brüggemann et al., 
2023; Stone & Davey, 2011). CAT aims to select and present test items at 
the most appropriate difficulty level, yielding a custom test form for 
each test-taker. Previous studies showed that test-takers are often more 
motivated to take a computerized test (i.e., CBT or CAT) over a tradi­
tional, paper-and-pencil assessment (e.g., Chua, 2012). However, 
administering an assessment in a computerized format does not neces­
sarily guarantee high motivation and test-taking effort for all test-takers. 
1.2. Student motivation in computerized formative assessments 
Computerized testing has enabled researchers to investigate various 
test-taking behaviors (e.g., effortful and non-effortful test-taking) 
through ancillary information obtained from the assessments, such as 
the amount of time students spend on each item, the number of response 
changes, and logs of how test-takers navigate through the items. Re­
searchers who investigated test-taking behaviors in computerized 
testing found that unmotivated test-takers might show non-effortful test- 
taking behavior in both summative and formative assessments (Barry 
et al., 2010; Simzar et al., 2015). However, students who participate in 
formative assessments are more likely to show non-effortful test-taking 
behavior as there are fewer or no personal consequences for them, given 
the low-stakes nature of formative assessments (Ekl¨of, 2010). In sum­
mative assessments, students are more likely to be driven to achieve 
their best due to personal consequences (e.g., grades, graduation, and 
diploma) (Angrist & Lavy, 2009). Formative assessments, however, have 
fewer, if any, personal consequences for students. Although intrinsic 
motivations such as peer competition or a desire to impress teachers can 
inspire students to provide their best effort, some students may be hes­
itant to engage in formative assessments and consequently fail to opti­
mize their performance (Ekl¨of, 2010; Wise, 2006). 
Unfortunately, scores obtained from unmotivated test-takers with 
non-effortful test-taking fail to reflect the test-takers’ actual ability 
levels in the target construct being measured and threaten the validity of 
decisions and inferences made based on the test results (Ekl¨of, 2006, 
2010; Haladyna & Downing, 2004). To address this problem, re­
searchers have begun to look for alternative ways to maintain student 
motivation while taking a computerized formative assessment to mini­
mize the threats to the validity of decisions and inferences made based 
on educational assessments. Recent studies showed that student moti­
vation during the test administration could be maintained through 
timely interventions such as sending a proctor notification to the com­
puter screen for students who appear to be disengaged based on rapid 
guessing behavior (Wise et al., 2019). This finding highlights the 
importance of interactivity that is missing in PPT, CBT, and CAT to 
motivate test-takers when participating in an assessment. There seem to 
be promising approaches to increase students’ test-taking motivation 
through some interaction (e.g., a timely intervention) before they are 
completely disengaged from the assessment. However, there is still a 
need for natural interactivity in educational assessment (e.g., teacher 
and student interaction or student and student interaction) rather than 
performing a reactive intervention repeatedly (e.g., sending a warning 
message to each disengaged test-taker’s screen). Today’s emerging 
technologies involving artificial intelligence and natural language pro­
cessing can address the lack of interactivity in formative assessments 
and move the design and administration of assessments beyond linear 
and adaptive computerized testing. Providing students (or test-takers) 
with the opportunity to be interactive during a test administration can 
increase their motivation towards the assessment process, resulting in 
more effortful test-taking behavior and valid test results representing 
their actual ability levels. 
1.3. A hybrid approach to assessment 
The research into technology-based support in education has been 
motivated by the increasing demand for diverse and personalized 
educational needs. The intelligent tutoring system (ITS) was one of the 
first attempts. In the late 1970s, ITS was introduced to create comput­
erized learning environments that could optimize each student’s 
learning (D’Mello & Graesser, 2013). ITS blends instruction and 
assessment for instructional purposes by answering student questions in 
real-time, giving immediate feedback to student responses, and guiding 
them on what to do next (Graesser et al., 2014). Research showed that 
ITS applications helped students learn more effectively while out­
performing both computer-based training applications and novice 
human tutors (Corbett, 2001; Dodds & Fletcher, 2004; Wisher & 
Fletcher, 2004). 
Although ITS has great potential for personalized education, they 
still have some limitations, such as treating learners as passive knowl­
edge recipients and not considering the notion of interaction within the 
learning context. To address these limitations, researchers have 
attempted to develop more advanced systems that could naturally 
interact with students by simulating human teachers (i.e., conversa­
tional agents; e.g., Rus et al., 2013). A conversational agent can produce

2. Conversational agents 
Conversational agents build on existing education systems and are a 
natural interface for the students (e.g., Aleven et al., 2001, 2004). They 
can be integrated into the learning environment and defined as inter­
active systems that enable students to learn interactively. Conversa­
tional agents can mimic a human-like conversation with the user in 
natural language through different communication channels (e.g., 
Weerasinghe et al., 2009). They simulate human dialogue mechanisms 
by processing a user’s natural-language text or voice input and gener­
ating meaningful responses. The conversations are generally managed 
through simple text-based forms where students type their answers or 
questions using a keyboard, and the conversational agents also send a 
reply through text (e.g., QuizBot; Ruan et al., 2019). Some systems use a 
simpler avatar (e.g., CALMsystem; Kerly et al., 2008a), while others use 
embodied conversational agents that display emotion and gesture (e.g., 
AutoTutor; Graesser et al., 1999; 2003). Other systems can manage the 
conversation through voice output with text-to-speech synthesis (e.g., 
AutoTutor; Graesser et al., 1999; 2003). 
Conversational agents enable and highlight learning from social 
interaction because students learn through conversations with teachers, 
family, or peers (e.g., Azevedo et al., 2009). Students can engage with 
conversational agents by doing tasks while conducting conversations. 
They also create a more interactive learning environment for students, 
similar to one-on-one interactions with teachers. They may play a sig­
nificant role in delivering educational content as they convincingly 
mimic how a human teacher or student would behave as a conversa­
tional partner, improvingthe technology used for educational purposes 
(Rus et al., 2015). With the help of conversational agents, it is possible to 
provide a personalized learning environment where each student learns 
at their own pace (e.g., McNamara et al., 2004). A conversational agent 
can tailor the length and content of authentic dialogue with each student 
and provide them with interactive feedback (e.g., Katz et al., 2021). 
Conversational agents can revolutionize students’ learning by providing 
help anytime and anywhere. Thus, conversational agents have the po­
tential to reach a wide range of educational goals in both K-12 and 
higher education contexts, and as a result, in the last few years, there has 
been an increase in their use. Although these agents are mainly designed 
for instructional purposes –– such as virtual teaching assistants, tutors, 
and peers (or learning companions) –– they have a wide range of 
application possibilities in the field of education. 
2.1. Examples of conversational agents in learning 
During the learning process, students are likely to have questions 
about the course content or structure, and human teachers attempt to 
answer these questions as much as possible. The issue, however, is the 
unbalanced ratio of human teachers to students in the educational 
setting, meaning that each student cannot receive the individual support 
they need. Conversational agents for instructional purposes address this 
issue and come into play, answering questions and assisting all students 
with their learning. Therefore, conversational agents can help human 
teachers accommodate students’ questions and address individual 
learning needs (e.g., Cai et al., 2009). 
In the educational setting, conversational agents have been primarily 
used for delivering instructional materials to students and designed to 
act as teaching assistants, tutors, or peers. The agents play the role of 
teaching assistants, tutors, or peers as they try to teach, help, and 
mentor. A tutor or teaching assistant agent teaches and supports 
learning, while a peer agent acts as an informed teaching companion to 
guide human students through the learning process or acts as a novice or 
inexperienced to be guided by the human student (Kerly et al., 2008b). 
Even though a conversational agent typically plays a single role (e.g., 
tutor or peer), some conversational agents can handle multiple roles 
simultaneously (e.g., tutor and peer in CALMsystem; Kerly et al., 2008a). 
To reflect the current uses of conversational agents in learning, some 
examples of conversational agents are explained further. 
2.1.1. Conversational teaching assistants 
Conversational teaching assistants have been designed and used to 
answer student questions about the course content or structure (Bene­
detto et al., 2019). They can help teachers in their daily tasks by 
answering questions from students. This timely support can address the 
lack of real-time responses to the student questions because teachers’ 
responses to student questions might be delayed and this delay often 
grows as the number of students and questions increases. Jill Watson is 
an example of a conversational teaching assistant designed by Goel and 
Polepeddi (2016) to be used in their online graduate-level course. Jill 
Watson operated the online discussion forums in different tasks, 
including responses to student introductions and frequently asked 
questions (e.g., length and format of the assignments) and weekly course 
announcements. 
2.1.2. Conversational tutors 
Conversational tutors act as human tutors and provide content sup­
port through tutorials and feedback (e.g., Aleven et al., 2001, 2004; 
Azevedo et al., 2009; Heffernan, 2003; Weerasinghe et al., 2009). A 
conversational tutor can teach or revise the subject, answer questions, 
and assist students in answering questions. AutoTutor is a well-known 
conversational agent that was created with the aim of tutoring. The 
project began in 1997 (Graesser et al., 1999), and the researchers have 
been working on and updating the system for over two decades. Auto­
Tutor is designed to interact with students in natural language, simu­
lating human tutors such as their dialogue moves and pedagogical 
tactics (Graesser et al., 2004, 2008). It is supplemented by 
three-dimensional interactive simulations that include interactions be­
tween two animated conversational agents (a tutor and a student) and 
students to improve engagement and learning (Graesser et al., 2005; 
Jackson & Graesser, 2006). 
2.1.3. Conversational peers 
Conversational peers (or learning companions) can mimic a high- 
performing or low-performing student peer and act as an activity part­
ner (e.g., Cai et al., 2009; Howard et al., 2017; Kerly et al., 2008a; 
McNamara et al., 2004). Peer agents are designed to be learning partners 
to encourage peer-to-peer interactions. Although this is not a 
tutor-to-student approach, the agents are frequently presented as more 
knowledgeable and high-performing peers who lead the human students 
along a learning path (e.g., KSC-PaL). KSC-PaL is a peer collaborative 
agent in computing science education. It interacts with a student in 
one-on-one 
problem-solving 
verbally, 
graphically, 
and 
in 
a 
process-oriented form (Howard et al., 2017). Its goal is to track student 
collaborative behavior and try to steer them in the direction of more 
productive behavior. The peer agent encourages students to examine the 
contributions more thoroughly, but it does not leave them to struggle for 
a long time when they are confused.

Another possible use of conversational agents in education can be 
assessing student learning. Recently, researchers have studied the po­

findings supported using CBA to measure student knowledge and skills 
in a conversational environment (e.g., Jackson & Zapata-Rivera, 2015; 
Lopez et al., 2021). In fact, instruction-integrated conversational agents

Conversational agents can be integrated into assessments by creating 
a natural conversation between students and computer agents where 
students can take an assessment with a computer agent through natural 
language conversations (Lehman & Jackson, 2018). They create an 
interactive environment where conversational agents mimic the peda­

combine assessment and feedback to improve student motivation and 
learning while measuring their knowledge and providing timely feed­
back. This back-and-forth dialogue within a CBA is intended to be a 
turn-taking conversation where the conversational agent asks a ques­
tion, and the student answers (e.g., Lopez et al., 2021). 
3.1. Potential item formats in conversation-based assessments 
CBA combines the measurement and communication of various 
interacting skills (e.g., cognition, communication, and emotion) in a 
standardized setting (Jackson & Zapata-Rivera, 2015). CBA can enable 
students to demonstrate their knowledge, skills, and abilities, scaffold 
their learning, provide them with relevant feedback, and increase their 
motivation. CBA can consist of selected-response (e.g., multiple-choice 
and true-false) and constructed-response (e.g., open-ended and short 
answer) items and can be used to deliver feedback to students or 
teachers. Thus, CBA has the potential to expand the item formats in 
traditional digital assessments. 
3.1.1. Selected-response items in CBA 
CBA can ask questions and provide feedback in a turn-taking format 
with selected-response items (e.g., Lopez et al., 2021). Thus, it combines 
assessment and feedback to provide real-time assessment and feedback 
to students, resulting in an assessment that helps students understand 
what they know and need to study more. In CBA with selected-response 
items, the student’s response to a question is sorted into correct or 
incorrect response paths, feedback is presented to the student, and then 
the next question is administered. If the student’s response is correct, 
then the correct conversation path is utilized. If, however, the student’s 
response is incorrect, then the incorrect conversation path is adopted 
(see Fig. 1). 
3.1.2. Constructed-response items in CBA 
CBA can necessitate the construction of responses to questions, and 
the use of natural-language processing to deliver adaptive follow-up 
prompts that target specific information (Jackson et al., 2018). 
Constructed-response items are commonly used in educational assess­
ment to elicit student responses that reflect underlying knowledge or 
skills, as students must recall knowledge and compose their responses 
(Jackson et al., 2018). On the other hand, students can incorporate 
off-topic or missing information in their answers. Thus, according to 
previous research, students should be given a second chance to answer 
questions to promote learning (Attali & Powers, 2008). Through the 
flow of a human-like, natural conversation, CBA can be designed to hold 
social interactions, ask questions, provide feedback on the quality of 
responses, give hints to elicit additional or missing information, and 
allow a second attempt for an initial incorrect response with 
constructed-response items (e.g., Jackson & Zapata-Rivera, 2015). 
Like a conventional constructed-response item, CBA starts the con­
versation with a question (see Fig. 2). Traditional digital assessments 
would end after students respond to each question. Within a CBA, 
however, the student responses to that question are examined and sorted 
into one of several subdivisions to guide the students by feedback, hint, 
or follow-up question: (1) correct conversation path, where a student’s 
initial response is correct, (2) partial correct conversation path, where a 
student’s initial response is not correct, but the final response is correct 
(3) incorrect conversation path, where both initial and final responses 
are incorrect, or (4) out of scope response path, where CBA is unable to 
determine whether their response is correct or incorrect (see Fig. 2). The 
out-of-scope response occurs when a student says something irrelevant 
(i.e., unrelated to the question) or gives unexpected on-topic answers 
that cannot be classified.

The Volcano Scenario is a conversational system including one tutor 
and one peer agent (Liu et al., 2013). The system explains the content 
related to volcanic eruptions and then shows constructed-response 
items. In their study, Jackson et al. (2018) investigated the benefits of 
the Volcano Scenario as a potential CBA design and found that compared 
with constructed-response items, CBA items allowed 41% of students to 
submit a more thorough response and enhance their scores.

4. Challenges of conversational agents in learning and 
assessment 
Even though the examples of conversational agents for both learning 
and assessment seem promising, conversational agents are still a chal­
lenging work in progress. Previous research reported some challenges in 
the design and implementation of conversational agents, although sig­
nificant progress has been made in their design to recognize student 
inputs accurately and provide relevant and accurate responses (e.g., 
Zapata-Rivera et al., 2015). 
4.1. Irrelevant responses 
Conversational agents may be unable to manage the majority of 
student inputs (e.g., questions and answers) and deliver relevant and 
correct responses since student inputs are often unpredictable, making it 
difficult to train these conversational agents with all possible scenarios

designed to give positive feedback for a more complete answer (i.e., if a 
student’s answer is partially correct, the agent still provides negative or 
neutral feedback for their partial answer, e.g., AutoTutor) (Graesser, 
2016). Students may receive inappropriate or irrelevant follow-up

misclassify the student’s response (Lopez et al., 2021; Zapata-Rivera

correctly answer a small percentage of student inputs, and when 
breakdowns (e.g., unresponsiveness to what the student says) occur, 
students may become frustrated (Bailey et al., 2021; D’Mello & Graesser,

Even though conversational agents show high performance in 
interpreting student input if they are designed with selected responses 
(e.g., multiple-choice) or short responses (e.g., typing either yes or no), 
they do not have high performance with open-ended responses (Huang 
et al., 2019; Val´erio et al., 2018). Researchers suggest simple commu­
nication options to minimize miscommunications, such as simple short 
responses or button-delivered response options (e.g., Val´erio et al., 
2018). 
4.2. The amount of the interaction 
Students rated the conversational agents as unsatisfactory and un­
helpful since they may spend more time on concepts that the student 
already understands and less time on concepts that they struggle with 
(Katz et al., 2021). Furthermore, students may become frustrated if they 
believe the conversational agent forces them to engage in lengthy talks 
(e.g., Jordan et al., 2018; Kopp et al., 2012). In this regard, researchers 
compared two versions of a conversational agent: control and experi­
mental (Kopp et al., 2012). The control version engaged students in 
dialogues about six conceptual questions. In contrast, the experimental 
version engaged students in dialogues about three conceptual questions 
and presented three additional questions. In addition, the experimental 
version provided a predetermined short response and explanation. A 
similar study by Jordan et al. (2016) compared two versions of a 
conversational agent: (1) the control version that always decomposes a 
step to its simplest sub-steps regardless of the student’s knowledge level 
and (2) the experimental version that adaptively decides to decompose a 
step based on student knowledge. The results from these two studies 
showed that students in the experimental conditions learned as much as 
students in the control conditions in less time, implying that a large 
amount of interaction is not always required. 
In their study, Ruan et al. (2019) found practicing with the conver­
sational agent more time-consuming compared to the flashcard app 
(Ruan et al., 2019). Students, however, stated that the conversational 
agent was more beneficial for learning and, despite the fact that it was 
more time-demanding, chose to spend more time with the agent when 
given the option. Ruan et al. (2019) argued that conversational agents 
are more engaging but less efficient in terms of time spent. However, 
students can still prefer these systems as they enhance motivation and 
learning, and feedback helps enhance the testing effect regardless of 
whether the attempted answers are correct. 
5. Benefits of conversational agents in learning and assessment 
The literature review on conversational agents has shown an evo­
lution from didactic machines to interactive social systems influencing 
student motivation and learning outcomes. They facilitate learning and 
motivation and open new possibilities for increasing engagement, 
feedback, and personalization levels in the learning process (Maedche 
et al., 2019). Previous research has highlighted the benefits of conver­
sational agents using student-oriented learning (Graesser, 2016). They 
can provide a constructivist environment to students by simulating 
dialogue moves between students and human tutors (or learning com­
panions) who guide students in constructing knowledge by asking

5.1. Learning 
Conversational agents have several benefits, including the ability to 
provide assistance at any time and in any location and personalize stu­
dent learning by performing tasks while conversing. Conversational 
agents can create an ideal environment for interactive information ex­
change and thus show student knowledge (Graesser et al., 2008). They 
can improve learning by increasing engagement, feedback, and 
personalization. They help students learn by holding a conversation in 
natural language and adapting it to students’ responses (Graesser, 
2016). Conversational agents can provide individualized support to each 
student and build on their strengths, interests, and abilities to enhance 
engaged and independent learning (Kerly et al., 2008b). 
A comparison of a tutor agent and a human tutor showed that the 
student’s average learning gains were virtually equivalent on the same 
topic (VanLehn et al., 2007). Researchers compared a tutor agent with a 
condition where the course textbook was assigned to read for an 
equivalent amount of time and with a condition where no reading ma­
terial was assigned to read (Graesser et al., 2003). According to the re­
sults, the tutor agent produced significantly better learning than the two 
comparison conditions. 
Another study showed that students interacting with a peer collab­
orative agent could improve their knowledge, although there was no 
difference in learning gains when comparing two agent versions that do 
and do not track and attempt to change the student collaborative 
behavior (Howard et al., 2017). Heffernan (2003) found a strong posi­
tive impact of conversational agents on learning and reported that stu­
dents who used a conversational agent solved fewer problems but 
learned as well as or better than students who were given the solution. 
Jackson et al. (2018) found that students improved their responses and 
scores in CBA compared with conventional constructed-response items. 
Similarly, Ruan et al. (2019) observed that students gave more correct 
responses to questions in QuizBot compared to the flashcard app. 
5.2. Motivation 
Conversational agents can help student motivation in several ways, 
including increasing engagement, boosting socialization, and making 
the learning environment more interactive and dynamic (e.g., Divekar 
et al., 2021). Researchers investigated the emotional states of students 
when interacting with conversational agents. Among different emotion 
states, engagement was the most frequent state (D’Mello & Graesser, 
2013), and a significant relationship was found between learning and 
engagement (Craig et al., 2004). Another study revealed that conver­
sation effectively kept students motivated and positively impacted their 
motivation level (Heffernan, 2003). 
In another study, students found conversation-based environments 
as an engaging and easy way to practice and learn English as a second 
language (Hong et al., 2014; Yang & Zapata-Rivera, 2010). Students 
who interacted with the conversational agent were shown to be more 
actively engaged in learning activities and outperformed those who did 
not (Hong et al., 2014). Moreover, students expressed an interest in 
using conversational agents in their other subjects. So et al. (2015) 
revealed that trialogue-based activities engaged students, and students 
had a favorable impression of these tasks. According to Forsyth et al.’s 
(2019) study, learning through CBA was well-received by the students. 
6. A new perspective on conversation-based assessments 
Computerized formative assessments are used for various purposes, 
such as measuring and monitoring student learning, screening learning 
problems, and tailoring instructional strategies based on students’ 
learning needs (January et al., 2018; Tolar et al., 2014). Thus, accurate 
estimates of student performance are essential to ensure the validity of 
inferences and decisions being made about students. However, as it has 
been discussed in the literature, some students may not be motivated to 
perform their best due to the lack of personal consequences in formative 
assessments, and test scores obtained from students with low test-taking 
effort may not reflect their actual levels in the construct being measured 
(e.g., Ekl¨of, 2006, 2010; Haladyna & Downing, 2004). Due to the 
low-stakes nature of formative assessments, it is difficult to develop 
engaging and motivating assessments, and thus, the betterment of dig­
ital assessments (e.g., CBT and CAT) is necessary and inevitable. To deal 
with the motivation problem in formative assessment given its 
low-stakes nature, conversational agents can be used to measure student 
learning in an interactive way of assessment to increase student moti­
vation towards the assessment process, resulting in more effortful 
test-taking behavior and valid test results representing their true ability 
levels. 
Using conversational agents to measure student learning through 
CBA is a relatively new approach that has shown promise in a variety of 
educational settings (e.g., Lopez et al., 2021). Previous studies have 
argued that CBA can be used for formative assessment purposes and 
promote students’ motivation and learning (e.g., Forsyth et al., 2019). 
Using CBA as a novel approach to motivate students to increase their 
test-taking effort in formative assessment can be a proactive solution to 
the validity issues. By engaging students in a conversation about their 
learning during formative assessments, students can receive feedback 
and support to help them understand the material better and increase 
their motivation to do well. This can lead to increased test-taking effort 
and ultimately result in more accurate and valid test scores. 
CBA can become a part of classroom assessments and even large- 
scale formative assessments at the national and international levels to 
enhance students’ test-taking effort in low-stakes assessments. They can 
be integrated into classroom assessments to monitor student learning 
and provide human-like personalized guidance to each student based on 
their performance. For example, in classroom assessment, previous 
research reports that teachers can ask one-on-one questions to assess 
student learning as a highly interactive approach (Chi et al., 2008). With 
this approach, teachers can review student responses and ask further 
questions to better understand their knowledge. The human-to-human 
interaction between the student and the teacher can improve student 
engagement in the learning and assessment environment (Graesser 
et al., 2008). It can create an ideal assessment environment and provide 
significant insight into student knowledge; however, this approach takes 
more time and effort, making it neither practical nor financially feasible 
to utilize with large student groups. CBA can address this issue in the 
classroom and build interactive opportunities for students by simulating 
human teachers and increasing motivation through sustained 
human-like communication. 
CBA can also be utilized in large-scale formative assessments to 
motivate students to participate in such assessments and yield more 
accurate results. For example, the Programme for International Student 
Assessment (PISA) is a large-scale assessment in education to measure 
student achievement to inform policymakers. The results from the PISA 
assessment are expected to provide guidelines to shape educational re­
forms. However, because the assessment does not have any personal 
consequence for students, a potential lack of motivation may threaten 
the validity. The interactive environment of CBA can support such as­
sessments to enhance student test-taking motivation, resulting in more 
effortful test-taking behavior and more accurate representations of their 
ability levels to inform policymakers. The possible uses of CBA can be 
extended as they have the potential to be an integral component of 
education and assessment in the future and make significant improve­
ments to the test-taking effort in low-stakes assessments.

7. Discussion 
The computer science and technology sectors continue to proliferate 
and produce cutting-edge applications that can enhance the quality of 
education. In recent years, novel technologies (e.g., conversational 
agents) involving artificial intelligence and natural language processing 
have enabled computers to process linguistic input more accurately and 
create human-like communication to interact with learners. To date, 
conversational agents in education have been mainly used for instruc­
tional purposes through virtual forms of teaching assistants (e.g., Goel & 
Polepeddi, 2016), tutors (e.g., Graesser et al., 1999), and learning 
companions (e.g., Howard et al., 2017) as well as assessment purposes 
(e.g., Ruan et al., 2019). 
Despite the challenges that come with the design and implementa­
tion of conversational agents, such as irrelevant responses and interac­
tion amount (e.g., Katz et al., 2021; Zapata-Rivera et al., 2018), these 
challenges can be addressed with careful consideration and planning 
because the benefits of these systems outweigh the difficulties. 
Conversational agents provide personalized, interactive, and engaging 
learning experiences that can increase student motivation (e.g., Forsyth 
et al., 2019), facilitate knowledge construction and improve learning 
outcomes (e.g., Jackson et al., 2018). They can provide assistance at any 
time and at any location, providing learners with flexibility in their 
learning process. Moreover, conversational agents can provide imme­
diate and individualized feedback, which enhances the testing effect to 
address any misconceptions or gaps in knowledge (e.g., Ruan et al., 
2019). Therefore, the benefits of conversational agents in learning and 
assessment cannot be overlooked, and the development of these systems 
must continue with a focus on addressing the identified challenges to 
ensure that they can be used effectively in educational contexts. By 
addressing the identified challenges, the potential benefits of conver­
sational agents can be fully realized. 
CBA has the potential to create selected-response or constructed- 
response items and measure how students recall, explain, and commu­
nicate their knowledge in an interactive way of assessment (e.g., Lopez 
et al., 2021). Open responses to constructed-response items allow stu­
dents to express their understanding in their own words, and CBA ex­
pands this opportunity with follow-up questions and provides more 
information about the student’s knowledge (e.g., Jackson et al., 2018). 
CBA combines the benefits of constructed-response items with adaptive 
conversations (e.g., hints and follow-up questions) to explore and pro­
vide thorough information about students. Within a CBA, students can 
answer selected-response items in a turn-taking way wherein assessment 
and feedback are combined for formative assessment purposes to pro­
vide real-time assessment and feedback to students, resulting in an 
assessment that motivates students and helps them understand what 
they know or need to study more. With the benefit of motivation in CBA, 
conversational agents can become even more effective and be utilized 
for different purposes in an assessment context. 
The use of conversational agents has the potential to be a powerful 
tool for improving student motivation and test-taking effort in formative 
assessments. CBA can enhance student motivation (e.g., Lopez et al., 
2021), and high motivation can prevent the occurrence of disengage­
ment while taking low-stakes assessments, resulting in higher and valid 
performances. Students who interact with conversational agents in CBA 
are likely to be more engaged with the material and show more effortful 
test-taking behavior. Even though the previous research has discussed 
the impact of conversational agents on student motivation (e.g., Howard 
et al., 2017) and the use of CBA as a formative assessment tool (e.g., 
Forsyth et al., 2019), they have not addressed CBA as a potential solu­
tion to the lack of motivation in formative assessments. This paper has 
addressed this and discussed a new perspective of conversational agents 
in assessment (i.e., CBA) in the context of test-taking behavior (i.e., 
effortful and non-effortful). Our discussion is consistent with the pre­
vious research on using CBA as a formative assessment. However, it 
extends this by proposing the use of CBA as a solution to the lack of 
motivation in formative assessments, given their low-stakes nature. 
8. Practical implications and future research 
Conversational agents have the potential to greatly enhance forma­
tive assessment, which is an assessment for learning. CBA can be inte­
grated into classroom assessments and can be used in conjunction with 
existing classroom assessments to monitor student progress and provide 
personalized guidance based on individual performance. This can pro­
vide teachers with additional evidence to identify areas where students 
may require further support. Therefore, the use of conversational agents 
in the classroom has the potential to greatly enhance formative assess­
ment, benefiting both students and teachers alike. 
CBA has also the potential to address the issue of test-taking effort in 
large-scale formative assessments, which are used to measure student 
achievement and inform educational reforms. Given their low-stakes 
nature, students may lack engagement with such assessments, which 
may impact the reliability and validity of scores. CBA can enhance 
student test-taking effort, resulting in more accurate representations of 
their ability levels. Future research is needed to explore the use of CBA in 
the context of large-scale assessments. 
Providing students with the opportunity in CBA to be interactive 
during a test administration can enhance their motivation towards the 
assessment process, resulting in more effortful test-taking behavior and 
reliable and valid test results. Future research should investigate the 
impact of CBA on test-taking engagement and the reliability and validity 
of test scores obtained from CBA. For example, a future study could 
compare the test-taking engagement levels of students who take a 
computerized assessment versus those who take a CBA. The study could 
also compare the reliability and validity of the scores obtained from both 
assessment formats. The results of such a study could provide insight 
into whether CBA is a more effective assessment tool than traditional 
methods and whether it has a positive impact on student motivation and 
engagement in low-stakes assessments. 
Similar to reliability and validity, as with any assessment tool, it is 
important that CBA is fair and free from bias against any group of stu­
dents. There are several issues that can lead to unfair assessments and 
scores including content, format, and scoring. For example, in terms of 
the assessment format, even though the technology skills required to 
take CBA may not be considered more demanding than any other digital 
assessments, some student subgroups can likely be advantaged over 
others. On the other hand, non-effortful test-taking behavior occurs at 
different proportions across student subgroups, leading to additional 
inequities among them (e.g., Soland, 2018a; 2018b). However, the 
interactive assessment environment of CBA has the potential to enhance 
motivation and increase test-taking engagement, addressing one po­
tential source of fairness. Future research should investigate and address 
any potential sources of bias in the use of CBA. 
9. Conclusion 
With advances in artificial intelligence, conversational agents have 
begun to play an increasingly important role in education. A conversa­
tional agent can provide a learning or assessment environment that 
shifts the K-12 or higher education system to a proactive way of learning 
and assessment. Today’s conversational agents are primarily designed to 
improve learning and motivation by supporting learning and teaching 
activities through the tutor, teaching assistant, and peer agents. 
Recently, these agents have been designed and used for assessment 
purposes as they are a natural fit for assessment and be administered to 
motivate students to take assessments by holding human-like conver­
sations with a conversational agent. The evidence mentioned above 
from the literature on the benefits of conversational agents shows that 
conversational agents can be a potential motivation source through 
sustained conversation.
