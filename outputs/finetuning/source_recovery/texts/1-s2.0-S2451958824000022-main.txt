A B S T R A C T  
This study aimed at exploring the pattern of computer usage among early adolescents in Italy, about one year 
before the spread of the COVID-19 pandemic. The study also aimed at developing and testing models on the 
associations between socio-demographic characteristics, computer-user profiles, and mathematics achievement. 
A three-step estimation of Latent Class Analysis models and auxiliary variable models was applied to data 
collected on a sample of 1155 students attending grade 8 in Italy in the school year 2018–19. Four sub­
populations were uncovered, which differ both with regards to their activities with computers (desktop, laptop, 
and tablet computers) and the likelihood of being daily users. Students’ socioeconomic and cultural background 
and gender predicted their pattern of computer usage, which in turn predicted digitally assessed mathematics. 
The main results suggested that excessive and almost exclusive use of computers for entertainment and 
communication purposes may depict an “at-risk" profile in young adolescents, with possible negative conse­
quences for their learning achievements. This profile was more likely to be found among students from more 
disadvantageous backgrounds and males, at least before the COVID-19 pandemic started.   
1. Introduction 
Over the last decades, Information and Communication Technology 
(ICT) has been playing a pivotal role in young people’s lives and society 
at large, changing the ways many people live, work, and learn (OECD, 
2020). This process of change also involves the landscape of education, 
and an increasing interest has been emerging in the potentialities offered 
by integrating ICT into learning and teaching processes (OECD, 2020; 
Schleicher, 2020; UNESCO, 2017), as well as in assessment practice (e. 
g., Boitshwarelo, Reedy, & Billany, 2017; Redecker & Johannessen, 
2013). Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the use of digital tools in edu­
cation has dramatically increased. School closures were one of the first 
non-pharmaceutical restrictive measures adopted by many governments 
as a response to the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic. In a very short 
time, students and teachers were forced to quickly adapt to distance 
learning, stopping to share the same physical environment. All of a 
sudden and for several weeks, the ICT became a lifeline for supporting 
education and training continuity (Schleicher, 2020). In this context, the 
already existing digital inequalities became even more critical (OECD, 
2020; Goudeau et al., 2021), thus highlighting the importance of having 
a more nuanced picture of ICT use among adolescents with different 
backgrounds. A more nuanced picture of ICT uses among adolescents is 
also of relevance to advance our understanding of the relationships 
between ICT use and educational achievement, which is far to be 
established. By adopting a person-centered approach, the present work 
explores heterogeneity in computer usage outside of school among early 
adolescents in Italy, about one year before the spread of the COVID-19 
pandemic. The work aims at contributing to the streams of literature 
on digital inequalities and the associations between computer use and 
academic achievements by investigating the relationships between 
eventual latent “typologies” of computer users, students’ gender and 
socio-economic status (SES), and digitally assessed mathematics. 
1.1. Digital youth and digital inequalities 
Nowadays, children and teenagers are often said to have grown up 
with ICT as an integral part of their everyday lives, surrounded by and 
using tools of the digital age, which they navigate with confidence, al­
ways seeking opportunities to be connected. This way of conceptualizing 
the relationship between young people and ICT arises from the concept 
of “digital natives” (Prensky, 2001). However, over the last decades, 
empirical research has been challenging the “myth of digital native” (for

a review, see Eynon, 2020), suggesting the need for a more complex and 
fine-grained picture of technology access, use, and skills in young peo­
ple. As suggested by Eynon (2020), this is of relevance to avoid 
neglecting the realities of digital inequalities within the digital native 
generation. The vision of the “digital native” as a homogenous genera­
tion could, indeed, obscure the presence of eventual inequalities and 
asymmetry in the opportunities to take advantage of ICTs possibilities. 
The first-level digital divide, the gap between those who have access 
to ICT and those who do not, has been the focus of early research on 
digital inequalities (for a review, see van Dijk, 2020). The global crisis 
due to the COVID-19 pandemic shed renewed light on the persisting 
first-level digital gaps across and within countries. UNESCO (2020) re­
ported that half of the total number of learners kept out of the classroom 
in the first phase of the COVID-19 pandemic did not have any access to a 
household computer, and 43% had no internet at home, with large 
discrepancies between countries. Consistently, the report “Learning 
remotely when schools close: How well are students and schools prepared? 
Insights from PISA” (OECD, 2020), showed that the percentage of 
15-year-old students having access to a computer for schoolwork and the 
Internet varied between countries in 2018. The gap also varied within 
countries, as a function of students’ SES. Accordingly, in their recent 
review Goudeau, Sanrey, Stanczak, Manstead, and Darnon (2021) re­
ported that social class is a strong predictor of digital disparities in 
France, the United Kingdom, and the United States (US). For instance, in 
April 2020, 59% of parents with lower income in the US indicated three 
barriers to distance learning: a lack of reliable internet at home, no 
computer at home, or the need to use a smartphone to complete 
schoolwork (Vogels, 2021). Therefore, although the first-level digital 
divide is shrinking (OECD, 2020), it does not seem to be disappeared and 
how it combines with other sources of inequalities in education needs 
further investigation. This is of relevance not only during the pandemic, 
when not having access to a computer and the internet can imply to be at 
least in part excluded from the stop-and-go solutions offered by ICT to 
the crisis (Goudeau et al., 2021) but even beyond it, to catch up the 
eventual opportunities offered by ICT. 
The extensive use of digital devices during the COVID-19 pandemic 
also leads to a renewed interest in the pre-existing inequalities in ICT 
patterns of usage among students. In ‘normal’ times, when schools are 
open, students can actively engage with digital tools outside of school 
for learning and school-related goals, such as searching the Internet for 
information, downloading class materials from school websites, 
completing homework assignments, or for self-motivated learning ac­
tivities. On the other hand, pupils can use ICT for several leisure pur­
poses, such as playing games, surfing the internet, communicating with 
friends and relatives, watching movies, etc. Several studies found dis­
crepancies in patterns of ICT usage along with students’ SES (e.g., OECD, 
2015; Gui, 2015; van de Werfhorst, Kessenich, & Geven, 2020; Goudeau 
et al., 2021). In the report “Students, Computers and Learning: Making the 
Connection” (OECD, 2015) based on PISA 2012 data, emerged that in 
countries where the social-economic gap in access to the internet is 
small, 15-years-old advantaged students were more likely to read the 
news and use the Internet to obtain practical information than their 
disadvantaged peers. Similar results were found by Gui (2015). In his 
research carried out on Grade 10 students in Northern Italy, the author 
found that a higher parental education level is associated with students’ 
larger breadth of use of ICT and more frequent use of the internet for 
information seeking and reading newspapers online. Moreover, they run 
a lower risk of Internet over-consumption. In their recent study, Xiao 
and Sun (2022) adopted a person-centered approach to detect ICT user 
profiles on a sample of US students from PISA 2018 data; their findings 
revealed that higher SES was associated with a higher probability of 
being in the subgroup of 15-years old students characterized by mod­
erate ICT use for school-related activities in and outside of schools and 
comparatively lower frequency of ICT use for entertainment, whereas 
low SES students were more likely to belong to the subgroup of 15-years 
old students characterized by excessive ICT use for entertainment at 
home. As for younger adolescents, a recent comparative study based on 
data from the 2018 International Computer and Information Literacy 
Study (ICILS), van de Werfhorst et al. (2020) found that eighth-grade 
students’ ICT usage for educational purposes outside of school is posi­
tively associated with their SES. Larger differences between disadvan­
taged and advantaged students emerged in some countries, including 
Italy. 
Beyond social disparities, some studies also point out the existence of 
gendered patterns in the use of ICT. Literature reports several possible 
causes of the gender-based digital divide, including females’ attitudes 
and confidence in their ICT capabilities, as well as inherent gender 
biases and sociocultural norms (for a review, see OECD, 2018). In their 
recent review, Tyers-Chowdhury and Binder (2021) observed that the 
gender digital divide community of knowledge is overwhelmingly 
focused on adulthood, whereas less is known about the digital realities 
for girls. Results from adults suggest that, compared to men, women 
have a lower frequency of use of ICT and a narrower range of online 
activities (for a review, see Robinson et al., 2015). Some studies sug­
gested a similar pattern of lower access and use for girls, as for women 
(for a review, see Tyers-Chowdhury & Binder, 2021), and that girls have 
less positive attitudes toward technology use compared with boys, as 
boys are more likely to believe in the benefits of technology use in so­
ciety (Cai, Fan, & Du, 2017). However, other pieces of evidence suggest 
that the difference between female and male students in terms of fre­
quency of use decreased over time (for a review, see Gebhardt, Thom­
son, Ainley, & Hillman, 2019). As for computer usage, according to 
ICILS 2013 on average girls made slightly greater use of computer 
technologies for study purposes than boys, while boys used ICT more 
frequently for exchanging information and for recreational purposes 
(Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Friedman, & Gebhardt, 2014). However, dif­
ferences emerged across countries (Gebhardt et al., 2019). Similar re­
sults emerged from PISA-2015 data (OECD, 2017). More recently, the 
ICILS 2018 international report (Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Friedman, & 
Duckworth, 2020) confirmed that female students are stronger users of 
ICT for general school-related tasks, although this difference varies 
across countries. For instance, in Italy, no significant difference emerged 
between males and females in ICT use for school-related activities. 
Further insights about the association between gender and ICT use in 
adolescents are provided by studies on ICT user profiles. For instance, 
Scherer, Rohatgi, and Hatlevik (2017) identified two profiles of ICT 
users in Northway using ICILS 2013 data. They reported that girls were 
more likely than boys to use ICT across different contexts and for 
different purposes (profile 2), whereas boys were more likely to show 
less consistent use of ICT such that the lowest frequencies were identi­
fied for leisure-related Internet activities. In their study on PISA2018 
data in the US, Xiao and Sun (2022) reported that boys are more likely to 
be members of the subgroup of students who use ICT heavily for 
entertainment. In contrast, Kastorff, Sailer, and Stegmann’s (2023) 
findings on data collected in Germany in 2019 suggested that gender is 
not a significant predictor of adolescents’ ICT user profiles. Therefore, 
much uncertainty still exists about the relationship between gender and 
ICT use in adolescents, plausible due to the changes in the realm of 
digital inequalities across time and countries. 
1.2. Discrepancies in the use of digital devices and mathematics 
achievement 
Although an increasing number of studies suggests that digital in­
equalities can emerge even among children and adolescents (Eynon, 
2020; OECD, 2015), there is no theoretical or empirical consensus on 
whether disparities in access to and use of computers and the internet 
from home are related to educational outcomes in ‘normal’ times, when 
schools are open. 
On the one hand, computer technology and the internet are deemed 
to have the potential to support learners’ knowledge discovery and 
construction (Salomon, 1998), foster students’ engagement and active

learning (O’Connor, 2003; Bond & Bedenlier, 2019), and offer powerful 
tools to share thinking and knowledge construction, accordingly with 
the view of learning as a social process (Vygotsky, 1978). Engaging with 
digital tools can provide learners new opportunities to interact with 
society and the environment, and to explore the world by themselves 
gaining access to information, that through the internet is retrievable 
anywhere and anytime. When engaging with computers and the 
internet, students can potentially promote their ICT knowledge and 
skills (learning by doing, Dewey, 1938), and reinforce the cognitive 
processes involved also in subject-based achievements. In particular, the 
use of digital tools can support cognitive processes and skills that are 
highly relevant in the scientific and mathematical content area, such as 
problem-solving strategies (e.g., Wittwer & Senkbeil, 2008), visuospa­
tial skills (Subrahmanyam, Greenfield, Kraut, & Gross, 2001) and 
higher-order thinking skills (Papanastasiou, Zembylas, & Vrasidas, 
2003). Ecological theories of educational technology (e.g., Chiu, 2020; 
Johnson, 2010; Johnson & Puplampu, 2008) also highlight the potential 
positive effects of ICT on learning outcomes, which are assumed to be 
affected by students’ characteristics and environmental influences. 
On the other hand, scholars have also highlighted the potential 
negative effects of ICT on learning and school achievements. For 
instance, distraction theory argues that computers and the internet offer 
a multitude of activities and information that might call for the attention 
of the computer user during their self-oriented learning activities or 
their schoolwork, or even in the classroom (Junco & Cotten, 2012; Ros´en 
& Gustafsson, 2016; May & Elder, 2018). Therefore, students might 
engage in multitasking activities in parallel with learning activities; in 
turn, task-switching may overload students’ capacity for cognitive pro­
cessing, especially attention processes, and hence preclude deep and 
meaningful learning. A possible negative effect of computer use on 
students’ achievements is also predicted by the displacement hypothesis 
(Huston et al., 1992; Subrahmanyam et al., 2001), which states that if 
electronic media takes the place of “developmentally meritorious” ac­
tivities, this may have negative effects on social development and 
learning. Other scholars suggest that the effect of access to ICT varies as 
a function of specific uses and activities performed (e.g., Meggiolaro, 
2018). For instance, excessive use of ICT for leisure may lead students to 
neglect their studies, being a waste of time plausible harmful to stu­
dents’ attention, and can even lead to addiction, thus having negative 
effects on students’ achievements (e.g., Gentile, Lynch, Linder, & Walsh, 
2004; Junco & Cotten, 2012; Luu & Freeman, 2011). 
Empirical evidence on the association between access to and/or time 
spent on digital devices out of school and mathematics achievement is 
mixed, with some studies founding a negative association (e.g., Ayieko, 
Gokbel, & Nelson, 2018; Hu, Gong, Lai, & Leung, 2018) and others 
founding opposite results (Zhang et al., 2016). Contrasting results also 
emerged among studies investigating the direction and the strength of 
association between ICT types of usage (e.g., for leisure or learning) and 
educational outcomes (e.g., Agasisti, Gil-Izquierdo, & Han, 2020; Biagi 
& Loi, 2013; Gumus & Atalmis, 2011; Hu et al., 2018; Ponzo, 2011; 
Skryabin, Zhang, Liu, & Zhang, 2015; Zhang & Liu, 2016). Therefore, 
the interplay between individual differences in ICT uses and learning 
outcomes warrants more research. 
Noteworthy, most of the studies have used a traditional variable- 
centered approach to evaluate the relative importance of one (or 
more) variables about ICT usage (e.g., ICT for leisure vs ICT for learning 
purposes) in explaining variability in educational outcomes, controlling 
for covariates (e.g., sociodemographic variables). Recently, some 
scholars have advocated the adoption of a person-centered approach, 
such as Latent Class Analysis (LCA), to advance research on ICT use in 
adolescence (e.g., Kastorff, Sailer, & Stegmann, 2023; Scherer et al., 
2017; Xiao & Sun, 2022). Whereas variable-centered approaches focus 
on examining relations among variables in a certain population, 
person-centered approaches aim at identifying sub-groups of people 
based on their similarities on a set of variables. Therefore, 
person-centered approaches seem to be well suited to explore the 
heterogeneity in computer use among adolescents, by detecting sub­
groups with different patterns of computer usage (computer user pro­
files). Further, recent advances in person-centered approaches (e.g., 
McLarnon & O’Neill, 2018) could inform about associations between 
traditional sources of inequalities in education, computer user profiles, 
and achievements. To date, relatively few studies have adopted a 
person-centered approach, and most of them have explored the associ­
ation between adolescents’ patterns of computer use and their computer 
and information literacy (Bundsgaard & Gerick, 2017; Scherer et al., 
2017), whereas few studies have explored the association with mathe­
matics achievement (e.g., Wittwer & Senkbeil, 2008; Xiao & Sun, 2022). 
The most recent studies (e.g., Karakoyun & Bas¸aran, 2022; Xiao & Sun, 
2022) on the association between ICT use and mathematics achieve­
ments are based on the OECD-PISA data, therefore on students who are 
15 years old, thus requiring an investigation in other ages and grades. 
1.3. Overview of the study and research questions 
In response to the need for a more nuanced understanding of stu­
dents’ actual usage of digital technologies, the current study adopts a 
person-centered approach, LCA, to explore the patterns of computer 
usage outside of school among early adolescents in Italy, just a few 
months before the spread of the COVID-19 pandemic. Our general hy­
pothesis is that actual computer use outside of school was not homo­
geneous among early adolescents in Italy before the COVID-19 
pandemic started. Therefore, we expected to identify unobserved “sub­
groups” or “typologies” of grade 8 students characterized by different 
patterns of computer usage outside of school. 
The study also aims at investigating the associations between stu­
dents’ sociodemographic characteristics, the eventual “typologies” of 
computer users, and digitally assessed mathematics by integrating latent 
classes into auxiliary models. Extensive research has provided evidence 
about inequalities in educational achievements based on the students’ 
SES in Italy (e.g., National Institute for the Evaluation of the Education 
and Training System, INVALSI, 2019; Volante, Schnepf, Jerrim, & 
Klinger, 2019). Furthermore, according to international large-scale 
assessment data, Italy is one of the countries in the OECD still display­
ing a relatively large gender gap in mathematics (Contini, Di Tommaso, 
& Mendolia, 2017). For instance, in PISA 2018 (OECD, 2021) boys 
outperformed girls with a statistically significant difference of 16 points 
(OECD average: 5 points higher for boys). Less is known about the 
interplay between gender, SES, the use of digital technologies, and 
students’ mathematics achievements. In light of this research gap, we 
explored how latent subgroup membership related to sociodemographic 
characteristics and performance on computer-based assessment of 
mathematics. 
This study will therefore pursue the following Research Questions 
(RQs).  
RQ1 Do different computer-user profiles exist among eighth-grade 
students based on the time they spent on computers (desktop/ 
laptop/tablet pc) and their activities with computers (desktop/ 
laptop/tablet pc) outside of school?  
RQ2 Does students’ gender and SES predict the likelihood to belong to 
a given computer user subgroup?  
RQ3 Does computer-user subgroup membership predict mathematics 
ability, beyond the (direct) paths from students’ gender and SES 
to mathematics?

2.1. Participants and procedure 
Participants were 1155 students (49.5% female and 50.3% male; 
gender missing data = 2; 12.6 % of students with an immigrant back­
ground) attending grade 8 in Italy (North-West = 19.3%; North-East =

18.5%; Center = 30.2%; South = 31.9%; n of classes = 72). All students 
participated in the 2019 Field Trial (FT) of the INVALSI National 
Assessment Study, carried out at the end of the school year. Mathematics 
tests were administered to all students of a representative sample of 
classes (two randomly selected classes within each randomly selected 
school). An external examiner monitored students during the test 
administration. External monitoring is thought to improve data reli­
ability by reducing biases due to cheating phenomena. Students were 
assessed at school, in the classrooms or computer labs with an external 
observer. Five computer-based test forms were randomly assigned to the 
students, along with a short questionnaire. All mathematics tests were 
administered online, as well as the additional questionnaire. All data 
were collected and kept anonymously. 
2.2. Measures 
Mathematics ability. Five test-forms composed of 41 items each were 
assembled from a large item pool for mathematics assessment for grade 
8, developed by INVALSI. The construct under investigation is described 
in the INVALSI Theoretical Framework (INVALSI, 2018), which is 
aligned with the Italian national guidelines for the curriculum and takes 
into account the main international research in mathematics education. 
The test blueprint was the same one used for the Main Study of the 
INVALSI National Assessment. Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient 
ranged between 0.896 and 0.904. The items underwent qualitative 
evaluation by experts and iterative psychometric analyses based on the 
Rasch model of measurement, then they were calibrated onto the same 
metric of the INVALSI mathematics item bank for the National Assess­
ment program. This item bank refers to a unidimensional set of items 
calibrated on the same scale that define and “quantify" a common 
construct and therefore can be conceptualized as the operationalization 
of a single latent variable, consistent with the definition of Rasch item 
bank (e.g., Choppin, 1981; Wright & Bell, 1984; Wright & Stone, 1999). 
For the purposes of the current study, the software AcerConquest 
(Adams, Cloney, Osses, Schwantner, & Vista, 2023) was used to draw a 
set of five Plausible Values (PVs) for each student for the mathematics 
scale from a latent regression model. The latent regression included as 
regressors the background variables collected from the students’ ques­
tionnaire and considered for subsequent analyses in the present study. 
The Person Separation Reliability for the items administered in the 
current study was equal to 0.888, thus implying that the instrument was 
sensitive enough to distinguish between high and low performers, and 
the Item Separation Reliability was equal to 0.978, therefore fully 
satisfactory. 
Economic, social and cultural status (ESCS) index. A ESCS index 
analogous to that used in the INVALSI National Assessment was 
computed by combining into a single score the indicators of students’ 
home educational resources (HOMEPOS), also including students’ ac­
cess to a computer they can use for studying and their access to the 
internet, the highest level of education of the student’s parents (PARED), 
converted into years of schooling, and the highest level of the students’ 
parents’ International Socio-Economic Index of Occupational Status 
(HISEI). The ESCS is a measure of students’ SES. Further details about 
methodological aspects can be found in Campodifiori, Figura, Papini, 
and Ricci (2010). 
Computer uses. Students were asked how often they use a computer 
(desktop/laptop/tablet computers) outside of school for the following 
activities: “reading books or other reading material”; “doing home­
work”; “seeking information online for school-related purposes”; “free- 
time activities (e.g., playing games or listening to the music)”; 
“communicate (e.g., through social networks, chat, e-mail)”; “seeking 
information online for personal interest”. A 5-Point Likert-type scale was 
used (1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = occasionally, 4 = very frequently, 5 =
always). 
Frequency of computer usage. Students were asked: “How often do you 
use a computer (desktop/laptop/tablet computers)?” and a 4-point 
response scale was used, from rarely to every day. For the current 
study, the response was recoded into a dichotomous variable (0 = less 
frequently than daily; 1 = daily). 
Besides these variables, the following ones have been considered or 
computed from the background questionnaire: student gender (0 = fe­
male; 1 = male), immigrant background (0 = native; 1 = students born 
outside Italy and whose parents were also born in another country and 
students born in Italy but whose parents were born in another country), 
and the geographical location of the school (North-West, North-East, 
Center, South). 
2.3. Statistical analysis 
All statistical analyses were carried out using Mplus 8.3 (Muth´en and 
Muth´en, 2019). Three-step Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was applied to 
identify meaningful latent subgroups (i.e., Latent Classes, LCs) of early 
adolescents with similar patterns of computer usage and test auxiliary 
models on the associations between sociodemographic characteristics 
(auxiliary predictors), LC membership, and mathematics ability (distal 
outcome). Simulation studies have supported the three-step approach 
for including covariates in the mixture model building process (e.g., 
Nylund-Gibson & Masyn, 2016). For all models, we used a maximum 
likelihood estimation with standard errors that are robust to 
non-normality and the non-independence of observations (students 
clustered within classes). Missing data on the LCA indicators were 
limited to a small number of observations (n = 5) and were treated with 
full information maximum likelihood estimation approach. Therefore, 
the unconditional LC models were estimated on 1155 subjects. When 
testing the auxiliary models, cases with missing values on the exogenous 
variables (sociodemographic variables, n = 2) were treated with listwise 
deletion, and the auxiliary models were estimated on 1153 subjects. 
Analyses of the relationships between the LC variable and the auxiliary 
variables were carried out on multiple data sets (5), differing only for the 
Plausible Value (PV) of the mathematics ability estimate (the distal 
outcome). These PV data sets were analyzed by using the Mplus 8.3 
(Muth´en and Muth´en, 2019) implementation of Rubin (1987) method 
(Muth´en & Muth´en, 2019). In doing so, parameter estimates were 
averaged by the latent variable modeling program over the set of ana­
lyses, and standard errors were computed using the average of the 
standard errors over the set of analyses and the between analysis 
parameter estimate variation (Muth´en & Muth´en, 2019; Rubin, 1987; 
Schafer, 1997). 
2.3.1. Unconditional latent class analysis (RQ1) 
In the first step of our analyses, we used LCA to investigate the 
presence and nature of subpopulations of computer (desktop/laptop/ 
tablet) users within the sample of 8th grade students (RQ1). A major 
advantage of LCA over other classification methods (e.g., cluster anal­
ysis) is that classification uncertainty is an explicit part of the statistical 
model (Bauer, 2022). LCA was used to recover and describe distinct 
latent subgroups that share a meaningful and interpretable pattern of 
responses on the following set of mixed-metric indicators: the 6 items 
about computer use outside of school and the item about the frequency 
of computer use. The computer use items are on a 5-point Likert scale 
and were treated as continuous. The frequency of computer use item (1 
= daily; 0 = less than daily) was treated as a binary variable. Auxiliary 
variables were not directly included in the identification of the optimal 
LC solution, to avoid confounding the nature and meaning of LC variable 
(Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014; McLarnon & O’Neill, 2018). Uncondi­
tional models with incremental numbers of LCs (from 1 to 5 classes) 
were estimated to find the optimal number of classes. To protect against 
local solutions, the number of random starts was set to 10000. The 
optimal class solution was selected based on fit indices, parsimony, and 
interpretability. For substantive interpretation of the target solution, we 
examined the class-specific mean across the 6 items about computer use 
outside of school and the conditional probability that someone in each

2.3.2. Auxiliary models relating sociodemographic characteristics, latent 
class membership, and mathematics ability (RQ2 and RQ3) 
We applied a (manual) three-step VAM approach (Asparouhov & 
Muth´en, 2014; McLarnon & O’Neill, 2018) to relate LC membership to 
external variables of interest, namely students’ sociodemographic 
characteristics and mathematics ability (PVs), while accounting for 
classification uncertainty. After identifying the optimal unconditional 
LC model (Step 1, Section 2.3.1), each student was assigned to the most 
likely class (Step 2). We then specified two auxiliary models (M1 and 
M2) in which the most likely class variable was used as indicator of the 
LC variable, and the measurement error of the LC variable was fixed and 
specified to values that account for the classification uncertainty 
observed in the unconditional model. Once the measurement parame­
ters were fixed, the auxiliary models M1 and M2 were specified in 
conjunction with the optimal unconditional mixture model to address 
RQ2 and RQ3. In both models, we specified students’ ESCS and gender 
to predict their pattern of computer use (LC variable), which in turn is 
expected to predict students’ mathematics ability (distal outcome). 
Students’ immigrant background and schools geographic location were 
included as control variables in the auxiliary models. M2 (Fig. 1) also 
included the direct paths from sociodemographic characteristics to 
mathematics ability. 
Because of the nominal nature of the LC variable “computer user 
class”, sociodemographic predictors of LC membership (RQ2) were 
examined by including in the models a Multinomial Logistic Latent Class 
Regression (MLLCR) with the LC categorical variable as the dependent 
variable and sociodemographic variables as covariates. The MLLCR 
allowed us to test whether students’ gender and SES predict the likeli­
hood of belonging to a particular computer user subgroup (RQ2). The 
relationship between the LC categorical variable and the distal outcome 
was assessed by testing the mean differences in the math ability esti­
mates (PVs) across the different LCs. In M1, means were estimated as 
unconditional to other auxiliary variables. In M2, a linear regression of 
mathematics ability (PVs) on sociodemographic variables was added to 
the M1 model, so that the effect of the latent class variable on the distal 
outcome was estimated as conditional upon the influence of socio­
demographic characteristics (RQ3). Before estimating mean differences 
across LCs, an overall test of equivalence of the regression of 
mathematics ability on sociodemographic characteristics across LCs was 
performed. 
3. Results 
3.1. Identifying and describing computer user profiles: unconditional LCA 
results (RQ1) 
Descriptive statistics and zero-order correlations among the observed 
indicators are summarized in Table 1 in Appendix. For all LC solutions, 
the estimation revealed no error messages, and the output provided the 
information that the highest log-likelihood value was replicated. The 
following relative fit indices (Information Criterion, IC) were evaluated 
to determine the best solution: the Akaike IC (AIC), the Bayesian IC 
(BIC), and the sample-size-adjusted BIC (aBIC). The ICs are reported in 
Table 1 and on an Elbow plot (Fig. 2). 
Vuong-Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT) and Lo- 
Mendell-Rubin adjusted LRT, which quantify specific comparisons be­
tween a given model and a model with one fewer class, were also per­
formed; the corresponding p-values are reported in Table 1. 
The aBIC reached its minimum value at the 4-class solution, then 
increased again. Therefore, it pointed at the 4-class solution as the best 
one. The other ICs decreased as the number of classes increased, even 
though a plateau was observed from the 4-class solution (Fig. 2). The 
LRTs indicated that adding a latent class to the 1-class, 2-class, and 3- 
class solutions significantly improved the model fit to the data. The 5- 
class solution did not explain the data better than the 4-class solution. 
Therefore, statistical indices indicated that the 4-class solution was the 
optimal solution. In the 4-class solution, the diagonal probabilities in the 
posterior probability matrix ranged from 0.82 to 0.94, which was 
acceptable (Weller, Bowen, & Faubert, 2020). The entropy, which 
measures how accurately the model defines classes, was above 0.80 
(Table 1), indicating “good” classification of individual cases into classes 
(Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018). 
The interpretability of the proposed optimal solution was also 
assessed in the selection of the retained model. The class-specific mean 
of the 6 items on computer use outside of school (Fig. 3) and the con­
ditional probability that someone in each LC uses a computer daily 
(Fig. 4) were examined for interpretative purposes. The 4-class solution 
indicated a clear substantive distiction between the proposed sub­
populations. Although one LC emerged with a low prevalence (LC1: 
4.80% and n = 56), this small class made conceptual sense as it 
described students who used a computer less frequently than the other 
LCs (Figs. 3 and 4). For this reason, we decided to label this class the 
“sporadic users” class. Classes 2 (prevalence = 55.05%) and 4 (preva­
lence = 13.79 %) described the subpopulations of students most likely to 
use a computer daily (Fig. 4). LC2 comprised students whose computer 
use was spread across multiple purposes and activities. Therefore, we 
labeled this class as “multipurpose users”. Otherwise, LC4’s computer 
use was primarily for free-time activities and communication, with less 
frequent use for activities such as doing homework and seeking infor­
mation for school-related activities. We labeled LC4 as “recreational 
users”. LC3 (prevalence = 26.36%) describes students who use a com­
puter outside of school mainly to find information for school-related 
activities and to do their homework. They rarely use a computer 
outside of school for communication, and less often than classes 2 and 4 
for free-time activities. We labeled this class as “educational users”.

4. Discussion 
A person-centered approach has recently been advocated as a 
potentially rewarding perspective from which to look at heterogeneity 
in ICT usage among adolescents (e.g., Karakoyun & Bas¸aran, 2022; 
Kastorff et al., 2023; Lai et al., 2022; Scherer et al., 2017). Findings from 
the current study are promising in this respect. Results from latent class 
analyses (LCA) on data collected in Italy just one year before the 
outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic are consistent with the hypothesis 
that grade 8 students were not homogeneous in their computer (desk­
top/laptop/tablet computers) use outside of school. The profiles that 
emerged among Italian adolescents from our LCA resemble, at least in 
part, those obtained by previous studies. The largest subgroup of stu­
dents (prevalence = 55.05%) were “multipurpose users”, who reported 
using a computer outside of school quite often and across multiple ac­
tivities, including leisure and communication and, to a slightly lesser 
extent, school-related activities and information seeking. Their profile is 
similar to that of the largest subgroup of adolescents recovered through 
Latent Profile Analysis by Kastorff et al. (2023) in their pre-COVID-19 
pandemic sample in Germany and, less recently, by Scherer et al. 
(2017) work on Norwegian ninth-grade students (profile 2). Similar 
profiles also emerged in earlier works on heterogeneity in computer 
usage in adolescents carried out by Wittwer and Senkbeil (2008, “smart 
users”) and Tømte and Hatlevik (2011, “Digi-zappers”). Besides this 
profile, others emerged indicating different ways of using a computer 
outside of school. A “recreational users” class was detected, whose 
characteristics are similar to those of the “Active user of ICT for enter­
tainment” profile recently individuated by Xiao and Sun (2022) in the 
US. This subgroup of students uses a computer out of school very often, 
but mainly for free-time activities (e.g., playing games, listening to 
music) and to stay connected with others (e.g., by social networks, chat, 
e-mail). The “educational users” subgroup describes students using a 
computer outside of school less often and mainly for searching infor­
mation for school-related activities and doing their homework, like the 
“Moderate user of ICT” (Xiao & Sun, 2022), and the largest subgroup 
(Profile 1) described by Scherer et al. (2017). Similar profiles were 
described by Wittwer and Senkbeil (2008) and Tømte and Hatlevik 
(2011). Finally, a small subgroup of “sporadic users” emerged, like the 
“analogous students” described by Tømte and Hatlevik (2011). 
Besides describing heterogeneity in grade 8 students’ uses of com­
puters outside of school, the present research aimed at investigating the 
relations between profiles of computer users, sociodemographic char­
acteristics (RQ2), and mathematics achievement (RQ3). An extension of 
Auxiliary Variable Approaches (McLarnon and O’Neill, 2018) was 
applied to the data to test the direct paths from sociodemographic var­
iables to the latent class variable and the distal outcome (i.e. mathe­
matics ability estimate), and from the latent class variable to the distal 
outcome, within an integrated mixture model. Two sets of results are 
worth emphasizing. First, students’ gender and ESCS predict students’ 
class membership. Second, the pattern of computer usage predicts

Within the framework of digital inequalities among adolescents, the 
findings from the present study indicated that students from more 
disadvantaged backgrounds were more likely to belong to the two 
subgroups of students (namely, “recreational users” and “sporadic 
users”) who more rarely reaped the benefits of computers in learning 
activities such as doing homework and searching for information outside 
of school. Our results corroborate the findings of a great deal of the 
previous work in showing a lower use of ICT for educational purposes 
among those with lower ESCS (e.g., van de Werfhorst et al., 2020; 
Goudeau et al., 2021). Noteworthy, only for a small subgroup of stu­
dents, the “sporadic users” class, the low use of computers for learning 
activities corresponds to a low use of computers in general. It may be 
that these adolescents had limited access to computers (deskto­
p/laptop/tablet PC) and/or did not benefit from an affordable, reliable 
internet service. By contrast, in the “recreational users” class the low use 
of computers for learning activities is associated with the tendency to 
use computers outside of school very frequently but mostly for enter­
tainment and communication. It is encouraging to compare this figure 
with that found by Xiao and Sun (2022) who found that students with 
more disadvantaged backgrounds tended to excessively use ICT for 
entertainment, but insufficiently use ICT for learning. 
As far as the gender-digital divide was concerned, the main finding is 
that boys were more likely than girls to be in the “recreational users” 
subgroup with respect to each of the other subgroups. This result is 
consistent with recent findings of Xiao and Sun (2022) who reported 
that more boys were found in “Active users of ICT for entertainment 
profile” than the “Moderate user of ICT”. The findings of the current 
study do not support the previous research showing that boys are more 
likely than girls to be computer users who often exploit the manifold 
possibilities digital technologies are offering across different activities 
(CERI & OECD, 2010; Tømte & Hatlevik, 2011). Gender was not a sig­
nificant predictor of latent class membership, except for the “recrea­
tional users” one. Therefore, little gender differences emerged among 
most of the user profiles, consistent with recent findings reported by 
Kastorff et al. (2023) in Germany. Extant research suggested that the 
difference between female and male students in terms of frequency of 
use decreased over time (Gebhardt et al., 2019); future research may 
explore if an analogous trend also emerges in the association between 
gender and computer user profiles. 
Turning to results on the association between computers users’ ty­
pologies and digitally assessed mathematics, findings from the present 
study suggested that, at least in young adolescents, a moderate and 
“rational” (Wittwer & Senkbeil, 2008) use of a computer out of school, 
mostly oriented to learning activities, is associated with the best per­
formance in digitally assessed mathematics. Although we cannot rule 
out that the “multipurpose users" benefitted from their frequent use of a 
computer out of school on other outcomes (e.g., ICT skills; ICT 
self-efficacy), they performed worse in digitally assessed mathematics 
than those using a computer less frequently and mainly for learning. A 
possible explanation for our findings is that when the frequency and 
variety of computer usage are both high, the likelihood of engaging in 
multitasking activities also increases. Consistently with the distraction 
theory, this might have negative consequences for students’ attention 
processes during their self-oriented learning activities or their school­
work (Junco & Cotten, 2012; Ros´en & Gustafsson, 2016; May & Elder, 
2018). Our result echoes recent findings from Gubbels, Swart, and Groen 
(2020), who showed that moderate use of ICT outside the school for 
schoolwork is associated with the highest digitally assessed reading 
performance. 
Another key finding from our analysis is that other things being 
equal, the “recreational users” class perform worse than other classes in 
digitally assessed mathematics. This result is in agreement with those 
obtained by Xiao and Sun (2022). An excessive- and almost 
exclusive-use of ICT for leisure may depict an “at-risk” profile in young 
adolescents, with possible negative consequences for their achieve­
ments. There are several possible explanations for this result. On the one 
hand, “recreational users” are less likely to exploit the potential benefit 
of digital technologies in supporting their knowledge discovery and 
construction (Salomon, 1998) and to explore the world by themselves 
gaining access to information. On the other hand, their excessive use of 
ICT for leisure may lead them to neglect their studies (Luu & Freeman, 
2011) taking place of more educationally meritorious activities, as 
suggested by the displacement hypothesis (Huston et al., 1992; Sub­
rahmanyam et al., 2001). Relatively lower parent involvement in stu­
dents’ learning activities outside of school may also explain this result. 
Another finding is that other things being equal also “sporadic users” 
perform better than “recreational users”. Otherwise, they perform worse 
than “educational users”. On the one hand, this result encourages a 
moderate use of digital resources for learning purposes, on the other 
hand, it further highlights the possible risks of using ICT excessively for 
entertainment and communication purposes. 
5. Limitations 
The short questionnaire of the current research explicitly addresses 
students’ use of devices like desktop PCs, laptops, and tablets. However, 
it does not consider smartphones, which are emerging as common de­
vice for adolescents. We cannot rule out that some students reporting a 
given user profile in desktop PC, laptop, and/or tablet usage, have 
different patterns of smartphone usage. Therefore, it is problematic to 
generalize the findings to the use of smartphones. However, recent 
research adopting a person-centered perspective on smartphone usage 
individuates typologies of smartphone users that are consistent with 
those from our study on other devices. For instance, Yoon and Yun 
(2023) distinguished between a learning group, a recreational group, 
and a minimal-use group, and reported that the learning group had a 
significantly higher score for the achievement goals than the minimal 
and recreational user groups. Future research can enrich the picture of 
ICT uses among adolescents, for instance by comparing smartphone user 
profiles with those of other digital devices. Secondly, we are keenly 
aware that cross-sectional designs are not suited to establish causal 
ordering, and therefore our interpretation of socioeconomic and cultural 
background and gender as antecedents of the categorical latent class and 
digitally assessed mathematics, as well as the categorical latent class as a 
predictor of digitally assessed mathematics is mainly based on theoret­
ical arguments. Further, it was not possible to control other variables 
that might have affected the respondents’ computer usage patterns, for 
instance, parents’ attitudes toward digital devices and parental rules for 
computer use. A further study could address the role of these variables, 
for instance by distinguishing parent profiles and including them in a 
comprehensive model of ICT user profiles, background variables, and 
educational achievements. Lastly, the current research only focused on 
students’ performance in a computer-based assessment of mathematics. 
Further research is needed to investigate the association between soci­
odemographic characteristics, computer usage patterns and other sub­
jects (e.g. science) among early adolescents. 
6. Conclusions 
Unequal use of digital tools is one of the critical aspects of digital 
divides in recent times (Goudeau et al., 2021). As the impact of digital 
technologies in teaching and learning deepens, it is also crucial to ensure 
that all pupils have an equal opportunity not only to have access to 
digital resources but also to exploit the opportunities offered from ICT to 
learning. The present research focused on 8-grade students’ computer 
uses in Italy in 2019, about one year before the spread of the COVID-19 
pandemic. 
The relevance of focusing on ICT user typologies is supported by the 
current findings. As suggested by Scherer et al. (2017), identifying dif­
ferences and similarities between students in the use of ICT can provide

parents and teachers with valuable information about their potential 
needs for support in exploiting the opportunities offered by ICT, for 
instance by motivating and scaffolding students in using digital re­
sources as a tool for supporting knowledge discovery and construction 
(Salomon, 1998) and active learning (Bond & Bedenlier, 2019; O’Con­
nor, 2003)), as well as to identify potential risks teachers and parents 
might want to prevent (Scherer et al., 2017), such as those due to 
extensive use of computers for leisure (Gentile et al., 2004; Junco & 
Cotten, 2012; Luu & Freeman, 2011). 
Further, results of auxiliary models from the current research pro­
vided novel data corroborating the idea that, in recent times and among 
digital natives, the pathway from students socioeconomic and cultural 
background to school achievements may be channeled at least in part by 
students’ pattern of computer uses (Goudeau et al., 2021), with students 
from more disadvantaged background being more likely to belong to 
more “at risk” subpopulations. 
The present research also provides novel insights into the association 
between computer use and adolescent gender. Males were more at risk 
of using excessively and almost exclusively computers for leisure and 
communication. However, no evidence emerges of girls’ lower use of 
technology. The latter result is encouraging, especially considering that, 
as indicated in the report made by Plan International and Bocconi 
University (Plan International, 2021), recent data on the digital gap 
between men and women in the European Union Member States depict a 
troubling picture for Italy. For instance, based on the European Union 
Women in Digital (WiD) score in 2019, Italy is ranked 25th out of 28 
Member States in terms of digital gender parity. A similar picture 
emerged from the 2020 WiD Scoreboard (European Commission, 2020). 
Women in Italy are among the least likely to take part in the digital 
economy, with a difference favoring men in the indices of Internet use, 
Internet user skills, and specialist skills and employment. Turning to 
early adolescents, this picture seems to be not confirmed, thus sug­
gesting further investigation of male and female adolescents’ uses of 
digital technology. 
A natural progression of this work is to study individuals’ transition 
between latent classes over time, for instance before, during, and after 
the COVID-19 pandemic. A further study could assess the long-term 
associations between the pattern of computer usage before the COVID- 
19 pandemic started, and educational outcomes during the pandemic. 
As suggested by Goudeau et al. (2021), unequal uses of digital tools for 
learning purposes before the COVID-19 crisis could contribute to the 
accentuation of academic inequalities during the lockdown, when stu­
dents and parents have been forced to quickly adapt to a new educa­
tional context, from face-to-face to distance learning.
