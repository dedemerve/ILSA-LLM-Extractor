A B S T R A C T
International large-scale assessments such as TIMSS administer achievement tests that are based on an analysis of
national curricula to compare student achievement across countries. The organizations that coordinate these
studies use Rasch or more generalized item response theory (IRT) models in which all test items are assumed to
measure a single latent ability. The test responses are then used to estimate this ability, and the ability scores are
used to compare countries.
A central but yet-to-be-contested assumption of this approach is that the achievement tests measure an un-
observed unidimensional continuous variable that is comparable across countries. One threat to this assumption
is the fact that countries and even regions or school tracks within countries have different curricula. When
seeking to fairly compare countries, it seems legitimate to account for the fact that applicable curricula differ and
that some students may not have been taught the full test content yet. When seeking to fairly compare countries,
it seems imperative to account for the fact that national curricula differ and that some countries may not have
taught the full test content yet. Nevertheless, existing IRT-based rankings ignore such differences.
The present study proposes a direct method to deal with differing curricula and create a fair ranking of
educational quality between countries. The new method compares countries solely on test content that has
already been taught; it uses information on whether students have mastered skills taught in class or not and does
not consider contents that have not been taught yet. Mastery is assessed via the deterministic-input, noisy, “and”
gate (DINA) model, an interpretable and tractable cognitive diagnostic model. To illustrate the new method, we
use data from TIMSS 1995 and compare it to the IRT-based scores published in the international study report. We
find a mismatch between the TIMSS test contents and national curricula in all countries. At the same time, we
observe a high correlation between the scores based on the new method and the conventional IRT scores. This
finding underscores the robustness of the performance measures reported in TIMSS despite existing differences
across national curricula.
1. Introduction
The main objective of international large-scale assessments is to
compare student performance across countries. Their goal is to construct
an international test that reflects the educational goals of the partici-
pating countries. Currently, there are two dominant strategies to achieve
this goal. The first approach is to conduct an analysis of national
curricula and the second is to appoint a panel of experts to develop the
test. The normative validity of the latter approach has been criticized
because the experts may be selected to reflect a particular ideology
rather than with a view to capturing the sector’s full social and cultural
diversity at an international level (e.g., Grek, 2009; Meyer & Benavot,
2013).
The key challenge for the test developers is to identify content areas
and corresponding test items that are considered important in all
countries. For example, if certain subareas of mathematics—such as
common fractions or 3-D geometry—have not been taught to students in
all countries, it is problematic to include items that relate to this content
subarea when estimating their math skills. International assessment
rankings might be biased, as the overlap between the tested content and
taught content may differ between programs (Leinhardt & Seewald,
1981). It seems plausible that performance on a test in a given country

1.1. Threats to the construct validity of international assessments based
on national curricula
The development of an achievement test for an international
assessment which is based on actual curricula begins with a compre-
hensive analysis of the curricula of various countries. The goal of a
curriculum analysis is to identify the content areas that precisely define
the construct of interest for a specific assessment at a specific point in
time, such as mathematics at the end of primary school. Subsequently,
test items are developed to measure the corresponding content areas.
The term curriculum analysis does not refer exclusively to the analyses
of formal school curricula—it can also include other data sources such as
semi-official documents, textbooks, or even interviews with experts,
teachers, and students (e.g., Ariav, 1986; Robitaille et al., 1993;
Torney-Purta et al., 1999; Tyler, 2013).
Yet, regardless of how exactly a curriculum analysis is conducted,
there are still major threats to the validity of such international assess-
ments, namely construct underrepresentation and construct-irrelevant
variance (e.g., American Educational Research Association, American
Psychological Association, and National Council on Measurement in
Education, 2014; Messick, 1989). In the context of international
large-scale assessments (ILSAs), we define construct underrepresenta-
tion and construct-irrelevant variance in terms of curriculum mismatch.
Construct underrepresentation refers to the absence of certain content

Against this backdrop, our aim is to develop a scoring that minimizes
construct underrepresentation and construct-irrelevant variance for all
countries. In an international study including a large number of coun-

curricula, it is difficult to strike a balance between the two. A focus on
optimizing construct underrepresentation typically makes the issue of
construct-irrelevant variance worse, since both concepts are interrelated
in international assessments. Minimizing construct underrepresentation
requires broad test materials, which may lead to construct irrelevant
variance.
1.2. International differences in content coverage: the example of TIMSS
1995
Correspondence between national curricula and TIMSS assessment is
examined using a procedure referred to as test-curriculum matching
analysis (TCMA). In this process, National Research Coordinators from
each country are asked to appoint experts familiar with the national
curricula of the tested grades to determine if the TIMSS items match
their country’s curriculum (Mullis et al., 2020). The most comprehen-
sive curriculum analysis to date was conducted as part of the Third In-
ternational Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) in 1995. TIMSS
1995 tested students in five different grades in primary, secondary, and
upper secondary school in mathematics and science. In the area of
mathematics, a total of 245 curriculum guides and 318 textbooks from
48 countries were studied. Content analyses were used to map which
topics were covered in which grade in each country (e.g., common
fractions, 3-D geometry).
A similar study was conducted in the area of science. Yet, learning
content described in formal documents may not necessarily reflect
exactly what is covered by teachers in class. For this reason, a teacher
survey was conducted to examine the within-country variation in the
coverage of learning across classrooms (Howson, 1998; Schmidt et al.,
2001,1997). The key outcomes of the curriculum analyses were
so-called topic trace maps, which show whether and when different
topics were covered in school for each country. These maps were used to
construct the TIMSS assessment. For example, the TIMSS eighth grade
assessment tested 26 topics in mathematics and 48 in science (Schmidt
et al., 2001).
Although the TIMSS tests were constructed based on a curriculum
analysis, not all topics were reflected in all national curricula. In fact, the
curriculum analyses in TIMSS 1995 revealed considerable variation in
the coverage of the 26 mathematics topics across the 36 participating
countries (Schmidt et al., 2001). Contents that are not covered in

and for science varied for individual countries, but the general finding of
construct-irrelevant variance in the international tests also applied here.
The observed variation in the coverage of various content in national
curricula raises questions about how to deal with this variation when
scoring the achievement data. It should be noted that TCMA has some
limitations. First, not all countries have a national curriculum (e.g.,
federal systems) or they do not exactly specify what must be covered in a
certain grade. Instead, they only list the curricular goals for a range of
levels (Kelly et al., 2020). Furthermore, national curricula describe the
intended curriculum. The implemented curriculum, that is, whether
teachers cover all the contents of their national curricula or others, is not
the same. Finally, it must also be emphasized that the judgments of
national experts themselves can be faulty or unreliable. TCMA is con-
ducted at the country level and considers only the intended curricula.
The approach we introduce in this study focuses on the classes and
teachers’ actual practices.
1.3. Developing a curriculum-sensitive measure of performance
Since the mid-1970s, Rasch models and more general item response
theory (IRT) models have been used to score test data from international
assessments (Wendt et al., 2011). Although the models differ, what they
share is that they use all of the test-takers responses to estimate per-
formance—i.e., regardless of whether the content has been taught or
not. A central assumption of the models, generally used in international
assessments, is unidimensionality – there is exactly one latent variable in
the model, interpreted as the test-taker’s general ability. In practice,
modeling a unidimensional IRT model is justified either if the tested
ability is truly unidimensional or if the tested abilities are highly
correlated (essentially unidimensional). For example, the assumption of
a general mathematical ability is often justified even though it includes
various content areas. However, if some content areas have not been
taught in class the correlation between the skill of solving questions
measuring this content area and the overall ability decreases. For
example, if a student has never learned about measurement units, he or
she is unlikely to answer a unit conversion question correctly, even
though he or she has high math ability. Therefore, it seems questionable
if measuring a unidimensional ability is fair to all students.
If a test claims to be curriculum-valid, then it seems unfair to include
topics that are not taught to the students. To create a performance
measurement that is curriculum-sensitive, differences in covered con-
tent should be taken into account. We suggest only evaluating each
student on those contents covered in their received school education. To

1.4. Foundations of CDM
CDM allows researchers to analyze students’ strengths and weak-
nesses in a multidimensional test. The model is based on the assumption
that the probability of a test-taker correctly responding to a test item is a
function of whether the test-taker possesses one or more skills required
to solve the items. In the absence of the required skill or skill set, the
probability of providing a correct answer will be low. The aim is to es-
timate a binary skill vector for each student, indicating the presence or
absence of specific skills (De La Torre, 2009). The term skill is in this
model class very broadly defined and can reflect process strategies and
knowledge about topics or attributes (Dogan & Tatsuoka, 2008). The
skills are part of the model specification and determined in a so-called
Q-matrix, henceforth termed Q. The Q-matrix consists of dichotomous
entries that indicate the skills required to solve each item. Each row of Q
corresponds to an item and each column to a skill. Therefore, if skill k is
required to correctly answer item j, the element on the j-th row and k-th
column is equal to 1; otherwise, it is 0. Often, Q is developed through a
careful review of the test contents, for example, by having curriculum
experts determine what content is measured by each test item. Alter-
natively, Q is designed based on statistical considerations (Chiu et al.,
2009), and then items are written to match Q. As international assess-
ments typically use conventional IRT models and therefore estimate a
single ability, these models do not routinely consider a Q-matrix.
In recent years, several CDM specifications have been developed to
model test item responses. In the present study, we will use the DINA
approach, which assumes that solving an item requires all skills specified
in the Q-matrix (De La Torre, 2009). The relationship between (unob-
served) skills and (observed) test-taking behavior is stochastic: In the
DINA model, a latent response towards an item is defined as the product
of all required skills from the skill vector. The latent response will be 1 if
the student mastered all skills required for the item and 0 otherwise. By
random influences, the latent response and observed binary response
will often differ. The model captures random influences with slipping and
guessing parameters. The guessing parameter is defined as the probability
of giving a correct response without having the necessary skills. Anal-
ogously, the slipping parameter is the probability of giving the wrong
answer despite possessing the required skills. In this context, guessing is
not defined as randomly choosing an answer but as responding without
all the required skills. The model assumes the conditional independence
of the responses given the skill vector and randomly sampled test-takers.
After estimating guessing and slipping parameters for each item using the
expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm (De La Torre, 2009) skill
patterns can be estimated using the MAP (Maximum a Posteriori)
method (Huebner & Wang, 2011). As the response behavior might differ
between groups, a multi-group extension (MG-DINA) is useful. Rather
than having to fit separate DINA models for each group, researchers can
use MG-DINA-based skill estimates to compare between groups without
bias. The marginal skill distribution differs between groups, while the
probabilities of guessing and slipping for each item are the same in all
groups, i.e., they are invariant (Johnson et al., 2013). This invariance
assumption means that items are free of differential item functioning
(DIF). Especially, the changes of sovling an item conditional on the
mastery or non-mastery of the required skills does not differ between
groups only the skill distribution does. Note that the marginal latent skill
pattern distribution is very flexible (2K −1 degrees of freedom). Hence,
the MG-DINA model potentially captures nuanced country differences
while providing measurement invariance. Several fit statistics can be
used to evaluate the fit of MG-DINA models to data. The popular MX2
test is defined as the mean of all pairwise Pearson’s χ2 statistics. It ex-
amines the average difference between the model-implied and observed
response frequencies (Lei & Li, 2016). A common problem with testing
fit is that, with a large sample size, the power to detect even minor
misfits at the global level is extremely high, so virtually all models would
be rejected (Von Davier & Lee, 2019, p. 145). Absolute fit indices should
therefore be taken into consideration. Q-matrix selection in the DINA
model could be based on the agreement among information criteria
(AIC, BIC), the negative of the log-likelihood (−2LL), MADcor, and
MADres (Lei & Li, 2016). The statistic MADcor is defined as the average
of absolute deviations between observed and predicted pairwise item
correlations (DiBello et al., 2006). MADres is defined as the average of
absolute values of pairwise item covariance residuals (McDonald & Mok,
1995).

ering the content that was taught to students when estimating student
achievement. We assume that there are differences in the topics cover-
ed—not only between countries but also between classrooms within
countries because teachers may skip a topic that is in the national cur-

Note that the classroom size is neglected. The number of topics

teaching performance more fairly and hence create a more meaningful
ranking of education systems—the Tc values are placed in increasing
order as smaller values speak for a better education system.
2. Empirical example using TIMSS 1995 data
We will next use real data to illustrate how to consider construct-
irrelevant variance in an international assessment that aims to mea-
sure the curricula of various countries. For this purpose, we will use
mathematics data from TIMSS 1995 (Beaton et al., 1996, p. 12). For the
evaluation, the statistical software R (R Core Team, 2019) was used, in
particular the package CDM developed by Robitzsch et al. (2019) for
estimating the DINA model. For details, we refer to George and
Robitzsch (2015). Alternatively, the package GDINA from Ma and De La
Torre (2020) can be used for implementation. The R code for this
analysis is available on OSF https://osf.io/7mruf. For the sake of brev-
ity, we will use three-letter ISO codes for the included countries, see the
footnote of Table 3.
2.1. Data
We use the TIMSS 1995 data because the achievement test was
developed based on a comprehensive analysis of national curricula and
an extensive teacher survey was additionally conducted to collect in-
formation on the topics covered in class. In addition for newer TIMSS
cycles, the skills measured by each item could not be provided which
prevents the specification of the Q-matrix.
2.1.1. Sample
The target populations were 13-year-old secondary school students
— seventh, eighth, and ninth graders in most countries. In total 45
educational systems participated in TIMSS 1995 with secondary school
samples. However, in some of them, the participation rate of teachers
was low and we chose countries with at least 60 % valid information on
the classroom level for inclusion in the analysis, resulting in 19 relevant
countries, with a total of 142,077 students. After excluding students
with missing teacher data for the DINA model the effective sample size
reduced to 56,525 students.
2.1.2. Math assessment
The mathematics test covered more than 150 test items. TIMSS used
a multi-matrix design to divide the test items into eight test booklets
with 33 to 43 items per booklet. Each item appeared in multiple booklets
and the booklets were administered at random to the students. The test
contained some partial credit items that enabled students to earn more
than one point for a correct response. In the present study, we coded
such items as correct if the student received the maximum number of
points. If a student did not answer an item in the booklet, it was coded as
a wrong answer. Items that were missing due to the booklet design were
coded as missing and are therefore just not considered in a person’s
ability estimate.
To meet the model assumption of conditional independence between
responses for MG-DINA as strictly as possible, we excluded students who
did not answer more than 20 questions. This seems reasonable since
students who could not finish the test in time produced a series of
dependent all wrong answers otherwise and were also likely to have
other distortions in their answering patterns. However, only 230 stu-
dents are excluded due to this exclusion criteriam, which is 0,09 % of the
full sample. For the remaining students, we argue that the test did not
create time pressure and a failure to answer questions means that test-
takers were unable to do so. Furthermore, the fact that the test was
conducted via booklets and that the items at the end of the test differed
means that missing values occurred at random.
2.1.3. Content coverage
Data from the teacher survey was used to determine whether stu-
dents have been exposed to the various topics covered in the TIMSS test.
For this purpose, math teacher were asked if they have covered 32 math
topics such as number theory, geometric congruence, percentages,
linear equations, or slope and trigonometry. The response options were
“have taught this year”, “will teach later this year”, “not taught this
year”, and “taught a previous year”.2 We considered a topic to be taught
if it has been covered in previous years or the present school year; we
considered a topic not covered if the teacher planned to cover it later in
the current school year or future school years. As some skills included
different topics than those represented in the teacher questionnaire, we
aggregated their answers. If the teacher had answered at least one of
those aggregated topics, we used this information. If there was more
information about one topic, we considered the aggregated topic taught

2.2. Methods
To achieve the best possible fit between the test content and the
topics included in the teacher’s questionnaire, the Q-matrix was devel-
oped based on theoretical, practical, and empirical considerations. In-
formation on what topic each item measured was kindly provided by
Eugenio Gonzalez, who was the lead international analysis manager at
the international study center of TIMSS at Boston College in 1995.
First, we grouped topics that are conceptually related (for example,
the two topics “meaning of whole numbers; place value and numera-
tion” and “operations with and properties of whole numbers” were
combined to to “whole numbers”). Second, there were sometimes too
few items in the test for certain topics and or a topic was only measures
in few booklets; in such cases, we combined certain neighboring topic
areas. Third, and least significantly, we considered different configura-
tions at the end, with this empirical criterion being secondary. While the
choice of aggregated abilities was based on mainly on theoretical and
practical reasoning, we also used data from Germany to estimate
different slightly different candidate Q-matrices. The model fit resulting
from multiple slightly differing Q-matrices was evaluated based on
MADcor, MADres, MX2, AIC, and −2LL. The best-fitting matrix was then
used for further analysis with the pooled international data.
Table 2 shows the chosen eleven skills and the topic(s) related to
each skill. Altogether, we excluded 24 items from further analysis
because the underlying skills were not well represented in the TIMSS
tests. After excluding them, the total number of items is 126, which is
more than ten items per skill on average. This ensured that all abilities
could be measured for each student.
The theoretical considerations for the development of the Q-matrix
were supported by the good empirical model fit (MADcor = 0.0413;
MADres = 0.0087) for the MG-DINA that included all 19 countries of
interest. A detailed Q-matrix of this kind shows the advantage of
aggregating the given information: A total of 211 = 2, 048 different skill
profiles can result, which can easily make the analysis of the skill profiles
confusing. The statistic Tc (1) displays all information relevant to the
research question while taking all the detailed information resulting
from the DINA model into account.

2.4. Results
The ranking resulting from statistic Tc is displayed in Table 5.
Countries with low Tc values performed better than those with a higher
test statistic, as a comparison of the proportion of not-learned topics
with the taught topics shows. It should be noted that although the range
of realised values is very large, they usually only differ slightly between
countries with neighbouring ranks. This is particularly striking in the
middle ranks.
The standard deviation of Tcclass, within a country, is likewise inter-
esting, since it shows the heterogeneity of teaching quality within one
country. A high standard deviation, as evident in the USA, HKG, and
AUS, is an indicator of different teaching quality between classrooms,
which can result in unequal opportunities. Note that the standard de-
viation does not correlate significantly with the ranking. This means
that, when rating educational systems, the desirability of having high
educational quality levels in all classrooms and not only some very high-
performing ones should be accounted for. Therefore, a high correspon-
dence of topics taught between classrooms within a country is desirable.
Fig. 1 plots the values of the ranking resulting from Tc against those
from the average achievement based on the IRT scores from the public
use files. For the IRT scores, high values indicate high performance,
whereas for the Tc statistic, low values indicate high performance. Both
rankings are based on the same reduced sample of students where
teacher data was available (see data section). The most striking result
when comparing Tc and the average achievement is that the ranking
does not differ a lot. The plot quite vividly illustrates the high correlation
(r = −
. 95).
For the average achievement, the analysis used almost the full
sample (about 146 thousand student observations). Only 1327 students
were excluded in the international study report (Beaton et al., 1996, p.
200ff), and we excluded these for Tc, too. About 39 % of this information
was used for Tc, as classrooms with missing teacher information were
excluded. The overlap is impressive, not only because of the smaller
sample size but also because of additional selection effects. The fact that
this does not seem to have had a huge impact on the results suggests that
sampling within the countries was sufficient. This assumption is sup-
ported by the fact that the IRT-based ranking resulting from the full
sample and the reduced sample as used for Tc are highly correlated
(r = .99).
When looking at the rankings in more detail, note that eleven of the
19 countries scored the same ranking, six showed a difference of one
rank and only two showed a difference of two ranks. Extreme rankings
exhibited higher correspondence with each other than middle rankings.
There were blocks of countries in which the ranking differed. Since the
scores of the middle-ranked countries were close to each other in both Tc
and average achievement, it is questionable whether the differences
between them are significant. This assumption is also supported by the
official TIMSS report (Beaton et al., 1996), as it states that differences in
mean achievement from one country to the next are often negligible.
This makes it questionable whether the differences in the rankings are
due to the consideration of the curriculum or are random according to
the different statistics. As the descriptive investigation of classroom
curricula shows a mismatch, it seems reasonable to consider the
different curricula, even though changes in the ranking are rather small.
3. Discussion
International large-scale assessments in education are designed to
measure students’ performances across countries and compare the
effectiveness of educational systems. A major challenge in ILSAs is
curricular disparities, i.e., differences in the curricula of the various
participating countries. Students in some countries might be tested on
topics that have not been covered in their curriculum. This obviously
results in an unfair assessment with misleading outcomes. In this study,
we suggested a new scaling strategy that only takes the covered topics
into account and compares countries on the basis of the contents that are
covered in their curricula. The suggested statistic Tc is the average
proportion of taught materials that were not learned by the students.
The Tc statistic requires both students’ responses and teachers’ reports of
what they have taught in class. An empirical example using the eighth
grade mathematics assessment of TIMSS 1995 data showed that the Tc
statistic performs highly similar to the IRT based ranking which neglects
whether content has been covered or not. This high similarity could be
an indicator that accounting for the curriculum might not affect the
country ranking substantially in ILSAs. However, it is also plausible that
in our example, the match between the evaluated Q-matrix and the
teachers’ questionnaire was insufficient and therefore did not strongly
affect the ranking.
In adopting the Tc statistic for scaling, several challenges emerged.
The majority of tests, such as TIMSS, are designed to be scaled with item
response theory models. As a result, they are not always suited for
analysis with CDMs, which require knowledge of the Q-matrix. For a test
to be applicable for CDM analysis, the test must follow the structure of a
Q-matrix, necessitating the incorporation of multiple subdomains and a
sufficient amount of information for each subdomain. In addition, the
information indicating which subdomain is measured with each item
must be obtainable. This is the case if either all items are released and
experts can determine the subdomains measured by each item, or if the
test constructor provides this information directly.
Furthermore, teachers’ information is currently not utilized for the
country rankings, so obtaining valid teacher information was not the
main focus of the study. This resulted in a large amount of missing
teacher information. Since both student test data and teacher curriculum
data are needed when utilizing Tc, we had to use a reduced sample. This
could have led to selection effects and a biased ranking. One way of
circumventing this problem would be to conduct the study using the
intended curricula that are described by officials in formal documents.
Such intended curricula may be perceived as the official expectations
within a country articulated in formal documents. However, since the
topics taught vary a lot within countries, the actual value of such official
documents for teaching seems to be limited. Emphasizing the impor-
tance of teacher information in the evaluation of student performance
when conducting the study could increase response rates. In addition to
the high missingness of teacher information, the quality of this infor-
mation can be questioned. There was no clear match between the topics
in the teacher questionnaire and the topics measured by the test items.
Note that to obtain more reliable information, we would have required a
teacher questionnaire with unequivocal descriptions of the topics, clear
questions, and a perfect match between the measured skills. Ideally, the
Q-matrix would have been considered when conducting the test and
questionnaire. An investigation with a test conducted for evaluation
with a DINA model and a matched teacher questionnaire would have

generated more reliable results.
Since Tc depends on the accuracy of the taught topics, an ideal
dataset contains an objective and reliable topic measurement. Conse-
quently, a potential limitation is the quality of the teaching answers. In
the dataset, items were generally not solved more often when required
topics had been taught. The assumption that teaching leads to improved
performance is one of the most rudimentary ones in teaching and its
violation raises questions. Valuable information could be gained by
further investigating teacher-reported taught topics, for instance, by
comparing these to the official curriculum. A study of the correctness of
teachers’ reports could provide information about teachers’ response
behaviour and be valuable for further studies. Since the main issues
associated with the application of Tc are related to data quality, the
proposed statistic seems useful for taking the implemented curriculum
into account when ranking countries’ performances.
Besides limitations of the empirical example, general properties and
limitations of the proposed measurement should be discussed. When
calculating Tc, it is possible to control for construct-irrelevant variance.
If Tc is already considered when constructing the test, construct under-
representation could also be minimized. This is possible because the
trade-off between the constructs is not necessary, since students are only
assessed on topics that were relevant to them.
One could also remove items not taught before IRT scoring. The IRT
score could then be seen as representing achievement on taught subjects.
However, we caution that in general, a change in the item set changes all
item parameters and potentially shifts the construct.
A potential criticism of the statistic Tc is that it does not penalize a
low number of topics taught. However, whether or not there should be a
speed penalty is of course a normative question. Future research could
explore the effect of adding a penalty for few topics taught. The trade-off
between quantity (number of topics taught) and quality (students’
mastery of the topics taught) would have to be carefully chosen and
context dependent.
A nice advantage of measuring performance with a CDM is the ease
of interpretation of the test results. For each individual, the mastery or
non-mastery of the skills tested can be reported. This information can be
used to inform teachers about the strengths and weaknesses of their
students and which content could be further trained. In addition, this
detailed information could be used to create rankings based on each skill
measured. Such a more detailed comparison could help to deepen the
understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of education systems.
3.1. Practical recommendations
Summing up the limitations above, the following three crucial rec-
ommendations result for future implementations of the proposed Tc
methodology: (i) Identify skills based on a substantive construct model
and guide item writing so that at least part of the items is well suited for
a CDM analysis, ideally with an a priori Q-matrix following principles of
the design of experiments (Chiu et al., 2009). Avoid a less principled
empirical posthoc specification (Chen et al., 2015). (ii) Develop a
teacher-topic questionnaire parallel to the skills model guiding student
test development. Validate the questionnaire via mixed methodology
involving national or local curricula and domain experts. (iii) Ensure the
teacher questionnaire is obligatory and monitor teacher data quality in
the field.
4. Conclusion
The curriculum can have an important impact on performance, as
countries differ in their focus on important topics and it seems impos-
sible to conduct a test that suits all countries equally. The goal of this
study was to conduct a fair ranking method for school achievement tests
between countries by directly taking the different curricula into account.
This ranking is used to re-evaluate the country ranking of TIMSS 1995.
This ranking involves estimating students’ abilities via the MG-DINA
model and comparing whether they successfully acquired the skills
taught in class.
The resulting ranking and the official TIMSS ranking were contrasted
to investigate how accurately the original ranking represented the
quality of educational systems. Both rankings are highly consistent with
one another. The three best-rated countries—KOR, HKG, and BFL—and

Only two of the nineteen countries differed by more than one rank. Our
findings add further evidence to the validity of TIMSS for comparing
different participating countries. Similar to the results for the TCMA we
also found evidence that the TIMSS measure is robust to construct-
irrelevant variance, i.e. math contents that are not covered by teach-
ers in their classes. This is in line with previous research which shows
that curriculum differences has little impact on country rankings in
TIMSS (Bl¨omeke, 2024; Daus & Braeken, 2018; Hencke et al., 2009;
Luyten, 2017).
It should be kept in mind that the actual position is not necessarily
meaningful. Even though rank differences resulting from the use of the
two methods were rather small, a curriculum mismatch was evident.
Therefore, valuable information can be drawn from the proposed
ranking. It can offer information on how well the different curricula are
represented in the tests and enables comparisons of skills between
countries to reveal the strengths and weaknesses of various systems in
more detail.
This investigation has provided insight into the effects of curriculum
mismatch and also prepares the ground for future research to more
thoroughly investigate the matter. For instance, differences in curricular
adherence within countries could be compared. Comparing country-
wise curricula would lead to equal expectations within a country,
which might be a reasonable demand, as the goal is high educational
quality at all schools. The question of whether the MG-DINA model of-
fers the best representation of the data or if another CDM would be more
appropriate could also be discussed. Additionally, the impact of time
spent on teaching each topic could be evaluated. Students who have
been taught a topic for a longer period should be more confident in
solving items concerning those topics. A study that takes this into ac-
count would have the benefit of indirectly weighting the number of
taught topics as well.
