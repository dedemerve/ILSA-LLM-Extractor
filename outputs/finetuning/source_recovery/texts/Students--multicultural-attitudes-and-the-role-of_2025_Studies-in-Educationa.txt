Students’ multicultural attitudes and the role of background characteristics
Demos Michael 1 , Leonidas Kyriakides 2,*
Department of Education, University of Cyprus, Nicosia, Cyprus

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Students’ multicultural attitudes
Educational measurement and evaluation
Validation
Student background characteristics
Social desirability bias

In recent years, global efforts have intensified to foster inclusive school environments and promote positive
attitudes toward diversity. This study examines certain aspects of validity of an instrument designed to measure
students’ multicultural attitudes and investigates the influence of students’ background characteristics. Using
data from 450 secondary education students, Confirmatory Factor Analyses confirmed that the Munroe Multicultural Attitude Scale Questionnaire (MASQUE; Munroe & Pearson, 2006) consistently measures the underlying
constructs (i.e., cognitive, affective, and behavioral domains) within the examined context, as theoretically
proposed. Structural regressions revealed that immigrant background and language had no significant impact on
students’ multicultural attitudes, but gender did, with female students exhibiting higher scores. Social desirability bias was also detected in students’ responses related to the affective and behavioral, but not the cognitive
component of multicultural attitudes. The findings contribute to the research on multicultural instrumentation,
with implications for theory, research, and practice.

In recent years, mass migration has led to significant demographic
shifts, fostering unprecedented cultural diversity in many countries
(UNHCR, 2024). One notable consequence is the increasing multiculturalism of school populations. Projections from the U.S. Census Bureau
indicate that population diversification will continue to rise in the
coming decades (Vespa, Armstrong, Medina, & Census Bureau, 2018).
Most European countries have seen a rise in immigrant representation
within local schools over the past decade (OECD, 2023). These rapid
and, in some cases, unforeseen shifts have introduced complex challenges across various aspects of school life.
The integration of students with immigrant background is hindered
by interconnected barriers operating at multiple levels, including socioeconomic, linguistic, cultural, psychological, and educational. This
study focuses on social barriers, particularly students’ attitudes toward
diversity. Such attitudes can either facilitate or impede the integration
process of incoming students, affecting their educational experience,
engagement in learning opportunities, and acquisition of academic
outcomes (Baysu, Agirdag, & De Leersnyder, 2022; OECD, 2015). In
recent years, assessing attitudes toward immigration has become a focus
of international and national studies, reflecting broader political
agendas in education (Heath & Richards, 2016; Schulz et al., 2023).
While it is difficult to accurately measure the extent of negative attitudes

toward diversity in schools, evidence suggests that prejudice and
discrimination remain prevalent and are increasing in many countries
(e.g., Baysu et al., 2022; Johansson, Myrberg, & Toropova, 2022; Zick,
Pettigrew, & Wagner, 2008). In line with educational equity goals,
practitioners, policymakers, and researchers have a shared responsibility to foster positive attitudes toward diversity as part of
creating inclusive learning environments and ensuring equitable opportunities for all students (Banks, 2014; Kyriakides, Creemers, Panayiotou, & Charalambous, 2020). Therefore, assessing students’
multicultural attitudes is crucial for understanding the current state of
educational systems, identifying areas where negative attitudes may
persist, and developing initiatives to promote inclusion. However, previous reviews on multicultural instrumentation have highlighted gaps in
capturing students’ multicultural attitudes, with a key issue being the
lack of strong psychometric properties of the available instruments
(Sarraj, Carter, & Burley, 2015).
Apart from issues in measuring multicultural attitudes in educational
research, another central question is whether students’ attitudes are
influenced by specific background characteristics, such as gender or
immigrant status (Hogg & Vaughan, 2018). Identifying such relationships can help schools and researchers implement more targeted interventions tailored to the needs of specific student groups (Kyriakides,

The concept of intergroup attitudes as another example, was used to

In response to managing the growing cultural diversity in modern
societies, the concept of multiculturalism emerged. A multicultural
school is one that not only respects but actively protects cultural
pluralism and promotes harmonious coexistence among different cultures (Banks, 2014). This view is less influenced by intergroup dynamics,
such as the power imbalance, because it ideally places majority and
minority groups on the same level of importance. It ultimately aims for
the coexistence of diverse cultures within the school, ensuring that all
individuals enjoy equal rights and responsibilities (Guilherme & Dietz,
2015). In this respect, multicultural attitudes can be seen as awareness
of, sensitivity to, and comfort with cultural pluralism (Ponterotto,
Baluch, Greig, & Rivera, 1998). In light to the actions required to
managing cultural diversity, conceptualizations such as the tripartite
model of cultural competence emerged (Sue & Torino, 2005), involving
three key dimensions: awareness of how one’s own identity, experiences,
biases, and attitudes shape their perceptions and interactions with
others; understanding that people have diverse backgrounds, worldviews
and experiences, and recognition of the dynamics of phenomena such as
racism, oppression, and privilege; and ability to apply culturally relevant
behavior and engage with diverse individuals and groups.
To develop a more consistent implementation of multicultural education across schools, Banks & Banks (1995) developed a framework
referring to multicultural attitudes as one of the key outcomes of
multicultural education. In this respect, the framework adopts the three
domains of learning -cognitive, affective, and behavioral- to explain how
students can develop positive attitudes toward cultural diversity. Banks
(2014) explained that these components are interconnected and interdependent, urging that students should be guided "to know, to care, and
to act in ways that will develop and foster a democratic and just society in
which all groups experience cultural democracy and cultural empowerment"
(p. 56). Therefore, the development of multicultural attitudes involves
processes within the cognitive (e.g., thoughts that evolve into beliefs and
perceptions), affective (e.g., emotions and feelings toward people and
events), and behavioral domains (e.g., actions resulting from internal
processes).

1. Why address multicultural attitudes?
Students’ negative attitudes toward diversity can have adverse
consequences for individuals and the school community. However,
research shows that these attitudes may not always manifest overtly or
consistently as actions, making them challenging to detect within
schools (Dovidio, Hewstone, Glick, & Esses, 2010). For example, subtle
forms of bullying, such as marginalization, may stem from prejudice
(Mehari & Farrell, 2013). More overt manifestations can include physical or verbal bullying, active discrimination, or racism, all of which may
contribute to psychological issues for the victimized students such as
stress, reduced sense of belonging, or school dislike (Johansson et al.,
2022). Negative attitudes can also manifest through everyday school
practices. For instance, a school’s indifference to students’ backgrounds
may be reflected in its failure to recognize that not all students connect
with the celebration of certain national, religious, or cultural events.
Other exclusionary practices could include neglecting to accommodate
parents who do not speak the local language or failing to adapt the
curriculum and instruction to reflect the diverse cultural backgrounds of
students (Banks, 2014). Ignoring to address such issues might lead, even
involuntarily, to students’ exclusion from social, extracurricular, or
educational activities restricting their access to learning opportunities
(Sapouna, De Amicis, & Vezzali, 2023). These experiences can foster
feelings of non-acceptance and undermine students’ self-esteem and
self-worth (Greenwald et al., 2002). At the school level, negative attitudes and discrimination may harm social cohesion and contradict the
values of inclusion and respect (Basarkod et al., 2024).
Educational systems are expected to develop policies to support the
integration of all students into local schools. Schools should ensure that
every student feels included and can exercise their fundamental right to
an education free from discrimination (Block, Cross, Riggs, & Gibbs,
2014). This requires not only promoting inclusion, diversity, and equity
but also addressing social threats such as racism, bullying, and
discrimination stemming from negative attitudes toward diversity. In
this respect, schools must act to eliminate negative behaviors, raise
awareness of the mechanisms that shape attitudes toward diversity, and
replace implicit biases with an appreciation for cultural pluralism
(Castles, 2018). Students’ and teachers’ multicultural attitudes can be
seen as school’s readiness and competence in managing cultural diversity and practice multiculturalism. In case negative attitudes are
identified, it is useful to understand which aspects of diversity are
involved, who holds these attitudes, and what underlying biases
contribute to their formation. This understanding can inform the design
and implementation of targeted interventions that address prejudice at
its roots, ultimately fostering a deeper sense of inclusion and belonging
for all students within the school community.

2.1. Multicultural instrumentation
Several attempts have been made to develop instruments that capture students’ attitudes toward diversity. Some instruments focused on
the outcomes of negative attitudes toward diversity (e.g., Quick
Discrimination Index; Ponterotto et al., 1995), while others targeted
personality characteristics, based on the idea that these attributes are
related to the formation of a multicultural stance (e.g., Openness to
Diversity and Challenge scale; Trolian & Parker, 2022, or the Multicultural Personality Questionnaire-Short Form; Van der Zee et al.,
2012). Another group of instruments aims to capture the collective situation of the school environment (e.g., Multicultural School Climate
Inventory; Marx & Byrnes, 2012), though this approach is more suitable
for purposes other than exploring relationships between students’ attitudes and their background characteristics, as intended in this study.
Instruments designed to measure students’ dispositions are more common, with the Miville-Guzmán Universality-Diversity Scale (Miville

Previous studies have highlighted limitations in the measurement of
students’ multicultural dispositions across relevant instruments,
demonstrating how difficult it remains to accurately capture these attitudes (for reviews of instruments assessing attitudes toward diversity,
see Dunn, Smith, & Montoya, 2006 and Sarraj et al., 2015). A common
limitation in multicultural instruments is that they rarely build upon a
robust theoretical framework (Sarraj et al., 2015). Most instruments are
instead based on emerging themes from the literature, which may lead
to less accurate measurements and unclear relationships between these
themes. Second, the use of the instruments is often not accompanied
with sufficient psychometric evidence such as aspects of validity (e.g.,
factor structure) and reliability, or concerns related to social desirability
(Brown, 2004; Dunn et al., 2006; Hays, 2008). Third, while numerous
instruments exist to measure teachers’ multicultural attitudes, fewer
have been developed for students. Even so, Jensen, Whiting, and
Chapman (2018), in reviewing ten scales that attempt to measure
multicultural teacher dispositions, found that none demonstrated strong
psychometric properties across three general criteria: construct validity,
reliability (i.e., internal consistency), and factor structure. Fourth, instruments designed to capture students’ multicultural attitudes have
generally been developed and tested for students in tertiary education,
such as preservice teachers, counselling trainees, or health students
(Ruales, Agirdag, & Van Petegem, 2020). Assessing the attitudes of
secondary education students becomes more demanding due to the need
to ensure the age-appropriateness of terms and phrases used. For
instance, secondary students may not be familiar with terms like "bias"
or "prejudice" that are commonly used in cultural diversity and multiculturalism discussions. Related to this, according to a recent literature
review on multicultural instrumentation (Sarraj et al., 2015), the use of
wordy, leading, or double-barreled items appeared to be another common limitation. Such items may lead to imprecise operationalization
and inaccurate measurement due to respondents’ confusion. Last, most
instruments were developed and used in Western countries, which may
not ensure relevance or utility in cultures with different characteristics
(Ruales et al., 2020).
Building on the framework developed by Banks and Banks (1995),
the Munroe Multicultural Attitude Scale Questionnaire (MASQUE) was
developed by Munroe and Pearson (2006). The instrument uses the three
domains of learning -cognitive, affective, and behavioral- to assess students’ multicultural attitudes through self-reported responses on a Likert scale. In line with contemporary research, multicultural attitudes are
viewed more broadly, encompassing aspects of diversity beyond
ethnicity or immigrant background, such as socio-economic status
(SES), religion, language, sexual orientation, and gender (Banks, 2014).
This broader perspective enhances the instrument’s applicability for a
wider range of purposes. Several factors led to the selection of MASQUE
for this study. First, MASQUE is grounded in a theoretical framework,
unlike other instruments that primarily rely on emerging themes from
the literature (Sarraj et al., 2015). Specifically, Banks’ framework of
multicultural education provides well-defined operational definitions
and comprehensive measurement of the underlying constructs (Munroe
& Pearson, 2006). Second, the scale uses simpler and more concise
wording, making it more suitable for lower secondary education students. Finally, the view of diversity in a broader way has two advantages: first, it allows researchers and schools to identify which aspects of
diversity are associated with more negative attitudes, and second, it
enables the instrument to be used for a broader range of research aims
and projects focused on promoting inclusion and diversity in schools.
The MASQUE has been widely used across various disciplines, yet
certain gaps limit its broader application. First, there has been limited
examination and reporting of its psychometric properties beyond the
original study. For example, only a few studies have reported reliability
indicators (e.g., Kalibatseva & Yang, 2024; Lee & Kim, 2024; Weiler,

Helfrich, Palermo, & Zimmerman, 2013), and even fewer have
addressed aspects of validity or measurement equivalence across groups
(e.g., Tanujaya & Yudiarso, 2023). Second, most of the studies (e.g.,
Merlin-Knoblich & Dameron, 2021; Merlin-Knoblich, Summers, &
Abrams, 2023; Richardson, Volberding, & Zahl, 2020; Tinkler, Hannah,
Tinkler, & Miller, 2013) have employed relatively small samples (i.e.,
less than 150 participants), which may have contributed to the lack of
detailed reporting on psychometric properties. Third, most studies using
the MASQUE have focused on university students, likely because the
instrument was initially validated with this population (e.g., Weiler
et al., 2013). We argue for an earlier assessment of multicultural attitudes, such as in adolescence, when students experience an increasing
awareness of the external world and begin to form their personal and
social identities. Adolescence is therefore a key period for implementing
policies and programs aimed at promoting multicultural attitudes.
Finally, the majority of MASQUE research has been conducted in the
United States, with only a few studies (e.g., Karatekin, Akcaoğlu, &
Taban, 2019; Lee & Kim, 2024; Polat & Barka, 2012) in other countries
(e.g., Korea, Austria, and Switzerland). No studies were identified to
apply the MASQUE in Greek-speaking contexts, leaving its applicability
in such cultural and linguistic settings unexplored. In this study, we seek
to address these gaps by investigating construct validity and measurement invariance of the MASQUE with responses from secondary students, thereby contributing to the field of measuring secondary students’
multicultural attitudes.
2.2. Issues of social desirability bias
People often form attitudes through quick evaluations of readily
available information, influenced by socialization, observation, direct
experiences, or emotional reactions (Hogg & Vaughan, 2018). These
influences, however, vary across social contexts. For example, a child
may develop negative attitudes toward diversity due to exposure to
negative stimuli (e.g., parents’ ideologies or social media hatred) or by
observing peers modelling negative behaviors (Priest et al., 2014). On
the other hand, schools aim to foster positive attitudes toward diversity
through information processing, experiential learning, or classical and
instrumental conditioning (Hello, Scheepers, Vermulst, & Gerris, 2004).
As a result, students may hold negative attitudes internally but adjust
their explicit responses to align with school expectations, potentially
introducing bias in their reported multicultural attitudes, depending on
the social context in which they are assessed. This situation when respondents provide answers that they believe will be viewed favorably by
others (e.g., by the school staff) rather than reflecting their true opinions, is called social desirability (Holtgraves, 2004). When measuring
sensitive constructs, such as multicultural attitudes that are often associated with socially unacceptable phenomena like discrimination,
bullying, or racism, social desirability bias might be more prevalent.
Researchers have developed measures to account for this bias in their
analyses and examine the extent to which the collected responses are
influenced by it. These measures typically use indirect questioning to
subtly assess tendencies to overreport socially approved responses (e.g.,
Crowne & Marlowe, 1960).
Previous studies exploring multicultural-related constructs generally
supported a positive relationship with social desirability. However,
systematic efforts have primarily addressed this relationship with
teachers’ responses rather than students and have not examined multicultural attitudes in particular. For example, a systematic review of 15
studies examining the relationship between practitioners’ cultural
competence and social desirability resulted to a small to moderate
positive correlation, though the strength of this association varied
depending on the measures used to assess both multicultural competence and social desirability (Larson & Bradshaw, 2017). However, one
may argue that teachers are more prone to social desirability bias than
students, as adults are more aware of socially (un)acceptable values,
while their attitudes tend to become more egalitarian over time (Baron

4. Research aims
The present study aims to explore the relationships between students’ background characteristics (namely gender, immigrant status,
and language spoken at home) and their multicultural attitudes. This
investigation enables schools to identify student groups in which negative attitudes may be more prevalent and to develop targeted programs
or interventions to address them. An additional aspect of this study is the
consideration of social desirability bias and its potential influence on
students’ responses on their multicultural attitudes. If such bias is present, the study examines whether it is associated with students’ background characteristics and whether it is related with multicultural
attitudes. To achieve these objectives, the study first develops the
measurement model and evaluates the construct validity of the two main
instruments (MASQUE for measuring multicultural attitudes and
MCSDS-Form C for assessing social desirability), as well as their measurement equivalence across different student groups. Therefore, the
aims of the study are: (a) to systematically assess the construct validity
of the MASQUE and its measurement equivalence across student groups
(i.e., gender, immigrant status, and language spoken at home); (b) to
investigate the influence of students’ background characteristics on
their multicultural attitudes; (c) to examine the relationship between
students’ multicultural attitudes and social desirability by considering
the effect of any student background characteristic.

3. The relationship between multicultural attitudes and student
background characteristics
In search of factors that affect the formation of multicultural attitudes, the quest has begun examining students’ background factors.
Although different conceptualizations for multicultural attitudes are
used in each study, the literature tends to converge that both students’
gender and immigrant background matter. For example, previous analyses on the ICCS cycles of 2009, 2016, and 2022, showed that higher
endorsement of equal rights for immigrants has been consistently associated with female and immigrant students (Losito, Agrusti, Damiani, &
Schulz, 2018; Munck, Barber, & Torney-Purta, 2018; Schulz, Ainley,
Fraillon, Kerr, & Losito, 2010; Schulz et al., 2023). Similarly, findings
from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
showed that in almost all countries female students held more positive
attitudes towards people from other cultures (OECD, 2020). With regard
to studies using MASQUE, results are mixed which raises concerns about
the scale’s ability to discern gender differences. Specifically, a study
identified that female university students exhibit higher multicultural
attitudes in Hungary and Turkey but not in Austria (Karatekin et al.,
2019). Two other studies did not identify any differences between male
and female students (Richardson et al., 2020; Weiler et al., 2013);
however, their samples were relatively small and potentially not sufficient to detect such effects (i.e., 75 and 60 students respectively).
With respect to students’ ethnic background (i.e., students with vs
without immigrant background), the findings tend to support that those
who come to integrate in the new society have higher multicultural
attitudes, but contextual differences seem to also exist (Schulz et al.,
2023). For example, in PISA 2018, students with an immigrant background expressed higher respect for people from other cultures in 20
countries (OECD, 2020). In 11 countries, the differences were not statistically significant and in only 2 countries students without immigrant
background had higher multicultural attitudes (OECD, 2020). There are
certain reasons that may explain these differences such as the host society perceiving incoming populations as a social, economic and cultural
threat (Verkuyten, Mepham, & Kros, 2018). On the other hand, ethnic
minority groups may share more positive multicultural attitudes than
the majority group as they seek to be respected and integrated in the
society (Verkuyten & Martinovic, 2006). In educational settings for
example, more ethnic diversity in the classroom was positively related to
students’ endorsement for multiculturalism (Van Geel & Vedder, 2010).
Moreover, their experience with more than one social context, intergroup contact, and attempts for collaboration might be additional factors contributing to immigrant students’ higher respect to cultural
diversity (Kraus & Daenekindt, 2021). However, multicultural attitudes
between immigrant and non-immigrant students might vary from

A stage sampling procedure was used. Specifically, four public secondary schools in Cyprus that had a relatively high diversity in terms of
the percentage of students with immigrant background (i.e., more than
30 %) were selected. This selection aimed to ensure the sample was
relevant for assessing students’ multicultural attitudes and allowed to
examine the influence of immigrant status as a student background
factor. At the school level, a stratified sampling approach was employed.

students of each class participated in the study. In total, 450 students

rest in the 8th grade (see Table 1). These two grades in the local
educational system typically represent age groups of 13–14 and 14–15
years old. Among the participants, 51.8 % were male. In terms of

No prior studies were identified using the MASQUE in a Greekspeaking context. To address this, the research team followed rigorous
translation procedures to develop a Greek version of the instrument,
adhering to established high-quality standards (Fenn, Tan, & George,
2020). First, the two authors independently translated the MASQUE
from English to Greek. This initial translation was then reviewed by a
Greek literature teacher with more than ten years of experience teaching
in secondary education schools and a superintendent with extensive
experience of the secondary education context. Their feedback delt with
clarifying expressions that might be confusing or unfamiliar to students,
such as the phrase “I am sensitive…” and less familiar terms like
“prejudice”. Adjustments to the translation were made based on a
consensus among the two researchers. Subsequently, the Greek version
was back-translated into English by a research colleague proficient in
English, who was unaware of the study’s purpose or the instrument’s
specifics. The back-translation was reviewed and compared with the
original version by the authors and the superintendent, revealing minor
discrepancies in three items. These items were adjusted to correct the
observed issues, ensuring the final Greek version was both linguistically
consistent and conceptually faithful to the original instrument. The final
translation is available in the Appendix.
5.2.2. Social desirability bias
The Marlowe-Crowne Social Desirability Scale (MCSDS) is widely
recognized as one of the most used instruments globally for assessing
respondents’ “need to obtain approval by responding in a culturally
appropriate and acceptable manner” (Crowne & Marlowe, 1960, p. 353).
Shorter forms of the original 33-item scale were developed to reduce
item redundancy and the length of the assessment. In the present study,
the MCSDS-Form C (Reynolds, 1982), a concise adaptation derived from
the original work of Crowne and Marlowe (1960), was used to measure
social desirability bias. This 13-item short form consists of statements
designed to detect the tendency for social approval and is scored on a
dichotomous scale (1 =True, 0 =False) based on whether the statement
applies to the respondent. Eight items reflect socially approved but
improbable behaviors (e.g., "It is sometimes hard for me to go on with
my work if I am not encouraged"), while five items indicate socially
undesirable behaviors (e.g., "I’m always willing to admit to it when I
make a mistake") and thus, were recoded. A higher total score of the
scale indicates a greater tendency to respond in a socially desirable
manner.
The translation of the MCSDS–Form C was based on the work of
Lavidas and Gialamas (2019), who adapted the scale for a
Greek-speaking student sample using a back-translation method. Their
validation study demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties for
the scale (Lavidas & Gialamas, 2019). However, as the original adaptation was conducted with university students, a review of the Greek
version was necessary to ensure age-appropriateness for the secondary
education students who constituted the sample of this study. In collaboration with a Greek literature teacher and a superintendent of secondary education, certain terms in three items were simplified to
enhance comprehensibility for younger participants (e.g., “feel resentful” was revised to “feel angry”). A consensus was reached that the
adapted version of the MCSDS–Form C maintained its conceptual
integrity while being relevant to the study’s target population.
The MCSDS–Form C has been extensively studied in various cultural
and contextual settings, predominantly in North America, Europe, and
Asia, demonstrating strong psychometric properties and high correlations with the original full scale (e.g., He et al., 2015; Loo & Loewen,
2004). Investigations into the scale’s factor structure have produced

5.3. Procedures
At the end of the second term of the 2023–2024 school year, members of the research team administered the two instruments and a short
questionnaire about students’ background (i.e., gender, immigrant
background, language spoken at home) to the participating classes using
a paper-and-pencil format. The study was authorized by the national
authority (i.e., Centre for Educational Research and Evaluation) and
permission was granted by participating schools’ management. Student
participation was voluntary, with parental consent obtained for all
participants. Anonymity and confidentiality were ensured by not collecting any identifying information.

6. Results
6.1. Measuring students’ multicultural attitudes with the MASQUE
6.1.1. Construct validity
To investigate the extent to which the MASQUE can be used to
measure students’ multicultural attitudes, its construct validity was
initially assessed. Based on the structure proposed by Munroe and
Pearson (2006), a three-correlated-factor model was tested for the
MASQUE with the factors representing Know, Care, and Act. Initial results indicated poor model fit (e.g., CFI=.810), while examination of
item loadings revealed that three items (i.e., K6, K7, A18) had substantially low loadings (<.40) on their respective factors. Following a
model re-specification approach, these items were removed from the
model, and the analysis was rerun. The modified model demonstrated a
slightly improved fit (e.g., CFI=.862), with all item loadings exceeding
the acceptable threshold. With the introduction of six items’ error correlations based on theoretical considerations and suggestions of model
modification indices, the model reached a substantially improved fit (see
Table 2). All global fit indices surpassed acceptable thresholds, with
item loadings ranging from .44 to .68 (Know: .44–.59, Care: .46–.68, Act:
.44–.58) and factor correlations ranging from .50 to .77. Average Variance Extracted (AVE) was .48 for the Know factor, .52 for the Care factor,
and .49 for the Act factor.
To ensure that multicultural attitudes were best represented by the
hypothesized three-factor structure rather than a single-dimensional
construct, the one-factor model was also tested. In this model, all
items were loaded onto a single factor. The initial results indicated an
unacceptable model fit (e.g., CFI=.780). Although with certain modifications to the model (i.e., exclusion of three items with low item loadings and introduction of five items’ error correlations) that led to
marginally acceptable fit indices (see Table 2), a comparison with the
three-factor model revealed that the one-factor model demonstrated
significantly poorer fit (Δχ²=38.34, p < .001). Consequently, the threefactor model that is grounded in theoretical foundations (Munroe &
Pearson, 2006), was confirmed.

5.4. Data analysis
The first objective of the present study was to assess the construct
validity of the adapted versions of the MASQUE and the MCSDS–Form C.
To achieve this, we employed Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) to
evaluate the factor structures as posited in the original studies (Munroe
& Pearson, 2006; Reynolds, 1982). For the MASQUE, we tested two
models: the hypothesized three-factor structure and a unidimensional
structure. For the MCSDS, we tested the unidimensional model. Once the
models demonstrated an acceptable fit, we proceeded to test for measurement invariance to ensure that the instrument elicited similar
response patterns across various student groups based on gender,
immigrant background, and language spoken at home. Measurement
invariance for MASQUE was assessed using multigroup CFA at three
sequential levels: configural, metric, and scalar (Kline, 2015). Configural invariance tested whether the pattern of fixed and free factor
loadings was consistent across groups. Metric invariance assumed equal
factor loadings across groups, while scalar invariance further constrained intercepts to be equal across groups. For the MCSDS–Form C,
measurement invariance was examined at two levels: threshold invariance and threshold and loading invariance, appropriate for ordered
categorical data (Svetina, Rutkowski, & Rutkowski, 2019). Threshold
invariance constrained the thresholds to be equal across groups, while
the most restrictive model required both thresholds and factor loadings
to be equal. Invariance was assessed through model comparison using
the Δχ² difference test (Muthén & Muthén, 2012) and ΔCFI (Cheung &
Rensvold, 2002). Achieving invariance at the most restrictive levels
suggests that the constructs’ meaning and interpretation are consistent
across groups, allowing for comparisons and enabling the examination
of the effects of students’ background factors.

student groups. The results of this step supported scalar invariance in all
cases as well (see Table 2), indicating that the MASQUE measures
multicultural attitudes in a consistent manner across the examined
student groups. In all cases, parameter estimates (i.e., item loadings and
factor correlations) remained similar to those of the initial three-factor
model and within acceptable levels.

6.2.2. The effect of students’ background characteristics on multicultural
attitudes and social desirability
After identifying the appropriate factor structures for the revised
MASQUE and MCSDS–Form C, we examined the influence of students’
background characteristics on multicultural attitudes and social desirability bias. Structural regression analyses were conducted, modelling
the effect of each background characteristic (i.e., gender, immigrant
status, and language spoken at home) one-by-one on the two constructs
separately. The results indicated that immigrant background and language did not have any statistically significant effect on multicultural
attitudes neither on social desirability.
Instead, gender was the only characteristic found to have a statistically significant impact on both constructs. Specifically, the standardized coefficients for the effect of gender on multicultural attitudes were
estimated at: .44 (p < .001, R2=.19) for the Know factor, .35 (p < .001,
R2=.13) for the Care factor, and .37 (p < .001, R2=.14) for the Act factor,
overall showing that female students exhibited higher multicultural attitudes compared to male students. All effect sizes were medium to large,
with Cohen’s d estimated at .969, .756, and .796, respectively. This
model demonstrated good fit indices (χ²(95)= 121.94*, CFI= .962,
RMSEA= .026, SRMR= .040).
Regarding social desirability bias, gender had an effect estimated at
.27 (p < .001, R2=.07) with a medium effect size (Cohen’s d=.566),
suggesting that female students displayed a slightly higher tendency to
present their attitudes in a socially desirable manner. This model also
had a satisfactory fit (χ²(54)= 70.337, CFI= .960, RMSEA= .026,
WRMR= .877).

6.2.3. The relationship between students’ multicultural attitudes and social
desirability
Based on these results, a structural model was developed, incorporating gender as independent variable influencing both multicultural
attitudes and social desirability. Moreover, the relationship between
multicultural attitudes and social desirability was considered (see
Fig. 1). The analysis confirmed statistically significant correlations between social desirability and only two dimensions of multicultural attitudes (i.e., Care and Act). Consequently, the relationship between
social desirability and the Know factor was constrained to zero, and the
analysis was rerun.
The final model revealed that social desirability had a positive
moderate correlation with the Care and Act factors, with standardized
coefficients estimated at .34 (p < .05) and .43 (p < .01), respectively.
Additionally, the effect of gender on multicultural attitudes and social
desirability remained consistent in magnitude as in the separate structural regressions. The standardized coefficients of the final model are
presented in Fig. 1. The MASQUE factor correlations ranged from .48 to
.73 (p < .001), with the highest correlation observed between the Care
and Act factors. The model demonstrated acceptable fit indices
(χ²(310)= 429.640, CFI= .904, RMSEA= .029, WRMR= 1.016).

The present study addresses a current issue in psychoeducational
research related to the evaluation of students’ multicultural attitudes. As
schools worldwide become increasingly multicultural, there is a growing

need for educational systems to create welcoming and inclusive school
environments that facilitate the integration of students from diverse
backgrounds. Recognizing the challenge of detecting subtle attitudes in
schools, instruments that effectively measure students’ stances toward
diversity are required. This study provides validity evidence for the
MASQUE, which, with some modifications, can be used to assess students’ multicultural attitudes in secondary education within the examined context. Specifically, the study confirms that the MASQUE
comprises three distinct components (i.e., Know, Care, and Act) as
theoretically envisioned (Munroe & Pearson, 2006).
The confirmed structure reflects the approach to viewing multicultural attitudes into three interrelated domains: understanding and
building awareness (cognitive domain) about multiculturalism and
related issues (e.g., discrimination); developing sensitivity and
expressing care toward diversity (affective domain); and ultimately,
acting in ways that support diversity and inclusion (behavioral domain)
(Banks, 2014; Banks & Banks, 1995). This perspective is valuable not
only for research purposes but also for practical applications, particularly when schools aim to cultivate positive multicultural attitudes
among students. For example, schools could initially aim to develop
students’ knowledge of topics such as social inequities, discrimination,
or racism, and a deeper understanding of the way individual characteristics may confer either opportunities or disadvantages in education
and social life. Subsequently, schools and teachers could focus on
cultivating empathy and concern for individuals and peers who may face
such social risks (Banks, 2014). Finally, they could model inclusive behaviors and involve students in activities that promote multiculturalism
within their environment, thereby enhancing the expression of positive
attitudes toward diversity into actions (Basarkod et al., 2024; Castles,
2018).
Students’ immigrant background and language spoken at home did
not account for any differences in their multicultural attitudes. Although
the literature suggests a tendency for more positive attitudes among
students with an immigrant background due to their intentions to
integrate into the new society, contextual differences likely exist, as also
demonstrated in previous studies (e.g., Schulz et al., 2023; Verkuyten,
2021). A potential explanation for the lack of differences identified in
our study could be attributed to the highly heterogeneous composition
of the participating schools (i.e., more than 30 % of students with an
immigrant background), a factor that has been linked to positive attitudes toward diversity (Beyer & Brese, 2024; Sincer, Volman, van der
Veen, & Severiens, 2020). As a result, potential differences may have
been diffused. Nevertheless, this finding indicates that efforts to promote positive attitudes toward diversity in these schools, through
intervention programs for instance, can be applied universally
addressing both local and non-local students.
On the other hand, our study identified significant differences based
on gender, in line with international literature that consistently showed
that female students tend to exhibit more positive multicultural attitudes (Losito et al., 2018; Munck et al., 2018; OECD, 2020; Schulz et al.,
2010; Schulz et al., 2023). Previous studies have attempted to explain
this persistent gender gap by highlighting differences in emotional
awareness (Barrett, Lane, Sechrest, & Schwartz, 2000), stronger
empathy skills, greater care for social issues (Noddings, 2003), and
higher intercultural sensitivity (Mičić, 2018) among women. These explanations emphasize variations in the affective domain. However, our
study found that the effect of gender was similarly pronounced across all
MASQUE factors, encompassing the cognitive, affective, and behavioral
domains (see Fig. 1). This finding suggests that gender differences may
not stem solely from affective characteristics, which are often viewed as
more innate and less malleable. Instead, fostering male students’
knowledge in multicultural issues (i.e., the cognitive domain) could
provide a starting point for enhancing their multicultural sensitivity.
This gender gap, which appears to be a global issue, highlights the need
for initiatives to promote equity in education by actively engaging male
students in multicultural education and diversity-promoting

programmes.
Issues of social desirability bias in students’ responses about their
multicultural attitudes were also detected. It is noted that only a few
studies have addressed this relationship specifically in relation to students’ multicultural attitudes (e.g., Covacevich, 2010; Miville et al.,
1999; Munroe & Pearson, 2006), while most focus on multicultural
competence and often target practitioners rather than students. These
studies overall report weak to moderate relationships, however, there is
variability in findings due to the differences in the instruments used,
target groups, and cultural contexts (Larson & Bradshaw, 2017). For
instance, it has been argued that individuals in Asian contexts may have
a stronger tendency to "save face" in social situations (Sodowsky,
Kuo-Jackson, Richardson, & Corey, 1998). Moreover, the few studies
that examined this relationship used the overall scale of multicultural
attitudes rather than its factors’ scores. For example, Munroe and
Pearson (2006), who used the same instruments employed in this study
with university students in the U.S., reported a correlation of r = .16. In
our study, social desirability was associated with only two components
of the MASQUE (i.e., Care and Act) but not with Know. These results
suggest that students were less inclined to respond in a socially desirable
manner when reporting their knowledge about multicultural issues
(cognitive domain); however, they were more likely to do so when
reporting their care for and behavior supporting multiculturalism (affective and behavioral domains). Although this is an area that requires
further investigation, there are plausible concerns in the literature
suggesting that attitudinal and behavioral constructs may have stronger
associations with social desirability than cognitive ones, due to their
direct connection with stigmatization and social sanctions (Keefer,
2015; Vesely & Klöckner, 2020). Nevertheless, this finding further justifies the separation of the three components of the MASQUE, as they
seem to represent interrelated yet distinct constructs. It is also noted that
the constructs related to social desirability (Care and Act) were more
strongly related to each other than with the Know factor, which was
unrelated to social desirability (see Fig. 1). This may indicate a substantial leap beyond the cognitive domain and a closer connection between the affective and behavioral domains of multicultural attitudes.
However, researchers when dealing with the factor scores of MASQUE
should consider that scores of the Care and Act scales may be “inflated”
as influenced by the tendency to respond in a socially acceptable way.
8. Limitations and future research
The present study addressed only certain aspects of validity for the
MASQUE within a specific context using cross-sectional data. Other
methods (e.g., interviews) could complement this approach to examine
other aspects of validity (e.g., internal and/or concurrent validity) to
ensure that students’ multicultural attitudes are interpreted as intended
by the MASQUE, and/or compare their responses with verbal explanations or expressions of multicultural behavior. In addition, future
research could employ different samples of students from various
educational levels and incorporate multiple waves of data, which would
provide the opportunity to examine the predictive validity of the instrument and capture potential changes in students’ multicultural attitudes over time. Such efforts would further enhance the instrument’s
trustworthiness for use in longitudinal studies and interventions aimed
at fostering multicultural attitudes in school settings. In this regard,
MASQUE could inform schools about specific areas to target for
improving multicultural sensitivity, such as focusing on male students,
addressing a specific aspect of diversity (e.g., ethnicity), or concentrating on a particular component of MASQUE (e.g., cognitive, affective,
or behavioral), which would support the instrument’s interpretive
validity.
It is acknowledged that minor modifications were applied to the
MASQUE during the factor analysis, which may suggest the need for
further investigation into the instrument’s functioning within the
examined context. For instance, the exclusion of three items due to low

factor loadings may reflect either weaknesses in measurement or limited
contextual relevance of the items, which might also explain the marginal
AVE of latent factors that raise concerns of convergent validity (Hair,
Black, Babin, & Anderson, 2010). For example, the low loading of item
K6 (“I accept the fact that languages other than [Greek] are spoken”) on
its latent factor (i.e., Know) may be explained by a semantic inconsistency: unlike the other items in this factor, which use
cognitively-oriented verbs such as ‘I know’ or ‘I understand,’ the verb ‘I
accept’ introduces an affective or attitudinal stance (e.g., tolerance),
potentially making the item less aligned with the intended cognitive
construct. The exclusion of item A18 (“I do not take action when witnessing bias based on people’s preferred sexual orientation”) might
reflect issues of contextual relevance in the local context, as topics
related to sexual orientation are still considered sensitive at schools,
while sexual education is not yet systematically embedded in the official
curriculum. Moreover, the introduction of correlated error terms may
reflect a degree of conceptual overlap among items that address similar
aspects of diversity or a similar attitudinal domain. For example, the
correlation between the residuals of item C11 (“I am sensitive toward
people of every financial status”) and item C13 (“A person’s social status
does not affect how I care about people’), both from the Care factor, was
allowed because both items relate to students’ sensitivity regarding socioeconomic status. Last, the correlation between item K3 (“I understand
religious beliefs differ”) and item C9 (“I am sensitive to differing expressions of ethnicity”), from the Know and Care factors respectively,
was justified due to their shared emphasis on understanding of different
types of cultural expression, which may elicit overlapping interpretations. It is noted, however, that the modifications made in this
study may not be applicable in other contexts, and additional weaknesses may emerge that require context-specific adjustments.
Future research could, therefore, aim to confirm the validity of the
instrument in secondary education in other countries and attempt to
demonstrate invariance across different versions of MASQUE (e.g., the
English vs. the adapted Greek version). Additionally, researchers could
investigate the presence and strength of relationships with other student
background characteristics such as SES, to provide insights for more
targeted interventions. In this context, practitioners may also benefit
from examining the influence of malleable factors within schools’ control, such as civic knowledge, intergroup contact, and participation in
multicultural education activities. Researchers could also be interested
to investigate characteristics of effective schools in fostering students’
multicultural attitudes and addressing issues such as discrimination,
bullying, or racism (Kyriakides & Creemers, 2013).
Finally, given the limited literature on the relationship between
students’ multicultural attitudes and social desirability bias, this study
provides further empirical support to the argument that social desirability may indeed pose a potential source of error variance in selfreported measures of multicultural attitudes (Holtgraves, 2004).
Therefore, researchers are encouraged to account for this bias and
further refine instruments to minimize such masking effects in measurement, which might reduce accuracy, for example in studies evaluating the impact of intervention programmes that aim to enhance
students’ multicultural attitudes.

9. Conclusion
Nowadays, there is an international quest for developing inclusive
school environments and fostering positive attitudes toward diversity.
For this reason, advancing research on multicultural instrumentation is
essential. The present study provides evidence supporting the validity of
an adapted version of MASQUE within a Greek-speaking context, which
adds to its international applicability across educational settings. Our
findings justify the theoretical approach of conceptualizing students’
multicultural attitudes through cognitive, affective, and behavioral
components, which can guide the efforts of teachers, schools, and
educational systems. Moreover, educational stakeholders can gain a
deeper understanding about the effect of students’ characteristics on
multicultural attitudes in each specific context, which would allow them
to design more targeted and context-specific interventions.
