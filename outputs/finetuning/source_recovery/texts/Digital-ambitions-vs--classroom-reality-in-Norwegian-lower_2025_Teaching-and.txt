Research paper
Digital ambitions vs. classroom reality in Norwegian lower secondary 
schools: What digital competencies are students developing over time?
Astrid Elisabeth Kure a,b,*, Marte Blikstad-Balas a, Lisbeth M. Brevik a
a Department of Teacher Education and School Research, University of Oslo, Norway
b Department of Languages, Literature and Culture, Østfold University College, Norway
A B S T R A C T
Despite high political ambitions, the increased use of digital technology in lower secondary school has resulted in little indication of the advanced use of digital skills. 
Drawing on video-recorded lessons (N = 29) from five schools in Norway over time and across two national curricula, this study mapped the opportunities students 
had to develop digital skills in English classrooms. The findings provide nuanced insight into the instruction of digital skills in highly digitalised schools. Despite a 
slight increase in digital skills after the 2020 reform, our findings indicate that digital ambitions in the curriculum were not reflected in classroom reality.
1. Introduction
As societies become increasingly digital, many school systems try to 
offer opportunities for students to use digital technology for various 
purposes in their educational and personal lives. They aim for students 
to be able to adapt to new technology and reflexively choose between 
digital opportunities at any given time. In other words, many school 
systems aim to develop students’ relevant digital competencies.
Across countries, educational systems connect students’ access to 
advanced digital skills and competencies with their ability to engage in 
critical thinking, problem-solving and expressing creativity (Council of 
Europe, 2024; Erstad & Voogt, 2018; Joynes et al., 2019; Norwegian 
Directorate for Education and Training [NDET], 2017a; Ottestad & 
Gudmundsdottir, 2018). Moreover, digital competence is considered 
key to fostering digital citizenship and addressing issues of cyber secu­
rity, online privacy and responsible digital behaviour (Bergsjø & Sve­
nungsen, 2022; NDET, 2017b; Strand, 2022). Policymakers recognise 
that preparing students to be digitally competent may help them in the 
future and ensure participation in and contribution to society. For this 
reason, providing students with sufficient digital opportunities is 
considered an important aspect of education (Reimers & Opertti, 2021; 
UNESCO, 2017; 2022).
The increase in the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in schools has left 
educational systems across the world grappling with the urgent question 
of how to prepare today’s students for their digital tomorrow. Educa­
tional systems strive to ensure that their students are competent in using 
AI so that they may become responsible citizens with regard to data 
privacy, algorithmic bias and the potentially negative consequences of 
increased automation (Long & Magerko, 2020; Mollick & Mollick, 
2023). Some teachers adopted AI immediately after its introduction, 
resulting in challenges regarding how to assess its potential educational 
impact, including the teacher assessment of students’ contributions to 
schoolwork (Bender, 2024; Erstad, Kucirkova et al., 2023; Sørhaug, 
2023). Researchers have noted the need for creative and innovative uses 
of AI, while acknowledging that the impact of AI on the future of edu­
cation is unknown (Erstad, Gilje et al., 2023; Munthe et al., 2022; Sætra, 
2023). However, researchers assume that AI drastically increases the 
need for digital competence among students and teachers alike.
An important aim of developing students’ digital competence is to 
ensure that young people master digital skills and technology through 
need-driven and results-based policies (Munthe et al., 2022). According 
to UNESCO (2017), such ambitions are imperative to meet sustainable 
development goal 4 concerning quality education. UNESCO (2017)
argued that information and communication technologies (ICT) can 
promote equality, flexibility and access to life-long learning with suffi­
cient data infrastructure and rigorous use of digital technology.
The educational sector is the primary arena for people to become 
competent users of digital skills (UNESCO, 2017). Intermediate digital 
skills are often considered necessary to meet the demands of modern 
society. These skills vary from “basic access, awareness and training to 
more sophisticated outcomes and critical understandings” (UNESCO, 
2017, p. 27). As such, teachers play a significant role in developing 
students’ digital skills, which may occur both unintentionally, such as by 
using digital technology during other activities, and intentionally, 
through digital technology instruction. Digital technologies could allow 
for changes in how people teach, learn and collaborate, according to

for developing digital skills, a UNESCO (2017) report identified little

longitudinal perspective on the use of digital skills in a natural learning

The Nordic countries have for several years been front runners in 
educational ambitions concerning digital technology, skills and 
competence, as well as in providing schools with the necessary tech­
nological infrastructure. Although the Nordic countries have different 
digital policies, they have recently taken a slightly more critical stance 
against the extensive role of digital technology in education, especially 
concerning younger children. For example, Sweden and Norway have 
recently regulated the use of smartphones in the early school years, and 
Sweden, Norway and Denmark emphasise securing students’ access to 
printed books alongside digital resources (Danish Ministry of Children 
and Education, 2024; Forsler & Guyard, 2023, pp. 1–14; Swedish Min­
istry of Education and Research [SMER], 2024). While highlighting the 
need for more research on digitalisation in schools, SMER (2024) argued 
that the decision to introduce digitalisation might have been hasty and 
resulted in too much digitalisation for students at an early age. While 
access to technology has been given priority in political debates, prior 
research has clearly demonstrated that both student and teacher uptake 
of technology varies extensively – and that the development of digital 
competence is not something that we can take for granted. Studies have 
shown that simply providing access to technology – even when coupled 
with general ambitions on a national level – is not enough to support 
teachers’ uptake and development of competence (Blikstad-Balas & 
Klette, 2021; Olofsson et al., 2015).
Most prior studies in Norway on the use of digital technology have 
included self-reports, small-scale case studies or interventions involving 
specific apps or educational technologies (Ahmadian & Brevik, 2024; 
Beiler et al., 2021; Blikstad-Balas & Klette, 2020). To move the educa­
tional field forward regarding technology uptake in classrooms, we 
believe it is of paramount importance to obtain data on naturally 
occurring instruction. In these situations, teachers are not simply testing 
out specific apps in targeted interventions, and students’ general use of 
technology is not measured or assessed through self-reported data. We 
argue that it is essential to carefully investigate naturally occurring in­
struction in digitally rich classrooms where each student has access to 
their own computer and where teachers have high autonomy against the 
backdrop of educational policies urging them to develop students’ dig­
ital competence, as in Norway (NDET, 2017a).
Few studies have investigated the actual uptake of digital technolo­
gies in the classroom, and even fewer have linked this application to 
students’ use of specific digital subskills in the classroom. While some 
studies have tried to map how teachers provide opportunities for stu­
dents to use available technology (Blikstad-Balas & Klette, 2020; Brevik 
& Davies, 2016), very few have followed technology uptake over time 
among the same schools or teachers in digitally rich classrooms. The lack 
of longitudinal insight into how teachers prompt and develop their 
students’ digital skills and the opportunities students get in class to 
develop such skills are key drivers behind the present study.
In a baseline study (Kure et al., 2023), we analysed how and to what 
extent students demonstrated digital skills during naturally occurring 
lower secondary English teaching. Following this study’s first student 
cohort (2015–17) across Years 9 and 10 (aged 13–15), the findings 
showed that students demonstrated digital skills in half of the lessons. 
However, the digital skills they used were basic rather than advanced, 
and their use was mainly prompted by the teachers. The present article 
extends our baseline study longitudinally, following the study’s second 
(2019–20) and third student cohorts (2021–23) at five of the seven 
original schools. We aim to answer the following research questions 
(RQs). 
RQ1.
To what extent and how do students in five lower secondary 
schools in Norway apply digital skills during naturally occurring English 
teaching?
RQ2.
What characterises students’ application of digital skills during 
naturally occurring English teaching when revisiting one of these classes 
three years later?
To answer both RQs, we drew on 29 video-recorded English lessons 
from five schools across four years. This approach enabled observation 
of students’ use of digital skills during English instruction across classes 
and over time. We used the Framework for Digital Skills (NDET, 2017b) to 
analyse students’ application of skills quantitatively on a scale from 1 
(basic digital skills) to 5 (advanced digital skills) and qualitatively to 
identify the digital subskills they applied. For RQ1, we used English 
lessons from all five schools during the 2019–20 school year. For RQ2, 
we used English lessons from one of the schools three years later, during 
the 2022–23 school year.
2. Technology uptake in digitally rich classrooms
Researchers, policymakers and other stakeholders have engaged in a 
continuing debate about whether technology has replaced traditional 
tools without adding anything of importance to the learning situation 
(Crompton & Burke, 2020). In short, a range of studies have indicated 
that providing access to technology in itself will not guarantee intended 
technological uptake. In Norway, Blikstad-Balas and Klette (2020) found 
that, while teachers tend to use digital technology, the use is often 
transmissive and traditional. Such use places students in the role of in­
dividual consumers of technology rather than active producers who 
collaborate. Their video study documented the limited and ineffective 
use of digital technology among teachers and indicated a conflict in the 
aims of education policy documents compared to what actually 
happened in the classroom.
Across studies highlighting traditional or basic use (e.g., Blik­
stad-Balas & Klette, 2020; Kure et al., 2023), researchers have 
commonly emphasised the importance of qualified teachers who are 
truly prepared to teach in digitalised environments. The OECD’s 
Teaching and Learning International Survey (OECD, 2020) showed that 
across the 48 OECD countries that participated, an average of 53% of 
teachers used ICT frequently or always and consequently asked for more 
professional digital competence (OECD, 2020). The OECD argued the 
need for increased teacher training in ICT skills to develop their curi­
osity, engagement and exploration of how to implement technology in 
the classroom (OECD, 2020). Although professional digital development 
increased during 2013–18, according to the TALIS surveys, teachers in 
Norway and Finland expressed a need for further professional digital 
development and reported that they frequently or always provided op­
portunities for students to use digital technology in school (OECD, 
2020). Many (e.g. Brevik, 2019; Blikstad-Balas & Klette, 2020) have 
pointed to the importance of teacher education when it comes to 
developing teachers’ digital competence, but research has found that 
teacher education does not prepare student teachers for increasingly 
digital classrooms. Instead, the emphasis is still often on instrumental 
purposes, rather than on fundamental epistemic challenges emerging in 
digital learning environments (Aagaard et al., 2024; Røkenes & 
Krumsvik, 2016; Tveiterås & Madsen, 2022).
The GrunnDig report (Munthe et al., 2022) was written to grasp the 
complexity of the term digitalisation in Norway. By analysing policy 
documents, the report provided a research-based investigation into the 
digitalisation of primary and secondary education in Norway and dis­
cussed central issues, such as terminology, experiences, research and 
future considerations. The report’s empirical research on the role of 
technologies in Norwegian schools showed that digitalisation provided 
advantages for language learning, allowing for digital possibilities in 
reading, writing, voice recording and listening. Munthe et al. (2020)
argued that the mobility of digital technology allowed for language 
learning practically everywhere. However, many studies referenced in

The GrunnDig report aligns with the perspective that success with 
digital technology in the classroom requires a digitally competent 
teacher and that digital competence develops over time (Krumsvik et al., 
2018). Digital skills are of no use to a student without the knowledge of 
how to use them and for what purpose. As such, it is critical to study how 
students use and are prompted to use digital skills in the classroom and 
to observe what types of digital skills are applied in the process. 
Although students may be familiar with digital technology and have 
experiences from their everyday life using a range of digital skills, the 
teacher is still responsible for showing students how to use these skills 
efficiently in a school context.
The present article adds to the existing literature by using an iden­
tical research design in revisiting the same schools over time and 
addressing the same age group and the same school subject longitudi­
nally. By observing how English teachers at the same schools provide 
digital opportunities over time, we hope to identify any changes in 
technology uptake and how teachers provide opportunities for the use of 
digital skills in the classroom context.
2.1. Theoretical framing: digital literacy
In this study, digital literacy is related to a sociocultural view of 
learning embedded in broader contextual practices (Vygotsky, 1978), 
including the use of digital skills as a social practice that informs stu­
dents’ involvement in digital literacy in the classroom. The Vygotskian 
framing addresses the social processes that unfold in the classroom 
among teachers and students (Ahmadian & Brevik, 2024). Building on 
Vygotsky’s 
(1978)
sociocultural 
theory 
and 
digital 
literacy 
(Buckingham, 2015) as complementary analytical lenses, this study 
considers how digital skills were used in naturally occurring English 
teaching.
Literacies emerging from digital technology are often categorised as 
digital literacies. Buckingham (2015) referred to digital media or other 
forms of digital technology when arguing that “education about the 
media should be seen as an indispensable prerequisite for education with 
or through the media” (p. 21). Likewise, he posited that teachers who 
want to use the internet or other digital media or technology to teach 
and communicate need to ensure that students understand and critique 
these rather than using computers or the internet in a merely functional 
way. Extrapolating from this understanding, digital literacy includes not 
only how to locate and retrieve information but also how to evaluate 
information and its representations of the world in relation to broader 
social, political and economic forces. In addition, digital literacy in­
volves transforming these aspects into knowledge.
Buckingham (2015) argued that teachers need to address a range of 
digital skills and competencies required by contemporary forms of dig­
ital communication rather than simply adding digital literacy to the 
curriculum or moving ICT into a separate school subject. Although 
digital literacy might not be the only relevant form of literacy when 
analysing students’ use of digital skills, it aligns with the complex uses of 
digital technology in the classroom (Blikstad-Balas, 2023). We examined 
how digital literacies might evolve within the classroom context where 
digital skills are applied. For example, the Norwegian curriculum 
identifies digital subskills, including use and understand, search and 
process, produce and revise, communicate and interact and digital re­
sponsibility, as skills need to be included in all subjects, as part of stu­
dents’ development of digital competence (NDET, 2017b).
3. The Norwegian context
Norwegians are widely exposed to technology in and out of school, 
and 98% of Norwegians have internet access compared with the global 
average of 67% (Internet World Statistics, 2022). Since 2006, the Nor­
wegian curriculum has established national, explicit ambitions for 
schools to develop students’ digital skills and competence (NDET, 2006; 
2017a; 2017b). Most classrooms are highly digitalised, in the sense that 
they have 1:1 computer access, high curricular ambitions for developing 
digital competencies and learning resources available through digital 
formats (e.g., learning platforms, collaborative platforms, digital 
textbooks).
The Norwegian educational context provides non-tracked and non- 
streamed mandatory K-13 education (ages 6–18) emphasising equal 
education for all students, regardless of language background or socio­
economic status. Classes typically comprise 20–30 students, with one 
main teacher for each subject. English is a mandatory common core 
subject throughout K-10 education and is allocated 222 teaching hours 
in lower secondary school (Years 8–10), which is the focus of the current 
article. Students have their final exams at the end of compulsory edu­
cation in Year 10. In addition, students have the right to another three 
years of voluntary education (Years 11–13). Teachers in Norway have 
high professional autonomy and the freedom to select their own 
teaching methodology. Digital skills are included as one of five basic 
skills in the Norwegian curriculum. This status entails that such skills 
should be developed within each subject, meaning that teachers are 
responsible for including digital skills whenever they are relevant to the 
lesson.
4. Methods
This study is part of the large-scale longitudinal project Linking In­
struction and Student Experiences (LISE), which was conducted to study 
teaching practices in seven Norwegian lower secondary schools in 
several school subjects, including English (Brevik, 2019; Brevik and 
Rindal, 2020). Building on naturalistic case study methodology, the 
main rationale for selecting this case was the need for information about 
the use of digital skills in English instruction over time. Research has 
shown that obtaining comparable data is crucial for being able to 
comment on development over time (Klette & Blikstad-Balas, 2018). The 
challenges of longitudinal studies are that they are often quite complex 
and resource demanding, but they may highlight tendencies and con­
nections that shorter studies may not provide. Based on the expressed 
concern related to the difficulty of comparing findings across studies and 
over time, due to different research designs (Klette & Blikstad-Balas, 
2018; Munthe et al., 2022), we decided to use a longitudinal case 
methodology, where we revisited the same schools over time.
4.1. Research design
We label this design longitudinal because it involves systematically 
recorded comparable video data of naturally occurring instruction 
across eight years (2015–23) and two curricular reforms. This design 
provides a unique opportunity to investigate how digital technology is 
used and how digital competence is taught and developed over time. The 
data were collected without any researcher intervention or manipula­
tion (Brevik, 2019; Brevik and Rindal, 2020). We informed the partici­
pating teachers that we were willing to collect data at any time during 
the school year, independent of the themes being covered or the 
teaching methods used, because we were most interested in their general 
teaching repertoires. Longitudinal research designs are used to ensure

The schools were strategically sampled for variations in geographic 
and demographic criteria across three school districts. The districts were 
characterised by medium to high socioeconomic status, language di­
versity and variation in student performance on national reading tests 
(Kure et al., 2023). For the first cohort (2015–17), one school withdrew 
from participation after the first year. For the second cohort (2019–20), 
we were unable to collect data at one school due to the Covid-19 global 
pandemic, leaving five participating schools. Among the schools with 
continued participation (2015–20), only one teacher had participated 
since the beginning. Such continuity is uncommon in the Norwegian 
school context, where teachers are regularly assigned to another class at 
school or change schools. This coincidence provided a unique oppor­
tunity to observe the same teacher’s digital practices over time. There­
fore, we decided to zoom in on this school as a case of an English 
teacher’s technology uptake over time (2019–23).
The sample for the present study thus comprised five schools and five 
English teachers for the second cohort (2019–20) and one school and 
teacher for the third cohort (2021–23). The teachers represented 
different genders, ages, levels of education in English teaching, and 
variations in teaching experience (see Table 1).
4.3. Video-recorded English lessons
Following the design established in our baseline study (Kure et al., 
2023), we video-recorded 4–5 consecutive English lessons in each 
classroom each school year, totalling 29 English lessons. As each lesson 
lasted between 45 and 70 min, depending on the school timetables, the 
video data amounted to 1622 min. The themes covered language, cul­
ture and literature in line with the national English curriculum in Nor­
way. Each classroom comprised between 23 and 30 students, which is 
typical in common core subjects, such as English, in Norwegian lower 
secondary schools.
Video data allow for systematic, comparative and longitudinal 
analysis (Rowan et al., 2020). Researchers can observe raw data mul­
tiple times, allowing for the reuse of already collected data for several 
studies (Blikstad-Balas, 2017; Klette, 2020). Using longitudinal 
video-recorded material supports detailed and systematic observation of 
digital classroom practices, enabling their accuracy to be confirmed by 
other researchers through an audit trail available for evaluation and 
confirmation (Klette, 2020; Nassaji, 2015). To our knowledge, this study 
is the first to use video data to systematically track the extent to which 
secondary school teachers provide opportunities for students to use 
digital skills in real classroom settings over time, contributing unique 
longitudinal data. To obtain high-quality video data on whole-class 
teaching and teacher–student interactions, we used two small cameras 
and two microphones simultaneously recording the same lessons from 
the front and back of the classroom, with one microphone on the teacher 
and one capturing students’ voices (Brevik, 2019; Brevik and Rindal, 
2020; Kure et al., 2023).
4.4. Digital skills framework as an analytic lens
We used the Framework for Digital Skills developed by the Norwegian 
Directorate for Education and Training (NDET, 2017b) as an observation 
protocol in our analyses. Several international frameworks for 
measuring digital competence exist, such as DigComp (Vuorikari et al., 
2022), the DigCompEdu framework (Redecker, 2017) and the ICILS 
framework (Hatlevik & Throndsen, 2015). In a comparison between the 
ICILS framework and the Norwegian Framework for Digital Skills (NDET, 
2012), Hatlevik and Throndsen (2015) found extensive thematic over­
lap, which justified the use of the most recent version of this framework 
(NDET, 2017b) as an observation protocol in our analysis. Moreover, the 
NDET framework addresses UNESCO’s (2022) emphasis on ensuring 
that young people have relevant digital skills and the ability to reflect on 
and conduct digital judgment. This emphasis also aligns with Bucking­
ham’s (2015) argument concerning a broad understanding of digital 
literacy, as outlined in the theory section. Our study is the second to use 
the framework to systematically record and analyse the opportunities 
provided for students’ use of digital skills in authentic teaching situa­
tions (Kure et al., 2023). In line with UNESCO’s (2022) emphasis on 
ensuring that individuals have relevant digital skills for an increasingly 
digital future, the framework defines digital skills as follows: 
Digital skills involve being able to find and process information, 
being creative and designing digital products using digital resources 
and communicating and interacting with other people in digital 
contexts. They involve being able to use digital resources efficiently 
and responsibly to solve practical tasks. Digital skills also include 
developing digital responsibility by acquiring knowledge and 
developing good strategies online. (NDET, 2017b, p. 3, our 
translation)
The framework operationalises students’ progression through five 
levels, including basic use (levels 1–2) and advanced use (levels 3–5), 
each representing five digital subskills: use and understand, search and 
process, produce and revise, communicate and interact and digital re­
sponsibility (see Fig. 2).
The video-recorded data were coded in line with the framework in 
Fig. 2 (NDET, 2017b). This framework involves multiple criteria for each 
digital subskill and further aligns with Buckingham’s (2015) definition 
of digital literacy (see the theory section). It is sufficient to observe one 
criterion to assign a level, but one or all digital subskills can be 
demonstrated simultaneously. When more than one level was observed 
at the same time, we chose the level for the more advanced skill (Kure

Use and understand captures students’ use and navigation of digital 
resources in and outside of networks to protect their information and 
data security. Digital resources include digital equipment, software and 
technology. The subskill also involves following digital requirements to 
emphasise and communicate messages using effects, pictures, sound, 
illustrations, tables, headings and points. A key consideration is the 
difference between seeing that students use a computer or search online 
without seeing the details of their use (level 1) and actually seeing that 
they navigate “different digital resources” (level 2) or “digital resources 
in networks” (level 3). Thus, level 1 was assigned when they lacked 
evidence of what they used the computers for or what kind of searches 
they conducted.
Search and process involves acquiring, processing, interpreting and 
evaluating information from digital sources and applying source criti­
cism and references. Information from digital sources includes text, 
sound, picture, video, symbols and interactive elements. The framework 
marks a conceptual difference between finding information (level 2) and 
evaluating information (level 3). Moreover, we might observe simple 
searches (level 1) while also observing more advanced searches through 
the teacher’s prompting, such as when a teacher explained how to limit 
searches to narrow findings (level 4).
Produce and revise captures creativity using digital resources. This 
subskill includes creating new digital products or developing and reus­
ing existing ones. A key consideration is the difference between creating 
“simple digital products” (level 1) and “various media types” (level 2).
Communicate and interact captures the use of digital resources for 
communication and interaction between students, teachers or external 
actors (e.g., online exchanges). Digital interaction also includes the use 
of digital resources with others in the classroom, for example, through 
co-writing or oral presentations. A key consideration is the divergence 
between adapting communication to the digital resource at hand (level 
3) and selecting a digital resource based on needs or audience (levels 
4–5).
Digital responsibility captures the ability to follow rules to protect 
privacy and use ethical conduct on the internet. It concerns the use of 
strategies to avoid unwanted situations and to demonstrate the ability to 
reflect ethically and evaluate one’s role on the internet and in social 
media. A key consideration is the difference between behaving respon­
sibly (levels 1–3) and reflecting on and managing online roles and 
identities (levels 4–5).

coding purposes (e.g., (Ahmadian and Brevik, 2024; Brevik, 2019; 
Tengberg et al., 2022), as they allow for finer-grained comparison than 
the lesson level, which may contain a range of different activities. As 
such, we eliminated segments where the teacher used ICT for pre­
sentations or where students had computers on their desks that were not 
in use.
4.5.2. Second stage of analysis: scoring ICT segments
In line with the framework (NDET, 2017b), Kure scored all ICT 
segments using the 5-point scale for the five digital subskills. Each 
segment was scored for levels 1–5 based on increasing evidence of each 
subskill, meaning that a subskill was assigned for each segment if there 
was visible evidence that one or more students demonstrated the skill. 
We wanted to systematically map (a) how frequently digital skills were 
applied in the lessons, (b) which digital subskills were applied in each 
segment and (c) on which level these subskills were applied. At the low 
end of the framework (levels 1–2), teachers provided opportunities for 
students to use basic digital skills, for example, by following instructions 
or making simple searches. At the high end (levels 3–4), students were 
expected to demonstrate more advanced digital skills. At level 5, 
teachers provided opportunities for students to evaluate or reflect on 
their use of ICT. This procedure enabled the systematic investigation of 
opportunities provided, prompting the use of digital skills within and 
across the video-recorded lesson segments.
4.5.3. Third stage of analysis: interrater agreement
To score reliably, Kure coded the entire material, and Blikstad-Balas 
double-coded 20%. The data manager for the project randomly selected 
lessons for double coding from the pool of ICT segments, ensuring a 
spread across classrooms, cohorts and years (see Kure et al., 2023). Their 
agreement percentage was high, with an exact interrater agreement of 
91.25% and reaching consensus on the remaining 8.75% after discussing 
disagreements. This way of validating the coding process when using 
pre-defined codes is in line with previous video studies (Ahmadian & 
Brevik, 2024; Klette & Blikstad-Balas, 2018; Tengberg et al., 2022).
4.6. Research ethics and limitations
The LISE project received approval from the Norwegian ethics 
committee before data collection and strictly followed all national 
guidelines for research ethics in all stages of the project, from obtaining 
consent to the safe storage of data. All teachers, students and parents of 
students under 15 years of age provided written voluntary and informed 
consent (National Committee for Research Ethics in the Social Sciences 
and Humanities, 2021) with the opportunity to opt out at any time 
without any consequences. As our sample was limited and very carefully 
selected (Brevik & Rindal, 2020), the study cannot be considered 
representative of English lessons in lower secondary school. Another 
limitation is that the process of filming, using the framework as an 
observation protocol and transcribing the observations involves 
complexity reduction (Blikstad-Balas, 2017). Video cameras do not 
capture everything on students’ screens unless verbally expressed, 
which means that we might have overlooked some students’ use of 
digital skills at a higher level than was evident in the recordings. 
However, since this study concerns the use of ICT and digital skills in 
naturally occurring instruction over time, the design is adequate and 
captures the digital skills operationalised in the framework (NDET, 
2017b). It also captures what digital skills are actually prompted, 
encouraged and discussed in classroom settings, which are the most 
relevant data to shed light on our research questions.
5. Findings
The English teachers in this study provided frequent opportunities 
for students to apply their digital skills during English instruction. The 
longitudinal design enabled a comparison over time, resulting in two 
main findings. First, when analysing all five schools (2019–20), we 
identified a digital divide. While teachers at three schools provided 
frequent opportunities to use digital subskills in complex ways, class­
room practices at the two remaining schools were characterised by 
missed opportunities, where teachers infrequently provided opportu­
nities to use one digital subskill only. Second, when following one of the 
schools for another three years (2021–23), we found increased oppor­
tunities to use digital skills but limited opportunities to develop digital 
skills beyond basic use.
5.1. Digital opportunities in five schools over time
This section presents the results from the first two phases of analysis 
to answer RQ1. Table 2 shows that among the 29 video-recorded English 
lessons, 24 involved ICT use.
Table 2 further shows that when dividing each lesson into 15-min 
segments for systematic comparison across schools, we identified 76 
segments in the 24 ICT lessons that contained ICT use (69%). These are 
the lessons and segments that we drew on in the analysis. In the 
following, we begin by delving into findings from all five schools 
(2019–20) before following one of the schools over time (2021–23).
5.2. A digital divide in digital opportunities (2019–20)
A main finding across the five schools was a digital divide. The 
teachers provided frequent opportunities to apply digital skills in three 
of the five schools (S17, S50, S51), whereas students were seldom 
offered opportunities to use digital technology in the two remaining 
schools (S07, S09).
5.2.1. Descriptive statistics of the observed ICT segments
Based on the teachers’ approaches to digital skills use, we labelled 
three schools high-frequency ICT schools and two schools low-frequency 
ICT schools. Although digital skills were prompted and used more flex­
ibly than such labels indicate, we scored considerably more use of digital 
skills in the former schools (86%) than in the latter ones (45%). Spe­
cifically, in the high-frequency ICT schools, we scored for the applica­
tion of digital skills in 75%–100% of lesson segments. In contrast, in the 
two low-frequency ICT schools, we scored digital skills in 35%–50% of 
the lesson segments (see Fig. 3).
Fig. 3 illustrates the scores for digital subskills for each school, where 
the two dominant subskills were use and understand and search and 
process. At the two low-frequency ICT schools, we identified only one of 
these, namely search and process in S07 and use and understand in S09. 
Conversely, at the three high-frequency ICT schools, we identified the 
use of four digital subskills in combination, with only minor differences 
in representation. Specifically, use and understand was scored most 
frequently (10–13 segments; n = 36), followed by search and process 
(8–11 segments; n = 29), produce and refine (9–10 segments; n = 29), and 
communicate and collaborate (6–10 segments; n = 23). Of note, no 
occurrence of digital responsibility was identified in any school.
When teachers provided opportunities for use and understand and 
search and process, these digital practices were likely due to students’ use 
of ICT to search and use information online. For example, students 
typically used their laptops to search for information in groups and use 
the information in oral presentations.
The two remaining subskills were scored in the high-frequency ICT 
schools. Communicate and collaborate typically occurred after in­
structions regarding sharing online information with the teacher or 
giving a digital presentation to the class (e.g., using PowerPoint or 
iMovie) or using their laptops for communicating and sharing notes with

5.4. Zooming in on a highly digitalised school longitudinally (2019–23)
In this section, we present the results from the first two phases of 
analysis to answer RQ2 by zooming in one school (S07). This school 
represented a case of an English teacher’s technology uptake over time 
involving two student cohorts (2019–23). While the school was highly 
digitalised, we found clear developments in ICT use over time.
When separating the case school (S07) from the other four schools, 
we found a markedly lower percentage of segments with ICT in 2019–20 
(35%) compared to the overall finding at all five schools (69%), as 
shown in Table 2. Furthermore, Fig. 3 illustrates that, based on the 
infrequent use of only one subskill in 2019–20, S07 was labelled a low- 
frequency ICT school, although at a highly digitalised school. Further­
more, S07 clearly changed to a high-frequency ICT school in 2022–23 
(89%). This development shows how the same teacher provided 
increased opportunities for ICT use over time (Fig. 5).
When considering the change from low-to high-frequency basic skills, 
we see that the main difference over time was the change from low-to 
high-frequency digital skills, as illustrated in Fig. 5. First, we observed 
that the teacher provided similar opportunities to use search and pro­
cessing skills in 2019–20 and 2021–22; however, he prompted the use of 
three additional basic subskills in 2021–22. This change from using one 
subskill to using four subskills in combination was repeated in the final 
year. Nonetheless, these increasingly more complex digital combina­
tions remained basic (levels 1–2), with one exception, where we 
observed more advanced use in the final year (level 3).
An explanation for scoring at the basic level could be that the teacher 
asked the students to combine digital and analogue methods at all three 
points in time. He prompted them to use pen and paper for writing, but 
to use the laptop for digital searches and dictionaries. For example, in 
2019–20, the students worked on the topic of South Africa, and the 
teacher instructed them to use their laptops for searches about Nelson 
Mandela and Biko (search and process) and to write their notes on paper. 
In 2021–22, the students wrote limericks using pens and notebooks 
while using their laptops to search for words using the rhyming dictio­
nary and thesaurus RhymeZone as instructed by their teacher. In 
2022–23, the teacher instructed the students to find a novel in the school 
library or an online version. The students used their laptops to write 
notes from their reading, and a few students did simple searches to find 
an interesting online novel.

6. Discussion
To achieve sustainable and lifelong learning, policymakers and 
educational institutions must be responsible for educating students to 
develop their digital skills and become digitally literate (Buckingham, 
2015; Butler et al., 2018; Organisation for Economic Co-operation and, 
Development., 2018). For the policies to be fulfilled as intended, one 
step is to develop teacher education and encourage more innovative use 
of digital skills that actually develop students’ digital competence to a 
larger degree than more traditional teaching method with or without 
technology (Munthe et al., 2022; OECD, 2020). In the present study, we 
investigated English teachers’ prompting of digital skills in five lower 
secondary schools over time.
Answering RQ1, the study revealed that, when revisiting the schools 
three years after our baseline study (Kure et al., 2023), we found a 
marked increase in the time and opportunities these English teachers 
provided for the use of digital skills in their classrooms. This distribution 
suggested almost twice as much ICT use in 2019–20 as in the baseline 
study (2015–17). Indeed, teachers at the same five schools provided 
increased opportunities to use digital skills over time and encouraged 
more advanced digital skills. At one school (S50), we observed no digital 
use in the first cohort (2015–17). In the second cohort (2019–20), it was 
labelled a high-frequency ICT school based on the teacher’s providing 
frequent opportunities to search and process in all segments, except one. 
Since the first cohort (2015–17), these three schools have demonstrated 
increasingly frequent combinations of digital subskills in the same ac­
tivities. This increase is an important change to note, as it suggests that 
the use of digital skills differs significantly across schools, as the themes, 
activities and methods vary greatly from school to school. Thus, the 
analyses clearly show the key role teachers play in developing students’ 
digital literacy skills and indicate that teachers who move beyond basic

skills do so purposefully and intentionally. Answering RQ2, the study 
found that when revisiting one of the five schools three years later, we 
observed not only increased use of digital skills over time but also more 
complex combinations of digital subskills in combination with analogue 
working methods. Still, we observed very few examples of advanced 
digital skills in the observed classrooms.
Across all schools and years, we observed increased frequencies of 
digital use, but the students were provided limited use for developing 
digital skills beyond basic use. Typically, students demonstrated simple 
use of digital technology by conducting simple searches on their com­
puters and sharing subject-related material on digital learning plat­
forms. Despite frequent use of technology, we also identified a striking 
lack of advanced use of digital skills, in line with the baseline study 
(Kure et al., 2023) and prior research, which indicated little or no 
development over time. Notably, we found that the English teachers did 
not focus on digital skills as an instructional aim or as something they 
should systematically develop or assess but merely as a means to reach 
subject-specific aims. This finding aligns with previous research from 
various subjects (Blikstad-Balas & Klette, 2020; Brevik and Davies, 
2016) and with tendencies in teacher education (Aagaard et al., 2024; 
Brevik et al., 2019). The students’ role when using technology was thus a 
rather passive role of consumers, as opposed to active agents who crit­
ically discuss their technological choices. Strictly speaking, digital 
technology was quite often used simply as a substitute for pen and paper, 
not leveraging the different affordances of the available technology. 
Interestingly, the same was found in Norway during the pandemic, 
where 100% digital schooling was characterised by individual tasks and 
traditional schooling, rather than the collaborative use of technology as 
a way to communicate (Blikstad-Balas et al., 2022).
Next, despite the predominance of basic digital skills, we observed 
examples of the advanced use of digital skills at some schools. These 
instances were always initiated by the teacher, resulting in what we 
called the digital divide. In these situations, we observed the use of 
digital tools for notetaking, modelled searches and the creation of an 
iMovie. In addition, we identified a few instances of more advanced 
levels of digital skills when teachers prompted or scaffolded students’ 
digital skills use, most often through dialogues and situations where 
digital skills were discussed or where teachers demonstrated how to 
effectively store digital work. Such circumstances provided opportu­
nities for students to reflect and more critically use digital skills at 
advanced levels, in line with prior research (Erstad & Voogt, 2018; 
Gudmundsdottir et al., 2020; Ottestad & Gudmundsdottir, 2018).
When teachers choose to include the use of ICT in the classroom, we 
should expect them to scaffold and prompt students to use more 
advanced levels of digital skills to a much higher degree than observed 
in our material. We observed instances of increased use of technology 
and digital skills over time, which may be explained as preparation for 
the final written exam. This exam is organised digitally in the English 
subject in Norway, demanding preparation and understanding of how to 
work effectively and accurately with digital technology. We observed an 
increased use of digital searches and teacher instructions to write digital 
notes and store these correctly, skills that might be useful for tests and 
exams the students will face at the end of K-10 education.
A further concern is the general lack of teachers prompting digital 
technology use to reach more advanced digital skills. This situation may 
result in increased individual differences as well as missed opportunities 
for students to develop critical thinking and reflection. This inequality 
may create social injustice and discriminate against students and their 
possibilities in future education and work. While we argue that oppor­
tunities to develop advanced digital skills are relatively infrequent in the 
present study, we found that the use of digital technology in the class­
room was strictly controlled by the teachers, allowing for little extra­
curricular use. Consequently, almost all observed practices of digital 
technology and use of digital skills were subject-relevant and time- 
limited. We also observed variations within classes between digital 
and analogue teaching methods, aligning with and adjusting to English 
subject themes and topics. We found complex combinations of different 
subskills in use at the same time, showing a varied use of digital skills as 
well as varied teaching methods in highly digitalised classrooms. These 
findings indicate that teachers decide upon tools and strategies ac­
cording to the topic and the need for variation in the classroom.
However, despite it being more or less expected to find an increase in 
the use of digital technology in the classrooms over time, it was con­
cerning to observe that the level of digital skills (i.e. basic vs. advanced) 
had not increased proportionally with the use. TALIS 2018 argued that it 
is important that teachers have a clear pedagogical intention when 
including digital technology in their teaching (OECD, 2020). Taking into 
consideration that digital skills have been part of the national curricu­
lum since 2006 and emphasised since 2020 and that students in Norway 
are taught digital skills from Year 1, a challenge with these findings is 
that students may not reach the aims described by the end of K-10 ed­
ucation (NDET, 2017b). These challenges need to be confronted, as 
teachers and school leaders have had several years to develop and refine 
digital aspects of their teaching. There can be no development in stu­
dents’ use of digital skills unless teachers facilitate it.
Previous research has explored the responsibility and expectations 
put on the educational sector to facilitate advanced digital skills among 
students through educational policies and frameworks, as well as 1:1 
classroom access to digital technology (Erstad & Voogt, 2018; Ottestad 
& Gudmundsdottir, 2018; UNESCO, 2017, 2022). However, good access 
and thorough curricular descriptions do not automatically result in an 
increase in advanced digital skills among students or the prioritisation of 
opportunities to use advanced digital competence among teachers 
(NDET, 2017a; 2017b). As such, it is concerning that the students in the 
observed classrooms were not provided opportunities to use the neces­
sary digital skills to become 21st century citizens; subsequently, we 
want to highlight two important findings for discussion.
The present study adds to the existing literature on the use of tech­
nology and digital skills in various ways. Using the framework provided 
by the Norwegian Directorate for Education and Training (NDET, 
2017b) facilitated the analysis of how students in secondary schools use 
digital skills through teacher prompting and instructions. Obviously, 
using this framework has some limitations (NDET, 2017b). The absence 
of certain subskills or levels in classroom observations raises the ques­
tion of whether this framework is descriptive enough to capture 
advanced approaches to the use of digital skills during classroom 
teaching. However, we see it as a valid observation protocol, as it is an 
existing assessment tool used by Norwegian teachers. Accordingly, the 
framework connects this study to the practice field, making it relevant 
for the alignment of policies, from policymakers to school leaders, 
through teachers and into classroom practice (Butler et al., 2018).
An avenue for further research could be to question whether obser­
vation through video recording of naturally occurring classroom 
teaching is adequate to capture more advanced uses of digital skills. We 
call for research that might elaborate on more detailed methods of 
zooming in on students’ laptops for this type of research, such as screen 
recordings or other ways of capturing details in individual uses of digital 
technology that need not rely on such uses being verbally expressed. We 
also believe there is a need for more studies connecting measurement of 
students’ digital competence with actual classroom teaching.
