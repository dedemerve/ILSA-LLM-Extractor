This study investigated the dual role of standardized testing as an informational and coercive policy tool in global
education governance. Using data from 65 PISA 2022 economies and three-level hierarchical linear modeling, we
examined how test frequency (informational) and test-based accountability (coercive) independently and
interactively shaped students’ mathematics achievement. Results revealed that frequent testing was positively
associated with performance, particularly benefiting low socioeconomic-status (SES) students. Conversely, highstakes test-based accountability was linked to lower achievement, especially among high-SES students. These
patterns remain consistent across high- and low-SES economies, suggesting that policy design contributes substantively to educational outcomes beyond national wealth. The findings highlight the necessity of distinguishing
formative from coercive uses of assessment. We argue for context-sensitive policies that leverage testing as
diagnostic feedback to support learning and equity, while cautioning against high-stakes accountability practices
that may undermine educational quality. This distinction is vital for balancing information and accountability in
global assessment systems.

external pressure and sanctions (Balzaretti & Triventi, 2025; Howlett &

accountability, three critical gaps remain.

Standardized testing has become a defining feature of contemporary
education systems, profoundly shaping how learning outcomes are
measured, compared, and governed. Policymakers across the world,
from long-established systems such as the United Kingdom and the
United States to rapidly developing economies including Singapore,
Japan, China, and South Korea, have adopted standardized assessments
to monitor quality, promote accountability, and track equity (e.g.,
Campos et al., 2025). Historically, examinations have been used to drive
school improvement, ensure fairness in educational opportunity, and
support human capital development as societies sought to maintain
global competitiveness.
Yet standardized testing remains deeply contested. Critics argue that
the global expansion of testing has produced an “audit culture” that
restricts teachers’ professional autonomy, narrows curricular experiences, and reproduces inequalities (Koretz, 2018; McNeil, 2002).

2. Theoretical framework and literature review
2.1. Policy instrument theory
Policy instrument theory provides a foundational framework for
analyzing how governments employ tools to achieve public objectives.
Hood’s (1983) NATO model, Nodality, Authority, Treasure, and Organization, offers a classic categorization of instruments according to their
informational, regulatory, financial, and organizational properties. This
framework emphasizes that instruments are not merely technical devices but carry embedded assumptions about governance, steering
mechanisms, and behavior change. Later developments by Howlett and
Ramesh (2006) refined this perspective by distinguishing between
voluntary (information-based), mixed (incentive-based), and coercive
(authority-based) instruments, illustrating that instruments vary in their
reliance on persuasion versus compulsion.
In education governance, policy instruments often operate simultaneously at multiple levels (classroom, school, and system) and differ in
their intended logic of action. Informational instruments seek to shape
behavior by altering knowledge and perceptions, whereas coercive instruments depend on rules, sanctions, and external pressures
(Lascoumes & Le Galès, 2007). Importantly, instrument choice is
consequential: studies show that informational tools tend to promote
professional learning cultures, while coercive tools more often trigger
compliance-oriented behaviors (Fullan, 2011). Applying this framework
to standardized testing clarifies why assessments can serve distinct
functions and produce divergent outcomes depending on whether they
are used primarily as feedback mechanisms or accountability levers.
Despite its utility, policy instrument theory has rarely been applied
explicitly to large-scale assessment systems. Existing research often
treats “standardized testing policies” as a singular category rather than
disaggregating informational and coercive components. A policy instrument perspective thus enables a more nuanced conceptualization of
how testing operates within broader governance arrangements and why
its impacts may vary across contexts.

2.3. Coercive policy tool: Test-based accountability
Coercive tools operate through authority, sanctions, and compliance
mechanisms. Test-based accountability is a paradigmatic coercive instrument because it links student performance to external consequences
such as school rankings, closure risks, or teacher evaluations (Hanushek
& Raymond, 2005; Schatz-Laliberté & Lüftenegger, 2025; Torres, 2021).
The underlying theory of action assumes that high-stakes incentives
motivate schools and teachers to improve instruction and efficiency.
At the same time, test-based accountability creates substantial risks.
Numerous studies document narrowing of curriculum, teaching to the
test, strategic exclusion of low-performing students, and manipulation of
test data (Figlio & Loeb, 2011; Jennings & Rentner, 2006; Koretz, 2017;
Schatz-Laliberté & Lüftenegger, 2025). Teachers may experience
reduced professional autonomy, lower morale, and increased burnout
(Ravitch, 2010). Accountability pressure can discourage deep learning
by incentivizing rapid gains in testable skills rather than sustained
conceptual understanding (Supovitz, 2009).
These mixed outcomes indicate that accountability’s effects depend
on institutional capacity, school resources, and the fairness of the
accountability design. Recent longitudinal evidence further underscores
the importance of disaggregating these dimensions. Vigna et al. (2026)

2.5.2. Equity effects of informational tools
Informational tools may support disadvantaged students by
providing frequent feedback, early identification of learning gaps, and
structured opportunities for intervention (Barana & Marchisio, 2023).
Low-stakes, formative use of assessments can help learners track progress, build self-regulatory skills, and maintain motivation. However,
frequent summative assessments may impose stress and anxiety,
disproportionately affecting students with fewer support resources
(Rahal et al., 2023). High-SES students often possess stronger test-taking
skills, greater access to tutoring, and more parental support, enabling
them to capitalize on regular testing and potentially widen gaps.
Thus, the equity implications of test frequency depend on whether
testing practices emphasize diagnosis or punishment. Cross-national
evidence on these dynamics remains sparse, highlighting the need for
comparative analyses that distinguish between informational and coercive uses of testing.

2.4. Standardized testing and academic achievement
Research suggests that informational and coercive tools shape
achievement through qualitatively different mechanisms. Informational
tools support learning by enhancing feedback, self-regulation, and
instructional responsiveness, whereas coercive tools rely on external
incentives and regulatory compliance. Although both tools influence
behavior, informational mechanisms tend to be more aligned with sustained learning processes, while coercive mechanisms may yield shortterm gains but undermine long-term instructional quality.
Cross-national evidence is limited and often inconsistent. Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD, 2017) reports
that students in systems with frequent low-stakes assessments tend to
perform better. Yet studies controlling for socioeconomic and institutional characteristics find weaker or null effects (Thomsen et al., 2022).
Coercive systems may initially raise performance but risk plateauing or
reversing due to stress, gaming behaviors, or narrowed curricula
(Koretz, 2018). Little empirical work tests these mechanisms simultaneously across countries—a gap this study addresses.
Additional literature indicates that the formative use of assessment
has one of the highest effect sizes in meta-analyses of educational interventions (Hattie & Timperley, 2007), whereas high-stakes testing
environments often produce volatile and context-dependent results
(Nichols & Berliner, 2007). This reinforces the conceptual differentiation between informational and coercive tools and motivates a multilevel comparative approach.

2.6. Integrating informational and coercive tools: Complementarity or
conflict?
Educational systems rarely rely solely on informational or coercive
instruments; instead, they combine tools in complex ways. Informational tools generate data, while coercive tools determine how that data
is used. In theory, constructive integration can enhance coherence:
frequent assessments provide rich information, accountability structures
set expectations, and together they may motivate both professional
learning and improved outcomes.
However, empirical evidence raises essential concerns. Case-level
research has documented these dynamics in detail. Klenowski (2011)
showed that in Queensland, Australia, the implementation of accountability mandates progressively constrained teachers’ capacity to use
assessment for formative purposes, illustrating how coercive policy
logics can override the informational potential of the same assessment
instruments. When coercive pressure dominates, the instructional value
of assessment is often undermined. Teachers may focus on test-specific
skills rather than on using assessment data to inform pedagogical
improvement (Schildkamp et al., 2020). Excessive accountability can
suppress experimentation and formative uses of assessment, turning
testing into a compliance-driven exercise. Conversely, weak accountability may lead to underutilization of test information, reducing incentives for data use and weakening system responsiveness (Balzaretti &
Triventi, 2025).
Scholars increasingly argue that alignment between assessment
purposes, feedback cultures, and accountability structures is essential
for system effectiveness (Hopfenbeck et al., 2018). Yet comparative
evidence examining how informational and coercive tools interact
across national contexts remains limited. Understanding whether these
tools function synergistically or antagonistically is crucial for designing
balanced assessment policies.

2.5. Standardized testing and educational equity
Educational equity is central to debates about standardized testing
because assessment policies can either ameliorate or exacerbate existing
socioeconomic disparities. Testing systems may redistribute resources
and attention toward underserved groups, yet accountability pressures
often disproportionately burden disadvantaged schools lacking organizational capacity.
2.5.1. Equity effects of coercive accountability
Evidence from the United States demonstrates that accountability
reforms sometimes widened achievement gaps. High-SES families
leveraged information to exercise school choice or relocate to higherperforming districts (Bifulco & Ladd, 2007; Hanushek & Raymond,
2005), while lower-performing students were more likely to be downgraded or deprioritized under pressure (Jacob & Levitt, 2003). These
dynamics intensified stratification and undermined the equity intentions
of NCLB.
Yet accountability can reduce disparities when implemented with
targeted support. Studies show that low-performing schools receiving
additional resources under accountability mandates experienced
meaningful gains, particularly among Hispanic, African American, and
low-SES students (Dee & Jacob, 2011; Jacob, 2005; Reback, 2008). International evidence is similarly mixed. External exit exams appear to
improve overall system quality but may widen variability by advantaging high achievers (Braga et al., 2013; Woessmann, 2005). These
contradictory patterns underscore the importance of contextualizing
accountability effects within broader institutional settings.

2.7. The present study
Building on the above literature, the present study applied policy
instrument theory to differentiate standardized testing into informational (test frequency) and coercive (test-based accountability) tools.
Using PISA 2022 data from 65 economies and multilevel modeling, this
research investigated:
(a) How national education systems vary in their use of test frequency and test-based accountability;
(b) How each tool independently influenced mathematics achievement; and
(c) Whether these effects differed across socioeconomic contexts at
the student, school, and country levels.
By integrating conceptual clarity with cross-national empirical

3.1. Data sources and participants

• Student level (Level 1): gender, socioeconomic status (ESCS), and
immigrant background.
• School level (Level 2): average school ESCS, student–teacher ratio,
and school type (public vs. private).
• Economy level (Level 3): gross domestic product (GDP) per capita.

This study drew on data from the 2022 cycle of the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA), conducted by OECD. PISA 2022
assessed the mathematics, reading, and science literacy of 15-year-old
students and collected extensive contextual information on family
background, school environments, teaching resources, learning climate,
and non-cognitive characteristics through coordinated questionnaires
administered to students, school principals, teachers, and parents.
Sampling employed a probability-proportional-to-size (PPS) design,
yielding nationally representative student samples nested within
selected schools. Of the 81 participating economies, those lacking valid
information on test frequency or accountability were excluded. The final
analytic sample consisted of 65 economies, 17,783 schools, and 479,178
students. Students (Level 1) were nested within schools (Level 2), which
were further nested within economies (Level 3), allowing for hierarchical modeling that accounted for both individual-level factors and
systemic influences.

To appropriately separate within- and between-group variation,
continuous student- and school-level variables were group-mean
centered, and economy-level predictors were grand-mean centered. At
Level 1, continuous student-level predictors (ESCS) were centered
around the respective school mean (group-mean centering), so that the
Level-1 coefficients captured within-school effects net of school
composition. At Level 2, continuous school-level predictors (school
average ESCS and student–teacher ratio) were centered around the
respective economy mean, thereby isolating within-economy, betweenschool effects. At Level 3, continuous economy-level predictors (GDP,
test-based accountability, and test frequency) were grand-mean
centered. This centering strategy ensured an unambiguous decomposition of variance across levels: student-level coefficients reflected individual deviations from school contexts, school-level coefficients
reflected institutional deviations from national norms, and economylevel coefficients captured cross-national differences in policy environments and economic conditions. Categorical variables (gender, immigration status, and school type) were left uncentered and entered as
dummy-coded indicators. Table 1 summarizes variable definitions and
scales, and Table 2 reports descriptive statistics.

3.2. Measurement
3.2.1. Dependent variable
The dependent variable was mathematics achievement. PISA provides ten plausible values (PVs) for each achievement domain to account
for measurement and sampling uncertainty in large-scale assessments.
Following OECD guidelines and the recommendations of Sebastian and
Huang (2016), all ten plausible values were included to obtain unbiased
estimates of population parameters and accurate standard errors.

3.3.1. Robustness checks
To assess the sensitivity of the main findings, two sets of robustness
checks were conducted. First, mathematics self-efficacy (MATHEFF) was
added as a Level 1 control to examine whether the effects of test frequency and accountability persisted after accounting for individual
differences in non-cognitive characteristics.
Second, the Gini coefficient for income inequality (sourced from the
World Bank for the year 2022 or the nearest available year) was introduced alongside GDP per capita at Level 3 to capture within-country
distributional characteristics that GDP alone may not reflect. Because
Gini data were not available for all 65 economies, this specification was
estimated on a reduced sample. To disentangle potential confounding by
the Gini coefficient from effects of sample attrition, an additional model
was estimated on the same restricted sample without the Gini
coefficient.

5.1. Summary and interpretation of findings
The present study examined the dual nature of standardized testing
as a policy instrument by distinguishing its informational and coercive
functions and assessing their respective effects on student achievement
and equity. Using data from 65 economies participating in PISA 2022, a
three-level hierarchical linear model was applied to investigate how test
frequency and test-based accountability independently and interactively

contrast, test-based accountability, representing a coercive mechanism,
was negatively related to performance and exerted the strongest adverse
effects in more affluent schools and student groups. This pattern resonates with prior findings that coercive instruments tend to trigger
compliance-oriented rather than learning-oriented behaviors (Fullan,
2011), and with documented risks of curriculum narrowing and teaching to the test under high-stakes regimes (Koretz, 2018; Figlio & Loeb,

on policy design, governance culture, and implementation practices
than on economic development per se (Hopfenbeck et al., 2018).
These findings confirmed our speculations that standardized testing
operates as a double-edged policy instrument, consistent with the
complementarity-conflict framework outlined in Section 2.6. Specifically, on one side, informational tools can enhance feedback, stimulate
instructional adjustments, and help narrow equity gaps by supporting
students and schools with fewer resources—outcomes consistent with
the high effect sizes reported for formative feedback in meta-analytic
research (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). Scotland’s assessment framework, for example, prioritized diagnostic feedback over high-stakes
accountability, enabling teachers to use assessment data constructively
to support learning (Hutchinson & Young, 2011).
On the other side, coercive accountability pressures may erode
instructional quality, constrain creativity, and intensify stress (Nichols &
Berliner, 2007; Ravitch, 2010), particularly when external evaluation
dominates the educational process and when stakes are high. The
absence of significant interaction effects between the two policy tools
suggested that accountability and frequency exert largely independent
influences, indicating neither the synergistic enhancement nor the
antagonistic undermining hypothesized in some theoretical accounts.
This independence underscores the importance of deliberate policy
design that encourages complementarity rather than allowing coercive
elements to overshadow the developmental purposes of assessment.

partially substitute for unequal out-of-school supports by strengthening
feedback and monitoring functions within the formal system.

A central contribution of this study is to show that, at the crossnational level, higher test frequency—conceptualized as an

compatible with a "capacity-building through information" account.
At the same time, we emphasize that test frequency is not inherently
equitable. As noted above, the PISA measure captures the structural
regularity of standardized testing, not whether results are embedded in
formative use through assessment literacy, targeted feedback, and
responsive instruction. If tests are administered in ways that heighten
stakes and anxiety and are disconnected from instructional adjustment,
frequent testing may yield limited learning benefits (Von der Embse

future studies should connect system-level testing regimes to
within-country evidence on classroom and school enactment, thereby
testing the mechanisms that our cross-national associations suggest.

5.4. Differentiated policy pathways across economic contexts
While national GDP was positively associated with mathematics
achievement, it did not significantly moderate the effects of testing. This
pattern indicates that economic development alone is not a decisive
determinant of policy effectiveness. Countries with similar GDP levels
often differ markedly in how testing is implemented, for example, in the
stakes attached to tests, the frequency of administration, the transparency of results, and the extent to which assessment data are integrated into teaching and learning.
These findings suggest that institutional design, governance culture,
and educational traditions play more decisive roles than economic
wealth per se. Systems with coherent governance structures, strong
cultures of data use, and established mechanisms for professional
learning are better positioned to convert test information into meaningful improvement, regardless of their GDP level. In such contexts,
assessment data are more likely to be interpreted collaboratively, used
diagnostically, and linked to supportive interventions rather than to
punitive sanctions.
Conversely, even affluent systems may struggle to use testing effectively when accountability is primarily punitive or when data are poorly
integrated into pedagogical practice. High levels of economic development do not automatically translate into effective policy design or
implementation. In some cases, resources may be channeled into
expanding testing without sufficient investment in teacher capacity,
data literacy, or student support services. Under these conditions, testing
can exacerbate stress and inequality rather than enhance learning.
Thus, the future of educational evaluation lies less in replicating
uniform “global models” of testing and more in developing contextsensitive, adaptive designs that reflect national priorities, institutional
capacities, and cultural values. Policy borrowing without careful adaptation may prove counterproductive. Instead, policymakers should
consider how assessment instruments, feedback mechanisms, and
accountability structures interact with local conditions and governance
arrangements to shape educational outcomes. Future research would
benefit from stronger contextualization of system-level testing regimes
by linking them to curricular documents, governance arrangements, and
broader education and economic policy goals. In cross-national work,
similar instrument “labels” (e.g., frequent testing or accountability) may
correspond to different curricular emphases and implementation logics
across systems. The case of Kazakhstan exemplifies how testing policies
interact with broader curricular and institutional reforms in complex
ways. Kazakhstan has undergone substantial education reforms over the
past two decades, driven in part by engagement with OECD reviews and
international assessment programs (OECD, 2014). Its PISA scores have
fluctuated and remained substantially below the OECD average, yet
showed notable improvement in science in 2022, alongside stability in
mathematics and reading (OECD, 2023). These performance trends
cannot be attributed solely to testing and accountability policies; they
must be understood within the context of Kazakhstan’s broader curricular transformation (Boman & Mussakhanova, 2024). Such dynamics
are likely present in many of the transition and emerging economies in
our sample, underscoring the importance of context-sensitive interpretation of cross-national patterns.

5.3. Coercive accountability and its pressure effects in affluent contexts
In contrast to the benefits of frequent testing, test-based accountability was found to have more substantial adverse effects on high-SES
students and schools. This “pressure effect” challenges the assumption
that accountability uniformly improves educational quality across contexts. Instead, the findings suggest that in high-resource settings, where
baseline achievement and expectations are already high, additional
external accountability tends to amplify competition and anxiety, ultimately contributing to reduced performance.
The cross-level interaction between economy-level accountability
and student-level SES indicates that in systems with stronger test-based
accountability, the association between student SES and achievement is
weaker (i.e., the SES–achievement gradient is flatter). One possible
interpretation is that, in advantaged contexts where achievementoriented norms are already salient, additional accountability pressure
may reinforce a performance-focused climate. Prior within-country
research links such conditions to higher test anxiety (Von der Embse
et al., 2018), greater emphasis on test performance at the expense of
deeper learning (Nichols & Berliner, 2007), and motivational shifts from
mastery orientation toward performance avoidance—dynamics that
align with the theoretical prediction that coercive instruments produce
compliance-driven rather than learning-oriented behavioral responses
(Fullan, 2011; Koretz, 2017).
The cross-level interaction between economy-level accountability
and school-level ESCS suggests that in systems with stronger test-based
accountability, the achievement advantage typically associated with
attending a higher-SES school is attenuated. One plausible interpretation is that, in high-capacity schools where internal evaluation systems
and professional cultures are already relatively well developed, additional external accountability may provide limited incremental informational value while increasing compliance pressures. Research on
accountability regimes documents that such conditions are often associated with curriculum narrowing toward tested domains (Jennings &
Rentner, 2006; Supovitz, 2009), which may constrain time for
inquiry-based, project-based, or interdisciplinary learning experiences.
Moreover, sustained accountability pressure has been linked to teacher
stress, burnout, and demoralization (Ravitch, 2010), creating a cycle of
overregulation and diminished morale that weakens schools’ capacity
for continuous improvement.
Taken together, these findings demonstrate that coercive accountability does not operate as a one-size-fits-all solution, consistent with the
broader policy instrument literature emphasizing that instrument effects
are mediated by institutional context and baseline capacity (Lascoumes
& Le Galès, 2007). In resource-rich settings, excessive external monitoring may crowd out intrinsic motivation and professional agency,
resulting in diminishing or even negative returns. For such contexts,
more nuanced, trust-based, and collaborative approaches to evaluation
may be preferable.

5.5. Policy implications
From a design and implementation perspective, the findings yield
several implications for policymakers and education leaders:
1. Calibrate accountability intensity. Excessive accountability may
narrow teaching goals and distort learning priorities. Governments
should strive to balance external evaluation with professional trust,
ensuring that accountability mechanisms emphasize support and
capacity building as much as control. Linking test data to formative
improvement, such as targeted support, school development
11

Y. Cui et al.

planning, and professional learning, rather than to punitive ranking
or sanctions, is crucial.
2. Redefine the role of testing frequency. Frequent testing appears
beneficial when it is used as a formative feedback mechanism rather
than as repeated high-stakes evaluation. Policymakers should
encourage the use of diagnostic, low-stakes assessments that are
closely integrated with instruction and curriculum. Test results
should inform pedagogical adjustments, differentiation, and school
improvement planning rather than being used primarily as a basis for
bureaucratic comparison or competitive league tables.
3. Promote differentiated policy strategies. The heterogeneous effects across socioeconomic groups underscore the need for contextsensitive policies.

which preclude strong causal inference. Future research should make
use of longitudinal or repeated cross-sectional data across multiple PISA

policy effects unfold over time. Quasi-experimental approaches, such as
difference-in-differences designs, regression discontinuity, or instrumental variable techniques, could further strengthen causal identification and provide more robust evidence on the impact of policy changes.
Moreover, the study relied on quantitative indicators of testing and
accountability derived from standardized questionnaires and aggregated indices. Because test frequency and accountability measures were
aggregated to the economy level, the findings primarily reflect macrolevel policy patterns and do not capture the considerable variation

enacted. For instance, schools within the same national system may
differ in the extent to which standardized assessments are used for
formative versus summative purposes, and teachers may vary in how
they administer assessments, interpret results, and integrate assessment
information into instruction. The present design does not allow us to
distinguish these school- and classroom-level differences; accordingly,
the results should be interpreted as characterizing broad system-level
tendencies rather than specific institutional or pedagogical practices.
Future research should therefore examine the extent to which teachers
implement testing policies in practice and how such implementation
varies across and within schools, and should test whether implementation indicators (e.g., assessment literacy, feedback practices, instructional adjustments, and data-use routines) mediate or condition the links
between system-level regimes and student outcomes. Complementary
qualitative or mixed-methods approaches, such as comparative case
studies, interviews with policymakers and practitioners, classroom observations, teacher surveys on assessment use, and policy process analyses, would help illuminate how different systems operationalize
assessment instruments at the school and classroom levels and how local
actors mediate their effects.
Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge the epistemological and
political dimensions of PISA as a data source. As an OECD-led assessment, PISA is embedded within particular assumptions about the purposes of education and the value of standardized measurement (Zhao,
2020; Sellar & Lingard, 2014). PISA and the OECD’s broader testing
regime increasingly exemplify a decontextualized "science of solutions"
whose legitimacy and effectiveness are being questioned(Sousa et al.,
2026). PISA’s definition of mathematical literacy, its item design, and its
contextual questionnaires reflect choices that privilege certain conceptions of knowledge and competence over others. The accountability and
frequency indicators used in this study, while derived from PISA’s school
questionnaire, are themselves products of PISA’s framework and may
not fully capture the complexity of how testing and accountability are
experienced in diverse institutional and cultural settings. Future
research should complement large-scale quantitative analyses with
qualitative investigations that interrogate how different education systems operationalize these constructs in practice and how local actors
mediate the effects of assessment policies (Gorur, 2016; Auld & Morris,
2016). A related limitation concerns the interpretation of the ESCS index
used as the primary measure of socioeconomic status. Although ESCS is
the standard SES measure in PISA-based research and captures meaningful variation in educational resources and opportunities, it is a
composite of parental education, occupational status, and home possessions—components that may partly reflect intergenerational transmission of cognitive ability rather than purely exogenous socioeconomic
conditions (Avvisati, 2020; Marks & O’Connell, 2021). Psychometric
research using bifactor models has shown that a substantial share of the
variance in PISA item responses is attributable to a general cognitive
factor rather than to domain-specific competencies alone (Pokropek
et al., 2022), raising questions about the extent to which PISA scores
reflect curriculum-based learning versus general cognitive ability.
Furthermore, studies controlling for parental cognitive ability find that
the SES–achievement association is substantially attenuated (O’Connell

o In low-resource contexts, frequent and well-designed diagnostic
testing can strengthen both quality and equity by providing essential

o In high-resource contexts, reducing external pressure and
expanding innovative, multidimensional assessments such as

support holistic learning, creativity, and well-being.
4. Institutionalize adaptive monitoring systems. Education reforms
should build multi-level evaluation structures that systematically use
ESCS-disaggregated data to track the equity effects of testing policies. Adaptive monitoring enables midcourse corrections when unintended consequences emerge, ensuring that policy effects remain
aligned with national educational objectives and principles of
fairness.
5. Strengthen capacity for data-informed improvement. Investing
in teacher professional development, assessment literacy, and data
interpretation skills is essential to transforming test results into
actionable insights. When educators understand how to analyze and
use data, standardized testing can become a means of “learning
about learning” rather than merely a mechanism for surveillance. In
this sense, testing should be regarded as a resource for professional
reflection and instructional refinement, rather than simply a yardstick of performance.
6. Precision and adaptiveness.
From a global perspective, education evaluation reforms should be
guided by precision and adaptiveness.
For countries with relatively low levels of economic development, priority should be placed on foundational capacity-building:
investing in teacher professional development, strengthening assessment infrastructure, and designing developmentally appropriate diagnostic tests to stimulate gradual improvements in teaching quality and
learning outcomes. Moderately frequent, well-designed assessments can
catalyze system improvement without imposing counterproductive
pressure.
For countries with high GDP levels, the challenge is different: they
must guard against resource waste created by redundant accountability
mechanisms and inefficient supervisory structures. Overemphasis on
test scores and rankings can crowd out broader educational aims. These
systems should explore holistic evaluation approaches that move
beyond traditional exams and better reflect students’ comprehensive
competencies, long-term development, and well-being.
Together, these insights promote a shift from accountability-driven
reform to learning-oriented governance, in which assessment serves as
a mechanism for reflection, developmental feedback, and equity
enhancement rather than as a driver of compliance.
5.6. Limitations and future research
Despite its large-scale, cross-national scope, this study has several
limitations. The analyses were based on cross-sectional PISA 2022 data,

gradients observed in PISA may partly reflect cognitive-hereditary

discussion. These considerations do not invalidate the use of ESCS in
cross-national secondary analyses, but they call for caution when
interpreting the cross-level interactions (e.g., accountability × ESCS) as
operating exclusively through socioeconomic resource mechanisms.
Future research incorporating direct measures of cognitive ability
alongside SES indicators would help disentangle these pathways.
Future research could also examine the interactions between testing
policies and other dimensions of schooling, including teacher collaboration, leadership practices, student well-being, and classroom innovation. Such studies would contribute to a more comprehensive
understanding of assessment-driven reform by situating testing within
the broader ecology of school improvement efforts, rather than treating
it as an isolated policy intervention. Additionally, future research would
benefit from examining how testing and accountability policies relate to
the content and structure of national curricula. Curricular documents
embody educational priorities shaped by broader economic, cultural,
and political goals, and the effectiveness of testing tools likely depends
on the degree of alignment between assessments and curricular objectives (Boman & Mussakhanova, 2024). Cross-national analyses that
integrate curricular analysis with assessment policy data could provide
deeper insight into why similar testing configurations produce divergent
outcomes across countries, particularly in transitional economies where
education systems are undergoing rapid and multidirectional transformation (OECD, 2020).

6. Conclusions
Grounded in policy instrument theory, this study provides a nuanced
understanding of standardized testing as both a tool and a constraint in
educational governance. Using cross-national data and a three-level
analytical framework, it shows that frequent, formative testing can
enhance learning and equity. In contrast, coercive, high-stakes
accountability often suppresses achievement and exacerbates
inequality. These findings call for recalibrating global educational
evaluation systems toward formative, diagnostic, and capacity-building
approaches that prioritize the intelligent use of information over
enforcement and sanctions.
In summary, standardized testing remains a defining feature of
global education governance, but its effects are not determined solely by
test frequency or stakes. Instead, effectiveness depends on purpose,
design, and context. When standardized testing is conceived and
implemented as an instrument for learning enhancement, rather than as
a mechanism for compliance enforcement, education systems are more
likely to develop policies that simultaneously advance quality, equity,
and human flourishing.
