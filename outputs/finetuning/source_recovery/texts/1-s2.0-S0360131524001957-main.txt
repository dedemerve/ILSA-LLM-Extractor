Development of a measurement instrument

A B S T R A C T
Computational thinking has become an integral component of curricula worldwide, necessitating 
teachers to develop this competence in their students. To effectively meet these curricular re­
quirements, teachers themselves need a solid foundation of computational thinking content 
knowledge, which refers to the understanding and skills they possess in this area. However, 
despite widespread recognition of this need, few studies have rigorously examined teachers’ 
content knowledge in this domain. Addressing this gap requires the development of high-quality 
measurement tools. This study details the development of an instrument, created as part of the 
International Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS) 2023 in Flanders, to measure 
lower secondary school teachers’ computational thinking content knowledge in a valid and 
reliable way. The article first outlines the construction process of the instrument, which involved 
close collaboration with experts in the field and drew upon the framework of Fraillon et al. 
(2023). Following this, the instrument’s psychometric properties are presented, which include 
both item-level and overall instrument characteristics. These properties were evaluated using 
data from a sample of 352 participants, applying both Classical Test Theory and Item Response 
Theory. The final tool consists of sixteen multiple-choice and short constructed response ques­
tions. The results indicate favorable item and overall instrument characteristics, thereby affirming 
its potential to measure the intended construct in a valid and accurate way.

2. Theoretical background
2.1. Content knowledge
2.1.1. The construct of content knowledge
Professional knowledge is at the core of teachers’ competence (Baumert & Kunter, 2013b). The three widely accepted dimensions 
of this knowledge are content knowledge, pedagogical knowledge and pedagogical content knowledge (Krauss et al., 2013). First, 
content knowledge refers to teachers’ understanding of the material to be taught (Mishra & Koehler, 2006; Shulman, 1986), including 
both knowledge and subject matter-related skills (Baumert & Kunter, 2013b; Depaepe et al., 2013; Star, 2005). Second, pedagogical 
knowledge encompasses a grasp of general pedagogical principles (Mishra & Koehler, 2006; Shulman, 1986). Third, pedagogical 
content knowledge represents the intersection of these areas, referring to the understanding that teachers have about how to teach 
specific subject matter (Depaepe et al., 2013; Mishra & Koehler, 2006; Shulman, 1986).
Mishra & Koehler (2006) expanded this typology by incorporating technology to the framework and its relationship with the three 
core dimensions. This led to three additional types of interrelated knowledge: technological content knowledge, technological 
pedagogical knowledge and technological pedagogical content knowledge. Several scholars have applied the framework of Mishra & 
Koehler (2006) to the field of computational thinking (e.g., Angeli et al., 2016; Kong et al., 2023). However, to achieve the study’s goal 
of developing an instrument for assessing teachers’ content knowledge in computational thinking, the focus will be on this first core 
dimension.
2.1.2. Measurement of content knowledge
In the research field of teachers’ professional knowledge, several attempts have been made to assess content knowledge, primarily 
within mathematics education. Some scholars have used indirect measures, including distal indicators, such as course attendance and 
qualifications (e.g., Darling-Hammond, 2000), as well as self-report questionnaires (e.g., Brouwer & Korthagen, 2005; Darling-­
Hammond et al., 2002). However, both categories face validity issues: distal indicators often provide only a marginal reflection of the 
concept of interest (Krauss et al., 2008), while self-report data suffer from bias, as people tend to misjudge their own abilities (Hargittai 
& Shafer, 2006).
Other researchers have tried to assess content knowledge more directly through testing. The COACTIV study (Cognitive Activation 
in the Mathematics Classroom and Professional Competence of Teachers) is one of the most significant large-scale investigations in this 
area. As part of this research, Krauss et al. (2013) developed an empirical measure to assess teachers’ mathematical content knowl­
edge, which included thirteen open-ended questions designed to assess their understanding and skills related to the mathematics 
curriculum. While most studies on teachers’ professional knowledge have concentrated on mathematics, research has also extended to 
other areas, including languages and sciences (e.g., Evens et al., 2017; Jüttner et al., 2013) and even computational thinking (e.g., 
Angeli et al., 2016; Gal-Ezer & Stephenson, 2010; Kong et al., 2023; Yadav et al., 2016).
2.2. An application to computational thinking
2.2.1. The construct of computational thinking
The term computational thinking was first introduced by Wing (2006), who described it as involving “solving problems, designing 
systems, and understanding human behavior, by drawing on the concepts fundamental to computer science” (p. 33). Shute et al. 
(2017) offered a more precise definition, characterizing computational thinking as the cognitive processes needed to solve problems 
algorithmically in an efficient and effective manner, whether with or without the use of computers, and to apply these solutions in

different contexts. Similarly, Fraillon et al. (2023) defined it as “the ability to recognize aspects of real-world problems which are 
appropriate for computational formulation and to evaluate and develop algorithmic solutions to those problems so that the solutions 
could be operationalized with a computer” (p. 47). While there is no universally accepted definition of the construct, computational 
thinking fundamentally involves adopting a computer scientist’s approach to problem solving across different domains (Grover, 2022; 
Grover & Pea, 2013).
Several frameworks have been developed to define computational thinking and its core concepts. As this study is conducted as part 
of ICILS 2023 in Flanders, it draws upon the framework of Fraillon et al. (2023), as illustrated in Fig. 1. This framework offers a 
comprehensive theoretical foundation, built on established definitions and frameworks (e.g., Barr & Stephenson, 2011; Selby & 
Woollard, 2013). According to Fraillon et al. (2023), the construct of computational thinking consists of two strands: conceptualizing 
problems and operationalizing solutions (see Fig. 1).
Conceptualizing problems comprises three aspects. The first aspect is knowing about and understanding digital systems, which is 
defined as the ability to identify and describe the properties and the functioning of a digital system (Fraillon et al., 2023). The aspect 
connects to the idea of abstraction, which involves extracting the essence of a system or problem (Barr & Stephenson, 2011; Shute 
et al., 2017; Wing, 2006). More specifically it relates to modeling (Shute et al., 2017) because it entails, for example, describing a 
digital system using a tree diagram or flow chart (Fraillon et al., 2023).
The second aspect is formulating and analyzing problems. Formulating problems entails breaking problems into smaller parts (i.e., 
problem decomposition) and specifying and systematizing their key features. Analyzing problems involves identifying connections 
between the properties and solutions of new and previously encountered problems to support the process of problem decomposition 
(Fraillon et al., 2023). This is linked to the concepts of pattern recognition and generalization: pattern recognition involves identifying 
patterns within and between problems, while generalization refers to the application of solutions to a wide array of problems (Shute 
et al., 2017).
The last aspect is collecting and representing relevant data which encompasses the knowledge and skills needed to effectively 
gather, organize and present data from a system. It also includes an understanding of tools, such as simulations, that can be used to 
make sense of system data (Fraillon et al., 2023). This last aspect is closely related to the concepts of abstraction, data collection, data 
analysis and data representation (Barr & Stephenson, 2011; Shute et al., 2017).
The second strand in Fraillon et al.’s (2023) framework is operationalizing solutions and includes two aspects: planning and 
evaluating solutions, and developing algorithms, programs and interfaces. Planning and evaluating solutions refers to the iterative 
process of planning solutions and making critical judgements about the quality of these solutions. Planning solutions entails estab­
lishing the characteristics of a solution, such as defining user needs and desired outcomes. Evaluating involves making critical 
judgement about the quality of these solutions against these predefined criteria (Fraillon et al., 2023). This process is tied to the 
concept of iteration, described as the repetitive refinement of solutions (Shute et al., 2017). It also involves testing and debugging 
(Fraillon et al., 2023), which refers to detecting and fixing errors in algorithmic solutions (Fraillon et al., 2023; Shute et al., 2017).
The second aspect, developing algorithms, programs and interfaces (between users and the system), centers on creating algorithms 
and code to solve problems. It entails developing and implementing the algorithm as well as automating it (Fraillon et al., 2023).
2.2.2. Measurement of computational thinking
Both indirect and direct measurement instruments are used to evaluate individuals’ computational thinking. Indirect instruments 
typically infer competence levels through self-assessment of one’s ability to accomplish a task (Aesaert and van Braak, 2018), closely 
aligning with the concept of self-efficacy. For instance, Kong et al. (2023) utilized a self-report questionnaire to measure K-12 teachers’ 
computational thinking content knowledge using a five-point Likert scale (e.g., I can think computationally). Similarly, Rich et al. 
(2021) developed the Teacher Beliefs about Coding and Computational Thinking scale, which evaluates elementary teachers’ 
self-efficacy in executing computational thinking tasks through a six-point Likert scale (e.g., I can describe fundamental computing 
concepts). Although such self-report measures have the benefit of being quick and easy to administer (Litt, 2013), they are inherently 
susceptible to self-report bias, meaning individuals often over- and underestimate their own abilities (Hargittai & Shafer, 2006; 
Weintrop et al., 2022).

In recent years, scholarly efforts have focused on developing direct measures of computational thinking (Tang et al., 2020), 
enabling test takers to demonstrate their competences (Siddiq et al., 2016). However, while efforts to assess computational thinking in 
students have advanced, to our knowledge, no direct tools have been specifically designed for teachers. The following overview, 
though not exhaustive, highlights existing instruments aimed at a similar educational level, namely lower secondary school students. 
These instruments can be broadly categorized into conventional and performance-based instruments. The first category refers to an 
objective form of measurement that commonly uses traditional item formats, such as multiple-choice and open-ended questions. For 
example, Rom´an-Gonz´alez et al. (2017) developed the Computational Thinking test for seventh- and eighth-grade students, using 
multiple-choice questions to assess computational concepts like sequences, loops and conditionals. Similarly, Grover and colleagues 
(2015) developed a multiple-choice assessment for sixth- to eighth-graders, focusing on task decomposition, sequences, loops and 
conditionals. Conventional assessments allow for a more valid measurement of individuals’ competences, as they do not rely on 
self-reporting. They are also efficient in terms of time and cost (Weintrop et al., 2022). However, traditional item formats may not fully 
capture real-world performance compared to performance-based assessments (Aesaert and van Braak, 2018; Darling-Hammond & 
Adamson, 2010; Weintrop et al., 2022).
Using a performance-based measure, test takers’ competence level is determined by analyzing their actual performance on 
authentic tasks (i.e., tasks that reflect a real-world context) (Aesaert and van Braak, 2018, Kane et al., 1999), such as creating 
computational artifacts (Weintrop et al., 2022). For example, Dr. Scratch (Moreno-Le´on et al., 2015) is an automated tool that assesses 
Scratch projects (aimed at grades 3–8) based on various computational thinking concepts: abstraction and problem decomposition, 
parallelism, logical thinking, synchronization, flow control, user interactivity, and data representation. Similarly, the Fairy Assessment 
(Werner et al., 2012) measures sixth- to eight-grade students’ computational thinking in Alice. Additionally, Koh et al. (2014) designed 
a formative assessment system, entitled Real Time Evaluation and Assessment of Computational Thinking, to assess sixth-grade stu­
dents’ computational thinking while they are coding in real time. Lastly, the assessment in ICILS also incorporates performance-based 
tasks requiring eighth-grade students to manipulate code blocks in a visual coding environment to create, test en debug computer code 
(Fraillon et al., 2023).
Regardless of their format, most of the measurement tools mentioned above are restricted to coding tasks. This is not surprising, as 
coding is often considered as a fundamental aspect of computational thinking (Fraillon et al., 2023; Weintrop et al., 2016) and a way by 
which someone’s proficiency in computational thinking is promoted and demonstrated (Grover & Pea, 2013). However, as highlighted 
in the previous section, computational thinking extends beyond coding. In addition, it is also recognized for its applications in contexts 
beyond computer science (Grover, 2022), such as mathematics, sciences, social studies and language arts (Barr & Stephenson, 2011). 
In light of this, there is another set of assessment tools that encompasses cross-domain applications of computational thinking. The 
Bebras challenge, for example, is an international competition aimed at students aged eight to eighteen, focusing on informatics and 
computational thinking. The contest consists of cross-disciplinary problem solving tasks, using a multiple-choice format (Dagien˙e & 
Futschek, 2008). Another example comes from Weintrop et al. (2022) who reported on the development of a set of authentic 
performance-based tasks for STEM-courses for secondary school students (Weintrop et al., 2016).
2.3. Validity
Collecting validity evidence is a fundamental step in instrument development (American Educational Research Association et al., 
2014). The Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing refer to validity as “the degree to which evidence and theory support 
the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses of tests” (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014, p.11). Applied to 
the measure in this study, the intended interpretation of the test scores involves describing teachers’ level of computational thinking 
content knowledge.
Test validation thus entails gathering various kinds of evidence to justify inferences that are drawn from the test scores. The 
standards (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014) distinguish between five sources of validity evidence: evidence 
based on test content, internal structure, response processes, relations to other variables and consequences of testing. This study fo­
cuses on the first two sources, test content and internal structure, since they represent logical first steps in the validation process.
The first one, evidence based on test content, is commonly referred to as content validity (e.g., Carmines & Zeller, 1979). This 
evidence is derived from an analysis of how well the test content, such as themes, wording, format of the items, tasks and questions, 
aligns with the construct being measured (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014). It involves ascertaining whether 
the test content adequately represents the content domain (Messick, 1986), often through evaluation by an expert panel. A key concern 
in this process is construct underrepresentation, which refers to the extent to which certain aspects of the construct are not adequately 
covered by the test (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014).
The second type of evidence is based on an analysis of the internal structure of a measure (American Educational Research As­
sociation et al., 2014). The specific types of analyses depend on the test’s purpose. However, dimensionality, reliability and differential 
item functioning are important considerations when evaluating the internal structure of a test (American Educational Research As­
sociation et al., 2014; Rios & Wells, 2014). Dimensionality involves examining the relationship among the items to determine the 
number of underlying dimensions that the instrument represents. Reliability indicates whether the instrument produces internally 
consistent results. Lastly, differential item functioning refers to the extent to which items function differently across manifest groups of 
test takers (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014; Rios & Wells, 2014). In addition to construct underrepresentation, 
construct-irrelevance is an important consideration. This refers to the degree to which test scores are systematically influenced by 
characteristics that are not part of the construct being measured (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014).

3. Research objective
The theoretical overview above indicates that numerous efforts have been made to assess individuals’ computational thinking. 
However, Tang et al. (2020) emphasize the need for measurement instruments specifically tailored to teachers. To the best of our 
knowledge, no well-established instruments exist that are suitable for describing teachers’ computational thinking content knowledge 
and that have undergone a thorough calibration process. Direct measures are preferred over indirect measures, which rely on 
someone’s own judgement (Hargittai & Shafer, 2006; Weintrop et al., 2022). Additionally, the field would benefit from a standardized 
instrument that is easy to administer in large-scale studies.
Considering this, the primary goal of the current study was to develop an instrument to directly measure lower secondary school (grade 
7–8) teachers’ computational thinking content knowledge in a valid and reliable way. The development process was divided into two main 
phases: the instrument construction phase and the instrument calibration phase, which entailed obtaining estimates of person and item 
parameters to establish a scale (de Ayala, 2022). For the calibration phase, both Classical Test Theory (CTT) and Item Response Theory 
(IRT) were applied to the data of pre-service teachers (see Section 4.2.1). When developing an instrument, gathering validity evidence 
is of utmost importance (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014). In this study, content validity and internal validity 
(including dimensionality, reliability and differential item functioning) were considered. Both were addressed in the instrument 
construction and calibration phases, respectively. In what follows, the instrument construction (see Section 4.1) and calibration 
process (see Sections 4.2 and 5) are discussed consecutively.
4. Method
4.1. Instrument construction
The instrument construction process, was guided by the Standards (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014). The 
first step involved defining and specifying the content and format of the instrument, beginning with a clear delineation of the construct 
to be measured (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014). As already indicated, the instrument was developed for 
administration during ICILS 2023 in Flanders. To ensure consistency with the other instruments within this large-scale study, the 
framework of Fraillon et al. (2023) was chosen as the conceptual foundation. To maintain international relevance, this framework was 
compared with other prominent frameworks (e.g., Barr & Stephenson, 2011; Shute et al., 2017). In addition, since this study was 
conducted in Flanders (the Northern region of Belgium), the framework was also compared with the attainment targets (i.e., the 
minimal learning goals that students should achieve) on computational thinking to ensure the instrument’s suitability for describing 
teachers’ content knowledge in the Flemish educational system (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014). This 
comparison involved the use of a cross-table to align the elements of the framework with the corresponding attainment targets to 
ensure comprehensive coverage. This analysis confirmed that the framework of Fraillon et al. (2023) effectively covers various aspects 
of the Flemish attainment targets. The attainment targets include the following computational concepts: decomposition, pattern 
recognition and generalization, abstraction, algorithm, modeling, testing and debugging, digital representation of information and 
principles of programming (Gesqui`ere & Wyffels, 2022). A blueprint was made which included a detailed description of the subskills to 
be measured. In order to establish content validity, the alignment between the assessment framework and the instrument content was 
checked iteratively. Table 1 shows the content coverage of the assessment framework in the instrument. As coding constitutes an 
important part of computational thinking (Grover & Pea, 2013), the decision was made to include coding as well as non-coding tasks. 
However, in order to avoid construct-irrelevant variation in the instrument scores, it was decided not to include items that call for 
familiarity with a particular programming language.
Besides these content specifications, choices were made regarding the format of the instrument. Although performance-based tests 
most accurately reflect real-world performance (Aesaert & van Braak, 2018) such as computational thinking, the deliberate choice was 
made to use a conventional format with multiple-choice and short closed-ended constructed response questions. This approach has the 
advantage of being easily administered and scored in large-scale studies (Weintrop et al., 2022) and of being less expensive, less 
time-consuming and less difficult to replicate than performance-based assessments (Aesaert & van Braak, 2018).
After delineating the instrument specifications, the items and scoring guide were developed. In order to ensure content validity, the 
items were constructed in close cooperation with a panel of experts from the field of computational thinking. The expert panel con­
sisted of a teacher trainer in mathematics and informatics and three academics in computer sciences. The development process 
consisted of three repetitive cycles in which the experts individually commented on the items under construction, after which their 
comments were discussed during a design session. During these sessions several issues were addressed, including the content of the

4.2. Instrument calibration
4.2.1. Participants and administration
The present study was conducted in conjunction with ICILS in Flanders 2023. Specifically, the instrument was developed with the 
purpose of administering it to the study’s teacher sample. In order to avoid overlap with this sample, consisting of lower secondary 
school teachers, it was decided to administer the test, for the purpose of calibrating the instrument, to a comparable group, that is 
senior pre-service teachers in the last year of their program (i.e., a three-year bachelor’s degree program). The target sample of the 
study consists of all senior pre-service teachers in Flanders. As such, all institutions that provide a teacher training program for lower 
secondary teachers were invited to participate in the study. The final sample consisted of senior student teachers from nine of the 
fifteen institutes in Flanders, which yielded a dataset with data from 352 pre-service teachers. The mean age of the pre-service teachers 
is 22.29 (SD = 4.42). Table 2 provides an overview of the distribution of the remaining characteristics. Male and female students are 
almost equally represented. In terms of home language, the majority of the students speaks the test language (Dutch) at home. The 
table also presents information on the specialized courses they follow, reflecting the subjects they plan to teach. It shows that the 
distribution of pre-service teachers studying non-STEM versus STEM subjects is almost balanced. A small percentage of students 
(around 4%) studies information technology and computer science.
The test was administered entirely through Qualtrics (https://www.qualtrics.com), an online survey platform. To ensure equal 
testing conditions, all participants completed the test under the supervision of a test administrator. The informed consent process was 
integrated into the digital version of the test. Participants first received an informational letter and then provided their consent to 
participate electronically. No personal data, such as names or email addresses, were collected, ensuring the anonymity of the par­
ticipants. Respondents were given 45 min to complete the test. Their responses on the test items were binary coded as correct (1) and 
incorrect (0).
4.2.2. Analyses
After administering the instrument to pre-service teachers, IRT was applied to the data. In essence, IRT models are used to calibrate 
instruments, estimating both individual and item traits using observed item responses, thereby placing individuals and items on the 
same continuous scale (de Ayala, 2022). As a first step, CTT was used for a preliminary quality assessment of the items. The following 
sections describe the analyses in more detail.
4.2.2.1. Classical Test Theory (item difficulty and item discrimination). CTT analyses were conducted to calculate the item difficulty and 
item discrimination parameters, using the R package CTT (Willse, 2018). Item difficulty refers to the percentage of individuals in the 
sample who answered the item correctly (Teo, 2013). A p-value closer to .00 indicates a very difficult item, meaning almost all re­
spondents answered the item incorrectly, while a p-value closer to 1.00 indicates a very easy item, meaning most respondents 
answered the item correctly. According to Lüdecke (2022), p-values should ideally range between .20 and .80. However, the inter­
pretation of p-values is more meaningful when considered alongside the discrimination index. Therefore, both item difficulty and item 
discrimination were assessed together to evaluate overall item quality.
Item discrimination was assessed using the point-biserial correlation, which is the correlation between the item and total test 
scores, corrected for the item in question. A negative value is problematic, as it suggests that individuals with lower ability are more 
likely to answer the item correctly than those with higher ability (Teo, 2013). Discrimination values of .20 or higher are considered 
acceptable (Fraillon et al., 2020a; Lüdecke, 2022). Negative values and values below .20 were discarded from subsequent IRT analyses, 
making item discrimination a critical factor in deciding whether to retain or exclude items.
Next, equal discrimination of the items was examined as a first indication of IRT model fit. In the Rasch model, only one parameter, 
the item difficulty, is estimated (de Ayala, 2022). Therefore, significant variation in discrimination indices would indicate that the

4.2.2.2. Dimensionality. IRT models are used to explore the relationship between item responses and a latent trait. These models 
assume unidimensionality to ensure that differences in item responses are attributable to variations in the underlying trait. Unidi­
mensionality means that all items are intended to measure one underlying trait (de Ayala, 2022; Hambleton & Swaminathan, 1985). 
The dimensionality of the instrument was evaluated by means of a nonlinear factor analysis, which is preferred for dichotomous data 
(de Ayala, 2022). For this analysis, the Normal Ogive Harmonic Analysis Robust Method (NOHARM) program was used within the R 
package sirt (Robitzsch, 2022).
In a first step, a unidimensional model was fitted to the data. Model fit was evaluated using Tanaka’s Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI), 
the Root Mean Square Residual (RMSR) and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), following de Ayala (2022). A GFI 
of .90, 0.95 and 1 indicates an acceptable, good and perfect model fit, respectively. The RMSR should be small, specifically smaller than 
four times the inverse of the square root of the sample size. For the RMSEA, values below 0.05 suggest a good fit (de Ayala, 2022). 
Besides model fit, factor loadings were studied. Items with factor loadings below .30 or those classified as Heywood cases (i.e., items 
with a factor loading of 1.00) were removed from further analysis (Aesaert et al., 2014). In a second step, a two-factor model was 
applied and assessed using the same criteria.
4.2.2.3. IRT item- and model-level fit. Subsequently, to determine the most suitable IRT model for calibrating the instrument, both 
item-level and model-level fit indices were calculated and evaluated. For dichotomous items, the commonly used models are the Rasch 
model, the two-parameter model (2 PL model), and the three-parameter model (3 PL model) (de Ayala, 2022). Compared to the Rasch 
model and the 2 PL model, the 3 PL model considers the likelihood of guessing by introducing a third parameter. However, by adding 
the I don’t know option, it is assumed that the data in this study are not influenced by guessing behavior. Therefore, the 3 PL model was 
not considered. As such, the Rasch model and the 2 PL model were applied, using the R package TAM (Robitzsch, Kiefer, & Wu, 2022).
In a first step, the Rasch model was fitted to the data. The Rasch model is a one-parameter model, allowing the difficulty parameters 
to vary, while holding the discrimination parameters constant. As such, when using the Rasch model, items are defined solely by their 
position on the continuous scale, indicating their difficulty (de Ayala, 2022). To evaluate the model’s performance, item fit and model 
fit indices were calculated. Item fit indices indicate how well each individual item fits to the model, while model fit indices reflect the 
overall fit of the model to the data. Regarding item fit, infit statistics were calculated for each item, which are based on a comparison 
between the observed responses on an item and the model’s expected responses (de Ayala, 2022). Infit statistics between 0.80 and 1.20 
indicate a good item fit (Wright & Linacre, 1994). To evaluate model fit, the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMSR) and the 
MADaQ3 were calculated, both of which are based on inter-item correlations (Maydeu-Olivares, 2013; Robitzsch, Kiefer, & George, 
2022). For both indices, small values, near to zero indicate a good fit to the data (Robitzsch, Kiefer, & George, 2022). According to 
Maydeu-Olivares (2013), the cutoff value of the SRMSR is 0.05.
In a second step, the 2 PL model was applied to the data. In contrast to the Rasch model, the 2 PL model allows the discrimination 
parameters to vary. To be more precise, when applying the 2 PL model, items are characterized both by their location on the con­
tinuum, indicating their difficulty, and their capacity to discriminate among individuals with varying abilities (de Ayala, 2022). 
Similarly, item and model fit indices were calculated for the 2 PL model. To be more precise, for item fit, the RMSEA was calculated, 
which is also based on a comparison between the model-predicted item responses and the observed responses. The RMSEA of each item 
was evaluated against a cutoff value of 0.05, where lower values indicate good item fit (Kunina-Habenicht et al., 2009). Regarding 
model fit, the SRMSR and the MADaQ3 were evaluated, following the same criteria as the Rasch model.
In a last step, both IRT models were compared in two ways. First, two relative model fit indices were calculated for model fit 
comparisons, that is the Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) (Akaike, 1974) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (Schwarz, 
1978). For both indices, lower values indicate better model fit (de Ayala, 2022). Second, the Chi-Square statistic was calculated to 
evaluate whether the difference in model fit between the two models was statistically significant.
4.2.2.4. Local item independence. In addition to unidimensionality, both the Rasch model and the 2 PL model assume local item in­
dependence and monotonicity (de Ayala, 2022; Hambleton & Swaminathan, 1985; van der Ark, 2007). After identifying the appro­
priate model, these additional assumptions were assessed. Local item independence implies that item responses only depend on a 
person’s ability (de Ayala, 2022). For this assumption to be met, a respondent’s performance on one item may not be dependent of his 
or her performance on another item (Hambleton & Swaminathan, 1985). In order to detect local dependence among the items, Yen’s 
Q3 statistic was calculated, using the R package TAM (Robitzsch, Kiefer, & Wu, 2022). The Q3 statistic is the correlation between the 
residuals of item pairs, corrected for the overall test performance. Correlations of .20 or higher are considered problematic (Yen, 1993).
4.2.2.5. Monotonicity. The final assumption is monotonicity, which means that the probability of correctly responding to an item, 
given a latent trait, is a monotonic nondecreasing function of the latent trait (Junker & Sijtsma, 2000; Sijtsma & Molenaar, 2002). In 
this study, this implies that as teachers’ computational thinking content knowledge improves, the likelihood of answering an item 
correctly also increases. To check this assumption, a Mokken scale analysis (Mokken, 1971; van der Ark, 2007) was conducted, using 
the R package Mokken (van der Ark et al., 2023). The results of this analysis report on the number of violations of monotonicity among 
the items (van der Ark, 2007). Next to this, in order to further investigate the monotonicity assumption, the item characteristic curves 
of all items were evaluated.

4.2.2.6. Differential item functioning. Before estimating the final scale, the items and the instrument as a whole were checked for 
exhibiting differential item functioning (DIF) and differential test functioning (DTF) for gender, home language and teaching subject. 
An item exhibits DIF if individuals from different manifest groups, such as males and females, having the same ability, do not have the 
same chances of answering the item correctly (de Ayala, 2022). These groups are referred to as the reference group and the focal group. 
The latter is the group being examined to see whether or not it is benefited by the item (de Ayala, 2022). DTF occurs when one or more 
items in a test show DIF and its effects are meaningfully large at the test level (Chalmers et al., 2016). To investigate DIF, the 
Mantel-Haenszel method (Mantel & Haenszel, 1959) was used. The Mantel-Haenszel Chi-Square statistic describes whether there is a 
relationship between group membership and item response, after taking the total test score into account (Magis et al., 2020). To 
conduct the DIF and DTF analyses the R package difR (Magis et al., 2020) was used.
4.2.2.7. Scale development and reliability. In the final step, the best-fitting model was applied to estimate the final scale, including both 
person and item characteristics. This analysis only included the items retained from the previous steps. The reliability index was 
calculated, using expected a posteriori (EAP) estimation, with the R package TAM (Robitzsch, Kiefer, & Wu, 2022). A reliability value 
closer to 1 indicates stronger internal consistency of the instrument.
5. Results: instrument calibration
5.1. Classical Test Theory (item difficulty and item discrimination)
As an initial quality check, item difficulty and item discrimination were assessed by conducting classical test analyses on the twenty 
items of the instrument. A detailed summary of the item parameters is presented in Table A.1 of Appendix A. Regarding item difficulty, 
the p-values lie within the range of .16 (i.e., 16% of the respondents answered the item correctly) and .71 (i.e., 71% of the respondents 
answered the item correctly), with a mean of .38 (SD = .17), indicating that the test is relatively difficult. Items 3, 6 and 17 have p- 
values below the critical value of .20.
Regarding item discrimination, point-biserial correlations were calculated to evaluate how effectively each item differentiates 
between respondents with varying ability levels. Items 6 and 17 have point-biserial correlations below the critical threshold of .20, 
indicating poor discrimination. Since these items neither have acceptable difficulty levels, nor discriminate well, they were excluded 
from further analysis. Despite its low p-value, item 3 demonstrates adequate discrimination and was retained. Finally, item 10 has a 
point-biserial correlation falling below .20 and a difficulty level near the .20 threshold. Given the importance of item discrimination in 
determining whether to keep or exclude items, item 10 was also excluded. In practical terms, items 6, 10 and 17 do not provide 
meaningful information about the respondents’ level of computational thinking content knowledge.
Additionally, the biserial correlations were examined to inform subsequent IRT analyses. Nine items are located outside the range 
of .27 and .57 (Mbiserial correlation = .42), suggesting a rejection of the equal discrimination assumption. This is a first indication that the 
use of the Rasch model, in which a constant discrimination index is considered appropriate enough to fit the data, is not recommended.

5.3. IRT item- and model-level fit
The Rasch and 2 PL models were both applied to the data, and item- and model-level fit indices were assessed to identify the most 
appropriate model. Tables A.2 and A.3 in Appendix A provide detailed information on item parameters and fit statistics for both 
models. For the Rasch model (Table A.2), all infit statistics fall within the critical range of 0.80 and 1.20, indicating a good fit for every 
item. In the 2 PL model (Table A.3), the RMSEA of three items slightly exceeds the threshold of 0.05, indicating a moderate to good 
item fit, while all other items demonstrate strong alignment with the model.
Regarding model fit, the absolute fit indices for the Rasch model (SRMSR = 0.073, MADaQ3 = 0.049) and the 2 PL model (SRMSR 
= 0.043, MADaQ3 = 0.048) are near zero, but only the 2 PL model meets the SRMSR critical threshold of 0.05. When comparing the 
relative fit indices, relative to the Rasch model (AIC = 6479,302, BIC = 6544.984), the 2 PL model (AIC = 6436.661, BIC = 6560.298) 
shows a decrease in AIC but an increase in BIC. However, the Chi-Square test reveals a significant difference in deviance (χ 2 = 72.48, 
df = 15, p < .001), favoring the 2 PL model. Based on these findings, it can be concluded that the 2 PL model is preferred over the Rasch 
model for measuring teachers’ content knowledge.
5.4. Local item independence
Besides, unidimensionality, both the Rasch model and the 2 PL model assume local item independence and monotonicity. Local 
item independence was checked by calculating the Q3 statistic for item pairs. Two item pairs show a Q3 value higher than .20. More 
specifically items 2 and 11 (.23) and items 2 and 19 (.38) seem to be positively related, which indicates that these items are locally 
dependent. Based on these findings, a closer look was taken at the items. With regard to item 2 and 19, a possible explanation is that the 
same context was used to introduce the question (de Ayala, 2022). For item 2, test takers have to choose the best code out of four 
options. In contrast, for item 19, test takers are required to partially construct the code themselves. A valuable option is to switch the 
position of both items in the test. With regard to item 2 and 11, item 2 does not seem to include information needed to solve item 11. In 
order to ensure content validity and because the items show suitable item characteristics, it was decided not to remove the items.

5.5. Monotonicity
To evaluate the monotonicity assumption, a Mokken scale analysis was conducted. Detailed results are presented in Table A.4 of 
Appendix A. The analysis shows no violations of monotonicity (i.e. minvi >0.03) across the items. An examination of the item 
characteristic curves supports this conclusion. To illustrate, the item characteristic curve of item 19 is shown in Fig. 3. The horizontal 
axis reflects the latent trait being measured, where lower values correspond to lower ability and higher values to higher ability. The 
vertical axis indicates the probability of a correct response on item 19. The black curve represents the observed data, while the blue 
curve shows the model’s expected response pattern. The black curve demonstrates that the probability of a correct response increases 
with higher ability, a trend observed across all items. These results confirm that the items accurately measure the intended construct, 
teachers’ computational thinking content knowledge, allowing for more confident conclusions about their proficiency in this area.
5.6. Differential item functioning
Lastly, DIF was evaluated both at the item level and the overall test level. The findings of the DIF analyses are displayed in Table A.5
in Appendix A. First, with regard to gender, the Mantel-Haenszel statistics and the accompanying p-values reveal that none of the items 
display DIF. Second, regarding home language, item 16 seems to favor pre-service teachers who do not speak the test language at 
home. Lastly, the results indicate that items 2 and 3 exhibit DIF for teaching subject. More specifically, item 2 advantages pre-service 
teachers with a STEM background, whereas item 3 favors the other group. Nonetheless, the results from the DTF analysis indicate that, 
for both variables, the test does not show a significant benefit for any particular group overall (see Figs. 4 and 5).
5.7. Scale development and reliability
For the 2 PL model, the reliability analysis shows a reliability of .78, indicating that the scale, comprising 16 items, demonstrates a 
relatively good internal consistency. Table A.6 in Appendix A provides an overview of the remaining items and their item parameters 
for the 2 PL model, with the easier items at the top of the scale and difficult items at the bottom.
6. Discussion
The aim of this study was to develop an instrument to measure lower secondary school teachers’ computational thinking content 
knowledge, consisting of both a construction and calibration phase. The items were created in close collaboration with an expert panel 
specialized in computational thinking and computer science. Throughout this process, the alignment between the blueprint, derived 
from the framework of Fraillon et al. (2023), and the instrument’s content was iteratively assessed. This established the instrument’s 
content validity, which is a crucial prerequisite for calibration (de Ayala, 2022) and an essential initial step in the development of any 
instrument.
The finalized items were subsequently administered to a larger sample of 352 lower secondary pre-service teachers as part of the 
calibration phase. The CTT analyses resulted in the retention of seventeen out of the twenty items, all of which exhibited sufficient 
discrimination, indicating their ability to accurately distinguish between individuals with varying levels of computational thinking 
content knowledge.

Nonlinear factor analysis suggested that teachers’ computational thinking content knowledge is best represented by a single scale, 
consistent with the ICILS framework, where computational thinking is treated as a unidimensional construct (Fraillon et al., 2020a; 
Fraillon et al., 2023 ). Despite confirming unidimensionality, two item pairs (i.e., item 2 and item 11; and item 2 and item 19) exhibited 
local item dependence. This was unexpected, as local item independence implies that only one factor, that is respondents’ ability level, 
impacts their responses to the items (de Ayala, 2022). Following Hambleton et al. (1991) and de Ayala (2022), it is possible that item 2 
provided clues for answering the other items. However, due to its strong psychometric properties and to maintain content validity, it 
was decided to retain item 2 and address the issue by swapping items 2 and 19. As for the monotonicity assumption, no violations were 
detected, meaning that the likelihood of a correct response increased with higher ability for all items. Moreover, the DIF and DTF 
analyses revealed no bias toward any particular group, ensuring the fairness of the instrument across groups (de Ayala, 2022). These 
results suggest that construct-irrelevant variance is minimal, further supporting the validity of the test score interpretations (de Ayala, 
2022).
The final scale, estimated using the 2 PL model, achieved a reliability coefficient of .78, indicating a satisfactory level of internal 
consistency. In conclusion, the instrument exhibits promising characteristics, suggesting its potential to measure the construct in a 
valid and reliable way.
Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged in both phases of the study. The first limitation concerns to the format of 
the test. To be more precise, multiple-choice items are less suited to measure some aspects of the construct, such as formulating and 
analyzing problems and developing algorithms, programs and interfaces. As a result, key components of the construct that the in­
strument aims to measure may be underrepresented. In this regard, performance-based instruments might be more appropriate for 
measuring the construct. Scholars have suggested that multiple-choice questions are less effective in assessing higher-order compe­
tences compared to performance-based assessments, which can more accurately reflect real-world performance (Darling-Hammond & 
Adamson, 2010). However, multiple-choice items provide a more time- and cost-efficient method for directly measuring teachers’ 
computational thinking content knowledge (Aesaert & van Braak, 2018; Weintrop et al., 2022). Additionally, they offer a more reliable 
measure than self-efficacy assessments, which depend on individuals’ ability to accurately assess their own competences (Hargittai & 
Shafer, 2006; Weintrop et al., 2022). Follow-up research could focus on gathering convergent validity evidence, one of the five sources 
(i.e. test content, internal structure, response processes, relations to other variables and consequences of testing) identified by the 
Standards (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014). Due to practical constraints, such as limited time, it was not 
feasible to examine all five sources in this study. Collecting convergent validity evidence involves examining the relationship between 
the test scores and other measures that intend to measure the same construct (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014; 
de Ayala, 2022). In this context, this could involve examining the relationship between the test scores and a performance-based 
measure, such as Dr. Scratch (Moreno-Le´on et al., 2015).
In addition, there are further constraints related to the calibration of the instrument, particularly regarding the study’s sample. 
When evaluating a measurement instrument, it is important for the sample of test takers to closely represent the target population 
(American Educational Research Association et al., 2014). However, to avoid overlap with the ICILS 2023 sample in Flanders, it was 
not possible to sample the target population of the test instrument, that is lower secondary school teachers. As an alternative, senior 
pre-service teachers were selected. Although this group was chosen for its similarities to the target population, there are still disparities 
in terms of, for example, age and teaching experience.
Another limitation involves the representativeness of the sample. Only nine out of the fifteen institutes agreed to participate, which 
may have affected the sample’s representativeness. Additionally, no data were available from the entire population to verify this 
assumption. A further issue relates to the sample size. As noted by de Ayala (2022), a calibration sample between 200 and 500 is

A final discussion point concerns the instruments applicability. The instrument was specifically developed to assess the content 
knowledge of lower secondary school teachers. More precisely, it was developed with this target group in mind and calibrated using a

7. Conclusion
Based on the aforementioned discussion, it can be concluded that the instrument demonstrates appropriate item and instrument 
characteristics. Despite the study’s limitations, we cautiously affirm the instrument’s potential to effectively measure lower secondary 
school teachers’ computational thinking content knowledge in a valid and reliable manner. Nevertheless, further research is warranted 
to confirm this assertion. The present study provides a tool to researchers and practitioners to measure lower secondary school 
teachers’ computational thinking content knowledge, which may lead to more research interest in teachers’ professional knowledge in 
the domain.
