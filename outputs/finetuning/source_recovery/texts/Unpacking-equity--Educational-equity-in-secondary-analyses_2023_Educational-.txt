A B S T R A C T  
The discourse of educational equity has captured public imagination and become an entrenched 
policy value. In the attendant struggle to improve educational equity, governments draw inspi­
ration from analyses based on data from highly standardized, international large-scale assess­
ments. As these analyses are used as a lever for policy formulation, it is important to understand 
how this concept of equity is appropriated. Lacking a synthesis of how equity is conceptualized 
and operationalized in this body of research, the current paper seeks to fill that gap by presenting 
a systematic review. Our analysis incorporates an identification of operational patterns and an 
exploration regarding the linguistic elements for defining equity. We chart the theoretical and 
methodological diversity among interpretations of equity, as clustered into five major research 
approaches. In light of these results, benefits and limitations of the concept’s complexity are 
discussed and implications for research are forwarded.   
1. Unpacking equity 
In the midst of our quality concerned educational climate, there is a growing interest for the ethical aspect of education, being 
equity, as an end in itself (Braathe & Otterstad, 2014; Field et al., 2007; Maiztegui-O˜nate & Santib´a˜nez-Gruber, 2008; Wood et al., 
2011). Educational equity has become a certain rectitude of mind, finding its derivation in questions about social justice (Anderson 
et al., 2020; Berne & Stiefel, 1999; Wood et al., 2011; Zajda et al., 2006) and, as such, it is almost always politically contentious (Levin, 
2003). The academic world, moreover, provides multiple interpretations of the concept. Due to this conceptual ambiguity, different 
perspectives on its definition and preferred way of operationalization prevail. Additionally, in the universal quest for educational 
improvement, the results of international large-scale assessments (ILSAs) are seen as a rich opportunity to evidence the current state of 
equity, as well as its preceding trend (Addey et al., 2017; Dong & Cravens, 2012; Kyriakides & Creemers, 2018). While governments 
use the conclusions drawn from secondary analyses of these ILSA results to make statements about their country’s educational equity, 
it is important to verify how this concept is approached by researchers in the study of equity based on ILSA data. To date, however, no 
such overview of this literature has been provided. This paper seeks to fill that gap by presenting a systematic review of the 
conceptualization and operationalization of educational equity within literature on ILSAs.

1.1. Educational equity 
With the rise of the idea that educational inequality implies that human potential is being wasted, equity became a second major 
policy aim, claiming its spot alongside the strive for quality (Field et al., 2007; Levin, 2003; Van Damme & Bellens, 2017; Zhu, 2018). 
At the heart of equity lies a growing awareness of diversity, and this awareness involves an inherent responsibility to deal with this 
diversity (Maiztegui-O˜nate & Santib´a˜nez-Gruber, 2008; Tomlinson, 2014). To many, educational equity as a construct is an attempt to 
answer the following question: “How can we give every child the opportunity to reach their full potential?” (Berne & Stiefel, 1999; 
Levin, 2003; OECD, 2012; UNESCO, 2007). It is unrealistic to expect all children to achieve equally (Berne & Stiefel, 1999; Field et al., 
2007; Levin, 2003; OECD, 2001; Zhu, 2018), raising a question about the degree to which inequality is acceptable (Levin, 2003; OECD, 
2001; Van Damme & Bellens, 2017). Parallel to Pirsig’s (1974) argument about quality (“while we may not be able to define [quality], 
we know it when we see it”), Levin (2003) states that “while we cannot define [equity], we know when we are far from it” (p. 3). 
Although educational equity has become an entrenched value, promoting talk of equity, the term is to be characterized as multi- 
layered, contested, and far from univocal. Since the concept is steeped in values, it evokes a variety of meanings and certain internal 
paradoxes. In the remainder of this introduction, we will demonstrate equity as a capricious ideal. 
1.1.1. Terminology 
The starting point in outlining the mainstream terminology regarding educational equity is a comment about the often- 
interchangeable use of the words, equity and equality, despite the fact that they are not synonymous (Field et al., 2007; Secada, 
1989; Zhu, 2018). Equality implies treating everyone in the exact same way, but then Sen’s (1992) question comes to mind: “Equality 
of what?” (p. 12). Equality is not a unique concept (Lafontaine et al., 2015), but has multiple levels that coexist and compete with one 
another (Kyriakides & Creemers, 2018; Lafontaine et al., 2015; Levin, 2003; Maiztegui-O˜nate & Santib´a˜nez-Gruber, 2008; Zhu, 2018). 
The most comprehensive classification of equality which has been proposed consists of four levels, distinguishing between (1) equality 
of opportunities (i.e., rights recognized in legislation); (2) equality of access (i.e., actual access to school); (3) equality of treatment (i. 
e., educational models); and (4) equality of outcome (i.e., opportunities to succeed). However, to achieve these equalities, an unequal 
provision of resources or treatment is sometimes necessary. When people begin to speak of equity instead of equality, they try to go 
beyond the notion of a ‘one size fits all’ policy by recognizing and treating diversities in the educational process (Maiztegui-O˜nate & 
Santib´a˜nez-Gruber, 2008; OECD, 2012; Zhu, 2018). The discourse on equity has developed toward a more complex idea that includes 
and transcends the different levels of equality (Maiztegui-O˜nate & Santib´a˜nez-Gruber, 2008). 
Scholarly literature differentiates between three forms of equity, an argument that is well developed in the work of Berne and 
Stiefel (1999). Embodied in their framework are (1) horizontal equity; (2) vertical equity; and (3) equal educational opportunities. 
Each of these forms can be distinguished from the other two by its perspective on dealing with diversity. Horizontal equity requires an 
equal treatment of those who have the same starting point, and categorically ignores past and present inequalities between children 
(Brown, 2006; Maiztegui-O˜nate & Santib´a˜nez-Gruber, 2008). However, children do not always find themselves in the same situation, 
and so horizontal equity is seen as a precondition for tackling another form, vertical equity, which recognizes different starting 
conditions and the need to treat these children differently. Subsequently, by recognizing the diversity of backgrounds, an unequal but 
equitable treatment can be established in order to reduce inequality (Zhu, 2018). The notion of equal educational opportunities (not to 
be confused with equality of opportunities) claims that all children should have an equal chance to succeed in education. This success 
should, again, be based on personal characteristics such as effort, rather than on circumstances outside of the child’s control. Maiz­
tegui-O˜nate and Santib´a˜nez-Gruber (2008) note that both horizontal equity and equal educational opportunities are directly related to 
the four levels of equality. Horizontal equity requires little or no variation in the dispersion of opportunities, access and treatment. The 
form equal educational opportunities is about levelling the playing field, thus, equality of outcome. Equity in its vertical form intends 
to remove barriers that limit the possibilities for full integration in schooling. For this strive, strategies need to be incorporated into 
each of the four levels of equality. 
A closer look at the literature reveals that other concepts are used to illustrate what could be meant by educational equity. 
However, these seem to be associated with to the core notion we described above. To take an example (and a lead) from the Orga­
nisation for Economics Co-operation and Development (OECD), equity is concerned with inclusion and fairness (Field et al., 2007; 
Kyriakides & Creemers, 2018; OECD, 2012; Zhu, 2018). Inclusion refers to the idea of equal educational opportunities, and implies that 
all students should reach at least a basic minimum set of skills. Fairness has more to do with vertical equity, since it means that factors 
beyond those within the child’s control should not serve as obstacles for achieving their educational potential. This is also the case with 
egalitarianism (Charalambous et al., 2018; Kyriakides & Creemers, 2018), a more philosophical idea related to the role of ‘the school as 
the great equalizer’ for counterbalancing inequalities outside of school life (Charalambous et al., 2018; Downey et al., 2004; Milner, 
2010; Wood et al., 2011). Meritocracy, on the other hand, too states that the same opportunities should be offered, but that each 
student is considered responsible for their own educational success, bringing this concept closer to the description of horizontal equity 
(Charalambous et al., 2018; Lafontaine et al., 2015). However, meritocracy is believed to be a myth, as hidden mechanisms beyond

1.1.2. Student background 
In reading about equity, it stands out that concerns about the concept are grounded in the attributably of educational outcomes to 
student background, which refers to elements beyond those within students’ control, and which hints at their circumstances rather 
than their effort (Kyriakides & Creemers, 2018). 
According to Kellaghan (2001, pp. 3–22), children of certain backgrounds are at educational disadvantage. Such disadvantages are 
then defined as the existence of “discontinuities between competencies […] which children bring to school and the competencies […] 
that are valued in school” (Kellaghan, 2001, p.3, pp. 3–22), which consequently lead to educational success. Here, we encounter the 
concept of capital, which involves factors in student background that affect the development of competencies, and which consequently 
facilitate the learning process. Bourdieu (1986) subdivided this concept into three important forms, claiming that a world of perfect 
equality of opportunity is imaginary. Economic capital refers to the available financial resources, and is the most well-known of 
Bourdieu’s forms. The less familiar cultural capital entails indicators such as cultural goods (e.g., books) and educational qualifica­
tions. Bourdieu’s third form, social capital, includes membership in a group. It follows that, if backgrounds differ, nourished com­
petencies will differ, and a child’s success in meeting educational demands depends on the extent of continuity between nourished and 
demanded competencies (Kellaghan, 2001, pp. 3–22). 
In outlining the theory on disadvantages and capital, it seems imperative to include the idea of equity concerns. Empirical research 
on equity often deals with the analysis of backgrounds as obstacles to the attainment of one’s full educational potential (OECD, 2012; 
Van Damme & Bellens, 2017). To assess how equitable education systems are, diversity in student background is frequently defined in 
terms of one or more equity concerns (Levin, 2003; Zhu, 2018). These concerns refer to the many factors that may influence 
educational opportunities, with a major focus on ethnicity, socioeconomic status (SES), and gender (Field et al., 2007; Herremans, 
2012; Kyriakides & Creemers, 2018; Levin, 2003; OECD, 2012; Zhu, 2018). Groups of students who can be identified at one specific 
end of one or more such concerns tend to experience the greatest educational difficulties (Levin, 2003; OECD, 2012; Zhu, 2018). At the 
same time, the idea that students fail because of their own personal shortcomings is being superseded by the idea of school failure 
(Field et al., 2007). School systems are increasingly seen as responsible for providing an adequate education that is appropriate to 
differing needs (Charalambous et al., 2018; Lafontaine et al., 2015; Levin, 2003). 
1.1.3. Achievement gap 
The aforementioned school failure refers to the existence of a deep divide, or gap, in academic performance between students. 
Contemporary educational literature is inundated with discussions about this perceived achievement gap, which is assumed to indicate 
educational inequity (Milner, 2010; Van Damme & Bellens, 2017), and the idea itself has been problematized. In the work of Irvine 
(2010), for example, the perceived achievement gap is related to other gaps that prevent economically, socially or culturally diverse 
students from experiencing school success. These other gaps include the teacher quality gap, the wealth gap, and the income gap. In 
this way, the achievement gap is really an outcome of a matrix of gaps, involving social equity issues (Bower, 2013; Wood et al., 2011). 
This draws attention to the intersections between areas of inequity in society, and how they may manifest within education. 
Within this deficit in thinking, it is imperative to notice that inquiries into equity can be shaped as either a gap-gazing at the 
achievement scores between different subgroups, or the correlation between student achievement and background variables (Bower, 
2013; Kyriakides & Creemers, 2011; Van Damme & Bellens, 2017). Both of which ought to be as small as possible. However, a 
counterargument has been made, in that a greater achievement gap can also imply that more students develop their talents to a greater 
extent. 
The foregoing discussion of educational equity indicates that empirical studies can take different paths. Researchers need to 
overcome obstacles in both their conceptualization and operationalization of educational equity. This is accompanied by a series of 
considerations, giving rise to the presumption that in research on ILSA data, the concept of equity has also undergone multiple in­
terpretations. Here, we are to pose the question of whether patterns are to be found in the ways researchers on ILSA data define and 
measure the concept. In answering this question, we will configure different understandings in an interpretive systematic review, 
based on the methodological guidelines of Alexander (2020) which address the art and science in facing multiple challenges in the 
process of conducting a systematic review (Dixon-Woods et al., 2006; Gough et al., 2019). Current contribution aims to make the study 
of educational equity which draws on ILSA data more transparent for researchers as well as policymakers. 
1.2. International large-scale assessments 
The main organizations responsible for developing and administering ILSAs are the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and 
Development (OECD) and the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA; Addey et al., 2017). Data 
is collected about dozens of individual countries’ educational systems (OECD, 2019; Oliveri & von Davier, 2014). By means of a 
repeated cross-sectional design, information is gathered about educational achievement in multiple topics. Additionally, background 
questionnaires attempt to capture various and, presumably, achievement-related characteristics at the individual student, teacher, 
school and country level, in order to aid in the understanding of every educational system (Wagemaker, 2013). 
This present study has a particular focus on the international surveys that measure an educational attribute at the student level in 
compulsory education. This limits the scope to five specific ILSAs: the Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS), 
the Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS), the International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS), the 
International Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS), and the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA).

Participating in these studies is quite an enterprise (Drent et al., 2013). To generate internationally comparative data, an invest­
ment of time and money is required (Rutkowski et al., 2020). In return, and catalyzed by an increasingly interdependent world, such 
investments open up opportunities to be plugged into international conversations about education, which in turn feeds the growing 
political impetus to look outwards the own educational system (Ng et al., 2020; Wagemaker, 2020). Moreover, ILSAs provide policy 
and research communities with rich contextual data about their educational system (Addey et al., 2017; Oliveri & von Davier, 2014). 
These are useful for conducting secondary analyses to derive further insights about education systems which go beyond the generally 
descriptive scope of the primary publications (Meinck et al., 2020; Ng et al., 2020; Rutkowski et al., 2020). Secondary analyses are 
analyses performed on data collected by others – in this case, by the organizations responsible for the ILSAs. Such analyses have made 
policymakers around the world aware of the level and location of existing inequalities (Wood et al., 2011). Questions related to policy 
formation and academic research tend to refer to data from TIMSS, PIRLS and PISA (Rutkowski et al., 2013; Wagemaker, 2013). Armed 
with such rich data, researchers worldwide have revealed variabilities across countries in terms of average performance, as well as 
between diverse student groups, allowing them to draw conclusions about educational equity (Contini & Cugnata, 2020). 
1.3. Research questions 
The growing impact of ILSAs on shaping the educational discourse of equity relevant to policy decisions, and the extant continuum 
in conceptualizing and operationalizing this concept of educational equity, begs for scholarly investigation. This study sets out to 
reveal how the concept of educational equity is interpreted in secondary analyses of data from these ILSAs. Specifically, the aim of this 
review is threefold: 
RQ1. What approaches or patterns can be found in the way educational equity is defined and measured in literature on secondary 
analyses of ILSA data? 
RQ2. How is educational equity defined and measured in each of these approaches?

2. Method 
2.1. Systematic review 
In order to develop answers to these questions, the present study conducted an interpretive systematic review of literature on 
secondary analyses drawing on data from ILSAs (Dixon-Woods et al., 2006; Gough et al., 2019). We label the systematic review as 
interpretive because our endeavours were directed to producing a rich synthesis that embraces the patterns and complexities as we 
interpret them in the inquiry of equity through ILSA data, rather than strictly assembling statistical data. 
Comprehensive searches were conducted to find relevant articles within the ERIC and Web of Science databases. To ensure that 
pertinent research documents were not overlooked, two additional, predominant resources for research in the field of ILSAs were 
scoured: the journal, Large-scale Assessments in Education; and the subject-specific book series, IEA Research for Education Series. These 
four education-related sources were selected in order to narrow the focus of our review beforehand. This selection procedure is in 
accord with the recommendations of the PRISMA guidelines for reporting the identification, screening, eligibility and inclusion steps of 
a systematic review (Page, 2021; see Fig. 1). 
The exploration of these databases took place by means of four combinations of keywords (see Table 2). Each search phrase started 
with the concept of interest, ‘equity,’ or its complementary synonym, ‘equality,’ since these two terms are quite often used inter­
changeably; no other synonyms were added. This subject was combined with the research area of focus (i.e., education* OR learn* OR 
school* OR stud*) and the desired dataset (i.e., TIMSS OR PISA OR PIRLS; ICCS OR ICILS). We created two study pools corresponding 
to the more established ILSAs when discussing political impact – namely, TIMSS, PIRLS and PISA - and the more recent voices in the 
debate - namely ICCS and ICILS (Wagemaker, 2013). After an exploratory trial, the search keys ‘IEA’ and ‘OECD’ were removed from 
the search, given that they are related to other ILSA studies and confused the focus of this literature search. The keywords were not 
limited to title or abstract, in order to include all potentially relevant studies. The application of these search phrases resulted in a total 
of 405 hits (search conducted in January 2021). For more information regarding the filters used within every database, we refer to 
Appendix C. 
In this identification stage, we further delimited the search to those articles that were peer reviewed, in an attempt to include 
studies of high quality. The OECD iLibrary, which was originally adopted in this review, does not include this type of information in 
their publishing workflow; for this reason, this library was excluded as a search database. A further criterion was placed in terms of 
language, in that only articles written in English were included. Next, a cutoff in terms of time was chosen, with a focus on articles 
published from 1995 and onwards, since the earliest ILSA study of focus (viz., TIMSS) was conducted in that year. 
After removing the duplicates (n = 108), the remaining articles (n = 297) went through two screening processes, based on the 
adopted inclusion and exclusion criteria to determine the suitability of articles in relation to the research questions of the current study 
(see Table 3). The title, abstract and conclusion were the focus of the first screening. The corresponding criteria delimited the search to 
those empirical studies that worked with the requested ILSA data, and which made statements in their conclusions about the equity or 
equality of compulsory education (n = 105). The key feature in the second screening was the naming of the concept ‘equity’ or 
‘equality’ within the specific research aim and research questions. With the completion of this screening phase 50 articles remained. 
Next, full texts were assessed for eligibility. A total of 25 articles remained, and were included in the content analysis. The surface 
characteristics of these articles are outlined in Table 4. Studies within the table were organized alphabetically by author. Perusing the 
surface characteristics, it becomes noticeable that only articles working on data from TIMSS, PIRLS and PISA met the prescribed 
inclusion and exclusion criteria; articles from ICCS and ICILS were not retained through the selection.

2.2. Literature analysis 
The present review of literature is an initiative undertaken to grasp how researchers working with data from ILSAs conceptualize 
and operationalize educational equity. In order to capture the comprehensive nature of this literature, careful data reduction was 
addressed through a process of coding and a Lines-of-argument synthesis (Dixon-Woods et al., 2006; Noblit & Hare, 1988). This 
synthesis integrates findings from across the reviewed articles into a coherent theoretical framework comprising a network of con­
structs and relationships between them. 
The supportive coding scheme was derived from the theoretical constructs discussed earlier, areas of interest devised in previous 
review articles, or developed inductively from the articles themselves after the iterative process of reading and rereading. A two-step 
coding procedure was used, based on two broad categories of codes: conceptualization and operationalization. The first step was to 
code information related to the conceptualization of equity. 
Index of explicitness in the definition. We identified the extent to which authors explicitly defined equity in line with the coding found 
in Dinsmore et al. (2008). Explicit definitions were direct statements of authors’ meaning of the concept. Implicit definitions were 
constructions of authors’ implied meaning of the concept, which derived from words that point toward meaning (conceptual), or 
references to the definition in other articles (referential), or the description of a measure (measure). When no type of definition could 
be found in the theoretical framework of the article, the code ‘absent’ was used. 
Terminology. A two-part code was added to the terminology used to refer to the concept of interest. First, the preferred word (i.e., 
‘equity’ or ‘equality’), and second, the formulation of this word (i.e., positive or negative) were coded. 
Theoretical framework. Coherent with the considerations made in the introduction of the present study, the theoretical framework of 
the article was coded. The three forms of equity (horizontal equity, vertical equity, and equality of educational opportunities) proved 
to be the most all-encompassing and theoretically elaborate. However, these forms were not suitable if the article focused on a specific 
form of inequality. The four levels of equality (equality of access, opportunity, treatment and outcome) were included to cover this 
void. 
In the second step, operational elements regarding the measurement of the concept and the subsequent analysis were identified and 
coded. In this regard, we should note that we included only those elements in the article which were considered as a measurement for 
equity; any variables that aimed to explain the level of equity were set aside for this purpose. 
Measure. The first phase of the operational coding scheme centered on the measure used to operationalize the concept of equity. We 
started by pinpointing the variable(s) that are used to represent the concept. In a second phase, this operationalization was associated 
with an equity concern (e.g., ethnicity or gender), and the ILSA material from which the information is derived. Also, available 
representations of equity concerns in the control variables were identified. In order to understand the rationale behind these oper­
ationalizations better, we listed potential concerns about the choice of a specific variable, as well as the reason and possible concerns 
about the use of achievement scores in the operationalization of equity as raised by the researchers of the study. 
Reflections. Finally, codes for concerns about the use of an already-existing dataset were added. 
When no information on a code was given, it was considered as not taken into account and scored accordingly. The explicit coding 
scheme needed to result in a reliable and valid coding procedure. Therefore, initial codes underwent modification early in the process 
to accommodate the diversity we encountered in literature. In addition, we engaged in extensive discussions to ensure that the codes 
were comprehensive. Once ambiguities in the coding scheme were addressed, the first author classified the identified articles. In the 
presence of any doubt about the allocation of a specific code to one or more article, all the articles were reclassified independently on 
this particular code by one of the co-authors. Full consensus was sought in this procedure. Due to its size and complexity, the full 
summary table of the coded information is divided into two separate, more comprehensible tables in the appendices. Appendix A 
provides the conceptualization codes, and Appendix B provides the operationalization codes. 
Based on the coded conceptualization and operationalization of equity, the studies were examined in terms of interconnectedness 
of the codes in order to display patterns, interesting features, and apparent inconsistencies. We began with detailed inspection of the 
codes, gradually identifying recurring themes. By constantly comparing the theoretical structures we were developing against the 
codes in the reviewed articles, we attempted to specify the categories of our analysis and the relationships between them. For every 
category we generated a synthetic construct, which is the result of a transformation of the underlying findings into a new conceptual 
form. This allows to differentiate between several disparate aspects of how educational equity is interpreted in the reviewed body of

3. Results 
In the following section of this paper, we draw on the review of scholarly research work and engage the relevant studies in making 
sense of equity as an educational concept within the field of ILSAs. We deductively chart the conceptual and operational diversity 
among the interpretations of equity as clustered into five major research approaches. Each equity approach is labeled with a unique 
identifier, invoked in order to distinguish the approach by its core trait. These core traits are determined by the equity concern present 
in the chosen operationalization of the reviewed article. Such individualization of approaches within the concept of equity constitutes 
a possibility of making fine-grained analyses about the meaning and measure of equity. Nevertheless, within the reviewed literature, 
the borders of these identifiers have been deliberately transgressed for ideological reasons. A single approach is often regarded as 
insufficient when it comes to unpacking such a contested concept; combinations of approaches are not unusual. 
The following analysis is delivered in two installments. Part one discusses the five approaches to equity, while part two goes beyond 
this strict separation of the five approaches by addressing their resemblances and how they may be seen as interconnected in order to 
acknowledge equity’s complexity. In each part, both conceptualization and operationalization are discussed. We use this way of 
presenting the data in order to offer a transparent navigation through the meaning of the concept of equity within research on ILSA 
data, as well as to facilitate subsequent understanding of its morphing state. This section is not comprehensive, but it meets its aim of 
providing a vigorous synopsis of what lies at the heart of equity in its inquiry through ILSAs. 
3.1. Approaches toward educational equity 
As stated, a review of the relevant scholarly literature led to the deductive identification of five major research approaches toward 
equity based on operationalization (see Fig. 2): (1) ability equity, (2) social equity, (3) ethnic equity, (4) gender equity, and (5) equity 
of treatment. 
In adopting a certain approach, each scholarly work provides a picture of how it understands educational equity. In such pictures of 
equity, as presented in the first four approaches, we find the roots of the following idea: The concept of educational equity is centered 
around differences in student characteristics. The meaning of these student characteristics boils down to the student’s background 
within social equity, ethnic equity and gender equity, and, more specifically, to unalterable background traits in ethical equity and 
gender equity. Equity of treatment, on the other hand, has its place in the entirety of more tangible and adaptable aspects of education. 
We consolidated important information regarding the definition and measurement of equity, classified by approach, in Table 5. This 
outline will be used to make sense of each approach in itself. 
Before describing each of these approaches separately, we need to touch on a linguistic aspect that manifested itself while reading 
the selected articles. To speak to the subject of interest, the terms ‘equity’ and ‘equality’ were often used interchangeably. In this 
respect, it stood out that the word ‘equity’ was used more often in a positive formulation, while ‘equality’ was usually used in a 
negative formulation. We will elaborate upon this finding in word usage as we discuss the results.

3.1.1. Ability equity 
When an approach toward equity dealt exclusively with a measure for achievement or ability, it was labeled as ability equity. We 
found the equity concern ability to be the basis of operationalization in five of the 25 articles (20%). Although the students’ perfor­
mance, as obtained through the achievement tests, was always the central element in this approach, the charted ways to translate these 
achievement scores into the operationalization of inequality displayed fluctuations. Four of the five studies used multiple oper­
ationalizations, which are situated on either the student level or the country level. Researchers examined the inequality of educational 
outcomes through the standard deviations of students’ scores, percentile deviations of students’ scores, mean students’ scores at the 
country level or for a certain group of students, or by using an individual student’s scores in their analyses. The idea that high 
achievement is a desired outcome of education was often seen as the reason to pursue this equity concern. It goes without saying that 
the varied translations of the students’ performance scores in the operationalization entails miscellaneous analyses. Inequality could 
take the shape of within country-variation, of a reference to the existing gap, or more specifically, by constructing (for example) a Gini 
index for achievement (see Parker et al., 2018). 
This line of reasoning can be extended to conceptualization, with two implicit conceptual definitions where we could derive 
meaning from the words “achievement gap” (Horn et al., 2006) or “inequality [of achievement] irrespective of its sources” (Gromada 
et al., 2019, p. 2), and one implicit measure definition that describes “[their] measure of educational [inequality]” as “the degree of 
variation in performance” (Parker et al., 2018, p. 837). The article by Burroughs et al. (2019) contained an explicit definition. Spe­
cifically, the authors stated that “the issue of educational equity” entails that “two countries can have very similar mean achievement 
but dramatically different distributions in achievement” (p. 101). L´opez Rup´erez et al. (2019) also formulated their definition 
explicitly. Noticeably, apart from this last article, when approaching equity through ability, the authors of the reviewed articles almost 
consistently use the word ‘inequality’ to address the core of their research. Disparities in student learning outcomes an sich are thus the 
core concern within this approach. 
3.1.2. Social equity 
By adding the element of socioeconomic status (SES) to the ability mixture described above, we arrive at the approach of social 
equity. Within this approach, both the formulation ‘equity’ and ‘inequality’ are used. The relationship between achievement and 
socioeconomic background is addressed conceptually in four different ways in 18 articles (72% of the total reviewed). For the authors 
of 11 of these 18 articles, equity involves the relationship itself. We deduced this from the nine implicit definitions (five concept, three 
measure, one reference). The other two articles in this cluster remain due to a definition in their theoretical framework. In their 
methodological explanations, however, the statements in which they reference a “regression coefficient for students’ ESCS” (Benito 
et al., 2014, p.114), and state that “larger coefficients imply that […] family-[…]level variables affect […] academic performance to a 
larger extent” (Tsai et al., 2017, p. 79) also focus on the relationship itself. We see this idea reflected in the implicit measure definitions 
from, for example, Horn (2009): "[T]he educational inequality of opportunity is understood as the effect of socioeconomic background 
on performance, controlled for the exogenous variables" (p. 345). The authors that formulate an implicit concept definition use phrases 
such as, “more equity is reached when keeping the relation between achievement and individual background to a minimum” (Bellens 
et al., 2019, p. 4), and “the strength of these relationships needs to be reduced” (Gustafsson et al., 2018, p. 16), to make their point. 
It would appear that this description of the aforementioned relationship between socioeconomic background and academic 
achievement fully fits this charted approach of social equity. However, a deeper examination revealed a certain degree of fluctuation 
across the conceptualizations. Specifically, of the remaining seven articles dealing with social equity, one study specifies equity as the 
degree to which the student is able to counterbalance the relationship between a disadvantaged socioeconomic background and 
achievement (implicit definition; Agasisti et al., 2017), while five other reviewed studies state that such equity is about the school 
counterbalancing this relationship. Of these five articles, one contained an implicit measure definition, one an implicit reference 
definition, and three an explicit definition. The explicit definition in the work of L´opez Rup´erez et al. (2019) delineates the importance

3.1.3. Ethnic equity 
The picture of ethnic equity is easier to take in at a glance. The overarching idea concerns the impact of ethnicity on student 
achievement. ‘Equity’ is the most common formulation in this cluster of seven articles (28%), although this pattern became blurry in 
the more recent studies where one used ‘equality’ and another ‘inequality.’ Five of these seven articles, we had already encountered in 
the description of social equity (i.e., Bellens et al., 2019; Bellens et al., 2020; Gilleece et al., 2010; Gromada et al., 2019; ¨Ozdemir, 
2016); however, despite their additional reliance on the equity concern ethnicity they did not formulate an additional definition. At 
this point, we need to acknowledge the general language used in these definitions, referring to ‘student background’ without further 
specifications. Contrarily, the remaining article of Xu and Dronkers (2016), with a sole focus on ethnic equity, using phrases such as 
“migrant children are either denied access to local public schools or charged a large amount of extra fees” (p. 274), creates a fairly 
specific conceptual definition, making it an outlier within the set of articles on ethnic equity. 
In terms of operationalization, we charted two variables used to investigate ethnicity empirically. This amounts to language spoken 
at home in five of the articles, and migration status in four of them; in all cases, they were used as categorical variables at the student 
level. As with social equity, and almost every article that we will discuss in the remainder of this paper, the measure for ethnicity was 
combined with a measure of achievement. 
3.1.4. Gender equity 
The influence of gender on achievement is the starting point for the fourth equity approach: gender equity. In these seven articles 
(28%), ‘equity’ again is the chosen formulation. Based on our analysis of the literature, we identified seven definitions, of which one is 
a measurement definition, two are reference definitions, two are conceptual definitions, and two are explicit definitions. Five of these 
articles have been mentioned above, and use rather general words to define equity (Gamboa & Waltenberg, 2012; Gilleece et al., 2010; 
Gromada et al., 2019; Saarinen et al., 2020; ¨Ozdemir, 2016). Zhu et al. (2018) and Meinck and Brese (2019), on the other hand, did 
design the conceptualization more specifically toward gender equity by stating explicitly that, for example, “differences in achieve­
ment between female and male students, often termed the gender gap, have always been of interest” (Meinck & Brese, 2019, p. 1). It 
should not be surprising that a measure for gender is consistently chosen as the operationalization in combination with a measure of 
student achievement. This gender variable takes the form of a dichotomous variable at the student level. 
3.1.5. Equity of treatment 
The final equity approach offers an expansion beyond the focus on differences enclosing student characteristics. We shall call the 
whole - consisting of school types, locations, sectors, opportunities to learn, and the actions into which education is woven - equity of 
treatment. Within the seven articles on equity of treatment (28% of the whole), the vocabulary pivots on both ‘equity’ and ‘inequality.’ 
In this context, only one article has not been referenced above, and will appear to be an anomaly in this systematic review because of its 
operationalization. Lafontaine et al. (2015) formulate in a measure definition that they “will consider as more equitable the education 
system with smaller variance between schools in opportunity to learn” (p. 2). Even though the other articles also use the equity concern 
treatment in their measurement and therefore surpass the overcoming of student background, they do not reflect this in great detail in 
their definition. When looking for clues, however, some words may point in this direction. For example, Gamboa and Waltenberg 
(2012) described parental education (i.e., social equity), gender (i.e., gender equity), and school type (i.e., equity of treatment) as 
“source[s] of unfair inequalities” (p. 695). Gilleece et al. (2010), as we discussed, wanted to look beyond the composite ESCS index, 
and found in the literature that some school characteristics could also emulate equity. The explicit definition of ¨Ozdemir (2016) talks 
about an “unequal treatment” (p. 197), which can be interpreted broadly. In the methodology section of Tsai et al. (2017), we can read 
that “larger coefficients imply [that] school level variables affect […] academic performance to a larger extent” (p. 79). 
While counterbalancing the focus on student features in the aforementioned four approaches, researchers inquiring into equity of 
treatment obtained their information partly from school questionnaires. The variable of school type (i.e., private or public) was used in 
three of the seven articles; school location (i.e., rural or urban) in three; and school sector (i.e., secondary, comprehensive or voca­
tional) in one. They were assimilated as categorical variables at the student level. This information from the school questionnaire is 
completed with a measure for student achievement in but for two studies. Both L´opez Rup´erez et al. (2019) and Lafontaine et al. (2015) 
obtained their information from the student questionnaire instead of the school questionnaire. With this information, L´opez Rup´erez 
et al. (2019) linked indices of treatment, prepared by PISA, such as the index of teacher support or of adaptive instruction, to 
achievement scores. Here, we collide with the anomaly of Lafontaine et al. (2015): They are the only authors in this collection of 
reviewed articles that do not use any measure of student achievement. Alternatively, intraclass correlations of the dimensions of 
opportunity to learn were computed to make statements about equity.

3.2.1.1. The conceptualization of equity. Studies were coded into one of the three following categories regarding their equity frame­
work:horizontal equity, vertical equity, or equal educational opportunities. However, when an article did not fit into one of these 
broader equity frameworks, an equality framework was coded: equality of access, equality of opportunities, equality of treatment, or 
equality of outcome (i.e., equal educational opportunities). The framework that we could extrapolate from the equity definition in the 
majority of the studies (76%) was equal educational opportunities; this underpins a strive for equality of outcomes. Given all the 
references to the importance of background not having an influence on achievement (e.g., “equity in education will not be achieved 
until differences are eliminated between groups in society in terms of performance“ (Gilleece et al., 2010, p. 475), and “the degree of 
variation in performance within a nation” (Parker et al., 2018, p. 837)), this will not come as a surprise. 
Six of the 25 studies (24%) deviate from this. Gamboa and Waltenberg (2012), ¨Ozdemir (2016), Sulis et al. (2020) and Zhu et al. 
(2018) enclose the vertical equity framework in their explicit definition (whether or not through reference). Their word choices 
strongly allude to the idea of unequal but equitable treatment of those with different needs. Xu and Dronkers (2016) are more in line 
with the framework of equality of opportunities, since they deal with the attendance of migrant children at public schools. Finally, the 
framework of equality of treatment is reflected in the work of Lafontaine et al. (2015) with their focus on opportunity to learn; 
however, considering the (often general) use of language in the identified definitions, frameworks were hard to fathom. Specifications 
were namely mostly limited to the word ‘student’s background’ (e.g., Agasisti et al., 2017; Bol et al., 2014). 
We sought to explore not only the content of the conceptualization, but also possible foundations for choices in this conceptual­
ization. In this regard, the source of the article was coded as the type of journal in which it was published (see Appendix A). This coding 
could be delineated to five different sources. Seventeen of the 25 charted articles were published in an education-related journal. Of 
these 17 articles, eight contained the formulation ‘equity,’ while the nine others included a formulation surrounding the term 
‘inequality.’ What is striking about this is that more recent studies use this term more often in positive formulations (e.g., equality; 
Saarinen et al., 2020; Teng, 2020). Five articles were published in a sociological journal, and preference seems to have been given to 
the use of the word ‘inequality?’ The remaining three articles were divided over a methodological journal, an economics journal, and 
an education-oriented book. An explicit link between journal type and the explicitness index of the definition, the overarching 
framework, and the equity approach was not found. However, what stands out is that the two articles that did not include a definition 
in their theoretical framework make use of the term ‘inequality.’ 
To conclude the discussion of conceptualization of equity, it is important to state that only two concerns were found in the articles 
regarding their defining of equity. This may be due to the limited degree to which equity was explicitly defined. 
3.2.1.2. The operationalization of equity. The operational choices in inquiries into equity decipher some of its theoretical vagueness. 
The preference for certain variables creates a more detailed picture of what approaches to equity were incorporated. Noticeable was 
that operationalizations related to ability equity or equity of treatment did not include elements or variables of student background. 
Regarding these choices for specific variables, a total of seven articles convey the concern that “other types of inequality” (Burroughs 
et al., 2019, p. 102) - that is, equity concerns other than the ones incorporated into their measure for equity - could also be important. 
The considerable interest shown to students’ academic achievement, as part of equity analyses, calls for a closer look. When it came 
to examining the reasons for adopting achievement scores, arguments were raised by 17 of the 25 articles (68%). The most common 
(32%) clarification was a reference to the idea that achievement is the desired outcome of education. Five articles (20%) found their 
justification through literature, and one (4%) claimed it to be a prerequisite for other effectiveness criteria, such as well-being and 
dropout rates in higher education. The three final ones (12%) referred to its societal importance, with a possible future impact on, for 
example, economic growth. Additionally, we examined possible concerns about the use of these achievement scores in research on 
equity. What we determined is that only one concern was raised, which related to questions about the multidimensionality of edu­
cation and the idea that achievement is a possible reduction of educational outcomes. Perhaps even more interesting is that this 
concern is raised in only two of the 25 reviewed articles (8%). 
Regarding the adoption of data from the ILSA studies, concerns in regard to this adaptation were raised among a majority of the 
researchers. One of the components that was most often questioned regards the sampling procedure that does not allow for class-level 
variation (24%). Also, concerns about the cross-sectional design that does not cover educational history (16%), nor which allows the 
establishment of causal relations (24%), are mentioned more often than others. These other concerns relate to the level of compa­
rability across countries (24%), possible limitations because of missing values (8%), the lack of a measure for preferred variables 
(16%), and an unsatisfactory quality of the measure (12%). Of the total set of articles, five (20%) did not report any thoughts on 
working with an already existing dataset. 
Perusing the use of ILSA data, it becomes noticeable that the majority of equity research is based on PISA data (76%). This pattern 
remained rather consistent over time. Five of the 25 articles (20%) worked with TIMSS data, and one (4%) was based on PIRLS data. 
Not a single charted article combined data from other ILSA studies. A pattern that seems to be developing in the more recent time frame 
is the growth of studies based on TIMSS data. However, this cannot be confirmed by the current review, and will have to be revisited at 
a later date. Furthermore, of the charted studies working on TIMSS or PIRLS data, we found that their focus is centered around the 
concepts of equity and quality, while studies on PISA data have a more exclusive focus on equity.

the combination of equity concerns does not seem to follow a specific pattern. We are thus able to identify pieces of equity, but the 
overall picture remains incomplete. Inundated with so much diverse information, we found it difficult to discern the precise nature of 
the discourse on educational equity. Here, we encounter equity in its morphing state. Different articles combined different equity 
concerns, and thus different approaches in different ways, to shape their discourse on equity. 
4. Discussion 
We began this review by outlining the educational quest for equity, its accompanying political discussion, and the associated 
theoretical boundaries of the concept of equity itself. Subsequently, we emphasized the importance of secondary analyses to the results 
of highly standardized ILSAs in political decision-making about educational equity. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic 
review of the interpretation of educational equity in secondary analyses drawing on data from ILSAs. Our goal was to understand 
patterns in this specific literature, rendering the concept more accessible in educational research. 
Before going into the insights garnered from this systematic review, we need to revisit delimitations deemed necessary from the 
outset of the review process. As a first such limitation, we would like to point out that equity is an important part of the story, but that it 
is not all of it. In the end, we are looking for social justice, which did not have a place in this review. Here, we think about the choice of 
keywords. It may well be that we have missed articles dealing with this topic; however, this choice was made consciously, and not only 
to limit the number of articles included in the review, but considering that the concept of social justice often refers to equity cir­
cumstances outside of education-related topics. A similar point can be made for the keyword quality. Although educational quality and 
equity are strongly intertwined, we wanted to keep the focus sharply fixed on equity. Given our requirement to incorporate only peer- 
reviewed articles, a second delimitation arises which implies that the OECDiLibrary, as another predominant resource for research in 
the field of ILSAs, could not be selected as a database for this review. Another potential limitation is the selection of international 
comparative assessments that has been put under review. We chose to only include the main, ongoing and globally assessed ILSAs. This 
could mean that information from certain countries may be over- or underrepresented, which in turn could lead to a selection bias. 
The framing question of the discussion is: “What patterns are to be found in the way researchers define and measure equity in their 
inquiries through ILSA data?” We encountered five research approaches toward equity: ability equity, social equity, ethnic equity, 
gender equity, and equity of treatment. In this discussion, an elaboration is made on the similarities and differences in choices made 
regarding the conceptualization and operationalization of the idea. This, likewise, requires consideration of the concept’s complexity. 
To conclude the article, suggestions are put forth in order to further academic thinking about equity. 
4.1. The conceptual fabric 
What is confusing about this topic is the uniform use of words in the definitions of equity. Equity is frequently defined in terms that 
are broad and nonspecific. The definition then pivots on the undesirable connection between the student’s background and 
achievement, leaving out any specifications concerning what is meant by this term, ‘background’ (Bourdieu, 1986; Kellaghan, 2001, 
pp. 3–22). What equity involves, exactly, is left to the imagination and preferences of the person in question. However, what did 
become clear in the present study is that equity concerns and achievement work together as an indivisible team in attempts to capture 
the concept (Milner, 2010). Moreover, only a limited number of authors formulated their definition of equity explicitly. Although it

may be fair to assume that explicit definitions are more desirable than implied ones (Dinsmore et al., 2008), the large amount of

meaning. This does not, however, diminish the fact that the idea of ‘student background’ is in great need of further elaboration. 
Further, the conceptualizations exhibit a number of inadequacies. First, the role of schools in counterbalancing educational 
inequity, as distinct from the responsibility of the child, is not always recognized (Charalambous et al., 2018; Field et al., 2007; Levin, 
2003). Second, the term that is being defined (viz., equity) is often also used in the definition. Third, little attempt is made to identify 
the impediments and facilities that might be key to understanding how the appointed equity concerns may or may not foster inequity. 
In this context, we think, for example, such as elements like perceptions and stereotyping, which again are multifaceted. 
To conclude, it can be stated that the conceptualization of equity in its inquiry drawing on ILSAs starts to look like a fabric: Un­
derneath, all sorts of cluttered things are happening, but on the surface the look is serene and simple. However, we ask ourselves: What 
would be gained by this assimilation of expressions? 
4.2. The methodological epiphany 
Efforts to operationalize the concept of equity may also be a reason for leaving out the underlying mechanisms creating inequity. 
For example, dealing with diversity entails motivating every student to reach their own zone of proximal development and is depended 
on teachers’ perceptions on, among other things, growth (Dweck, 2006; Tomlinson, 2014; Vygotsky, 1978). For this reason, it may be 
that the measurement is heavily steered toward the measurable parts of diversity (Biesta, 2009). Although ILSAs do not always provide 
a clear measure of those deeper affect levels or details regarding students’ educational histories, these elements that are harder to grasp 
seem to be easily neglected in both the conceptualization and operationalization of equity through ILSA data. On top of this, the 
mechanisms that take place in how equity concerns influence achievement are very rarely questioned. The influence of equity concerns 
on achievement as such remains a black box. 
Nevertheless, thanks to differences in methodology (and hence in the operationalization of equity and the choice of variables), we 
were able to distinguish five approaches. The methodology has thus created an epiphany amid the conceptual haze. A distinction was 
made here between types of background, and it became clear that talking about equity is not always limited to that background. The 
way schools deal with diversity became part of the story from this point on. Additionally, the operationalizations provide confirmation 
that the inquiry of equity through ILSAs inherently involves the relationship between background and achievement (Bower, 2013; 
Kyriakides & Creemers, 2011; Milner, 2010; Wood et al., 2011). The significance of this relationship clearly shapes the thinking about 
equity in this collection of literature. In a similar manner, we could open up a discussion here about gap-gazing, as equity cannot be 
reduced to a simple gap in achievement. In fact, we could see such a gap as more equitable education (Bower, 2013; Kyriakides & 
Creemers, 2011; Van Damme & Bellens, 2017), but a correlation between achievement and student characteristics unrelated to effort 
or talent can be considered as inequity, based on the equity conceptualizations found. 
4.3. The discomfort of complexity 
Still, when taking a glance at the measurement of equity in all of its aspects, we encounter the inevitable discomfort of its 
complexity. Looking at the separate equity approaches momentarily simplifies the concept for purposes of description, making the 
concept amenable; however, the concept of equity cannot be pigeonholed simply within one equity approach. Multiple studies 
combined equity concerns to look for educational equity. When looking more closely into the design of the concept of equity, the 
approaches become intimately intertwined. 
Within the scarcity of characterizations that do not require endless contextualization lies the assertion that equity is a multi- 
paradigmatic enterprise. Its identity is configured at the intersection of the various approaches to the concept. Even more so, we 
believe it to be found at the intersection of the mechanisms causing the effect of the various equity concerns. In our attempts to get to 
the bottom of this, we got the feeling of being stuck. We must keep in mind that equity remains a subjective, sociological and phil­
osophically rooted concept (Castelli et al., 2012; Charalambous et al., 2018; Contini & Cugnata, 2020). By enriching the (often lacking 
or very basic) conceptual framework surrounding equity research on ILSA data, it can be hoped that a language is acquired which can 
stimulate the epistemological discussion of educational equity and guide future study. 
How equity is defined and measured in its inquiry on data from ILSAs has consequences for governments that use this research to 
justify policy decisions in their struggle to improve the country’s educational equity. The fact is, this equity research discusses 
delineated parts of educational equity, and therefore, should be understood through the delimitations posed by inevitable choices 
made in both their conceptualization and operationalization, through which the complex, latent concept is translated into a 
measurable, overt one. Consequently, no statements ought to be made about educational equity as a whole, though it is possible to 
discuss a country’s equity within a single or a combination of specific research approaches. This appears to be merely a linguistic 
problem, yet words have a profound effect on what is pondered, and should therefore be chosen carefully. Governments should be 
rigorous and conscientious in reading and discussing the results of this body of equity research. 
4.4. The future 
What patterns are to be found in the way researchers define and measure equity in its inquiry through ILSA data? The present study 
describes an odyssey through empirical research based on ILSA data, which pivots on making statements about educational equity. We

encountered five research approaches, which were combined in a seemingly random manner. To sharpen this ensemble conceptually 
and lay a foundation for a strong measurement, further struggle with the concept is necessary. To conclude our observations and 
arguments, we put forward two steps that could be taken in the future to ensure that the literature adds optimally to our understanding 
of equity in education. 
First, different views on the nature of equity have consequences for the operationalization of the concept. That is to say, the 
ontology of equity cannot be separated from its epistemology. Still, the lack of a clear ontology leads to a lack of overall consensus 
about how it can best be measured. This puts the field at risk of losing a coherent view and direction for the future. The absence of a 
strong focus on choice of words is problematic for two reasons. First and foremost, the terms ‘equity’ and ‘equality’ do not entail the 
same meaning. Second, equality refers to sameness, but then again: “Equality of what?” (Sen, 1992, p. 12), knowing that not all 
sameness is perusable. The choice that remains is therefore between pursuing equity and counterbalancing inequality. This choice, 
again, should inspire researchers to rethink the purpose of and need for underlying frameworks. Additionally, further research could 
extrapolate on how the term ‘social justice’ could be welcomed into the discourse. 
Second, as this body of literature uniformly agrees that the level of equity can be found in the relation between student background 
and student achievement, attention should be paid to the mechanisms connecting equity concerns to achievement. This should lead to 
the discovery of different layers of a new theoretical framework for educational equity. How does the influence of equity concerns on 
achievement emerge out of interactions between the child, the environment, and the teacher. At the present juncture, little is known 
about these mechanisms. We found that the field could benefit from a broader perspective, with equity as an embedded, enacted and 
constructed concept. With such a framework, researchers may be better equipped to inquire into equity in education. 
As research on educational equity continues to flourish, it becomes increasingly important for those engaged in such research to 
have a shared basis for communicating their thinking about equity. This includes both a common lexicon and a comprehensive 
theoretical framework. Of course, and in line with Alexander et al. (1991), as long as researchers engage in the study of complex 
constructs, there will always be differences in the way these constructs are understood. This article does not argue that these differ­
ences should be eliminated; rather, by gaining visibility of the diversity of views and stimulating a dialogue within the research 
community, it may lead to a shared understanding of the construct, and further academic thinking about educational equity.
